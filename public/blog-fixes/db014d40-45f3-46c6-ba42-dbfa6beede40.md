# ECG Made Easy

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Cardiology (electrocardiography)**

> **In one line:** the ECG records the heart's electrical activity as a series of deflections. A wave of depolarisation moving towards a lead's positive electrode gives an upward deflection and moving away gives a downward one, so reading it in a fixed order (calibration, rate, rhythm, axis, then each wave and interval) turns the tracing into rhythm, conduction, ischaemia and electrolyte diagnoses.

## What you must know

- Standard speed is **25 mm/s** and standard calibration is **10 mm = 1 mV**. One large square = 0.2 s; one small square = 0.04 s.
- Regular rate = **300 / RR interval in large squares**. Irregular rhythm: count R waves on the 10 s rhythm strip and **multiply by 6**.
- Normal **PR 0.12 to 0.2 s** (3 to 5 small squares); normal **QRS under 0.12 s**; normal axis **-30 to +90 degrees**.
- **Pathological Q wave:** over 2 small squares deep and/or over 1 small square wide, or present in leads other than I, II, III, aVL, V5, V6.
- **LVH:** S in V1 plus R in V6 over 35 mm.
- MI evolution: peaked T and ST rise (hours), T inversion (24 hours), Q waves (days).
- AF: irregularly irregular, no P waves. VF: no cardiac output, needs immediate defibrillation.

## 1. Performing an ECG

**GRIP** is the approach to the patient: **G**reet, rapport, introduce, identify, privacy, explain the procedure, permission.

1. Lay the patient down; expose the chest, wrists and ankles.
2. Clean the electrode sites (shave if needed) and apply the self-adhesive dots.
3. Clip the leads onto the dots (**10 electrodes** give the 12 leads).
4. Turn on the machine, calibrate to 10 mm/mV, set the rate to 25 mm/s, record and print.
5. Label the tracing: name, date of birth, hospital number, date and time, reason for recording.
6. Disconnect and remove the electrodes if the tracing is adequate.

Tips: ask the patient to relax completely, because **skeletal muscle activity appears as interference**. If the trace is poor, check that the dots are stuck down. If the machine is unfamiliar, ask ward staff.

### Electrode placement

- **4 limb electrodes:** right arm (red), left arm (yellow), left leg (green), right leg (black). Mnemonic: **Ride Your Green Bike**. The right leg electrode is a **neutral (earth)**.
- **6 chest electrodes (V1 to V6):**

| Lead | Position |
|---|---|
| V1 | 4th intercostal space, right sternal edge |
| V2 | 4th intercostal space, left sternal edge |
| V3 | Halfway between V2 and V4 |
| V4 | 5th intercostal space, mid-clavicular line (over the apex) |
| V5 | Level with V4, anterior axillary line |
| V6 | Level with V4 and V5, mid-axillary line |

> **Remember:** to find the 4th space, palpate the **manubriosternal angle (of Louis)**. The 2nd rib is directly adjacent, with the 2nd space just below; count down to the 4th.

## 2. Electrophysiology and how the ECG works

- Conduction: **sinoatrial node (pacemaker)** then across the atria, to the **AV node**, down the **bundle of His** along the interventricular septum, which splits into **right and left bundle branches**, then the **Purkinje fibres**.
- Electrodes sense the voltage change as current flows between a **positive and a negative electrode**.
  - Impulse **towards** the positive electrode: **positive deflection**.
  - Impulse **away** from it: **negative deflection**.
- The overall direction of depolarisation is the cardiac **axis**.

### The leads

| Plane | Leads | Type |
|---|---|---|
| Coronal (limb leads) | I, II, III | Bipolar |
| Coronal (limb leads) | aVR, aVL, aVF | Unipolar (augmented) |
| Transverse (chest leads) | V1 to V6 | Unipolar |

- **Lead I:** right arm negative, left arm positive.
- **Lead II:** right arm negative, left leg positive.
- **Lead III:** left arm negative, left leg positive.
- aVR, aVL and aVF are composite leads computed from the others. This is how 12 leads come from 9 electrodes plus the neutral.

### Which lead looks at which wall

| Leads | Area of heart |
|---|---|
| I, aVL (and II) | Lateral |
| II, III, aVF | Inferior |
| aVR | Right side of the heart |
| V1, V2 | Anterior / right ventricle (septum) |
| V3, V4 | Anterior and septal |
| V5, V6 | Lateral / left ventricle |

### Components of the trace

- **P wave:** atrial depolarisation (right atrium first, then left; the P is the sum of the two).
- **QRS:** ventricular depolarisation.
- **T wave:** ventricular repolarisation.

## 3. Systematic interpretation

1. **Check the patient details:** name, date of birth, time and date, indication (for example chest pain or routine pre-operative), any previous or subsequent ECGs, and whether it is part of a serial sequence.
2. **Calibration:** reference pulse (rectangular wave near the left of the paper) should be 10 mm tall = 1 mV. Paper speed 25 mm/s, so 25 small squares (5 large) = 1 second.
3. **Rate**
4. **Rhythm**
5. **Axis**
6. **Each element of the tracing in each lead:** P, PR, Q, QRS, ST, T, QT.

### Rate

- **Regular rhythm:** rate = 300 / number of large squares between R waves. Example: RR of 4 large squares gives 300/4 = **75 beats per minute**.
- **Irregular rhythm:** use the rhythm strip (usually lead II, 25 cm or 250 mm = 10 seconds). Count the R waves and **multiply by 6**.

### Rhythm

- **Is it regular?** Line the edge of a sheet of paper along the R wave tips, mark 3 or 4 of them, then slide it along. If later R waves line up with the marks, the rhythm is regular.
- **Sinus rhythm:** the impulse arises in the sinus node, so **every QRS is preceded by a P wave**. This does not mean every P is followed by a QRS (in second degree block some P waves are dropped, yet it is still a sinus rhythm, though not a normal sinus rhythm).
- **Sinus arrhythmia:** heart rate varies with respiration; it **speeds up on inspiration**. Inspiration lowers intrathoracic pressure, raising venous return to the right atrium. Atrial stretch triggers a brainstem reflex (**Bainbridge reflex**) with sympathetic activation. It is physiological and more marked in children and young adults.
- **Sinus bradycardia:** sinus origin, rate under 60/min.
- **Sinus tachycardia:** sinus origin, rate over 100/min.

### Axis

The axis is the overall direction of the wave of depolarisation. An abnormal axis can hint at pathology.

- **Normal axis:** -30 to +90 degrees (up to +120 degrees according to some texts).
- Outside this: **left axis deviation**, **right axis deviation**, or extreme axis deviation.
- **Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome** can cause either left or right axis deviation.

> **Remember:** mnemonic **"RAD RALPH the LAD from VILLA"**.

| Right axis deviation (RALPH) | Left axis deviation (VILLA) |
|---|---|
| **R**ight ventricular hypertrophy | **V**entricular tachycardia |
| **A**nterolateral MI | **I**nferior MI |
| **L**eft **P**osterior **H**emiblock | **L**eft ventricular hypertrophy |
| | **L**eft **A**nterior hemiblock |

Other recognised causes of right axis deviation include pulmonary embolism and chronic lung disease (cor pulmonale).

## 4. The waves and intervals

### P wave

- Atrial depolarisation. No hard rules, but a P wave **over 2.5 mm tall** or **longer than 0.08 s (2 small squares)** should arouse suspicion.

| Finding | Definition | Cause |
|---|---|---|
| **P pulmonale** | Tall P over 2.5 mm | Right atrial hypertrophy: pulmonary hypertension, pulmonary stenosis, tricuspid stenosis |
| **P mitrale** | P over 0.08 s and bifid (notched) | Left atrial hypertrophy and delayed left atrial depolarisation: mitral valve disease, LVH |

### PR interval

- Measured from the **start of the P wave to the start of the QRS** (to the start of the Q wave if one is present, not the R).
- Represents the time from atrial depolarisation to the start of ventricular depolarisation (mostly AV nodal delay).
- **Normal 0.12 to 0.2 s (3 to 5 small squares).**

**Short PR (under 3 small squares):** suggests an **accessory pathway** between atria and ventricles, so the ventricles depolarise early. Example: **Wolff-Parkinson-White (WPW) syndrome**, with the accessory **bundle of Kent**. Ventricular depolarisation through this pathway bypasses the fast conduction system, so it spreads slowly and gives a slow-rising **delta wave**, until normal rapid conduction takes over and a more normal complex follows.

**Long PR (over 5 small squares or 0.2 s): heart block (AV node block)**

| Degree | ECG finding |
|---|---|
| **First degree** | Constant PR over 0.2 s; every P is followed by a QRS. A longer than normal delay at the AV node |
| **Second degree, Mobitz type I (Wenckebach)** | PR widens with each beat until a QRS is dropped. "(W)one, Wenckebach, widens" |
| **Second degree, Mobitz type II** | PR constant, then a QRS is suddenly dropped |
| **Third degree (complete)** | No relationship between P waves and QRS complexes (AV dissociation) |

> **Exam tip:** Mobitz II and third degree block are the dangerous ones, with a risk of asystole or syncope (Stokes-Adams attacks); they usually need a pacemaker. Mobitz I is often benign, especially with high vagal tone or inferior MI.

### Q wave

A Q wave is **pathological** if:

- deeper than **2 small squares (0.2 mV)**, and/or
- wider than **1 small square (0.04 s)**, and/or
- seen in a lead other than III or the leads looking from the left (I, II, aVL, V5, V6), where small Q waves can be normal.

Pathological Q waves mark **dead myocardium (previous infarction)**.

### QRS complex

- **Height:** very tall complexes in chest leads suggest **LVH**. **S wave depth in V1 plus R wave height in V6 over 35 mm** confirms LVH.
- **Width:** should be **under 0.12 s (3 small squares)**; some texts say under 0.10 s (2.5 small squares). A wider QRS suggests a ventricular conduction problem, usually **bundle branch block**.

| | LBBB | RBBB |
|---|---|---|
| Pattern | "W" in V1 and/or "M" in V6 | "M" (RSR pattern) in V1 and/or "W" in V6 |
| Notes | New-onset LBBB with chest pain: consider MI. The ST segment cannot be interpreted | Partial RBBB with normal QRS width can occur in healthy people |

> **Remember:** mnemonic **WiLLiaM MaRRoW** (LBBB: W in V1, M in V6; RBBB: M in V1, W in V6). Bundle branch block is due to infarction or fibrosis (ageing).

### ST segment

- Should sit on the **isoelectric line**. Planar (flat) elevation or depression is abnormal.
- **Planar elevation:** MI or **Prinzmetal's (vasospastic) angina**.
- **Planar depression:** ischaemia.
- ST elevation that is slanted (upsloping) may not be significant.
- **Raised ST in most leads**, especially **saddle-shaped**, suggests **pericarditis**, often with **PR segment depression**.

### T wave

- **Tall T waves** (generally not taller than half the preceding QRS; no definite rule): **hyperkalaemia**, acute MI.
- **Flat T waves:** **hypokalaemia**.
- **Inverted T waves:** **ischaemia**.

### QT interval

- Measured from the **start of the QRS to the end of the T wave**. It shortens as the heart rate rises.
- Corrected: **QTc = QT / square root of RR interval** (Bazett's formula).
- Normal QTc **0.38 to 0.42 s**. Normally QT is under 2 large squares (0.4 s) at 60 beats per minute.
- **Short QTc:** may indicate hypercalcaemia.
- **Long QTc:** many causes (for example hypocalcaemia, hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia, drugs such as macrolides, antipsychotics and antiarrhythmics, congenital long QT). It raises the risk of arrhythmia, notably torsades de pointes.

### U wave

- Follows the T wave and is often hard to see.
- Prominent U waves: **hypokalaemia**, hyperthyroidism.

> **Check:** the slides attribute the U wave to repolarisation of the atrial septum. The commonly accepted explanations are repolarisation of the Purkinje fibres or papillary muscles (or late mid-myocardial repolarisation); the origin is not settled.

## 5. Myocardial infarction on the ECG

**Evolution of changes:**

| Time | Change |
|---|---|
| Within hours | T wave may become peaked; ST segment begins to rise |
| Within 24 hours | T wave inverts (may or may not persist); ST elevation starts to resolve. If a **left ventricular aneurysm** forms, ST elevation may persist |
| Within a few days | **Pathological Q waves** form and usually persist |

**Site of infarct (leads affected):**

| Site | Leads |
|---|---|
| Inferior | II, III, aVF |
| Anteroseptal | V1 to V4 |
| Anterolateral | V4 to V6, I, aVL |
| Posterior | Tall, wide R and ST depression in V1 and V2 (mirror image) |

> **Exam tip:** a posterior MI looks like the mirror image of an anterior MI when viewed from V1 and V2. Always look for reciprocal ST depression in the opposite leads.

## 6. Arrhythmias

### Supraventricular tachycardias (SVT)

- The impulse starts in the atria (SA node, atrial wall) or AV node.
- With normal ventricular conduction the QRS is **narrow** (narrow complex tachycardia).
- With a ventricular conduction problem such as LBBB, the QRS is broad (a form of broad complex tachycardia).

### Atrial fibrillation (AF)

- May be tachycardic; rhythm **irregularly irregular**.
- **No discernible P waves**, with a shaky (fibrillating) baseline.
- Cause: no order to atrial depolarisation; different areas of the atrium depolarise at will.

### Atrial flutter

- **Saw-tooth baseline** rising above and dipping below the isoelectric line.
- Caused by circular (re-entry) circuits of depolarisation in the atria.
- Slides give an atrial rate of 250/min.

> **Check:** the typical atrial rate in flutter is about **300/min**, usually with 2:1 AV block giving a ventricular rate of about 150/min.

### Ventricular tachycardia (VT)

- **Wide, irregularly shaped QRS complexes**.
- Usually secondary to **infarction**: re-entry circuits form in damaged myocardium and cause recurrent early depolarisation of the ventricles.
- A cause of **broad complex tachycardia**; it must be distinguished from SVT with aberrant conduction. If in doubt, treat as VT.

### Ventricular fibrillation (VF)

- Completely disordered ventricular depolarisation, **not compatible with a cardiac output**.
- Irregular trace of broad complexes of varying width, height and rate. This is cardiac arrest: immediate CPR and defibrillation.

## 7. Other conditions to recognise

- **Pericarditis:** widespread saddle-shaped ST elevation, PR depression.
- **Hyperkalaemia:** tall peaked T waves (later wide QRS, loss of P waves).
- **Hypokalaemia:** flat T waves, prominent U waves, ST depression.
- **Hypercalcaemia:** short QT.
- **Long QT:** risk of torsades de pointes.
- **WPW:** short PR, delta wave, wide QRS.

## Key points to revise

1. Calibrate (10 mm/mV, 25 mm/s), then read rate, rhythm, axis, P, PR, Q, QRS, ST, T and QT in order.
2. Rate: 300 / large squares if regular; R waves on the 10 s strip x 6 if irregular.
3. Normal PR 0.12 to 0.2 s; short PR with a delta wave means WPW; long PR means heart block.
4. Mobitz I widens then drops (Wenckebach); Mobitz II drops without warning; third degree has no P to QRS relationship.
5. QRS over 0.12 s means bundle branch block (WiLLiaM MaRRoW); LVH when S in V1 plus R in V6 exceeds 35 mm.
6. MI: peaked T and ST rise, then T inversion, then Q waves; site is read from the leads involved.
7. AF has no P waves and is irregularly irregular; flutter is saw-tooth; VT is wide and fast; VF has no output.
8. Tall T for high potassium, flat T and U waves for low potassium, short QT for high calcium, long QTc risks arrhythmia.
