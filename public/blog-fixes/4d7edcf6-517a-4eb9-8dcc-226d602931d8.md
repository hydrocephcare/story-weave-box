## WEDNESDAY ## Principles of Rational Antimicrobial Prescribing & Infectious Diseases

## What is an Antimicrobial Drug?

An antimicrobial is any drug that fights microorganisms — tiny living things that cause disease. There are four types of microorganisms and a specific drug class for each:

- Bacteria → treated with **Antibiotics** - Viruses → treated with **Antivirals** - Fungi → treated with **Antifungals** - Parasites → treated with **Antiparasitics**

The word antimicrobial covers all of these. In this course, most of the focus is on antibiotics.

## Rational Prescribing — What Does it Mean?

Rational prescribing means giving the correct drug, to the correct patient, at the correct dose, for the correct duration, and only when it is truly needed. You do not give antibiotics randomly. You think before you prescribe. Wrong prescribing causes treatment failure, side effects, and drug resistance.

## How Does the Body Signal an Infection?

When bacteria or other organisms enter the body, the immune system responds with **inflammation**. The classic signs of inflammation are:

- Fever - Redness - Swelling - Pain - Loss of function in the affected area

The location of these signs tells you where the infection is. Different body sites show different signs:

| Site of Infection | Key Signs and Symptoms | |------------------|----------------------| | Brain and spinal cord (CNS) | Severe headache, stiff neck, sensitivity to light, high fever, confusion | | Lungs | Cough, difficulty breathing, chest pain, fever, sputum production | | Urinary tract | Burning when urinating, frequent urination, cloudy urine, flank pain (kidney involvement) | | Skin | Redness, warmth, swelling, pus, open sores | | Gut | Diarrhea, vomiting, abdominal pain, fever |

## Host Factors — The Patient Affects Drug Choice

Before prescribing, you must think about the patient. These patient-specific things are called host factors:

- **Age** — newborns and elderly have weaker immune systems. Some drugs harm children e.g. Tetracycline damages developing teeth and bones - **Pregnancy** — some drugs cross to the baby and cause harm. Avoid Tetracyclines and Fluoroquinolones in pregnancy. Safe options include Amoxicillin and Cephalexin - **Kidney function** — most drugs leave the body through the kidneys. Poor kidney function means drug builds up and becomes toxic. Dose must be reduced - **Liver function** — the liver breaks down many drugs. Liver disease means the drug stays in the body too long. Adjust dose accordingly - **Allergies** — if a patient is allergic to Penicillin, avoid all Penicillin-type drugs. Use a Macrolide (e.g. Azithromycin) instead - **Immune status** — a patient with HIV or on chemotherapy has a weak immune system and needs stronger, broader antimicrobial cover

## Empirical Therapy vs Definitive Therapy

This is one of the most tested concepts. Understand it clearly.

**Empirical therapy** means you start treatment before you even know exactly which organism is causing the infection. You make an educated guess based on the site of infection and the most likely organisms that infect that site. You start treatment immediately because waiting for lab results can cost the patient's life.

**Definitive therapy** means you now have the lab results. You know exactly which organism is causing the infection and which drug kills it. You then adjust the treatment to target that specific organism. This is called **de-escalation** — narrowing from a broad drug to a more specific one.

In short: - Empirical = treat first, based on best guess - Definitive = treat after lab confirms the organism - Always de-escalate once lab results return

## Isolating an Organism — How the Lab Confirms Infection

To find out exactly what is causing the infection, you collect a sample from the patient. This is called a **specimen**. Common specimens include:

- Blood - Urine - Sputum (mucus coughed up from lungs) - Wound swab - CSF (cerebrospinal fluid — fluid around the brain, collected by lumbar puncture)

The specimen is sent to the lab where they: 1. Grow the organism in a dish — this is called **culturing** 2. Identify the organism 3. Test which antibiotics kill it — this is called **sensitivity testing**

The final result is called a **Culture and Sensitivity (C&S)** report. It tells you exactly which drug to use.

## Antimicrobial Dosing

Getting the dose right is critical. Too little and the drug does not work. Too much and it becomes toxic.

- **Loading dose** — a high first dose given to quickly get the drug to a therapeutic level in the blood. Used when you need the drug to work fast - **Maintenance dose** — the regular dose given after the loading dose to keep the drug at the right level - **MIC (Minimum Inhibitory Concentration)** — the lowest concentration of a drug that stops bacteria from growing. The drug level in the body must stay above the MIC to be effective

Dosing is also affected by: - How severe the infection is - Where the infection is (some drugs do not penetrate certain sites well, e.g. the brain) - The patient's kidney and liver function - Body weight

## Treatment Failure — Why Does it Happen?

Sometimes a patient does not get better despite being on antibiotics. This is called treatment failure. Reasons include:

- Wrong drug chosen — the organism is resistant to it - Wrong dose — too low to kill the bacteria - Too short a duration — stopped before all bacteria were killed - Patient did not take the medication as prescribed (poor adherence) - The drug is not reaching the infection site e.g. some antibiotics do not penetrate the brain well - The patient's immune system is too weak to assist the drug - The patient has been reinfected

## Antimicrobial Toxicity — Side Effects of Key Drugs

Every drug has side effects. These are the most important ones to know:

| Drug | Major Side Effect | |------|------------------| | Aminoglycosides (e.g. Gentamicin) | Kidney damage (nephrotoxicity) and hearing damage (ototoxicity) | | Tetracyclines (e.g. Doxycycline) | Damages teeth and bones in children. Avoid in pregnancy and children under 8 | | Chloramphenicol | Aplastic anaemia (bone marrow stops making blood cells). In newborns — Gray Baby Syndrome (baby turns grey, collapses) | | Fluoroquinolones (e.g. Ciprofloxacin) | Tendon damage (especially Achilles tendon rupture). ## Antimicrobial Resistance (AMR) — A Major Global Problem

AMR means bacteria have changed in a way that makes them no longer killed by a drug that used to work. This is a major global health crisis.

**How does resistance happen?**

Bacteria are very adaptable. When exposed to antibiotics repeatedly or incorrectly, they develop strategies to survive:

- **Beta-lactamase production** — bacteria produce an enzyme that breaks down Penicillin-type drugs before the drug can work. This is the most common resistance mechanism - **Altered drug target** — the bacteria change the structure that the drug normally binds to, so the drug cannot attach - **Efflux pumps** — bacteria develop pumps that push the antibiotic out of the bacterial cell before it can cause damage - **Reduced permeability** — bacteria change their outer wall so the drug cannot enter

**How do we prevent AMR?**

- Only prescribe antibiotics when truly needed - Use narrow-spectrum antibiotics (drugs that target specific bacteria) rather than broad-spectrum when possible - Always complete the full course of antibiotics even if you feel better - Do not buy or take antibiotics without a prescription - In hospitals — strict hand washing, use of gloves and masks, sterilization of equipment - **Antimicrobial Stewardship Programs** — hospital programs that monitor and guide antibiotic use to reduce misuse

## Antimicrobial Prophylaxis

Prophylaxis means prevention. Antimicrobial prophylaxis means giving a drug to prevent an infection before it actually happens. You give the drug to a patient who does not yet have an infection but is at high risk of getting one.

**When is prophylaxis used?**

- **Surgical prophylaxis** — given 30 to 60 minutes before a surgical cut (incision) is made. This prevents bacteria from the skin entering the wound. The drug is stopped within 24 hours after surgery. Continuing longer than this increases resistance risk - **Post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP)** — given after exposure to a dangerous pathogen to prevent infection from developing. Example: giving ARV drugs after a needlestick injury in a healthcare worker exposed to HIV. Must be started within 72 hours - **Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP)** — given before expected exposure. Example: giving Tenofovir + Emtricitabine to HIV-negative people at high risk of HIV - **Recurrent UTI prophylaxis** — giving low-dose Nitrofurantoin or Trimethoprim long term to women who keep getting urinary tract infections - **Malaria prophylaxis** — giving Doxycycline or Atovaquone-Proguanil to travellers going to malaria-endemic areas

## CNS Infections — Meningitis

Meningitis means inflammation of the meninges — the three layers of tissue that cover and protect the brain and spinal cord. It is most commonly caused by infection.

**Types of meningitis:**

- **Bacterial meningitis** — the most serious type. It is a medical emergency. Can kill within hours if not treated - **Viral meningitis** — less severe, usually resolves on its own (self-limiting) - **Fungal meningitis** — mainly seen in patients with a very weak immune system, such as HIV patients with very low CD4 counts

**Common organisms by age group:**

| Patient Group | Most Likely Organism | |--------------|---------------------| | Newborns | Group B Streptococcus, Escherichia coli, Listeria monocytogenes | | Children | Neisseria meningitidis, Streptococcus pneumoniae | | Adults | Streptococcus pneumoniae, Neisseria meningitidis | | HIV patients | Cryptococcus neoformans (fungal) |

**Signs and symptoms of meningitis:** - Severe headache - High fever - Stiff neck — patient cannot touch chin to chest - Sensitivity to light (photophobia) - Sensitivity to sound (phonophobia) - Vomiting - Confusion or altered consciousness - Seizures - **Kernig's sign** — patient cannot straighten the knee when the hip is flexed at 90 degrees (pain and resistance) - **Brudzinski's sign** — when you flex the patient's neck, their knees automatically come up - **Petechial rash** — small red or purple spots on the skin that do not fade when pressed. This is specific to meningococcal (Neisseria meningitidis) meningitis and is a danger sign

**Treatment of meningitis:**

- Bacterial meningitis → **Ceftriaxone** intravenously (IV) — this is the drug of choice - If Listeria is suspected (newborns, elderly, immunocompromised) → add **Ampicillin** - Add **Dexamethasone** (a steroid) to reduce the brain inflammation and prevent complications like deafness - Fungal (Cryptococcal) meningitis → **Amphotericin B** + **Flucytosine** for 2 weeks, then switch to **Fluconazole** for maintenance (to prevent relapse)

## Respiratory Tract Infections (RTIs)

The respiratory tract includes the nose, throat, airways, and lungs. Infections can affect the upper or lower parts.

**Upper Respiratory Tract Infections (URTIs):** These affect the nose, sinuses, throat, and ears. Most are caused by viruses: - Common cold — viral, no antibiotics needed - Sinusitis — usually viral; only give antibiotics if bacterial signs persist beyond 10 days - Pharyngitis (sore throat) — mostly viral. If caused by Group A Streptococcus (Strep throat) → treat with **Penicillin V** or **Amoxicillin** - Otitis media (ear infection in children) — **Amoxicillin** is first line

**Lower Respiratory Tract Infections — Pneumonia:**

Pneumonia is infection of the lung tissue itself. The air sacs (alveoli) fill with pus and fluid, making it hard to breathe.

**Community-Acquired Pneumonia (CAP)** — pneumonia you get outside of hospital:

| Severity | Treatment | |----------|-----------| | Mild (treated at home) | Amoxicillin or Doxycycline | | Moderate (admitted to ward) | Amoxicillin-Clavulanate or Ceftriaxone | | Severe (ICU) | Ceftriaxone + Azithromycin or a Fluoroquinolone |

**Atypical pneumonia** is caused by organisms that do not show up on a standard Gram stain. Common atypical organisms include Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Chlamydophila pneumoniae, and Legionella pneumophila. These respond to **Azithromycin** or **Doxycycline**, not standard Penicillins.

**Hospital-Acquired Pneumonia (HAP)** — pneumonia developing 48 hours or more after hospital admission. More dangerous because hospital organisms are often resistant: - Common organisms: Pseudomonas aeruginosa, MRSA (Methicillin-Resistant Staphylococcus aureus), Klebsiella - Treatment: **Piperacillin-Tazobactam** or **Meropenem** — and if MRSA is suspected, add **Vancomycin**

## Viral Infections

Viruses are different from bacteria. They live inside human cells and hijack the cell's machinery to replicate. This makes them harder to treat because any drug that harms the virus may also harm the host cell. Antivirals are fewer in number than antibiotics.

**Malaria — extra detail because it is important:**

**Prevention of malaria:** - Insecticide-treated bed nets - Indoor residual spraying - Prophylactic drugs for travellers (Doxycycline, Atovaquone-Proguanil)

## HIV — Understanding the Basics First

HIV stands for **Human Immunodeficiency Virus**. It is a virus that attacks the immune system — specifically the **CD4+ T lymphocytes** (also called CD4 cells or T-helper cells). These cells are like the commanders of the immune system. They coordinate the body's response to infections.

HIV enters the CD4 cell, copies itself using the cell's machinery, and gradually destroys it. As CD4 cells are destroyed over time, the immune system gets weaker and weaker. The person becomes unable to fight off infections that a healthy person would handle easily. These are called **opportunistic infections** — infections that take the "opportunity" of a weakened immune system.

**AIDS (Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome)** is the advanced stage of HIV infection. A person is said to have AIDS when: - Their CD4 count falls below **200 cells per mm³** (normal is 500–1500), OR - They develop an AIDS-defining illness (a serious opportunistic infection or cancer)

**How HIV works — the viral lifecycle (important for understanding how drugs work):** 1. HIV attaches to the CD4 receptor on the T cell surface and enters the cell 2. HIV releases its RNA (its genetic material) into the cell 3. The enzyme **Reverse Transcriptase** converts HIV RNA into DNA 4. The enzyme **Integrase** inserts the viral DNA into the human cell's DNA 5. The cell reads the viral DNA and makes new HIV proteins 6. The enzyme **Protease** cuts these proteins into the right sizes to assemble new HIV particles 7. New HIV particles bud off and go infect more CD4 cells

Each step in this lifecycle is a target for antiretroviral drugs.

## Antiretroviral Therapy (ART) — Drug Classes

ART = Antiretroviral Therapy. These are drugs that target specific steps in the HIV lifecycle to stop the virus from replicating.

**HAART** stands for **Highly Active Antiretroviral Therapy**. It simply means using a combination of three or more antiretroviral drugs from different classes at the same time. Using one drug alone is never done because HIV quickly becomes resistant to it.

**Goals of ART:** - Suppress the viral load (amount of HIV in the blood) to undetectable levels - Allow the CD4 count to recover - Prevent opportunistic infections - Allow the patient to live a normal life - Prevent transmission to others

**The five drug classes:**

**1. NRTIs — Nucleoside/Nucleotide Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors** These drugs are "fake building blocks." HIV's reverse transcriptase tries to use them to build DNA, but they do not work — so the DNA chain stops. This blocks viral replication. - Tenofovir (TDF) - Zidovudine (AZT) - Lamivudine (3TC) - Emtricitabine (FTC) - Abacavir (ABC)

**2. NNRTIs — Non-Nucleoside Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors** These drugs bind directly to the reverse transcriptase enzyme and block it from working. They do not act as fake building blocks. - Efavirenz (EFV) - Nevirapine (NVP) - Rilpivirine

### Isotretinoin (Oral) — The Most Important Retinoid

Isotretinoin is the most powerful treatment for acne. It is the only drug that targets all four causes of acne simultaneously.

**Mechanism — what it does:** - Drastically reduces sebaceous gland size → less sebum produced - Normalises follicular keratinisation → prevents pore blockage - Reduces Cutibacterium acnes (indirectly — less sebum means less food for the bacteria) - Anti-inflammatory

**Indications:** - Severe nodular or cystic acne - Acne that has not responded to antibiotics and topical treatments - Acne causing significant scarring

- Used for acne and photoageing (fine lines, uneven pigmentation, sun damage) - Apply a thin layer at night — it breaks down in sunlight - Initial irritation, redness, and peeling is expected and normal. ## Sunscreen Preparations

### Why Sunscreen Matters

Ultraviolet (UV) radiation from the sun causes: - **Sunburn** - **Skin ageing** — wrinkles, pigmentation, loss of elasticity - **Skin cancer** — basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, melanoma - **DNA damage** to skin cells

There are two types of UV radiation: - **UVA (320–400 nm)** — penetrates deep into the skin. Causes ageing and contributes to melanoma. Present all year round, even on cloudy days, and passes through glass - **UVB (290–320 nm)** — affects the surface of the skin. Causes sunburn and most skin cancers. Varies with season and time of day

### Types of Sunscreen

**Chemical (Organic) sunscreens:** - Absorb UV radiation and convert it to heat, which is released from the skin - Examples: Oxybenzone, Avobenzone, Octinoxate - Tend to be lighter and more cosmetically elegant - Must be applied 15–30 minutes before sun exposure to allow absorption into the skin

**Physical (Inorganic) sunscreens:** - Sit on top of the skin and physically reflect and scatter UV rays away from the skin - Examples: Zinc oxide, Titanium dioxide - Provide immediate protection on application - More suitable for sensitive skin and children - Can leave a white cast on the skin

**Broad-spectrum sunscreens:** - Protect against both UVA and UVB - Most modern commercial sunscreens are broad-spectrum combinations of chemical and physical filters

### SPF — Sun Protection Factor

SPF measures how well a sunscreen protects against **UVB only**: - SPF 15 blocks about 93% of UVB - SPF 30 blocks about 97% of UVB - SPF 50 blocks about 98% of UVB

For daily use, a minimum of **SPF 30 broad-spectrum** sunscreen is recommended.

Important: SPF only measures UVB protection. A sunscreen must be labelled **broad-spectrum** to confirm it also protects against UVA.

**How to use sunscreen correctly:** - Apply 15–30 minutes before sun exposure - Use a generous amount — most people apply too little and get less than the stated SPF - Reapply every 2 hours, or after swimming, sweating, or towel drying

**Sunscreen is especially important when using photosensitising drugs:**

Several drugs make the skin more sensitive to sunlight and increase the risk of sunburn and skin damage: - Retinoids (Tretinoin, Isotretinoin) - Tetracyclines (Doxycycline) - Fluoroquinolones (Ciprofloxacin) - Thiazide diuretics - Sulfonamides

Patients on these drugs must be told to use sunscreen daily.
