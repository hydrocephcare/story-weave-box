# COPD and Bronchiectasis

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Respiratory Medicine (obstructive lung disease)**

> **In one line:** COPD (emphysema plus chronic bronchitis) is a smoking-related, progressive airflow obstruction caused by loss of elastic recoil, small-airway inflammation and mucus plugging; bronchiectasis is permanent, abnormal dilatation of bronchi caused by a cycle of infection, inflammation and impaired mucus clearance. Both are obstructive; both cause chronic cough and sputum, and both are confirmed by a reduced FEV1/FVC ratio, with CT as the key test for bronchiectasis.

## What you must know

- **COPD:** persistent airflow limitation that is **not fully reversible** (FEV1/FVC below 0.70 after bronchodilator), progressive, due to an abnormal inflammatory response to noxious particles or gases (smoke, occupational dust, cooking smoke).
- **Chronic bronchitis** is a clinical diagnosis (cough and sputum on most days for **3 months a year for 2 successive years**); **emphysema** is a pathological one (destruction of alveolar walls distal to the terminal bronchioles, without obvious fibrosis).
- **DLCO is reduced in emphysema** but normal in asthma and chronic bronchitis. **Clubbing is not a feature of COPD**; if present, look for lung cancer, bronchiectasis or fibrosis.
- COPD treatment that changes outcome: **smoking cessation** and **long-term oxygen therapy** (when hypoxaemic). Bronchodilators and inhaled steroids relieve symptoms and reduce exacerbations but do not reduce mortality.
- In exacerbations give **controlled oxygen (24 to 28%, target SpO2 88 to 92%)**, nebulised salbutamol plus ipratropium, **oral/IV steroids**, and antibiotics if sputum is purulent.
- **Bronchiectasis:** permanent dilatation of medium-sized bronchi; daily large volumes of purulent sputum, haemoptysis, recurrent pneumonia. **HRCT** is the investigation of choice (thick-walled dilated bronchi, "signet ring" sign, "tram tracks").
- Bronchiectasis treatment: **chest physiotherapy and postural drainage**, antibiotics guided by sputum culture (especially *Pseudomonas*), treat the cause.

# PART A: COPD

## 1. Definition and components

- A disease state of persistent airflow limitation that improves slightly with salbutamol but does not normalise; usually progressive and associated with an abnormal inflammatory response to noxious particles or gases (smoking, occupational gases, cooking smoke).
- Classified among the obstructive lung diseases, with asthma and bronchiectasis.
- Emphysema (the "pink puffer") and chronic bronchitis (the "blue bloater") are grouped under COPD because both are mainly caused by smoking (which damages elastin and so reduces elastic recoil) and they usually coexist. **Pure emphysema or pure chronic bronchitis is rare.**

| | Chronic bronchitis | Emphysema |
|---|---|---|
| Basis of diagnosis | **Clinical:** productive cough on most days for at least 3 months a year for at least 2 successive years | **Pathological:** permanent destructive enlargement of airspaces distal to terminal bronchioles (alveoli) without obvious fibrosis |
| Mechanism | Mucus gland hypertrophy, mucus plugs obstruct bronchioles | Inflammation and protease damage destroy alveolar walls |
| Notes | Simple chronic bronchitis may have normal CT and spirometry; later develops into COPD | Types by site (below) |

> **Exam tip:** a patient can have chronic bronchitis with normal spirometry. COPD requires obstruction on spirometry.

### Types of emphysema

| Type | Features |
|---|---|
| **Centri-acinar** (centrilobular) | Extremely common, in smokers. Damage concentrated around respiratory bronchioles (proximal acini); distal ducts and alveoli preserved. **Upper zones.** |
| **Pan-acinar** (panlobular) | Less common. Whole acinus involved. **Lung bases.** Inherited **alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency**: emphysema at a younger age. |
| **Irregular** | Scarring and damage to parenchyma patchily without regard to acinar structure. |

## 2. Epidemiology

- Prevalence follows tobacco smoking (developed countries: tobacco causes about 90% of cases); in low- and middle-income countries, **biomass fuel** smoke is added.
- The 4th leading cause of death in the USA; forecast to be 3rd worldwide by 2020.
- Usually after the age of **40**, unlike asthma, which begins at any age.
- Risk grows with the number of cigarettes (30 a day carries about 20 times the risk of a non-smoker), but not all smokers develop COPD, so individual susceptibility matters.

## 3. Risk factors

1. **Tobacco smoke** (about 90% of cases).
2. **Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency** (worse in combination with smoking).
3. Environmental: second-hand smoke, biomass fuel, occupational dusts, pollution.
4. Chronic asthma.
5. Childhood infections, low birth weight, HIV, TB, cannabis, low socioeconomic status.

## 4. Pathophysiology

Airflow limitation in the small airways arises by three mechanisms:

1. **Loss of elasticity and alveolar attachments** (emphysema): reduced elastic recoil, so airways collapse on expiration.
2. **Inflammation and scarring** narrow the small airways.
3. **Mucus secretion** blocks the airways.

Each causes **air trapping, hyperinflation** and breathlessness.

> **Remember:** in emphysema, elastases released by inflammatory cells overwhelm the protective **alpha-1 antitrypsin** (an inhibitor of neutrophil elastase), so the alveolar walls are destroyed. Deficiency means this happens early.

### Systemic components

- Muscle weakness and cachexia (inflammatory mediators such as CRP, TNF and interleukins have a catabolic effect on muscle and bone, so the patient can look like a cancer patient).
- Cardiac deconditioning; impaired salt and water excretion causing peripheral oedema; weight loss.
- **Osteoporosis** (immobility, inflammation, steroids), depression, social isolation.
- Chronic **respiratory acidosis with compensatory metabolic alkalosis** in advanced disease.

## 5. Clinical features

| Symptoms | Signs (non-specific, late) |
|---|---|
| Cough with white or clear sputum (purulent in infective exacerbation) | Tachypnoea, tachycardia, cyanosis |
| Wheeze and breathlessness (exertional dyspnoea is the commonest early symptom) | Prolonged expiratory phase, accessory muscle use, pursed-lip breathing |
| Weight loss | **Barrel chest**; hyperresonant percussion; liver dullness pushed down to the 6th to 7th intercostal space (normally 5th) |
| Oedema and morning headaches (cor pulmonale, hypercapnia) | Quiet breath sounds, end-expiratory wheeze, with or without inspiratory crackles |
| Haemoptysis (needs investigation) | Signs of cor pulmonale: raised JVP, oedema |

- Some patients are sedentary and avoid exertion, so they complain little. CO2-insensitive patients are often oedematous and cyanosed but not very breathless.
- Persistent crackles raise the possibility of **bronchiectasis**.
- **Finger clubbing is not a feature of COPD.**
- Differential: chronic asthma, TB, bronchiectasis, congestive heart failure.

## 6. Diagnosis and investigations

**Clinical diagnosis:** breathlessness and sputum in a lifelong smoker. If there is no smoking history, an initial diagnosis of asthma is usual, unless there is a family history suggesting alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency. Use GOLD criteria.

1. **Chest X-ray** (best initial test; spirometry is the most accurate). Low sensitivity: only severe advanced emphysema shows changes (hyperinflation, flat diaphragm, bullae). Essential to exclude lung cancer, heart failure and bullae (a bulla is an air space above 1 cm), and in exacerbations to exclude pneumonia and pneumothorax.
2. **HRCT:** shows emphysema and bullae (areas with no vessels or alveolar markings, no obvious fibrosis).
3. **Spirometry (definitive):** reduced FEV1 and FVC with reduced **FEV1/FVC (below 0.70)**, not improving fully with bronchodilator (unlike asthma).
4. **Lung volumes:** raised **TLC, FRC and residual volume**; reduced vital capacity. Measured by helium dilution (which may underestimate in bullous disease; use body plethysmography).
5. **Gas transfer (DLCO):** carbon monoxide crosses the alveolar-capillary membrane and binds haemoglobin; it measures the number and integrity of alveoli.
   - **Emphysema: DLCO reduced** (alveolar destruction); KCO also reduced.
   - Asthma: obstruction without alveolar destruction, so DLCO is normal.
   - Pulmonary haemorrhage (Goodpasture's): DLCO raised.
6. **Pulse oximetry:** below 93% suggests the need for oxygen assessment.
7. **Alpha-1 antitrypsin level:** premature emphysema (under 50 years), non-smokers, basal emphysema, or coexisting liver disease (the abnormal protein accumulates in hepatocytes, giving raised LFTs and cirrhosis).
8. **ABG:**

| Stage | PaO2 | PaCO2 | pH and HCO3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early/emphysema-predominant | Low | Normal | Normal |
| Later/bronchitis-predominant | Low | **High** | pH low or low-normal; **HCO3 raised** (renal compensation, incomplete) |

   Some patients die of normocapnic respiratory failure, others of hypercapnic failure.
9. **FBC:** raised haematocrit (secondary polycythaemia from chronic hypoxia).
10. **ECG:** right atrial and ventricular hypertrophy, P pulmonale, right bundle branch block, atrial fibrillation or multifocal atrial tachycardia in cor pulmonale.
11. **Echocardiography:** right heart hypertrophy, pulmonary hypertension.
12. Exercise tests (6-minute walk) for tolerance and prognosis.

### Spirometric severity (post-bronchodilator, FEV1/FVC below 0.7)

| Grade | FEV1 % predicted |
|---|---|
| Mild | 80% or more |
| Moderate | 50 to 79% |
| Severe | 30 to 49% |
| Very severe | Below 30% (or below 50% with respiratory failure) |

> **Remember:** the FEV1 fall is out of proportion to the FVC fall in obstruction, so the ratio falls. In restrictive disease both fall equally and the ratio is normal or raised.

### COPD versus asthma

| Feature | COPD | Asthma |
|---|---|---|
| Age of onset | After 40 | Any age, often childhood |
| Atopy, eosinophilia, IgE | Absent | Often present |
| Reversibility | Minimal | Marked |
| DLCO | Reduced (emphysema) | Normal |
| Bullae, chronic respiratory failure | Possible | No (asthma does not destroy lung) |
| Response to inhaled steroid trial | Little | Marked |
| Symptom pattern | Progressive | Variable, triggered |

## 7. Management

### 7.1 General and stable COPD

1. **Smoking cessation** (to the level of a never-smoker). Improves survival and quality of life; the early small-airway inflammation is reversible if smoking stops early. Offer counselling and nicotine replacement.
2. **Long-term oxygen therapy (LTOT):** only for patients with hypoxia or cor pulmonale (not just at night or on exertion). **Improves survival** when used at least 15 hours a day.
3. **Vaccination:** yearly influenza vaccine for all; pneumococcal vaccine (every 5 to 6 years, over 65 years or under 65 with severe disease).
4. **Bronchodilators** (improve symptoms; do not reduce progression or mortality; FEV1 rises only by 5 to 10%):

| Class | Short-acting (mild) | Long-acting (moderate to severe) |
|---|---|---|
| β2-agonists | Salbutamol, terbutaline | Salmeterol, formoterol, indacaterol (for frequent use) |
| Anticholinergics (muscarinic antagonists) | **Ipratropium** | **Tiotropium** (more effective in COPD; preferred because β-agonists may cause tachycardia) |

   - Salbutamol plus ipratropium together is more effective than either alone and improves adherence when in one inhaler.
   - Use a **spacer** to improve delivery.
   - **Theophylline (oral):** narrow therapeutic index, many interactions, so monitor serum levels; improves mucociliary clearance and central respiratory drive; for refractory disease or patients who cannot use inhalers.
5. **Corticosteroids:** **inhaled** steroids (usually combined with a LABA) reduce exacerbations; a LABA/ICS combination slows decline in lung function but does not reduce mortality. **Oral** steroids only in acute exacerbations (not long term: osteoporosis, Cushing's syndrome).
6. **Pulmonary rehabilitation** (dyspnoea grade 3 to 5): education, physiotherapy, exercise (2 supervised plus 1 unsupervised session; 6 to 12 weeks; 20 to 30 minutes; lower limbs more than upper). Improves walking distance by 25 to 40% (6-minute walk about +60 m) and well-being **without improving FEV1**. Benefit lasts 12 to 18 months without formal maintenance.
7. **Surgery:**
   - **Bullectomy:** rare; young patient, large bulla compressing lung, minimal airflow limitation, no generalised emphysema, FEV1 above 40%.
   - **Lung volume reduction surgery:** predominantly **upper-lobe** emphysema, preserved gas transfer, no pulmonary hypertension, FEV1 and DLCO above 20%.
   - **Lung transplantation** when all medical therapy is insufficient.

### Treatment ladder

| Mild to moderate | Severe |
|---|---|
| Inhaled bronchodilator (anticholinergic and/or β-agonist) as first line, with spacer | As mild-moderate |
| Inhaled corticosteroid if needed (lowest dose) | Continuous oxygen if hypoxaemic |
| Theophylline if symptoms are not controlled | Pulmonary rehabilitation |
| | **Triple therapy** (LABA + LAMA + ICS) |

> **Check (NICE-style stepwise approach in the cases):** start with a SABA or SAMA; if persistent breathlessness or exacerbations, add a LABA or LAMA (FEV1 50% or more) or LABA plus ICS / LAMA (FEV1 below 50%). Oral steroid maintenance is not advised. Mucolytics (carbocysteine) can be used with a chronic productive cough.

### 7.2 Indications for assessing LTOT

Very severe obstruction (FEV1 below 30%), cyanosis, polycythaemia, peripheral oedema, raised JVP, or SpO2 of 92% or less on air.

## 8. Complications of COPD

- **Respiratory failure** (COPD is the commonest cause).
- **Pulmonary hypertension and cor pulmonale:** hypoxia causes pulmonary arteriolar constriction, raising resistance, right ventricular hypertrophy, with or without right heart failure.
- Bacterial colonisation; **secondary polycythaemia**; haemoptysis.
- **Pneumothorax** (rupture of bullae), occasionally.
- Acute exacerbations (mostly from infection or poor compliance).
- Extrapulmonary: cachexia, osteoporosis.
- Poor prognosis: older age and worsening airflow limitation.

## 9. Acute exacerbation of COPD (AECOPD)

- **Definition:** worsening of symptoms (dyspnoea, sputum volume or purulence, cough) with deterioration in lung function and health status, needing a change in treatment.
- Home management: more bronchodilator, a short course of oral steroids, antibiotics when appropriate. **Go to hospital** with cyanosis, oedema or altered consciousness.

| Causes | Details |
|---|---|
| **Infection (commonest)** | A third viral (rhinovirus, influenza); bacterial (*H. influenzae*, *S. pneumoniae*, *Moraxella catarrhalis*; colonisation in 20 to 30% when stable, 30 to 50% in exacerbation) |
| Bronchospasm | Treated with bronchodilators |
| Others | Pneumonia, cardiac failure, pneumothorax, pollution or occupational exposure, poor adherence |

**Life-threatening features:** drowsiness or confusion, marked distress, paradoxical thoraco-abdominal movement, worsening blood gases despite oxygen and bronchodilators, comorbid heart or liver failure, poor social support.

**Management** (similar to acute asthma):

1. **Controlled oxygen (24 to 28%)**, titrated to **SpO2 88 to 92%** (PaO2 above 8 kPa) without worsening acidosis. Avoid 100% oxygen: patients depend on hypoxic drive; high oxygen reduces ventilation, CO2 rises, pH falls and they become comatose.
2. **Nebulised salbutamol and ipratropium**, alone or together.
3. **Systemic corticosteroids** (oral prednisolone, or IV methylprednisolone if hospitalised). Do **not** use inhaled steroids for the acute episode.
4. **Antibiotics** if sputum increases in volume or purulence, or breathlessness worsens (aminopenicillin, macrolide, doxycycline, levofloxacin; no antibiotic is clearly superior). Use amoxicillin-clavulanate, cefuroxime or a quinolone if 2 of 3 worsen (breathlessness, sputum volume, purulence) or in severe disease.
5. **Non-invasive ventilation** (for hypercapnic acidosis) can prevent respiratory failure and intubation.
6. Chest X-ray, ABG, ECG; consider diuretics for cor pulmonale.

> **Exam tip:** an oxygen-induced rise in PaCO2 is the classic trap. Give 24% oxygen, repeat the gases, and aim for 88 to 92%.

## 10. Prognosis

Progressive; worse with age, falling FEV1, weight loss and pulmonary hypertension (BODE index: BMI, obstruction, dyspnoea, exercise capacity).

# PART B: BRONCHIECTASIS

## 11. Definition and pathogenesis

- **Abnormal, permanent dilatation and irreversible damage of the bronchi** (medium-sized bronchi), with destruction of the bronchial wall, chronic inflammation, airway collapse and **ciliary loss or dysfunction**, causing impaired clearance of secretions. The cavities may be lined by granulation tissue, squamous or ciliated epithelium, with hypertrophy of the bronchial arteries.
- A cycle of **infection, inflammation, mucus stasis and more infection** ("vicious cycle").
- It is less common now because of better control of lung infections.

## 12. Causes

| Cause | Mechanism or comment |
|---|---|
| **Cystic fibrosis** | Autosomal recessive; thick mucus. Commonest cause in Western countries (about half); bilateral and upper lobe. Staph. aureus, then *Pseudomonas*. |
| **Primary ciliary dyskinesia** (immotile cilia) | Impaired clearance; also causes sinusitis and male infertility; about 50% have situs inversus (**Kartagener's syndrome**) |
| **Hypogammaglobulinaemia / agammaglobulinaemia** (e.g. common variable immunodeficiency) and other immune deficiency | Recurrent infections; **lower lobes** (bases) |
| **Foreign body, tumour, or enlarged nodes** | Obstruction, then infection, then destruction; **localised** bronchiectasis distal to the block |
| **Infections** | Measles, whooping cough, **tuberculosis**, severe pneumonia (*H. influenzae*, *K. pneumoniae*, *S. pneumoniae*, *S. aureus*); *P. aeruginosa* is linked to rapid FEV1 decline |
| **Allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis** | Fungal; proximal bronchiectasis |
| **Autoimmune and collagen-vascular disease** | Rheumatoid arthritis, SLE, Crohn's disease |

> **Check:** the slides say CF is the commonest cause. In Kenya and the tropics, **post-infectious causes (TB, severe childhood pneumonia, measles, whooping cough) and HIV-related lung disease** are commoner than CF.

- Systemic causes (CF, ciliary dyskinesia) cause **bilateral** disease; a foreign body or pus accumulation causes **localised** disease; agammaglobulinaemia affects the **bases**.

## 13. Clinical features

- Chronic cough with **large amounts of mucopurulent, copious, foul-smelling sputum** (yellow or green), leading to halitosis. Sputum often separates into layers on standing.
- **Haemoptysis** from rupture of bronchial vessels near the wall surface; can be life-threatening.
- Recurrent or persistent pneumonia (the blocked area is a good site for bacterial growth); dyspnoea, weight loss, fever.
- Examination: **coarse crackles** over affected areas (large sputum volume), or wheeze (when bilateral). **Clubbing** is uncommon now because of better infection control (never in COPD or asthma; seen in cancer).

## 14. Investigations

1. **Sputum culture:** often *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*; guides antibiotic choice.
2. **CT (HRCT):** best non-invasive test and study of choice (sensitivity about 97%). Shows thickened, dilated bronchi: the **bronchus is wider than the accompanying artery** ("signet ring"), and thick-walled cysts (unlike bullae, which have no wall).
3. **Chest X-ray:** may be normal (best initial test); in advanced disease shows dilated bronchi with thick walls, "tram tracking", crowded bronchi, and cysts with fluid levels.
4. **Look for the cause:** screen for ciliary dysfunction (nasal nitric oxide, ciliary biopsy), CF (sweat chloride, CFTR genotype), immunoglobulins, aspergillus tests, HIV, TB.
5. **PFTs:** obstructive pattern.
6. **Bronchoscopy:** in selected cases (localised disease, suspected foreign body or tumour, haemoptysis).

## 15. Management

1. Treat airflow obstruction as for COPD (inhaled bronchodilators and corticosteroids).
2. **Bronchial hygiene (essential):** chest physiotherapy (cupping, clapping), **postural drainage**, chest percussion, hydration.
3. **Antibiotics:** about 1 week (2 weeks in an exacerbation); as for COPD (aminopenicillin or macrolide) but longer and at higher doses. Guided by culture, especially *Pseudomonas* or *S. aureus*. For *Pseudomonas* colonisation: **nebulised gentamicin or tobramycin** twice daily.
4. **Haemoptysis:** bed rest, antibiotics, transfusion if needed; urgent fibreoptic bronchoscopy to find the source; if bleeding does not settle quickly, **bronchial artery embolisation**; surgical resection if embolisation fails.
5. **Surgery:** resection of focal lesions.
6. **Prevention:** treat bronchial obstruction early (remove foreign bodies early); vaccinate; prompt treatment of childhood infections (measles, whooping cough, pneumonia).

## 16. Complications and prognosis

- Complications: pneumonia, pneumothorax, empyema, metastatic cerebral abscess (all reduced by antibiotics), massive haemoptysis, respiratory failure, cor pulmonale.
- The disease is progressive with ciliary dysfunction and cystic fibrosis, and eventually causes respiratory failure; otherwise prognosis is relatively good with regular physiotherapy and aggressive antibiotics.

## Key points to revise

1. COPD = airflow obstruction not fully reversible (FEV1/FVC below 0.70); chronic bronchitis is clinical, emphysema is pathological.
2. DLCO falls in emphysema; clubbing is not a COPD feature.
3. Stopping smoking and LTOT (hypoxaemic patients) improve survival; bronchodilators and inhaled steroids do not.
4. Exacerbation: controlled O2 (88 to 92%), nebulised salbutamol and ipratropium, systemic steroids, antibiotics if purulent, NIV for acidosis.
5. Bronchiectasis = permanent bronchial dilatation from infection, obstruction, CF, ciliary or immune defects; purulent sputum and haemoptysis.
6. HRCT is the test of choice; culture the sputum (*Pseudomonas*).
7. Treat bronchiectasis with physiotherapy and postural drainage, targeted antibiotics (nebulised for *Pseudomonas*), and bronchial artery embolisation for severe haemoptysis.
