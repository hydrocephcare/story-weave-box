## IX: DEMYELINATING DISEASES
## Definition
Acquired conditions with **preferential myelin damage, relative axonal preservation**. Deficits arise from impaired electrical impulse transmission. CNS has limited remyelination capacity; axonal damage accumulates over time.

## Multiple Sclerosis (MS)
**Most common demyelinating disorder** — prevalence ~1/1000. Onset usually 20–40s; women 2× more affected.
### Pathogenesis
- **Autoimmune** — Th1/Th17 cells react against myelin antigens
- Th1 → IFN-γ → macrophage activation; Th17 → leukocyte recruitment → demyelination
- Infiltrate: CD4+ > CD8+ T cells + macrophages
- Oligoclonal IgG bands in CSF → B-cell involvement
- **Genetic**: 15× risk if 1st-degree relative affected; 150× if monozygotic twin; strong HLA-DR association; IL-2R/IL-7R gene associations
- **Environmental**: Higher prevalence away from equator (low vitamin D); EBV implicated but unconfirmed
### Morphology
- **Gross**: Firm, gray-tan, glassy "plaques" — well-circumscribed, depressed lesions in white matter
- **Location**: Periventricular (most common), corpus callosum, optic nerves/chiasm, brainstem, cerebellum, spinal cord
- **Active plaque**: Foamy macrophages (myelin debris), perivascular lymphocytes, myelin absent, axons preserved
- **Inactive plaque**: No inflammation, gliosis prominent, oligodendrocytes ↓, axons greatly reduced
- **Shadow plaques**: Thinned myelin = partial remyelination by surviving oligodendrocytes
### Clinical Features
- Relapsing-remitting episodes (weeks–years), partial recovery, eventual progression
- **Optic neuritis** — unilateral visual loss; common 1st presentation
- **Brainstem**: Cranial nerve signs, ataxia, nystagmus, internuclear ophthalmoplegia (MLF interruption)
- **Spinal cord**: Motor/sensory deficits, spasticity, bladder dysfunction
- CSF: Mild ↑ protein, pleocytosis (1/3), ↑ IgG, oligoclonal bands
- MRI: Plaques may be clinically silent
- **Rx**: Immunosuppressive/immunomodulatory agents (disease modifying, not curative)

## Neuromyelitis Optica (NMO / Devic Disease)
- Bilateral optic neuritis + spinal cord demyelination (synchronous or near-synchronous)
- **Distinct from MS** — greater female skew, poorer recovery from first attack
- **Pathognomonic**: Anti-aquaporin-4 (AQP4) antibodies — AQP4 is the major astrocyte water channel
- Histology: Necrosis, neutrophils + eosinophils, vascular Ig/complement deposition
- **Rx acute**: Glucocorticoids or plasma exchange; long-term: B-cell depletion or anti-C5 complement inhibitors

## Acute Disseminated Encephalomyelitis (ADEM)
- Diffuse, **monophasic** demyelinating disease post-viral infection or vaccination (1–2 weeks later)
- **All lesions same age** (cf. MS — different ages)
- Symptoms: Headache, lethargy, coma (not focal like MS)
- Mortality ~20%; survivors usually recover fully
- **Mechanism**: Likely autoimmune reaction to myelin (analogous to experimental allergic encephalomyelitis)
## Acute Necrotizing Hemorrhagic Encephalomyelitis (Weston Hurst Disease)
- **Hyperacute variant of ADEM** — follows URTI
- More severe tissue damage; often fatal; significant deficits in survivors

## Central Pontine Myelinolysis (Osmotic Demyelination Syndrome)
- Acute myelin loss in **base of pons** (± extra-pontine), symmetric pattern
- **Cause**: Rapid correction of hyponatremia (2–6 days after correction) → rapid ↑ osmolality → oligodendrocyte injury
- Histology: No inflammation; neurons/axons preserved; all lesions same stage (synchronous onset)
- **Spared**: Periventricular and subpial regions; never below pontomedullary junction
- Clinical: Quadriplegia → "locked-in" syndrome (conscious but unresponsive)
- **Key rule**: Correct hyponatremia slowly to prevent this

## X: GENETIC / METABOLIC DISEASES
## Vitamin Deficiencies
### Thiamine (B1) Deficiency — Wernicke-Korsakoff Syndrome
| Feature | Wernicke Encephalopathy | Korsakoff Syndrome |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Acute | Chronic, largely irreversible |
| Symptoms | Psychosis + ophthalmoplegia | Short-term memory loss + confabulation |
| Response to thiamine | Reversible | Poor |
- **Cause**: Chronic alcoholism (most common), gastric carcinoma, persistent vomiting, chronic gastritis
- **Morphology**: Hemorrhage + necrosis in **mammillary bodies** and walls of 3rd/4th ventricles → dilated capillaries → leakage → hemosiderin-laden macrophages → cystic space
- Dorsomedial thalamic nucleus lesions correlate best with memory disturbance
### Vitamin B12 Deficiency — Subacute Combined Degeneration of Spinal Cord
- Degeneration of **ascending AND descending tracts** (posterior columns + lateral corticospinal tracts)
- **Mechanism**: Defect in myelin formation (exact mechanism unknown)
- **Progression**: Bilateral numbness/tingling + ataxia (lower limbs) → spastic weakness → complete paraplegia (late)
- Mid-thoracic cord preferentially affected first
- **Histology**: Myelin layer swelling → vacuoles → axonal degeneration
- **Rx**: Prompt B12 replacement → improvement; if complete paraplegia → poor recovery

## Metabolic Disturbances
### Hypoglycemia
- Brain effects resemble hypoxia (same energy substrates)
- Vulnerable regions: **Large cortical pyramidal neurons** (layers III/V) → pseudolaminar cortical necrosis (deep layers); **CA1 (Sommer sector)** hippocampus; cerebellar Purkinje cells (less than hypoxia)
### Hyperglycemia
- Ketoacidosis or hyperosmolar coma (DM)
- No significant morphologic brain changes
- Dehydration → confusion → stupor → coma
- Correct fluid depletion gradually (rapid correction → cerebral edema)
### Hepatic Encephalopathy
- Elevated ammonia + proinflammatory cytokines
- **Alzheimer type II cells** — astrocytes with enlarged nuclei, minimal cytoplasm; in cerebral cortex, basal ganglia, subcortical gray matter

## Toxic Disorders
### Carbon Monoxide
- Impairs O₂ carrying capacity + inhibits cytochrome C oxidase (blocks mitochondrial electron transport)
- Selective injury: Cortical layers III/V, CA1 hippocampus, Purkinje cells
- **Characteristic**: Bilateral **globus pallidus necrosis**
- Late: Demyelination of white matter tracts
### Ethanol (Chronic)
- Wernicke-Korsakoff (thiamine deficiency)
- **Cerebellar degeneration** (~1% of chronic alcoholics): Truncal ataxia, unsteady gait, nystagmus
- Histology: Granule cell loss predominantly in **superior anterior vermis** → Purkinje cell loss → **Bergmann gliosis** (astrocyte proliferation between granular + molecular layers)
### Radiation
- >10 Gy: Immediate — nausea, confusion, convulsions, coma, death
- Delayed (months–years): Headache, nausea, vomiting, papilledema
- Histology: Coagulative necrosis in white matter, edema, gliosis, vascular fibrinoid necrosis + sclerosis
- Radiation + methotrexate = synergistic injury
- Dystrophic mineralization of axons/cell bodies near lesions
- Can induce secondary tumors (sarcoma, glioma, meningioma) years later

## VII & VIII: NEUROMUSCULAR JUNCTION DISORDERS & TOXINS
## NMJ Structure (Quick Review)
- Presynaptic boutons → release **ACh** into synaptic cleft → binds postsynaptic **AChR** on sarcolemma
- Sarcolemma has complex infoldings with clustered AChRs
- All NMJ disorders → **painless weakness and fatigue**

## Myasthenia Gravis (MG)
Prevalence 150–200/million; bimodal distribution (young F > M; older M > F)
### Pathogenesis
| Antibody Target | % | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| AChR (postsynaptic) | ~85% | Receptor aggregation/degradation + complement fixation → postsynaptic membrane damage + AChR depletion |
| MuSK (muscle-specific receptor tyrosine kinase) | ~15% | Impairs AChR trafficking/clustering; no complement fixation |
- **Thymic associations**:
  - 10% → **thymoma** (thymic epithelial tumor)
  - 30% (esp. young) → **thymic hyperplasia** (B-cell follicles in thymus)
  - Thymic myoid cells express skeletal muscle antigens → aberrant tolerance → autoimmunity
  - Older patients: usually no thymic abnormality
### Clinical Features
- **Fluctuating weakness worsening with exertion** and throughout the day
- **Diplopia + ptosis** (extraocular muscles) — hallmark; distinguishes from myopathies
- May progress to generalized weakness requiring ventilation
- MuSK-antibody cases: Neck, shoulder, facial, respiratory, bulbar muscles more affected
- EMG: **Decremental response** with repetitive stimulation (characteristic)
### Treatment
1. Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (↑ ACh half-life) — first line
2. Plasmapheresis, glucocorticoids, cyclosporine, rituximab
3. Thymectomy — effective with thymoma; uncertain benefit in hyperplasia
- Mortality dropped from >30% (1950s) to <5% currently

## Lambert-Eaton Myasthenic Syndrome (LEMS)
- Antibodies block **presynaptic voltage-gated calcium channels** → ↓ ACh release
- **Key distinction from MG**: Rapid repetitive stimulation **increases** (facilitates) muscle response
- Weakness of **extremities** (proximal); improves after brief activity
- ~50% paraneoplastic — most often **small cell / neuroendocrine carcinoma of lung**; symptoms may precede cancer diagnosis by years
- Non-paraneoplastic cases: Associated with other autoimmune diseases (vitiligo, thyroid)
- **Rx**: Drugs ↑ ACh release (e.g., 3,4-DAP) + immunosuppressives

## Congenital Myasthenic Syndromes
- Rare; autosomal recessive (mostly)
- Mutations in presynaptic, synaptic, or postsynaptic NMJ proteins
- Most common: Loss-of-function in **ε-subunit of AChR**; others affect AChR clustering proteins
- Present neonatally (hypotonia, ophthalmoplegia, breathing difficulty) or in adolescence/adulthood
- No autoantibodies — genetic, not autoimmune

## Disorders Caused by Toxins
- **Botulism** (*Clostridium botulinum*) — blocks **presynaptic ACh release** (blocks exocytosis)
- **Curare** — plant-derived; blocks **postsynaptic AChR** → flaccid paralysis; historically used surgically (now replaced)

## IX: PERIPHERAL NERVE SHEATH TUMORS
## Overview
All three major types arise from **Schwann cell lineage**. The transition from central myelin (oligodendrocytes) to peripheral myelin (Schwann cells) occurs as nerves exit the CNS — hence tumors can arise intradurally.
| Tumor | Benign/Malignant | Key Gene | Syndrome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Schwannoma | Benign | NF2 (chr 22) — merlin | NF2, sporadic |
| Neurofibroma | Benign | NF1 (chr 17q11.2) — neurofibromin | NF1, sporadic |
| MPNST | Malignant (85% high-grade) | Complex aberrations | NF1 (50%), sporadic |

## Schwannoma
### Molecular
- **NF2 gene** (chr 22q12) mutation → loss of **merlin** (cytoskeletal protein; regulates cell shape, growth, adhesion via actin cytoskeleton interactions)
- All schwannomas, even sporadic, show merlin loss
### Morphology
- **Well-encapsulated**, attached loosely to nerve without invasion → resectable without nerve sacrifice
- **Gross**: Firm, gray mass
- **Microscopy**: Biphasic pattern —
  - **Antoni A**: Dense, spindle cells in intersecting fascicles, nuclear palisading → **Verocay bodies** (central nuclear-free zones flanked by palisading nuclei)
  - **Antoni B**: Loose, hypocellular, myxoid matrix, microcysts
- Cells: Spindled nuclei, wavy/buckled shape
### Clinical
- Compression of nerve or adjacent structures
- Most common intracranial location: **Cerebellopontine angle** — vestibular branch of CN VIII
- Presents with **tinnitus + hearing loss** ("acoustic neuroma" = misnomer — not acoustic nerve, not a neuroma)
- Elsewhere: Trigeminal nerve, dorsal roots (sensory predilection), large nerve trunks
- **Surgical removal is curative**

## Neurofibroma
- More heterogeneous than schwannoma: neoplastic Schwann cells + perineurial-like cells + fibroblasts + mast cells + CD34+ spindle cells
- **Neoplastic cell = Schwann cell** (only these show complete NF1/neurofibromin loss)
### Types
| Type | Features | NF1 Association |
|---|---|---|
| Cutaneous (localized) | Small, pedunculated nodule in dermis/subcutaneous fat; unencapsulated; bland cells; loose collagen stroma | Sporadic (single) or NF1 (multiple) |
| Diffuse | Plaque-like dermal/subcutaneous infiltration; pseudo-Meissner corpuscles; entraps fat/appendages | Usually NF1 |
| Plexiform | Expands nerve fascicles; "bag of worms"; "shredded carrot" collagen; preserved perineurium | **Always NF1** |
### Pathogenesis (NF1)
- **Neurofibromin** = tumor suppressor → stimulates **RAS-GTPase** activity → inactivates RAS
- Loss → RAS trapped in GTP-bound active state → uncontrolled proliferation
- Haploinsufficient mast cells hypersensitive to KIT ligand from Schwann cells → cross-talk promotes Schwann cell growth
- **Malignant transformation to MPNST**: Mainly plexiform type; 5–10% lifetime risk in NF1; higher risk with large plexiform burden and large NF1 deletions

## MPNST (Malignant Peripheral Nerve Sheath Tumor)
- ~85% high-grade; low-grade variants exist
- 50% arise in NF1 (from malignant transformation of plexiform neurofibroma); 50% sporadic (de novo)
- Location: Large nerves — chest, abdomen, pelvis, neck, limb-girdle
- Complex chromosomal aberrations (gains, losses, rearrangements)
### Morphology
- Poorly defined mass, infiltrates along nerve axis, invades adjacent soft tissue
- Fasciculated spindle cells; "marbleized" at low power (varying cellularity)
- Mitoses, necrosis, nuclear anaplasia
- **Divergent differentiation** — focal glandular, cartilaginous, osseous, or rhabdomyoblastic areas
- Distinction from undifferentiated sarcoma: NF1 diagnosis + anatomic relationship to nerve/preexisting neurofibroma

## Neurofibromatosis Type 1 (NF1) — Von Recklinghausen Disease
- **Autosomal dominant**; frequency **1 in 3000** (common); gene at **17q11.2** encoding **neurofibromin**
- Manifestations:
  - Multiple neurofibromas (all types), MPNSTs
  - Optic nerve gliomas, other glial tumors
  - Pheochromocytomas, hamartomatous lesions
  - **Lisch nodules** (pigmented iris hamartomas)
  - **Café au lait spots** (cutaneous hyperpigmented macules)
  - Intellectual disability, seizures, skeletal defects
## Neurofibromatosis Type 2 (NF2)
- **Autosomal dominant**; frequency **1 in 40,000–50,000**; gene at **22q12** encoding **merlin**
- Also mutated in sporadic meningiomas and schwannomas
- Tumors: **Bilateral CN VIII schwannomas** (hallmark), multiple meningiomas, spinal ependymomas
- Non-neoplastic lesions: Schwannosis (Schwann cell ingrowth into spinal cord), meningioangiomatosis, glial hamartia
- **Genotype–phenotype correlation**: Nonsense/frameshift mutations → more severe than missense

**Quick Comparison — NF1 vs NF2**
| | NF1 | NF2 |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency | 1/3000 | 1/40,000–50,000 |
| Gene / Chr | Neurofibromin / 17q11.2 | Merlin / 22q12 |
| Key tumors | Neurofibromas, MPNST, optic glioma | Bilateral acoustic schwannomas, meningiomas, ependymomas |
| Skin signs | Café au lait, neurofibromas | Minimal |
| Eye signs | Lisch nodules | Posterior lens opacities (cataracts) |
