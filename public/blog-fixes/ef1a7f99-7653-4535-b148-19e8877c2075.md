## SECTION 1: HAEMOSTASIS

### Definition

Haemostasis is the physiological process by which bleeding is arrested following damage to a blood vessel wall. It involves a coordinated interaction between the blood vessel wall, platelets, and the coagulation system. Too little activity causes haemorrhage; too much causes pathological thrombosis.

### The Four Physiologic Events of Haemostasis

These occur in sequence following vessel injury:

- **Step 1 — Vascular Spasm:** Immediate reflex vasoconstriction mediated by local myogenic responses, neural reflexes, and chemical mediators including endothelin and thromboxane A2. This is always the first response to vessel injury.
- **Step 2 — Primary Haemostasis (Platelet Plug Formation):** Platelets adhere to exposed subendothelial collagen via von Willebrand factor, then activate and aggregate to form a loose primary plug.
- **Step 3 — Secondary Haemostasis (Coagulation Cascade):** Coagulation factors are sequentially activated, generating thrombin, which converts fibrinogen to fibrin. Factor XIII cross-links fibrin strands to form a stable, firm clot.
- **Step 4 — Fibrinolysis:** Tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) converts plasminogen to plasmin, which degrades the fibrin clot after vessel repair. D-dimer and fibrin degradation products (FDPs) are released.

Fibrinolysis is an integral part of haemostasis — it is not a separate process.

### Essential Cofactors

| Cofactor | Role |
|---|---|
| Calcium (Ca²⁺) | Required at multiple steps in the coagulation cascade |
| Vitamin K | Required for hepatic synthesis of Factors II, VII, IX, X and Proteins C and S |
| Phospholipid | Provided by activated platelets; forms the surface for coagulation complex assembly |

## SECTION 2: PLATELETS AND PRIMARY HAEMOSTASIS

### Structure and Origin

Platelets are small, anucleate cell fragments derived from megakaryocytes in the bone marrow. The primary growth factor driving platelet production is **thrombopoietin**. Normal platelet count is 150,000 – 450,000 per µL.

### Sequence of Platelet Plug Formation

- Vascular injury exposes subendothelial collagen
- Von Willebrand factor (vWF) binds to the exposed collagen
- Platelets bind vWF via glycoprotein Ib (GPIb) — this is platelet adhesion
- Adhered platelets activate and change shape from disc to spiny sphere
- Activated platelets release granule contents: ADP, serotonin, thromboxane A2, and platelet factor 4
- ADP recruits additional platelets; thromboxane A2 causes vasoconstriction and further aggregation
- GPIIb/IIIa receptors activate and bind fibrinogen, cross-linking platelets together — this is platelet aggregation
- A loose primary platelet plug forms, later reinforced by fibrin from the coagulation cascade

Platelets do NOT release urea, calcium ions, or antibodies. Their primary role is plug formation, not oxygen transport.

### Von Willebrand Factor (vWF)

vWF is a large glycoprotein synthesised in endothelial cells and megakaryocytes. It has two critical functions:

- Bridges platelets to exposed subendothelial collagen at sites of vascular injury
- Carries and protects Factor VIII in circulation from premature degradation

Deficiency or dysfunction of vWF causes Von Willebrand Disease — the most common inherited bleeding disorder.

## SECTION 3: THE COAGULATION CASCADE

### Overview

The coagulation cascade is a series of enzymatic reactions in which inactive zymogens are sequentially activated. The end result is generation of thrombin, which converts fibrinogen to fibrin, forming a stable clot. The cascade has three interconnected pathways: intrinsic, extrinsic, and common.

### Coagulation Factors

| Factor | Name | Pathway | Key Clinical Point |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | Fibrinogen | Common | Converted to fibrin — end product of coagulation |
| II | Prothrombin | Common | Converted to thrombin by Factor Xa |
| III | Tissue Factor (Thromboplastin) | Extrinsic | Initiates extrinsic pathway |
| IV | Calcium (Ca²⁺) | All | Essential mineral cofactor throughout cascade |
| V | Labile Factor | Common | Cofactor for Factor Xa in prothrombinase complex |
| VII | Proconvertin | Extrinsic | Shortest half-life; first affected by warfarin |
| VIII | Antihemophilic Factor A | Intrinsic | Deficient in Haemophilia A |
| IX | Christmas Factor | Intrinsic | Deficient in Haemophilia B |
| X | Stuart-Prower Factor | Common | Convergence point of intrinsic and extrinsic pathways |
| XI | Plasma Thromboplastin Antecedent | Intrinsic | — |
| XII | Hageman Factor | Intrinsic | Initiates intrinsic (contact activation) pathway |
| XIII | Fibrin-Stabilising Factor | Common | Cross-links fibrin; stabilises the clot |
| vWF | Von Willebrand Factor | — | Platelet adhesion; Factor VIII carrier |

Key name associations to memorise:

- Fibrinogen = Factor I
- Christmas factor = Factor IX — deficient in Haemophilia B
- Stuart-Prower factor = Factor X
- Hageman factor = Factor XII — initiates intrinsic pathway
- Factor VIII deficiency = Haemophilia A

### The Three Pathways

**Intrinsic Pathway (Contact Activation)**

- Initiated when Factor XII (Hageman factor) contacts a damaged surface
- Sequence: Factor XII → XI → IX → joins with Factor VIII
- Factor IXa and Factor VIIIa form a complex on the platelet phospholipid surface with calcium
- This complex activates Factor X, entering the common pathway

**Extrinsic Pathway (Tissue Damage)**

- Initiated when tissue factor (Factor III) is released from damaged tissue
- Tissue factor combines with Factor VII to form the Factor VIIa-tissue factor complex
- This complex activates Factor X directly, entering the common pathway
- Factor VII belongs exclusively to the extrinsic pathway

**Common Pathway**

- Begins at Factor X activation
- Factor Xa combines with Factor Va, calcium, and phospholipid to form the prothrombinase complex
- Prothrombinase complex converts prothrombin (Factor II) to thrombin (Factor IIa)
- Thrombin converts fibrinogen (Factor I) to fibrin monomers
- Factor XIII cross-links fibrin monomers into a stable, insoluble fibrin clot

### Pathway Summary Table

| Pathway | Key Factors | Laboratory Test |
|---|---|---|
| Intrinsic | XII, XI, IX, VIII | aPTT |
| Extrinsic | III (Tissue Factor), VII | PT (Prothrombin Time) |
| Common | X, V, II, I, XIII | Both PT and aPTT |

## SECTION 4: NATURAL ANTICOAGULANTS

The body prevents excessive clotting through a system of natural anticoagulants that act as checkpoints in the cascade.

| Anticoagulant | Mechanism | Clinical Note |
|---|---|---|
| Antithrombin III | Inhibits thrombin and Factor Xa | Activity enhanced by heparin; deficiency causes thrombosis |
| Protein C | Inactivates Factors Va and VIIIa | Requires Protein S as cofactor; activated by thrombin-thrombomodulin complex |
| Protein S | Cofactor for Protein C | Deficiency causes thrombotic tendency |
| Tissue Factor Pathway Inhibitor (TFPI) | Inhibits Factor VIIa-tissue factor complex | Limits extrinsic pathway activation |
| Prostacyclin (PGI₂) | Inhibits platelet aggregation | Released by intact, healthy endothelium |

- Antithrombin III deficiency predisposes to thrombotic disorders, not bleeding
- Protein S is a cofactor for Protein C — not for antithrombin and not for Factor VIII
- When thrombin binds thrombomodulin on endothelial cells, it activates Protein C — this is a negative feedback mechanism

## SECTION 5: COAGULATION TESTS

### Laboratory Tests

| Test | Pathway Assessed | Collection Tube | Normal Range | Clinical Use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PT (Prothrombin Time) | Extrinsic + Common | Light blue | 10–14 seconds | Monitor warfarin; assess liver function |
| aPTT | Intrinsic + Common | Light blue | 25–35 seconds | Monitor heparin; diagnose Haemophilia |
| Bleeding Time | Platelet function + vessel wall | — | 2–9 minutes | Platelet dysfunction, vWD |
| D-dimer | Fibrinolysis marker | — | Below 0.5 mg/L | Most sensitive test for DIC |
| Thrombin Time | Fibrinogen to fibrin conversion | — | 10–14 seconds | Fibrinogen defects, heparin effect |
| Platelet Count | — | Purple (EDTA) | 150,000–450,000/µL | Thrombocytopenia |
| Fibrinogen Level | — | Light blue | 2–4 g/L | DIC, liver disease |

- All coagulation studies are collected in light blue tubes containing sodium citrate anticoagulant
- PT and aPTT are both reported in seconds
- Heparin is monitored using aPTT
- Warfarin is monitored using PT/INR
- D-dimer is the most sensitive and specific test for DIC

### Interpreting Combined Results

| PT | aPTT | Likely Diagnosis |
|---|---|---|
| Prolonged | Normal | Factor VII deficiency; early warfarin effect |
| Normal | Prolonged | Haemophilia A or B; heparin therapy; vWD |
| Both prolonged | Both prolonged | Liver disease; DIC; Vitamin K deficiency; common pathway deficiency |
| Both normal | Both normal | Platelet disorder; vascular problem; vWD (mild) |

### The Mixing Study

When an abnormal PT or aPTT is found, the mixing study is performed. Patient plasma is mixed 1:1 with pooled normal plasma and the abnormal test is repeated:

- **Corrects** → Factor deficiency (e.g., Haemophilia A or B, Factor V deficiency, Factor X deficiency)
- **Does NOT correct** → Inhibitor present (e.g., lupus anticoagulant, acquired Factor VIII inhibitor)

The mixing study corrects in Haemophilia A and B because normal plasma supplies the missing factor. It does NOT correct in lupus anticoagulant because the inhibitor in the patient's plasma remains active even after dilution.

### Fibrinolysis

- Tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) is released from endothelial cells following vessel repair
- tPA converts plasminogen to **plasmin** (also called plasminolysin)
- Plasmin degrades cross-linked fibrin into fibrin degradation products (FDPs) and D-dimer
- D-dimer is a specific fragment produced only from cross-linked fibrin — its elevation confirms active clot formation and breakdown
- tPA (alteplase) is used clinically as a thrombolytic agent in stroke and myocardial infarction

## SECTION 6: BLEEDING DISORDERS

### Haemophilia A and B

Both are X-linked recessive disorders affecting males. Females are carriers and are usually asymptomatic.

| Feature | Haemophilia A | Haemophilia B |
|---|---|---|
| Deficient Factor | Factor VIII | Factor IX (Christmas factor) |
| Incidence | More common (1 in 5,000 males) | Less common (1 in 30,000 males) |
| PT | Normal | Normal |
| aPTT | Prolonged | Prolonged |
| Bleeding Time | Normal | Normal |
| Platelet Count | Normal | Normal |
| Mixing Study | Corrects | Corrects |
| Treatment | Factor VIII concentrate; DDAVP (mild cases) | Factor IX concentrate |

Both haemophilias affect only the intrinsic pathway — hence aPTT is prolonged and PT is normal. Both correct on mixing study because no inhibitor is present (in uncomplicated cases).

### Von Willebrand Disease (vWD)

Von Willebrand disease is the most common inherited bleeding disorder in the general population. It results from deficiency or dysfunction of vWF.

**Why both bleeding time and aPTT are prolonged:**

- Deficient vWF impairs platelet adhesion to collagen → prolonged bleeding time
- Deficient vWF reduces Factor VIII levels (because vWF normally carries and protects Factor VIII) → prolonged aPTT
- PT is normal because the extrinsic pathway is unaffected

| Lab Test | Result in vWD |
|---|---|
| Bleeding Time | Prolonged |
| aPTT | Prolonged |
| PT | Normal |
| Platelet Count | Normal |
| Factor VIII Level | Reduced (secondary to vWF deficiency) |
| vWF Antigen | Reduced or absent |

Treatment: DDAVP (desmopressin) for mild cases releases stored vWF from endothelial cells. vWF concentrate is used for severe cases.

### Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC)

DIC is an acquired syndrome of simultaneous widespread clotting and bleeding caused by uncontrolled activation of coagulation throughout the microvasculature. Clotting factors and platelets are consumed faster than they can be replaced, leading to both microvascular thrombosis (organ damage) and systemic bleeding.

**Common triggers:**

- Sepsis — the most common cause
- Obstetric complications: abruptio placentae, amniotic fluid embolism, eclampsia
- Massive trauma
- Malignancy, especially acute promyelocytic leukaemia (APL)
- Incompatible blood transfusion reactions
- Snakebite

**Pathophysiology in sequence:**

- A triggering event causes widespread release of tissue factor or inflammatory mediators
- Massive, uncontrolled thrombin generation occurs throughout the microvasculature
- Microvascular fibrin thrombi form, causing ischaemia to kidneys, lungs, brain, and adrenals
- Simultaneously, clotting factors and platelets are consumed faster than the liver can replace them
- Factor depletion and thrombocytopenia result in systemic bleeding — skin, mucous membranes, IV sites, and internal organs

**Laboratory findings in DIC:**

| Test | Result | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| PT | Prolonged | Clotting factors consumed |
| aPTT | Prolonged | Clotting factors consumed |
| Platelet Count | Decreased | Consumed in microthrombi |
| Fibrinogen | Decreased | Consumed |
| D-dimer | Markedly elevated | Most sensitive marker — confirms active clot breakdown |
| FDPs | Elevated | Products of fibrinolysis |
| Blood Film | Schistocytes present | RBCs mechanically sheared by fibrin strands |

D-dimer is the most sensitive test for DIC. Both PT and aPTT prolonged together with low platelets and low fibrinogen strongly suggests DIC.

### Acquired Factor VIII Inhibitors

- Autoantibodies develop against Factor VIII, neutralising its activity
- This is not inherited — it is an acquired condition
- Most common causes: idiopathic (no identifiable cause), autoimmune disorders (SLE), malignancy, pregnancy or postpartum period, drugs
- Laboratory: prolonged aPTT that does NOT correct on mixing study — because the inhibitor remains active

### Factor V Leiden Mutation

- The most common inherited thrombophilia
- A point mutation in the Factor V gene makes Factor V resistant to inactivation by activated Protein C
- Normally, Protein C with Protein S as cofactor cleaves and inactivates Factor Va, stopping coagulation
- In Factor V Leiden, Protein C cannot inactivate the mutant Factor Va, so coagulation continues unchecked
- Result: venous thromboembolism — deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism
- Factor V Leiden causes thrombosis, not bleeding
- Does not cause a prolonged bleeding time or prolonged PT

### Antithrombin III Deficiency

- Antithrombin III normally inhibits thrombin and Factor Xa
- Deficiency — inherited or acquired (liver disease, nephrotic syndrome) — results in uncontrolled coagulation and venous thromboembolism
- Heparin's anticoagulant effect depends entirely on functional antithrombin III — deficiency significantly reduces heparin efficacy

## SECTION 7: ANTICOAGULANT THERAPY

### Heparin

- **Mechanism:** Binds and activates antithrombin III. Activated antithrombin III then inhibits thrombin (Factor IIa) and Factor Xa, shutting down coagulation
- **Monitored by:** aPTT
- **Reversed by:** Protamine sulphate
- **Types:** Unfractionated heparin (UFH) acts on both thrombin and Factor Xa. Low Molecular Weight Heparin (LMWH — e.g., enoxaparin) acts predominantly on Factor Xa and is monitored by anti-Xa levels

**Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT):**

- An immune-mediated, paradoxical complication of heparin therapy
- Heparin binds platelet factor 4 (PF4), forming a new antigen
- IgG antibodies form against the PF4-heparin complex
- These antibodies bind to platelets via the PF4-heparin complex and cause platelet **activation** — not just destruction
- Result: platelet consumption causes thrombocytopenia, but platelet activation causes paradoxical thrombosis (both arterial and venous)
- Onset: typically 5–10 days after starting heparin
- Key point: low platelets plus thrombosis after heparin = HIT until proven otherwise
- Management: stop heparin immediately; switch to a direct thrombin inhibitor such as argatroban or bivalirudin

### Warfarin

- **Mechanism:** Inhibits Vitamin K epoxide reductase, preventing recycling of Vitamin K to its active form. This reduces hepatic synthesis of Vitamin K-dependent factors: II, VII, IX, X, Protein C, and Protein S
- Factor VII has the shortest half-life, so PT/INR prolongs first when warfarin is started
- **Monitored by:** PT/INR
- **Reversed by:** Vitamin K (slow, over hours to days); FFP for urgent reversal; Prothrombin Complex Concentrate (PCC) for immediate reversal
- Primarily affects the extrinsic pathway initially due to Factor VII's short half-life
