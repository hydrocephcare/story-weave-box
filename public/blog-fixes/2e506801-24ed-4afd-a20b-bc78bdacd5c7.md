# Adolescent Growth and Development

**Year 4 · Paediatrics · Adolescent Health and Growth**

> **In one line:** adolescence is the transition from childhood to adulthood, driven by rising GnRH pulses and sex steroids. It brings puberty (Tanner/SMR stages), a growth spurt, changes in body composition, maturing abstract thinking and moral reasoning, and a shift from parents to peers. The timing varies widely, but the sequence is predictable.

## What you must know

- Adolescence has **three phases** (early, middle, late), each with biological, cognitive and psychosocial milestones. Timing varies; sequence is predictable.
- Puberty starts with maturation of the **GnRH pulse generator**, then pulsatile LH and FSH (first at night), then gonadal androgens and oestrogens.
- **First sign:** girls, **breast buds (thelarche, SMR 2)** at 7 to 12 years; boys, **testicular enlargement (SMR 2)** from about 9.5 years.
- **Menarche**: within about 3 years of thelarche, at SMR 3 to 4, average 12.5 years (range 9 to 15).
- **Peak height velocity:** girls 8 to 9 cm/yr at SMR 2 to 3 (about 6 months before menarche); boys 9 to 10 cm/yr at SMR 3 to 4. 15 to 20% of adult height is gained in puberty.
- 50 to 65% of total body calcium is laid down in puberty.
- Psychosocial development tracks **pubertal stage** more than age; conflict with parents peaks in middle adolescence.

## 1. Definition and phases

- Adolescence involves changes in physical appearance and rapid change in **physiological, psychological and social** function.
- Hormonally driven changes and ongoing brain development occur within social structures that guide the move to adulthood.
- Phases: **early, middle, late** (roughly 10 to 13, 14 to 16, 17 to 19 years; limits are imprecise).
- Gender, culture, and physical, social and environmental factors strongly shape the course.

## 2. Physical development (puberty)

### 2.1 Endocrinology

- **Adrenarche:** adrenal androgens (mainly **DHEAS**) may rise from as early as 6 years, causing axillary odour and faint pubic hair. It is separate from gonadarche.
- **Gonadarche:** the **GnRH pulse generator** matures, the pituitary releases **LH and FSH** in pulses, mainly during sleep at first (this diurnal variation fades), and the gonads produce androgens and oestrogens.
- The trigger is incompletely understood; genes and environment (epigenetics) both influence timing.

### 2.2 Sexual maturity rating (SMR / Tanner stages)

- Scale from **1 (pre-adolescent) to 5 (adult)**, describing breast (girls), genital (boys) and pubic hair development.
- Timing of each change varies, but the **order is predictable**. Genetics, psychosocial environment, nutrition, general health and possibly environmental exposures influence the range.

| Feature | Boys | Girls |
|---|---|---|
| First sign (SMR 2) | **Testicular enlargement** (from about 9.5 years), then pubic hair | **Breast buds (thelarche)**, 7 to 12 years; pubarche may come first in some |
| Later changes | Penile growth at SMR 3; sperm in urine and nocturnal emissions by SMR 3; seminiferous tubules, epididymis, seminal vesicles and prostate enlarge under LH and testosterone | Ovaries, uterus, labia, clitoris enlarge; endometrium and vaginal mucosa thicken; clear physiological leucorrhoea before menarche |
| Landmark | Peak growth when testicular volume reaches about **9 to 10 cm3** (SMR 4) | **Menarche** SMR 3 to 4, average 12.5 years (range 9 to 15), within 3 years of thelarche |
| Other | **Gynaecomastia** (usually bilateral) in 40 to 65% at SMR 2 to 4, from relative oestrogen excess; usually resolves | Early cycles often anovulatory and irregular, but typically every **21 to 45 days** with **3 to 7 days** of bleeding, even in the first year |

> **Exam tip:** testicular volume is measured with an **orchidometer** (Prader). Pubertal onset is a volume of 4 mL or more. Pubertal gynaecomastia in boys is physiological and needs reassurance.

- Timing of menarche is largely genetic; adiposity, chronic illness, nutrition and environment contribute.

### 2.3 Somatic growth

- Linear growth accelerates in early adolescence in both sexes.
- Girls reach PHV earlier (SMR 2 to 3); boys start later, reach a higher peak, and keep growing 2 to 3 years longer after girls stop, which explains the adult height difference.
- Growth spurt proceeds **distally to centrally**: hands and feet, then arms and legs, then trunk. This gives the early adolescent an "awkward" look.
- **Body composition:** males gain lean mass (strength spurt); females gain a higher proportion of fat.
- **Bone:** growth precedes mineralisation and density gain, so **fracture risk rises** during rapid growth. Skeletal growth outpaces muscle growth, so sprains and strains are common. Peak bone mass is built now, so adequate calcium, vitamin D and exercise matter.

### 2.4 Other systems

- **Cardiovascular:** larger heart, higher blood pressure, greater blood volume and haematocrit (especially males).
- **Respiratory:** increased vital capacity and aerobic capacity.
- **Skin:** androgens stimulate sebaceous and apocrine glands, causing **acne** and body odour.
- **Larynx:** rapid growth of larynx, pharynx and lungs; voice breaking in males, preceded by voice cracking.
- **Eyes:** elongation of the globe may cause **myopia**.
- **Teeth:** jaw growth; last deciduous teeth lost; permanent canines, premolars and then molars erupt.
- **Sleep:** physiological shift gives more sleep need and delayed sleep onset, so difficulty waking in the morning.

## 3. Cognitive and moral development

- Increasing **formal operational thinking**; middle and late adolescents consider multiple options and long-term consequences; verbal expression improves. Brain structure and function keep maturing through adolescence.
- **Early:** egocentricity, belief of being "on stage" (the imaginary audience), stressful because they feel judged. Greater need for privacy.
- **Middle:** better empathy, creativity and intellect. Abstract thinking plus a sense of uniqueness gives a **sense of immortality** and risk-taking.
- **Late:** future-oriented, able to delay gratification, independent thought, compromise, stable sense of self. Under stress they may regress to younger coping.

| Stage | Moral reasoning |
|---|---|
| Pre-adolescent | Follows rules to please authority and avoid punishment |
| Early adolescent | Strong sense of right and wrong, seen as absolute |
| Middle and late | Morality driven by wanting to be seen as good, by place in society, or duty to care for others; rational conscience and own values (usually close to parents' values) |

- Moral decisions are strongly affected by emotional context. Groups offering simple answers to complex questions (religious or political) can be very attractive.

## 4. Psychosocial development

- Correlates more with **pubertal status** than chronological age; strongly shaped by culture and environment. Some late adolescents move straight into marriage, work and financial independence; others stay dependent while studying.
- **Separation from parents** is the hallmark: more privacy, less time with family, rejection of advice, alternative adult role models (teachers, coaches, friends' parents). **Parent-child conflict peaks in middle adolescence.** Parents remain a critical source of support and influence.
- **Peer group** grows in importance: early adolescents mix with same-sex peers (girls relationship-centred, boys activity-centred). Conformity in dress, speech and behaviour is normal. Peer influence may be positive, negative or neutral. Late adolescents are less vulnerable. Gang membership is one form of peer acceptance.
- **Sexuality:** early adolescents have heightened sexual awareness, talk and gossip, and masturbation/exploration is common; romantic relationships lack depth.
- **Body image:** poor or distorted body image and **eating disorders** typically start in early to middle adolescence.
- **Timing effects:** puberty usually brings a positive self-image in males; females, especially **early maturers**, may view changes negatively. Early-maturing girls and late-maturing boys are at higher psychosocial risk.

## 5. Practical implications for clinicians

- Reframe the "problems" of adolescence as normal milestones for parents to expect and accept.
- Present puberty and sexuality as positive, health-affirming changes, not only as risks.
- Support early-maturing girls and late-maturing boys; promote positive coping, especially in chronic illness.
- Examine in **private** (parent outside if the adolescent agrees) to allow confidential discussion; reassure about normal development.
- Remind parents that arguing does not mean the adolescent rejects their views; most adopt values like their parents' in the end.
- Use a **HEEADSSS** style psychosocial history (home, education, eating, activities, drugs, sexuality, suicide/depression, safety) and ensure confidentiality, consent and adolescent-friendly services, in line with Kenyan adolescent health guidelines.

> **Remember:** abnormal puberty is a different topic: **precocious puberty** (breasts before 8 in girls, testes enlargement before 9 in boys) and **delayed puberty** (no breast development by 13 in girls, no testicular enlargement by 14 in boys) need investigation.

## Key points to revise

1. Adolescence has three phases; puberty follows GnRH pulse generator maturation, then LH/FSH, then sex steroids.
2. Thelarche (7 to 12 years) in girls and testicular enlargement (from 9.5 years) in boys mark SMR 2.
3. Menarche averages 12.5 years, at SMR 3 to 4, within 3 years of thelarche; early cycles are irregular and anovulatory.
4. PHV: girls 8 to 9 cm/yr (SMR 2 to 3), boys 9 to 10 cm/yr (SMR 3 to 4); 15 to 20% of adult height gained.
5. Growth is distal to central; bone growth precedes mineralisation, hence more fractures; 50 to 65% of body calcium is laid down in puberty.
6. Pubertal gynaecomastia occurs in 40 to 65% of boys and usually resolves.
7. Early adolescence: egocentrism and privacy; middle: risk-taking and peak parental conflict; late: future focus and stable identity.
8. See adolescents alone, ensure confidentiality, and support early-maturing girls and late-maturing boys.
