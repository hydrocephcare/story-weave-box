# Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathies

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Rheumatology**

> **In one line:** the idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (polymyositis, dermatomyositis, inclusion body myositis) are rare autoimmune diseases in which the immune system attacks skeletal muscle, causing weakness, a raised creatine kinase and inflammation on biopsy. Polymyositis and dermatomyositis cause symmetric proximal weakness and respond to steroids; inclusion body myositis causes slow, asymmetric distal and proximal weakness and does not respond.

## What you must know

- Three main types: **dermatomyositis (DM), polymyositis (PM), inclusion body myositis (IBM)**.
- **PM and DM:** subacute (weeks to months), **symmetric proximal** weakness with neck flexor weakness; distal muscles spared; dysphagia in about one third.
- **PM is a CD8 T-cell, MHC class I, perforin-mediated** disease of muscle fibres. DM is a complement-mediated microangiopathy with a skin rash.
- **IBM:** over 50 years, male to female 3:1, insidious over years, **finger flexor, ankle dorsiflexor and quadriceps** weakness, **rimmed vacuoles** on biopsy, poor response to treatment.
- Investigation: **CK (up to 50 times normal or more)**, myositis-specific antibodies (anti-Jo-1), EMG, MRI, **muscle biopsy** (key).
- Treatment: **high-dose prednisolone** first line, then methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate or rituximab; physiotherapy.
- Associated with **cancer** (especially DM), interstitial lung disease and cardiac disease.

## 1. Introduction, history and classification

- A group of **rare autoimmune disorders** that primarily affect muscle: the immune system mistakenly attacks healthy muscle.
- Recognised in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. **Dermatomyositis** was first described by the dermatologist **Unna in 1891**; polymyositis was recognised as a separate entity in the 20th century.
- Subtypes: **dermatomyositis, polymyositis, inclusion body myositis** (immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and the antisynthetase syndrome are more recent additions).

## 2. Epidemiology and risk factors

- Annual incidence **0.2 to 1 per 100 000**; prevalence **2 to 25 per 100 000**. The epidemiology in Africa remains unclear.
- Risk factors: **genetics (HLA)**, environment, **infections**, **medications** (for example statins, penicillamine), **malignancy**, geography.

## 3. Polymyositis (PM)

### Features

- Muscular and extra-muscular involvement.
- Adults **over 20 years, more commonly women**; not usually seen in childhood.
- Can be associated with heart, lung and joint involvement, and with an **increased risk of cancer**.

### Pathogenesis

- Exact cause not fully understood.
- **Cell-mediated cytotoxic response:** **CD8+ T cells** invade muscle fibres that express **MHC class I** antigens, causing fibre necrosis through the **perforin pathway**.
- Triggers unclear, but viruses such as **HIV and HTLV** are implicated. They may invade muscle directly, damage vascular endothelium and release cytokines, which induce abnormal MHC expression and make muscle susceptible to destruction.
- HLA gene variation also matters (T-cell receptor development, tolerance, response to environmental agents).

### Histology (muscle biopsy)

- Variability in **fibre size**
- Cellular invasion of **non-necrotic** fibres expressing MHC antigens
- Scattered necrotic and regenerating fibres

### Clinical features

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Weakness** | Progressive **neck flexor and symmetric proximal limb weakness** developing over weeks to months; shoulder and hip girdle (difficulty climbing stairs, rising from a chair, lifting objects). **Distal muscles spared** |
| **Symmetry** | Both sides involved |
| **Fatigue** | Can be severe and not proportional to weakness |
| **Pain and tenderness** | Myalgia and tenderness are the commonest complaints, more in active disease |
| **Dysphagia** | In about **one third**; choking and aspiration (pharyngeal and upper oesophageal muscles) |
| **Joint pain** | Arthralgia in some; joints are not the main target |
| **Systemic** | Fever, weight loss, malaise |
| **Respiratory** | Respiratory muscle weakness in severe cases; **interstitial lung disease** |
| **Skin** | Gottron papules (raised scaly areas over knuckles) and **heliotrope rash** (purple rash on upper eyelids) are characteristic of **dermatomyositis** |

> **Check:** the slides list Gottron papules and heliotrope rash among the findings of polymyositis. These skin signs define **dermatomyositis**; polymyositis by definition has no rash.

### Work-up

- **Creatine kinase** raised, up to 50 times normal or higher.
- **Myositis-specific antibodies**, for example **anti-Jo-1** (antisynthetase syndrome: myositis, ILD, arthritis, mechanic's hands, Raynaud).
- **MRI and ultrasound** to show the extent of muscle involvement.
- **EMG** abnormal in **90%**: membrane irritability (fibrillations, positive sharp waves), myopathic motor units, and in chronic disease evidence of denervation-reinnervation.
- Muscle biopsy.
- Bohan and Peter criteria for PM and DM: symmetric proximal weakness, raised muscle enzymes, characteristic EMG, characteristic biopsy, and (for DM) typical skin rash.

## 4. Inclusion body myositis (IBM)

### Features

- One of the commonest inflammatory myopathies, in patients **older than 50 years**; **male to female 3:1**.
- **Insidious**, progressing over years.
- Unique in involving **both proximal and distal** muscles, **symmetrically or asymmetrically**.
- Characteristic pattern: early weakness of **finger flexors, ankle dorsiflexors and knee extensors (quadriceps)**. Axial weakness may cause **head drop and camptocormia**.
- **Dysphagia in over half** of patients.

### Pathogenesis

- Not completely understood; likely an interplay between **inflammatory** and **degenerative** pathways.
- Inflammatory component: mostly **CD8+ cytotoxic T cells** and macrophages invading non-necrotic fibres, with CD4+ T cells and macrophages around them.
- Degenerative component: abnormal **protein processing** with ageing and deposition of toxic polymers (amyloid-like, congophilic), which damage muscle and trigger inflammation.

### Histology

- Atrophic fibres
- Mononuclear cell infiltrates of **non-necrotic fibres** in an endomysial and perivascular pattern
- **Rimmed vacuoles** (the hallmark)
- **Congophilic inclusions**, intravacuolar or extravacuolar

### Clinical features

- **Distal and proximal** involvement, often **asymmetrical**, slowly progressive over months to years.
- **Atrophy**: quadriceps; forearm flexors and intrinsic hand muscles.
- Dysphagia.
- **Finger flexor weakness** (poor grip and pinch).
- **Foot drop**.
- Nocturnal myalgias.

### Work-up

- CK elevated (usually only mildly).
- Antibody against **cN-1A (Mup44)**.
- EMG: irritability at rest and on needle insertion, myopathic motor unit potentials on contraction.
- **MRI**: oedema, inflammation and atrophy; separates active from chronic inactive disease.
- ANA screening because of association with autoimmune disease such as SLE.

### Distinguishing features of IBM

- Asymmetric and distal muscle involvement
- Insidious onset, slow progression; average **5 years** of symptoms before diagnosis
- Muscle atrophy
- Poor response to immunosuppression

## 5. General diagnosis of inflammatory myopathy

1. **History:** onset and progression, family history, drugs, symptoms (weakness, fatigue, pain, dysphagia, joint pain, rash).
2. **Examination:** muscle strength testing for the pattern; skin examination.
3. **Laboratory:** **CK** (also raised in other muscle disease), **ESR and CRP** (may be raised).
4. **EMG and nerve conduction studies:** myopathic pattern, exclude neuropathy.
5. **Muscle biopsy:** the key test (inflammation, fibre damage, pattern specific to the type).
6. **Autoantibodies:** anti-Jo-1 for PM and DM.
7. **Imaging:** MRI for inflammation and extent; CT for muscle and lung involvement.

> **Exam tip:** always exclude other causes of proximal weakness and a raised CK: hypothyroidism, statin myopathy, muscular dystrophy, electrolyte disturbance (hypokalaemia), and steroid myopathy (normal CK).

## 6. Complications

- **Muscle weakness and atrophy** affecting mobility
- **Dysphagia**: weight loss, malnutrition, aspiration
- **Respiratory** muscle weakness and failure
- **Joint contractures**
- **Skin involvement** in DM (heliotrope rash, Gottron papules)
- **Calcium deposits** (calcinosis) in muscle, skin and connective tissue in DM
- **Interstitial lung disease**, particularly in PM
- **Cardiac**: myocarditis, cardiomyopathy
- **Cancer risk**, especially DM; screening is advised
- **Emotional and psychological impact**: anxiety and depression

## 7. Management

A multidisciplinary approach involving rheumatologists, neurologists and other specialists.

### Medical treatment

- **Corticosteroids:** high-dose **prednisolone** is first line to suppress inflammation.
- **Immunosuppressants** if poor response or to reduce steroid exposure: **methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab**. IV immunoglobulin for refractory disease and dysphagia.

### Rehabilitation

- **Physiotherapy:** maintains and improves muscle strength, prevents atrophy.
- **Occupational therapy:** adapts daily activities to preserve independence.

### Other measures

- **Monitor** disease activity (strength, CK) and drug side effects; adjust medicines to response.
- **Pain management:** medicines, physiotherapy modalities, lifestyle changes; NSAIDs or other analgesics.
- **Dermatological care (DM):** topical corticosteroids, sun protection, and sometimes immunosuppressants (hydroxychloroquine).
- **Cardiac and pulmonary monitoring** (lung function tests, echocardiography).
- **Supportive care:** psychological support, adequate nutrition and dietary adjustment, swallowing assessment.
- Steroid-sparing and bone protection (calcium, vitamin D, bisphosphonate) with prolonged steroids.

## 8. Prognosis

- **PM:** most patients improve with immunotherapy but usually need **lifelong treatment**. **Poor prognostic features:** cancer, increased age, lung or cardiac involvement, and late or previously inadequate treatment.
- **IBM:** no effective treatment. It does not directly shorten life expectancy, but may do so indirectly through the consequences of weakness: choking, injury from falls, disrupted breathing.

## Key points to revise

1. Three types: dermatomyositis, polymyositis, inclusion body myositis.
2. PM and DM: symmetric proximal weakness, neck flexor weakness, dysphagia in a third, distal muscles spared.
3. PM is CD8+ T-cell attack on MHC class I-expressing fibres (perforin pathway); HIV and HTLV are implicated.
4. CK up to 50 x normal, anti-Jo-1, EMG abnormal in 90%, MRI, and biopsy as the key test.
5. IBM: over 50, male, finger flexor and quadriceps weakness, rimmed vacuoles, 5-year delay, poor treatment response.
6. Treatment: prednisolone, then methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate or rituximab, with physiotherapy.
7. Screen for cancer (especially DM), interstitial lung disease and cardiac disease.
