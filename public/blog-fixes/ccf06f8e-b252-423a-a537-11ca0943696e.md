TOPIC 1: INTRODUCTION TO MYCOLOGY (Lecture 1)

**Q1. What is mycology and who is regarded as its father?**
- Mycology is the study of fungi
- Derived from Greek word *"mykes"* meaning mushroom
- Raymond Sabouraud is the Father of Medical Mycology (published *Les Teignes*, 1910)

**Q2. List FOUR beneficial effects of fungi.**
- Decomposition — nutrient and carbon recycling
- Source of antibiotics e.g. Penicillin from *Penicillium* spp.
- Fermentation — industrial production of alcohols and organic acids
- Ergot alkaloids from *Claviceps purpurea* — treat migraines, control bleeding, induce uterine contractions
- (Bonus) Model organisms for genetic studies e.g. *Neurospora crassa*

**Q3. List FOUR general properties of fungi.**
- Eukaryotic — have membrane-bound organelles
- Cell wall made of chitin
- Lack chlorophyll (cannot photosynthesize)
- Are chemoheterotrophs — require organic compounds for carbon and energy
- Have ergosterols in their membranes
- Possess 80S ribosomes
- No obligate anaerobes — all require oxygen

**Q4. How are fungi classified based on sexual reproduction?**
- **Zygomycetes** — produce zygospores
- **Ascomycetes** — produce ascospores inside asci
- **Basidiomycetes** — produce basidiospores on basidia
- **Deuteromycetes (Fungi Imperfecti)** — no known sexual spores

**Q5. What are dimorphic fungi? Give THREE examples.**
- Fungi that exist in two forms depending on temperature/environment
- Yeast form: in host tissue and at 37°C in vitro
- Mould form: in natural habitat and at 25°C in vitro
- Examples: *Histoplasma capsulatum*, *Blastomyces dermatitidis*, *Coccidioides immitis*, *Paracoccidioides brasiliensis*

**Q6. Differentiate between septate and aseptate hyphae.**
- **Septate hyphae** — have cross walls (septa) with pores; cytoplasm is continuous; damage to one compartment doesn't kill the whole strand
- **Aseptate hyphae** — no septa (non-septate); more primitive; damage kills the entire strand
- All moulds are septate EXCEPT Zygomycetes (e.g. *Rhizopus, Mucor*)

**Q7. List FIVE types of asexual fungal spores.**
- Conidia/Conidiospores
- Sporangiospores
- Arthrospores
- Chlamydospores
- Blastospores/Blastoconidia

**Q8. Differentiate between moulds and yeasts.**
- **Moulds** — filamentous, made of hyphae; mass of hyphae = mycelium; e.g. *Aspergillus*
- **Yeasts** — unicellular; reproduce by budding forming blastoconidia; e.g. *Cryptococcus neoformans*
- **Yeast-like** — yeasts that produce pseudohyphae e.g. *Candida albicans*

##  TOPIC 2: FUNGAL PATHOGENESIS (Lecture 2)

**Q9. List FIVE virulence factors of fungi.**
- Adhesion to host cells via cell wall glycoproteins
- Capsule production — resists phagocytosis
- Secretion of enzymes: keratinase, elastase, collagenase
- Ability to resist killing by phagocytes (as in dimorphic fungi)
- Thermal dimorphism
- Secretion of mycotoxins
- Block cell-mediated immunity of the host

**Q10. List FIVE host defense factors against fungal infections.**
- Physical barriers — skin and mucous membranes
- Fatty acid content of the skin
- Normal flora — keeps fungi in commensal state
- pH of skin and body fluids
- Phagocytic cells — PMNs, monocytes, macrophages
- Epithelial cell turnover
- Serum factors (chemical barriers)

**Q11. List SIX predisposing factors to fungal infections.**
- Prolonged antibiotic therapy — disrupts normal flora
- HIV infection, cancer, diabetes, obesity
- Immunosuppressive drugs/transplants
- Indwelling catheters
- Irradiation therapy
- Surgical procedures
- Drug addiction; extremes of age

**Q12. Why is cell-mediated immunity (CMI) more important than humoral immunity in fungal infections?**
- Fungi are intracellular pathogens — stay inside host cells
- CMI (T-lymphocytes, macrophages, PMNs) directly targets infected cells
- Antibodies have limited access to intracellular fungi
- However, antibodies assist through opsonization against *Candida* and *Cryptococcus*

**Q13. What are the five categories of mycoses?**
- Superficial mycoses
- Cutaneous mycoses
- Subcutaneous mycoses
- Systemic (deep) mycoses
- Opportunistic mycoses

## TOPIC 3: FUNGAL INFECTIONS / MYCOSES (Lecture 3)

**Q14. Differentiate between superficial and cutaneous mycoses.**
- **Superficial** — infects outermost layer of skin/hair/nails; no cellular immune response; cosmetic problem only; e.g. Pityriasis versicolor, Tinea nigra
- **Cutaneous** — invades keratinized tissues; evokes cellular immune response; can cause hypersensitivity reactions; caused by dermatophytes

**Q15. What is Tinea versicolor? How is it diagnosed?**
- Fungal infection of the stratum corneum; presents as hypo/hyperpigmented skin patches
- Caused by *Malassezia globosa* / *Malassezia furfur*
- Diagnosis:
  - KOH mount — "spaghetti and meatballs" appearance (short hyphae + budding cells)
  - Wood's lamp — yellow fluorescence
- Treatment: Topical imidazole

**Q16. Name the three genera of dermatophytes and the tissues they infect.**
- *Microsporum* — hair and skin; rarely nails; mostly in children
- *Trichophyton* — hair, skin AND nails; both children and adults
- *Epidermophyton* — skin and nails; rarely hair; mainly adults

**Q17. How are dermatophytes classified ecologically?**
- **Anthropophilic** — infects humans only; transmitted person-to-person; e.g. *T. rubrum*, *E. floccosum*
- **Zoophilic** — associated with animals; transmitted to humans by animal contact; e.g. *M. canis*
- **Geophilic** — found in soil; transmitted by direct soil exposure; e.g. *M. gypseum*

**Q18. How are dermatophytic infections (Tinea) classified clinically?**
- Named according to site of infection:
  - *Tinea capitis* — scalp
  - *Tinea pedis* — feet (Athlete's foot)
  - *Tinea corporis* — body
  - *Tinea cruris* — groin (Jock itch)
  - *Tinea unguium* — nails (onychomycosis)
  - *Tinea barbae* — beard area

**Q19. Describe the laboratory diagnosis of dermatophytoses.**
- Specimen: skin scales, nail/hair clippings
- Wood's lamp — fluorescence in infected hair (green/yellow)
- Direct microscopy with KOH (10–20%) — branching hyphae; ectothrix (spores outside hair) or endothrix (spores inside hair)
- Culture on SDA — identify by colony morphology and microscopy with LPCB stain

**Q20. What is the laboratory diagnosis of Candida infection?**
- Specimen: exudates, scrapings, aspirates, blood
- Direct microscopy (KOH/Gram stain) — budding yeast + pseudohyphae
- Culture on Bird Agar/SDA — off-white colonies with yeast smell
- Germ tube test — positive (presumptive diagnosis of *C. albicans*)
- CHROM Agar — different colored colonies for different *Candida* spp.
- API biochemical tests — carbohydrate assimilation/fermentation

**Q21. What are the virulence factors of *Cryptococcus*?**
- Capsule — inhibits phagocytosis
- Melanin production (by phenol oxidase enzyme) — antioxidant; protects from intracellular killing

**Q22. How is Cryptococcal infection diagnosed in the laboratory?**
- Specimen: CSF, blood, sputum, urine, biopsy
- India ink / nigrosin staining of CSF — demonstrates encapsulated yeast
- Latex agglutination — detects polysaccharide capsular antigen in CSF or serum (rapid test)
- Culture on SDA — creamy, mucoid colonies; Birdseed agar — brown (melanin) colonies
- Biochemical: urease test — positive; API tests
- Treatment: Amphotericin B + Fluconazole or + 5-Flucytosine

##
TOPIC 4: MYCOTOXINS (Lecture 4)

**Q23. Define mycotoxin and mycotoxicosis.**
- **Mycotoxin** — toxic secondary metabolites produced by fungi; low molecular weight, diverse chemical structures
- **Mycotoxicosis** — disease/condition caused by the action of mycotoxins; affects liver, kidney, lungs, nervous and immune systems

**Q24. Name the three main genera that produce mycotoxins.**
- *Aspergillus*
- *Fusarium*
- *Penicillium*

**Q25. What are aflatoxins? Which is most dangerous?**
- Toxic metabolites produced by *Aspergillus flavus* and *A. parasiticus*
- Types: B1, B2, G1, G2 (B = blue fluorescence; G = green fluorescence under UV)
- **Aflatoxin B1 (AFB1)** — most toxic; potent naturally occurring carcinogen; main target organ is the liver
- AFM1 is a metabolite found in milk of cows fed contaminated feed
- Associated crops: corn, peanuts, rice, oilseeds

**Q26. List THREE characteristics of mycotoxin-induced disease.**
- Not always transmitted between animals
- Pharmaceutical treatment does not alter the disease course
- Usually presents as uncertain, sub-acute or chronic condition
- No specific treatment for most mycotoxins

TOPIC 5: DIAGNOSIS OF FUNGAL DISEASES (Lecture 5)

**Q27. What are the methods used for laboratory diagnosis of fungal infections?**
- Direct examination (microscopy)
- Fungal cultures
- Serological tests
- PCR and molecular methods
- Skin tests

**Q28. How is specimen collected for diagnosis of superficial mycosis?**
- Clean infected area with 70% alcohol
- Collect in sterile paper/petri dish (to avoid drying, reduce bacterial contamination, maintain viability)
- Dermatophytic lesion — scrape outward from edge with scalpel
- Scalp lesion — scrape with blunt scalpel, include hair, scales and follicle contents
- Onychomycosis — stop antifungals one week before collection

**Q29. List the methods used in direct examination (microscopy) of fungal specimens.**
- KOH preparation (10–20%) — stained with blue/black ink; digests protein debris and keratin
- Unstained wet mount
- Calcofluor white — fluorescent stain; binds to chitin and glucan in fungal cell wall
- India ink — for capsulated fungi (e.g. *Cryptococcus*)
- Gram stain — fungi are Gram positive
- Histopathology with special stains: PAS (Periodic Acid-Schiff), H&E

**Q30. What findings on microscopy suggest a dermatophyte infection vs a yeast infection?**
- **Dermatophyte:**
  - Branching hyphae/mycelium
  - Arthrospores
  - Conidia
  - Ectothrix (spores outside hair shaft) or endothrix (inside hair)
- **Yeast infection:**
  - Budding yeast cells
  - Pseudohyphae

**Q31. Describe Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (SDA) and why it is used.**
- Contains 2% dextrose, antibiotics (gentamicin, chloramphenicol) and cycloheximide
- Antibiotics suppress bacterial contamination
- Gold standard culture medium for fungi
- Other selective media: Corn meal agar (chlamydospore formation), Birdseed agar (*Cryptococcus*), BHI agar (dimorphic fungi)

**Q32. What are the temperature and time requirements for fungal cultures?**
- Most pathogenic fungi: 37°C
- Superficial mycosis: 30°C
- Dimorphic fungi: 25°C AND 37°C (both temperatures needed)
- Incubation: at least 4 weeks
- Positive cultures often seen in 7–10 days
- *Candida* and *Aspergillus*: 24–72 hours

**Q33. How do you interpret a fungal culture result?**
- Isolation of established pathogen (e.g. *H. capsulatum*, *C. neoformans*) = evidence of true infection
- Isolation of commensal/opportunistic fungi (e.g. *Candida*) — consider:
  - Repeated isolation of same strain in multiple specimens
  - Same strain from different body sites
  - Immune status of the patient
  - Serological evidence to support

**Q34. What stain is used to identify fungi under the microscope in culture, and what are its components?**
- **Lactophenol Cotton Blue (LPCB)**
  - Lactic acid — preserves fungal structure
  - Phenol — kills live organisms
  - Glycerol — prevents drying
  - Cotton blue — imparts blue color to fungal structures

**Q35. What are the serological tests used in medical mycology?**
- Agglutination
- Immunodiffusion (most widely used)
- Counter immuno-electrophoresis (CIEP)
- Indirect fluorescent antibody detection
- ELISA
- RIA (Radio Immuno Assay)

**Q36. When is antigen detection preferred over antibody detection in serology?**
- In early stages of infection (antibodies not yet formed)
- In immunocompromised patients (poor antibody response)
- Antibody detection is used for: diagnosing systemic/subcutaneous mycoses, assessing prognosis, monitoring response to treatment

**Q37. List molecular/other methods used to diagnose fungal infections.**
- PCR (Polymerase Chain Reaction)
- RFLP (Restriction Fragment Length Polymorphism)
- Nucleic acid probes
- Protein electrophoresis
