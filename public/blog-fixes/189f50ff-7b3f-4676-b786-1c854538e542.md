# Pericardial Diseases

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Cardiology**

> **In one line:** the pericardium is a tough double-layered sac that limits cardiac filling, so inflammation (acute pericarditis), fluid (effusion and tamponade) or scarring (constriction) all end by restricting diastolic filling of the heart. Pain and a friction rub mark acute pericarditis, Beck's triad marks tamponade, and a rigid calcified shell marks constriction.

## What you must know

- **Acute pericarditis** is the commonest pericardial disorder: sharp pleuritic retrosternal pain relieved by sitting forward, a friction rub (about 85%) and diffuse concave ST elevation.
- **Diagnose** from pain, rub, typical ECG changes and a new or worsening pericardial effusion (any two to three of these).
- Treat with **NSAIDs plus a PPI** and add **colchicine** to cut recurrence. Steroids are second line because they increase recurrence.
- **Cardiac tamponade** is a clinical diagnosis and an emergency: **Beck's triad** (hypotension, muffled heart sounds, raised JVP), **pulsus paradoxus**. Treatment is **pericardiocentesis**, ideally echo-guided.
- Tamponade depends on the **speed** of fluid accumulation: 150 mL rapidly can tamponade, while 1000 mL slowly may not.
- **Constrictive pericarditis** gives right-sided failure with a thickened, often calcified pericardium. **Kussmaul's sign**, septal bounce and the "square root" sign are the clues. Definitive treatment is **pericardiectomy**.
- In developing countries (Kenya included) **TB** is the leading infective cause of constriction and of large bloody or exudative effusions.

## 1. Anatomy and function

- The pericardium is a **double-layered sac**: an inner **visceral** layer (epicardium) and an outer fibrous **parietal** layer.
- Between them is a small amount (**15 to 50 mL**) of fluid, an **ultrafiltrate of plasma**.
- Functions:
  - Exerts a **restraining force** that prevents sudden dilatation of the cardiac chambers, especially the right atrium and ventricle, during exercise and hypervolaemia.
  - Fixes the heart in position and probably slows the spread of infection from lung and pleura to the heart.
  - Reduces friction between the beating heart and surrounding structures.
- **Total absence** of the pericardium (congenital or after surgery) usually causes no obvious disease.
- In **partial left pericardial defects**, the main pulmonary artery and left atrium may bulge through; rarely the left atrium herniates and strangulates, causing sudden death.

## 2. Classification of pericarditis

| Type | Duration | Subtypes |
|---|---|---|
| **Acute** | Under 6 weeks | Fibrinous; effusive (serous or sanguineous) |
| **Subacute** | 6 weeks to 6 months | Effusive-constrictive; constrictive |
| **Chronic** | Over 6 months | Constrictive; adhesive (non-constrictive) |

## 3. Aetiology

| Group | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Idiopathic** | Commonest in developed countries; often presumed viral |
| **Viral** | Coxsackievirus A and B, echovirus, herpesviruses, mumps, adenovirus, hepatitis viruses, **HIV**, EBV, CMV |
| **Bacterial** | **Tuberculosis**, pneumococcus, streptococcus, staphylococcus, Neisseria, Legionella, Chlamydia, Lyme disease |
| **Other infections** | Mycoplasma; fungal (histoplasmosis, coccidioidomycosis, Candida, blastomycosis); parasitic (Echinococcus, amoebiasis, toxoplasmosis); infective endocarditis with a valve-ring abscess |
| **Neoplasms** | Metastatic: lung and breast cancer, Hodgkin disease, leukaemia, melanoma. Primary: rhabdomyosarcoma, teratoma, fibroma, lipoma, leiomyoma, angioma. Also paraneoplastic |
| **Cardiac** | Early post-infarction pericarditis; late post-cardiac injury syndrome (**Dressler's syndrome**); myocarditis; dissecting aortic aneurysm |
| **Autoimmune** | SLE, rheumatoid arthritis, vasculitis, scleroderma, mixed connective tissue disease, granulomatosis with polyangiitis, polyarteritis nodosa, sarcoidosis, IBD, rheumatic fever |
| **Metabolic** | **Uraemia**, hypothyroidism, ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome |
| **Trauma** | Iatrogenic (cardiac surgery, catheter procedures), blunt or penetrating chest injury |
| **Radiation and drugs** | Mediastinal radiotherapy; drug-induced (for example hydralazine, procainamide, methysergide) |

> **Remember:** in Kenya consider **TB and HIV** in every patient with a large pericardial effusion, tamponade or constriction.

# Part A. Acute pericarditis

## 4. Definition

Inflammation of the pericardial sac. It is the **most common disorder involving the pericardium**.

## 5. Clinical features

### Chest pain

- Sudden onset, **retrosternal or substernal**.
- **Sharp and pleuritic**; worse on coughing and inspiration.
- **Worse supine, better sitting upright or leaning forward**.
- Often radiates to the **neck, left shoulder or trapezius ridge** (the trapezius radiation is characteristic of pericardial pain, because the phrenic nerve supplies both).

### Pericardial friction rub

- Present in about **85%** of cases.
- **High-pitched, scratchy or squeaky**; best heard with the **diaphragm** at the **left sternal border**, with the patient **leaning forward** and holding expiration.
- Classically has **three components**: atrial systole, ventricular systole and early ventricular diastole. It may be heard as one or two components and can come and go, so listen repeatedly.

### Other features

- Low-grade fever, malaise, and symptoms of the underlying cause (recent viral illness, TB, uraemia, connective tissue disease).

## 6. Diagnosis

Diagnostic criteria (any two or more support the diagnosis):

1. Classic type of chest pain
2. Pericardial friction rub
3. Typical ECG changes
4. New or worsening pericardial effusion

### ECG evolution

| Stage | Timing | Findings |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | First hours to days | **Diffuse ST elevation, concave upwards**, with PR depression |
| **2** | First week | ST and PR segments return to normal |
| **3** | Later | Diffuse **T wave inversion** |
| **4** | Weeks onwards | ECG normalises, or T inversion persists indefinitely |

> **Exam tip:** pericarditis versus STEMI. Pericarditis has **diffuse** (not territorial) concave ST elevation, **PR depression**, no reciprocal ST depression (except in aVR and V1) and no Q waves. STEMI has convex, territorial elevation with reciprocal changes.

### Imaging and other tests

- **Chest X-ray:** normal, or enlarged "water-bottle" heart if effusion is large; also shows pleural effusion and any lung disease.
- **Echocardiography:** confirms and sizes the effusion and looks for tamponade.
- **CT and MRI:** show pericardial thickening, effusion and inflammation, and help in a complicated course.
- **Blood tests:** FBC, ESR/CRP, urea and creatinine, troponin (myopericarditis), and tests for the cause (TB, HIV, ANA, rheumatoid factor, thyroid function).

## 7. Management

- **Treat the underlying cause.**
- **First line for inflammation: NSAIDs** (ibuprofen, naproxen, indomethacin or aspirin). Give them **with a proton-pump inhibitor** to protect the stomach.
- **Colchicine as an add-on to NSAIDs** reduces recurrence. Its contraindications are the same as for NSAIDs, and it should be avoided in **peptic ulcer disease and end-stage renal disease**.
- **Steroids** (prednisone, methylprednisolone) are **second line**, used when NSAIDs are contraindicated, because steroids are associated with a **high rate of recurrence**. They are **contraindicated in uraemic pericarditis and in autoimmune pericarditis** (here the cause itself is treated).
- Specific causes:
  - **Uraemic:** dialysis
  - **Neoplastic:** chemotherapy or radiotherapy
  - **Autoimmune:** immunosuppressants
  - **TB:** antituberculous therapy (see Part C)

### Criteria for hospitalisation

- Fever above 38 degrees Celsius and leucocytosis
- Evidence suggesting **cardiac tamponade**
- **Large effusion (over 20 mm)**
- Immunosuppressed patient
- History of **oral anticoagulant therapy**
- Acute trauma
- **No response to NSAIDs within 7 days**
- **Raised troponin**, suggesting myopericarditis

> **Remember:** monitor every patient with acute pericarditis for an effusion. Serial echocardiography and venous and arterial pressure monitoring detect early tamponade, and a large effusion should be drained.

# Part B. Pericardial effusion and cardiac tamponade

## 8. Definitions

- **Pericardial effusion:** an abnormal amount (more than the normal 15 to 50 mL) or abnormal character of fluid in the pericardial space. It may be serous, serosanguineous, haemorrhagic or chylous.
- **Cardiac tamponade:** a clinical syndrome in which fluid or blood accumulating in the pericardial sac **compresses the heart, reducing ventricular filling and causing haemodynamic compromise**.

## 9. Causes of tamponade

- Malignant disease
- Uraemia
- Idiopathic pericarditis
- Infection: **TB, HIV**
- **Anticoagulation**
- Connective tissue diseases
- Dressler syndrome or post-pericardiotomy
- Chest trauma, cardiac surgery (including cardiac perforation during procedures)

Any cause of pericarditis can lead to tamponade.

## 10. Pathophysiology

- The volume that impairs filling depends on the **rate of accumulation** and the **compliance** of the pericardium.
  - **150 mL, rapidly,** can markedly raise pericardial pressure and severely impede cardiac output (for example haemopericardium after trauma).
  - **1000 mL, slowly,** may accumulate with no significant effect, because the sac stretches.
- Three phases:

| Phase | What happens |
|---|---|
| **I** | Fluid impairs ventricular relaxation and filling, so higher filling pressures are needed. Left and right ventricular filling pressures are still **above** pericardial pressure |
| **II** | Pericardial pressure rises **above** ventricular filling pressure, and cardiac output falls |
| **III** | Pericardial and left ventricular filling pressures **equalise**, with a further fall in output |

- **Tachycardia** is the first compensation. Systemic venous return is impaired, the right atrium and ventricle are compressed and collapse in diastole.

## 11. Clinical features

- **Beck's triad:** **hypotension, soft or absent (muffled) heart sounds, raised JVP**.
- Dyspnoea, collapse, tachycardia.
- **Pulsus paradoxus:** a fall in systolic BP of more than **10 mmHg** on inspiration. When severe the pulse can be felt to weaken or vanish on inspiration. Usually it is measured with a sphygmomanometer during slow respiration.
  - Mechanism: both ventricles share a tight, non-expandable pericardial sac. Inspiratory filling of the right ventricle bulges the septum to the left, reducing left ventricular volume, stroke volume and systolic pressure.
  - It also occurs in about **one third of constrictive pericarditis**, and in hypovolaemic shock, acute and chronic obstructive airway disease and pulmonary embolism.
- **Kussmaul's sign** (JVP rises paradoxically on inspiration) is described in this slide set among the signs; it is classic in constriction and less typical of tamponade.

> **Exam tip:** pulsus paradoxus is more than 10 mmHg fall in systolic BP on inspiration. In tamponade there is **no y descent** on the JVP; in constriction the y descent is **prominent**.

## 12. Differential diagnosis

- Cardiogenic shock
- Constrictive pericarditis
- Effusive-constrictive pericarditis
- Tension pneumothorax
- Pulmonary embolism

## 13. Investigations

- **Clinical diagnosis** with a high index of suspicion. Further tests assess the patient's condition and the cause.
- **Chest X-ray:** cardiomegaly, **"water-bottle" shaped heart**, evidence of chest trauma.
- **2D echocardiography:** **early diastolic collapse of the right ventricular free wall**, right atrial collapse, "swinging" of the heart in the sac. Doppler shows exaggerated respiratory variation of flow.
- **CT scan:** compression of the coronary sinus is an early sign.
- **ECG:** sinus tachycardia, **low-voltage QRS**, PR depression (and electrical alternans in large effusions).
- **Laboratory:**
  - Cardiac enzymes (raised in MI or cardiac trauma)
  - FBC (infection), ANA, ESR, rheumatoid factor (connective tissue disease)
  - HIV testing
  - **Pericardial fluid analysis:** **GeneXpert for TB**, cytology for neoplasm, culture, protein, cell count

## 14. Management

- Tamponade is a **medical emergency**. Prognosis depends on prompt recognition and treatment of the tamponade and its cause.
- **Pericardiocentesis** is the treatment: drain at once, by the **apical, parasternal or subxiphoid** approach, **under echocardiographic guidance** if possible. Measure intrapericardial pressure before withdrawing fluid, then drain as completely as possible.
- **IV saline** infusion is given while preparing for the procedure to support filling pressure, but it must **not delay drainage**.
- **Subxiphoid surgical drainage (pericardial window)** is used for recurrent tamponade, loculated effusions, and when tissue is needed for diagnosis.
- For recurrent tamponade or effusions, a **sclerosing agent** (corticosteroids, tetracycline or an antineoplastic drug) can be instilled through an intrapericardial catheter.
- A **pericardio-peritoneal shunt** can be made in malignant recurrent effusions.
- Fluid analysis: an **exudate** suggests neoplasm, renal failure or cardiac trauma in developed countries. In developing countries **TB** causes exudative and/or bloody effusions.
- **Medical management has a limited role** in tamponade.

> **Exam tip:** do not give diuretics or vasodilators in tamponade; they drop preload and worsen the output.

# Part C. Chronic constrictive pericarditis

## 15. Definition and pathology

- A chronic inflammatory process of both the fibrous and serous layers of the pericardium, causing **thickening and constriction of the ventricles**. The result is impaired **diastolic filling** and reduced cardiac function.
- It follows healing of an acute fibrinous or serofibrinous pericarditis, or resorption of a chronic effusion, with **obliteration of the pericardial cavity** by granulation tissue that contracts into a firm, often **calcified** scar encasing the heart.
- Pericardial fluid is usually absent or of normal volume.
- The pericardium is **3 to 20 mm** thick (normal 1 to 2 mm).
- Scarring is symmetric and limits filling of all chambers. In a subset (especially **after cardiac surgery**) it develops rapidly and is **reversible (transient constriction)**.

## 16. Causes

- **Idiopathic** (commonest in developed nations)
- **Infectious, mainly TB** (commonest in developing nations)
- Irradiation
- Post-surgical
- Neoplastic
- Autoimmune (connective tissue) disorders
- Uraemia
- Post-trauma
- Sarcoidosis
- Methysergide therapy
- Implantable defibrillator patches

## 17. Pathophysiology

- **Systemic venous congestion:** hepatic congestion, peripheral oedema, ascites, sometimes anasarca and **cardiac cirrhosis**.
- **Reduced cardiac output** (from impaired ventricular filling): fatigue, muscle wasting, weight loss.
- In "absolute" constriction, contractility is preserved, though ejection fraction may fall because of reduced preload. If the **myocardium** is also involved by inflammation and fibrosis, contractile dysfunction can be severe and **predicts a poor response to pericardiectomy**.

## 18. Clinical features

- Usually **right-sided heart failure**: early on leg oedema, vague abdominal complaints and passive hepatic congestion.
- Later: ascites, anasarca, jaundice (cardiac cirrhosis).
- Raised pulmonary venous pressure: exertional dyspnoea, cough, orthopnoea.
- **Atrial fibrillation** and tricuspid regurgitation.
- End stage: severe fatigue, muscle wasting, **cachexia**; recurrent pleural effusions and syncope.
- It can be mistaken for any cause of right-sided heart failure, or for end-stage liver disease.
- Signs: raised JVP with **Kussmaul's sign** (inspiratory rise in right atrial pressure), a pericardial knock in early diastole, hepatomegaly, ascites, oedema.

## 19. Investigations

| Test | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Chest X-ray** | **Pericardial calcification** |
| **Echocardiography** | Pericardial thickening; abrupt **septal "bounce"** in early diastole; premature pulmonary valve opening (raised RV early diastolic pressure); exaggerated respiratory septal shift. **Transoesophageal echo** measures thickness better than transthoracic and correlates well with CT |
| **CT / MRI** | Thickened pericardium; dilated SVC relative to the ascending and descending aorta |
| **Cardiac catheterisation** | **CVP above 14 mmHg**; near **equalisation** of CVP, PA diastolic and wedge pressures; reduced cardiac output; **"square root" sign** (early diastolic dip then plateau) in RV and LV traces; prominent **y descent**; accentuated by a 500 mL volume change |

> **Exam tip:** constriction versus restrictive cardiomyopathy. Calcification, pericardial thickening and exaggerated ventricular interdependence (septal shift with respiration) favour **constriction**, which is curable by surgery.

## 20. Management

- **Surgical pericardiectomy is definitive**, except in transient constriction (which may resolve with anti-inflammatory therapy).
- Relative contraindications: major comorbidity, severe debilitation, and **radiation-induced** disease.
- Medical therapy only supports: **diuretics and salt restriction**.
- Avoid **beta blockers and rate-slowing calcium antagonists**, because sinus tachycardia is compensatory (stroke volume is fixed).
- In **AF with rapid ventricular response**, use **digoxin** first, before beta blockers or calcium antagonists.

### Pericardiectomy

- Usually via **median sternotomy** (bilateral thoracotomies or a left anterolateral thoracotomy are alternatives).
- Midline incision from just below the suprasternal notch to the xiphoid; sternum divided with a saw and opened with a retractor.
- Incise the thin anterior pericardium; develop a plane between epicardium and pericardium by sharp dissection; open the pericardium in the midline from the diaphragm to the base of the great vessels.
- **Free the left ventricle first** to avoid right ventricular distension.
- Free the anterior pericardium 1 to 2 cm anterior to both **phrenic nerves**. Dense epicardial scar can be scored in a grid of crossing lines 1 to 2 cm apart.
- Once freed, excise with low-power electrocautery for haemostasis.
- **Anterior pericardiectomy** gives maximal haemodynamic benefit for right ventricular constriction and can usually be done **without cardiopulmonary bypass**. The benefit of removing diaphragmatic, atrial and posterior pericardium is unclear.

### Outcome

- Excellent symptomatic improvement: **85% of late survivors are NYHA class I**, the rest class II.
- **Operative mortality 14%**, in 70% of deaths from low cardiac output.
- Mortality by NYHA class: **1% for class I to II, 10% for class III, 46% for class IV** (operate early).

## 21. Tuberculous pericarditis

- Affects **1 to 2%** of TB patients, by direct extension from mediastinal nodes, and occasionally by haematogenous or contiguous myocardial spread.
- **Triple-drug antituberculous therapy for a minimum of 9 months** (as given in the lecture). Current practice is the standard 6-month regimen of four drugs (HRZE then HR) as for other forms of TB, so follow the national guideline.
- If the effusion persists or recurs despite treatment, **3 months of corticosteroid** may be an adjunct.
- **Pericardiectomy early and as radical as possible**. Chemotherapy combined with surgery gives gratifying results.

## Key points to revise

1. Acute pericarditis: sharp pleuritic pain eased by sitting forward, friction rub (85%), diffuse concave ST elevation with PR depression.
2. Treat with NSAID plus PPI, add colchicine to reduce recurrence; steroids are second line (they increase recurrence).
3. Tamponade: Beck's triad (low BP, muffled sounds, raised JVP) and pulsus paradoxus (over 10 mmHg).
4. Rate of fluid accumulation matters more than volume: 150 mL fast can kill, 1000 mL slow may be tolerated.
5. Tamponade is treated by urgent echo-guided pericardiocentesis; saline helps but must not delay drainage.
6. Constriction: right heart failure, Kussmaul's sign, calcified thick pericardium, septal bounce, square-root sign.
7. Pericardiectomy is definitive; avoid rate-slowing drugs; operative mortality rises with NYHA class.
8. TB is the main infective cause in developing countries; use GeneXpert on fluid and treat early.
