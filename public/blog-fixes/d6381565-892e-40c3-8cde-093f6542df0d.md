## Summary
This article provides a comprehensive, exam-focused overview of blood transfusion medicine, covering fundamental concepts of blood groups and compatibility, essential pre-transfusion testing, and the various blood products available. It details both immediate and delayed transfusion reactions, outlining their causes, clinical features, and management strategies. Special blood product modifications, the principles of massive transfusion, and critical clinical scenarios are also addressed, emphasizing patient safety and effective practice.

## Key Points
*   ABO and Rh are the most critical blood group systems for compatibility, with O-negative being the universal RBC donor and AB-positive the universal plasma donor.
*   Pre-transfusion testing involves ABO/Rh typing, antibody screening, and crossmatching to ensure compatibility.
*   Transfusion reactions range from mild allergic responses to life-threatening acute haemolytic reactions, TACO, TRALI, and TA-GvHD, each requiring specific recognition and management.
*   Clerical error is the most common cause of acute haemolytic transfusion reactions.
*   Leukoreduction prevents many common reactions and infections, while irradiation is crucial for preventing TA-GvHD in susceptible patients.
*   Massive transfusion protocols aim to replace blood volume rapidly while preventing common complications like hypothermia, hypocalcaemia, and coagulopathy.
*   Careful patient identification and adherence to protocols are paramount for transfusion safety.

## Detailed Notes

### Fundamentals of Blood Groups and Compatibility

The understanding of blood group systems is fundamental to safe transfusion practice. The ABO and Rh systems are the most clinically significant.

#### ABO Blood Group System
The ABO system is characterized by the presence or absence of A and B antigens on red blood cells (RBCs) and naturally occurring antibodies in the plasma.

| Blood Group | Antigens on RBCs | Antibodies in Plasma | Can Donate To | Can Receive From | Notes |
| :---------- | :--------------- | :------------------- | :------------ | :--------------- | :---- |
| **A**       | A                | Anti-B               | A, AB         | A, O             |       |
| **B**       | B                | Anti-A               | B, AB         | B, O             |       |
| **AB**      | A and B          | None                 | AB only       | All              | Universal Recipient (RBCs) |
| **O**       | None             | Anti-A and Anti-B    | All           | O only           | Universal Donor (RBCs) |

Universal RBC donor is O negative, while the universal plasma donor is AB positive (due to the absence of anti-A or anti-B antibodies).

#### Rh Blood Group System and Hemolytic Disease of the Newborn (HDN)
The Rh system is the second most important, primarily due to the D antigen. Individuals are Rh-positive if they have the D antigen and Rh-negative if they lack it. Unlike ABO, anti-Rh antibodies are not naturally present but develop only after exposure (sensitization) to Rh-positive RBCs, typically through transfusion or pregnancy.

**Pathogenesis of HDN:**
1.  **First pregnancy:** An Rh-negative mother carries an Rh-positive fetus. This pregnancy is typically uneventful for the fetus as maternal antibody production is still developing.
2.  **Sensitization at delivery:** During delivery, fetal Rh-positive RBCs can enter the maternal circulation, leading to the mother forming anti-D IgG antibodies.
3.  **Subsequent Rh-positive pregnancy:** In a second or later pregnancy with an Rh-positive fetus, the pre-formed maternal anti-D IgG antibodies can cross the placenta and attack the fetal RBCs, causing haemolysis.
4.  **Consequences:** This can range from mild jaundice to severe anaemia, hydrops fetalis (severe edema), and even intrauterine death.
**Prevention:** Administering anti-D immunoglobulin (Rhogam) to Rh-negative mothers within 72 hours of delivery or any sensitizing event (e.g., miscarriage, amniocentesis) and at 28 weeks of gestation can prevent sensitization.

#### Other Clinically Important Blood Group Systems
Beyond ABO and Rh, several other blood group systems are clinically significant due to their potential to cause haemolytic transfusion reactions and HDN.
*   **Kell system (3rd most immunogenic):** Strong immunogen, can cause HDN and haemolytic transfusion reactions.
*   **Duffy system (4th most immunogenic):** Individuals lacking Duffy antigens (Duffy-null) are resistant to *Plasmodium vivax* malaria.
*   **Kidd system (5th most immunogenic):** Notorious for causing delayed haemolytic transfusion reactions.
*   **MNS system (6th most immunogenic):** Has variable clinical significance.

### Pre-Transfusion Compatibility Testing

Rigorous testing is essential to ensure donor blood is compatible with the recipient, minimizing the risk of adverse reactions.

#### Steps of Pre-Transfusion Testing
1.  **ABO and Rh typing:** Determines the patient's blood group and Rh status.
2.  **Antibody screen:** Patient's serum is tested against a panel of reagent RBCs (known antigens) to detect unexpected or irregular antibodies (alloantibodies) that may cause a reaction.
3.  **Crossmatch:** The patient's serum is directly mixed with donor RBCs. Agglutination or haemolysis indicates incompatibility, and the blood should not be transfused. Crossmatching primarily detects irregular antibodies in the patient's serum that react against specific antigens on the donor RBCs.

#### Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT) vs. Indirect Antiglobulin Test (IAT)
These tests, also known as Coombs tests, are critical for detecting antibodies involved in haemolytic conditions.

*   **Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT):**
    *   **Detects:** Antibodies already bound to the patient's own RBCs *in vivo* (inside the body).
    *   **Uses:** Diagnosing haemolytic transfusion reactions, autoimmune haemolytic anaemia, and HDN.
    *   **Positive DAT:** Indicates that antibodies are actively coating the patient's RBCs.
*   **Indirect Antiglobulin Test (IAT):**
    *   **Detects:** Free antibodies present in the patient's serum *in vitro* (in the test tube).
    *   **Uses:** Pre-transfusion antibody screening to identify unexpected antibodies and in crossmatching. The crossmatch is essentially a form of IAT.

#### Metabisulfite Test
The metabisulfite test is a screening tool used to detect the presence of sickle haemoglobin (HbS). Sodium metabisulfite is added to a blood sample, creating a deoxygenating environment. If HbS is present, it becomes insoluble and polymerizes, causing RBCs to sickle, which is visible microscopically. A positive result confirms the presence of HbS but does **not distinguish between sickle cell trait (HbAS) and sickle cell disease (HbSS)**. Haemoglobin electrophoresis is required to differentiate between these conditions.

### Blood Products and Their Use

A variety of blood products are available, each with specific components, storage requirements, and indications.

| Blood Product      | Contents                                                                      | Storage Conditions                 | Shelf Life             | Indications                                                                   |
| :----------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :--------------------------------- | :--------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Packed Red Blood Cells (pRBCs)** | Red cells, minimal plasma                                                     | 1–6°C                              | 35–42 days             | Symptomatic anaemia, acute haemorrhage                                        |
| **Fresh Frozen Plasma (FFP)** | All coagulation factors, fibrinogen, albumin, immunoglobulins; NO platelets | −18°C                              | 12 months              | Coagulopathy (e.g., liver disease, warfarin reversal), DIC, TTP               |
| **Platelets**      | Platelets in plasma                                                           | 20–24°C with agitation             | **5 days**             | Thrombocytopenia, platelet dysfunction, prevention of bleeding in surgery     |
| **Cryoprecipitate** | Fibrinogen, Factor VIII, Factor XIII, von Willebrand Factor (vWF), fibronectin; NO Factor IX, VII | −18°C                              | 12 months              | Hypofibrinogenaemia, Factor XIII deficiency, von Willebrand Disease (vWD), haemophilia A (if recombinant FVIII not available), DIC |
| **Albumin**        | Heat-treated, virus-safe albumin                                              | Room temperature                   | Long (up to 3 years)   | Hypoalbuminaemia, burns, cirrhosis with refractory ascites, nephrotic syndrome |

### Transfusion Reactions: Immediate

Transfusion reactions can occur acutely, often during or immediately after the transfusion. Prompt recognition and management are critical.

#### Acute Haemolytic Transfusion Reaction (AHTR)
This is the **most serious immediate transfusion reaction**.
*   **Cause:** Primarily ABO incompatibility, most commonly due to **clerical error** (transfusing the wrong blood type to the wrong patient).
*   **Mechanism:** Recipient's preformed IgM antibodies bind to donor ABO antigens on RBCs, leading to rapid complement activation and **intravascular haemolysis**.
*   **Features:** Acute onset of fever, rigors, back or flank pain, haemoglobinuria (red/brown urine), haemoglobinaemia, hypotension, tachycardia, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and acute renal failure.
*   **Management:**
    1.  **STOP TRANSFUSION IMMEDIATELY** (most important first step).
    2.  Keep the intravenous (IV) line open with normal saline to maintain venous access.
    3.  Check patient identity against the blood product label at the bedside.
    4.  Send blood samples (e.g., for repeat crossmatch, DAT, FBC, renal function) and a urine sample for haemoglobin to the lab.
    5.  Monitor urine output and blood pressure; treat DIC and acute renal failure supportively.
    6.  Notify the blood bank and complete an incident report.

#### Febrile Non-Haemolytic Transfusion Reaction (FNHTR)
This is the **most common transfusion reaction overall**.
*   **Cause:** Typically, recipient antibodies reacting against donor leukocyte (HLA) antigens or due to the accumulation of cytokines in stored blood.
*   **Features:** Fever (defined as a rise of ≥1°C from baseline), chills, and malaise. Crucially, there is **no evidence of haemolysis**.
*   **Management:** Slow or stop the transfusion, administer antipyretics like paracetamol.
*   **Prevention:** **Leukoreduction** (filtration to remove white cells from the blood product before storage) is highly effective.

#### Allergic and Anaphylactic Transfusion Reactions
These are hypersensitivity reactions to donor plasma proteins.
*   **Allergic/Urticarial Reaction:**
    *   **Cause:** Recipient IgE antibodies react against donor plasma proteins.
    *   **Features:** Urticaria (hives), pruritus (itching), flushing. There is no fever or haemolysis.
    *   **Management:** Slow the transfusion; administer antihistamines (e.g., chlorpheniramine). If symptoms resolve, transfusion can often be continued cautiously.
*   **Anaphylactic Reaction:**
    *   **Cause:** Most commonly seen in **IgA-deficient patients** who have pre-formed anti-IgA antibodies. Even trace amounts of IgA in donor plasma can trigger a severe reaction.
    *   **Features:** Rapid onset of severe hypotension, bronchospasm, laryngeal oedema, abdominal pain, shock. This is a medical emergency.
    *   **Management:** **STOP TRANSFUSION IMMEDIATELY**. Administer adrenaline, corticosteroids, and IV fluids.
    *   **Future transfusions:** Require **washed RBCs** (to remove plasma proteins including IgA) or products from IgA-deficient donors.

#### Transfusion-Associated Circulatory Overload (TACO) vs. Transfusion-Related Acute Lung Injury (TRALI)
These are two critical causes of acute respiratory distress post-transfusion.

| Feature             | Transfusion-Associated Circulatory Overload (TACO) | Transfusion-Related Acute Lung Injury (TRALI)                       |
| :------------------ | :------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Mechanism**       | Volume overload, acute cardiogenic pulmonary edema | Donor antibodies (anti-HLA or anti-neutrophil) activate recipient leukocytes in pulmonary vasculature, causing non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema |
| **Onset**           | Often during or within a few hours (up to 6 hours) | Within **6 hours** of transfusion                                     |
| **Blood Pressure**  | **Elevated** (hypertension)                        | **Normal or low** (hypotension)                                     |
| **JVP**             | **Raised**                                         | Normal                                                              |
| **Response to Diuretics** | **YES**                                            | **NO**                                                              |
| **CXR Findings**    | Bilateral infiltrates with **cardiomegaly**        | Bilateral infiltrates with **normal heart size**                      |
| **Management**      | Stop/slow transfusion, diuretics, oxygen             | Supportive care: high-flow oxygen, mechanical ventilation if severe |

#### Bacterial Contamination
This is a potentially life-threatening reaction, particularly associated with specific blood products.
*   **Most commonly affects:** **Platelet concentrates** because they are stored at room temperature (20–24°C), which facilitates bacterial growth.
*   **Common organisms:** *Staphylococcus epidermidis*, *Staphylococcus aureus*, and various gram-negative bacteria.
*   **Features:** High fever, rigors, severe hypotension, and features of **septic shock** typically developing during or immediately after the transfusion.
*   **Management:**
    1.  **STOP TRANSFUSION IMMEDIATELY**.
    2.  Obtain blood cultures from the patient AND the implicated blood bag.
    3.  Initiate broad-spectrum antibiotics empirically.
    4.  Provide aggressive resuscitation for septic shock.

### Transfusion Reactions: Delayed

Delayed reactions manifest days to weeks after the transfusion, often after the patient has left the hospital.

#### Delayed Haemolytic Transfusion Reaction (DHTR)
*   **Timing:** Typically occurs **3–10 days** after transfusion.
*   **Cause:** Occurs when a recipient has low-level alloantibodies (often from previous transfusions or pregnancies) that were not detected by the initial antibody screen. After transfusion, an anamnestic (memory) immune response leads to a rapid increase in antibody levels, which then attack the transfused RBCs. Antibodies from the **Kidd blood group system** are notorious for causing DHTRs.
*   **Features:** Unexpected fall in haemoglobin level, mild jaundice, and a positive DAT. Symptoms are generally milder than AHTR.
*   **Management:** Usually mild and self-limiting; monitor renal function and manage symptoms supportively.

#### Post-Transfusion Purpura (PTP)
*   **Timing:** Occurs **5–10 days** after transfusion.
*   **Mechanism:** The patient develops antibodies against a specific platelet-specific antigen (most commonly **HPA-1a**) found on donor platelets. These antibodies then cross-react with and destroy the patient's **own platelets**, leading to severe thrombocytopenia.
*   **Features:** Sudden, severe thrombocytopenia leading to widespread purpura and bleeding.
*   **Predominantly affects:** Women sensitized through previous pregnancies.
*   **Management:** High-dose **intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG)** is the treatment of choice.

#### Transfusion-Associated Graft-Versus-Host Disease (TA-GvHD)
This is a rare but highly fatal complication of transfusion.
*   **Timing:** Occurs **1–6 weeks** after transfusion.
*   **Mechanism:** Viable donor T-lymphocytes, present in the blood product, engraft and proliferate in a severely immunocompromised recipient. These donor T-cells then recognize the recipient's tissues as foreign and mount an immune attack (graft-versus-host reaction).
*   **Features:** A characteristic triad of rash (erythroderma), diarrhoea, liver dysfunction, and bone marrow suppression leading to pancytopenia.
*   **Mortality:** Exceeds **90%**, as there is no effective treatment once established.
*   **Prevention:** **Irradiation** of blood products with gamma radiation (25–50 Gy) effectively inactivates donor T-lymphocytes, preventing their proliferation and engraftment.
*   **Who needs irradiated blood:**
    *   Severely immunocompromised patients (e.g., T-cell deficiencies, solid organ/haematopoietic stem cell transplant recipients, Hodgkin lymphoma patients).
    *   Intrauterine transfusion recipients and premature neonates.
    *   Directed donations from blood relatives (due to shared HLA haplotypes which can reduce recipient T-cell recognition of donor lymphocytes as foreign).

### Special Blood Product Modifications

Blood products can be modified to reduce specific risks or to meet the needs of particular patient populations.

| Modification       | What it Prevents                                                                 | When it is Used                                                                                                                   |
| :----------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Leukoreduced**   | Febrile non-haemolytic transfusion reactions (FNHTR), CMV transmission, HLA alloimmunization, platelet refractoriness | Standard for most transfusions; immunocompromised patients, multiple transfusion recipients, potential transplant recipients, CMV-negative recipients (as an alternative to CMV-negative blood) |
| **Irradiated**     | Transfusion-associated Graft-Versus-Host Disease (TA-GvHD)                       | Immunocompromised patients (e.g., HSCT recipients, Hodgkin lymphoma), intrauterine transfusions, directed donations from relatives, premature neonates |
| **Washed RBCs**    | Anaphylactic reactions in IgA-deficient patients with anti-IgA antibodies, severe/recurrent allergic reactions to plasma proteins | Patients with IgA deficiency and anti-IgA antibodies, patients with severe or recurrent allergic reactions unresponsive to antihistamines |
| **CMV-negative**   | Cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection                                                  | CMV-seronegative immunocompromised patients (e.g., HSCT recipients, HIV patients), pregnant women, premature neonates; leukoreduced blood is often an acceptable alternative |

### Massive Transfusion

Massive transfusion is a critical emergency requiring rapid, coordinated management.

#### Definition and Complications
*   **Definition:** Replacement of >100% of a patient's blood volume in 24 hours (roughly >10 units of pRBCs in 24 hours for an adult) OR >4 units of pRBCs in 1 hour.
*   **Complications:**
    *   **Hypothermia:** Cold stored blood lowers the patient's core body temperature, which worsens coagulopathy and cardiac function.
    *   **Hypocalcaemia:** Citrate, an anticoagulant in stored blood, chelates (binds) circulating calcium, leading to reduced ionized calcium.
    *   **Hyperkalaemia:** Potassium leaks from RBCs into the plasma during storage, increasing serum potassium levels, especially with older units.
    *   **Dilutional coagulopathy:** Replacing significant blood volume with pRBCs (which lack platelets and coagulation factors) dilutes the patient's remaining clotting factors and platelets.
    *   **Metabolic acidosis:** Stored blood has a low pH and lactate accumulation; however, the citrate in transfused blood is metabolized to bicarbonate, often leading to a paradoxical metabolic alkalosis in the long term, though initial acidosis can occur.

#### Massive Transfusion Protocol (MTP) and Additional Management
The goal of MTP is to rapidly restore blood volume and oxygen-carrying capacity while correcting or preventing coagulopathy.
*   **Ratio:** A balanced ratio of blood products, typically **1:1:1 (FFP : Platelets : pRBCs)**, is often used. This aims to mimic whole blood and prevent dilutional coagulopathy.
*   **Additional management:**
    *   **Tranexamic acid (TXA):** Administered early, especially in trauma, to inhibit fibrinolysis and reduce blood loss.
    *   **Calcium supplementation:** Intravenous calcium gluconate or chloride to counteract citrate chelation and prevent hypocalcaemia.
    *   **Warm all blood products:** Using blood warmers to prevent hypothermia.
    *   **Correct acidosis:** Address the underlying cause of acidosis, as acidosis impairs coagulation factor function.

### Clinical Scenarios and Long-Term Considerations

Practical approaches to common transfusion challenges and long-term effects.

#### Emergency Transfusion Management
*   **Unknown blood group in an emergency:**
    *   Immediately transfuse **O negative pRBCs** (universal RBC donor).
    *   If plasma is needed, give **AB positive FFP** (universal plasma donor).
    *   Switch to type-specific blood as soon as the patient's blood group is identified.
*   **Suspected acute haemolytic reaction:** (See details under AHTR, but key steps are: **STOP TRANSFUSION IMMEDIATELY**, keep IV open with saline, check identity, send samples, notify blood bank).

#### Complications of Repeated Transfusions
Patients requiring chronic transfusions face unique long-term complications.
*   **Iron overload:** Each unit of pRBCs contains approximately 250 mg of iron. The body has no physiological mechanism to excrete excess iron, leading to accumulation in organs. Management involves **iron chelation therapy** (e.g., desferrioxamine, deferasirox).
*   **Alloimmunisation:** Repeated exposure to foreign RBC antigens can lead to the development of multiple alloantibodies, making subsequent crossmatching increasingly difficult.
*   **Delayed haemolytic reactions:** Result from previously formed alloantibodies that become clinically significant after re-exposure (anamnestic response).
*   **CMV infection:** Risk of transmission, mitigated by using leukoreduced or CMV-negative blood products.
*   **Hypersplenism:** Can develop in patients with chronic haemolysis and repeated transfusions, leading to increased destruction of blood cells by the spleen.

    **A:** O negative.

    **A:** ABO and Rh typing, antibody screen, and crossmatch.
3.  **Q:** What is the most common cause of an acute haemolytic transfusion reaction?
    **A:** Clerical error (e.g., wrong blood type given to the wrong patient).
4.  **Q:** Which blood product has the shortest shelf life and highest risk of bacterial contamination?
    **A:** Platelet concentrates.

    **A:** Stop the transfusion immediately.
6.  **Q:** What is the primary difference in blood pressure between TACO and TRALI?
    **A:** TACO typically presents with elevated blood pressure, while TRALI usually has normal or low blood pressure.

    **A:** Severely immunocompromised patients (e.g., HSCT recipients, Hodgkin lymphoma) and directed donations from blood relatives.

    **A:** Iron overload; managed with iron chelation therapy (e.g., desferrioxamine, deferasirox).

    **A:** Sudden severe thrombocytopenia; 5-10 days post-transfusion.
10. **Q:** In an emergency with an unknown blood group, which blood products should be administered for red cells and plasma?
    **A:** O negative pRBCs and AB positive FFP.
