# Glomerular Diseases: Nephrotic and Nephritic Syndromes

**Year 4 · Paediatrics · Nephrology**

> **In one line:** glomerular disease presents in two broad patterns. When the **podocyte** is the target (foot process effacement) the glomerulus leaks protein, giving **nephrotic syndrome**; when the **capillary wall, mesangium or basement membrane** is inflamed, blood and protein leak and filtration falls, giving the **nephritic** picture (glomerulonephritis).

## What you must know

- A healthy glomerulus lets through **no protein, no red cells, no casts**. Normal protein excretion is **below 150 mg/day**.
- **Nephrotic syndrome** = heavy proteinuria (**above 3.5 g/day** in adults), **hypoalbuminaemia** (below 30 g/L), **oedema**, **hyperlipidaemia**, with lipiduria.
- **Nephritic syndrome** = **haematuria with dysmorphic red cells and RBC casts** (active sediment), reduced GFR/AKI, hypertension, oedema, oliguria.
- **Minimal change disease (MCD)** causes about 90% of nephrotic syndrome in children under 10 and is steroid-responsive: nephrotic syndrome in a child under 10 is MCD until proven otherwise.
- **FSGS** and **membranous nephropathy** are the other major nephrotic causes (membranous is the commonest primary cause in adults).
- **Post-streptococcal GN (PSGN)**: nephritis 1 to 3 weeks after a throat infection, with **low C3** and raised ASO; supportive treatment, good prognosis in children.
- **Crescents** on biopsy (rapidly progressive GN) are a nephrological emergency: treat the same day.
- Biopsy gives the diagnosis in most adult cases; it is usually not needed in a typical child with nephrotic syndrome.

## 1. Normal glomerular function and how disease starts

A normal glomerulus keeps filtration normal, maintains urine volume (preventing fluid retention, oedema and hypertension) and keeps blood cells and proteins out of the urine. The **renal cortex** holds the glomeruli.

**Primary glomerular disease** is mostly immune: autoantibodies against glomerular structures, or deposition of **immune complexes** (antigen-antibody), triggers local inflammation. The pathology depends on the component hit (basement membrane, mesangium, endothelium, podocyte). Diseases are named by their **histology**, which is why a kidney biopsy is usually needed, together with urinalysis and blood tests.

| Target | Result |
|---|---|
| **Podocytes** | Foot process effacement, large protein leak: **nephrotic syndrome** |
| **Endothelium, mesangium or GBM** (any or all) | Capillary wall inflammation, blood leaks into the urinary space: haematuria, proteinuria, reduced GFR: **nephritic syndrome (glomerulonephritis)** |

General points: glomerular diseases are usually chronic, GFR falls as disease advances (renal failure, dialysis or transplant), biopsy is the most accurate test, steroids are often used (some resolve on their own), and cyclophosphamide or mycophenolate are added in resistant or severe disease.

The degree of proteinuria separates the two patterns: nephrotic syndrome has heavy proteinuria with a bland sediment; nephritis has blood and active sediment with variable proteinuria.

## 2. Proteinuria: key definitions

| Level | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Below 150 mg/day | Normal (all proteins; albumin only a few mg) |
| Albumin 30 to 300 mg/day | **Microalbuminuria** (early renal disease) |
| Above 300 and below 3500 mg/day | Overt proteinuria |
| Above 3500 mg/day | **Nephrotic range** (heavy) proteinuria |

> **Check:** in children nephrotic-range proteinuria is defined by spot urine protein:creatinine ratio above 200 mg/mmol (or above 40 mg/m²/hour) rather than 3.5 g/day.

Why does a 3.5 g/day loss cause hypoalbuminaemia when the liver can make 10 to 12 g/day? Partly because albumin reabsorbed in the proximal tubule is catabolised, and synthesis cannot keep up with the losses.

## 3. Nephrotic syndrome

### 3.1 Pathology and mechanism

The primary lesion is the **podocyte**. Its foot processes flatten (**effacement**) while the cell bodies remain. Podocytes and the GBM are negatively charged and normally repel negatively charged albumin; once effaced, albumin passes into the urine.

### 3.2 Features

| Finding | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Urine**: heavy proteinuria; few or no RBCs, no RBC casts, few WBCs; **lipiduria** (fatty casts, oval fat bodies) | Best initial test is urinalysis with 24-hour protein (or protein:creatinine ratio) |
| **Hypoalbuminaemia** (below 30 g/L; normal 35 to 55) | Urinary loss |
| **Hyperlipidaemia** | Liver increases protein synthesis including lipoproteins to compensate for albumin loss |
| **Oedema**: periorbital (worse after waking in children, settles after walking and playing as gravity moves fluid down), pitting leg oedema, ascites, pleural effusion | Low oncotic pressure and **renal sodium retention** (uncontrolled activation of epithelial sodium channels, ENaC) |
| Frothy urine, fatigue, anorexia, nausea, abdominal pain, weight gain, breathlessness | Gut oedema reduces appetite and peristalsis; effusion causes breathlessness |

> **Exam tip:** nephrotic patients get **pleural effusions, not pulmonary oedema**, because the pulmonary circulation depends on hydrostatic, not oncotic, pressure.

### 3.3 Management

**General measures**

- **Sodium restriction** and a **loop diuretic** (furosemide or bumetanide). Resistant cases need furosemide 40 to 120 mg daily or more, plus **amiloride 5 mg daily** (monitor potassium). Gut oedema may impair absorption, so give IV initially. Patients may be hypovolaemic; accept moderate oedema rather than risk postural hypotension.
- **Normal protein intake**; high protein (80 to 90 g/day) increases proteinuria.
- **Thrombosis prevention:** loss of antithrombin and increased hepatic fibrinogen make the blood hypercoagulable (very common in membranous nephropathy). Avoid prolonged bed rest; consider prophylactic anticoagulation; permanent anticoagulation if renal vein thrombosis occurs.
- **Infection:** loss of immunoglobulins; sepsis is a leading cause of death. Give **pneumococcal vaccine**; treat infections early and aggressively rather than giving long-term prophylactic antibiotics.
- **Hyperlipidaemia:** start a **statin** (HMG-CoA reductase inhibitor).
- **ACE inhibitor and/or ARB** for their antiproteinuric effect.
- **Specific:** treat the underlying cause.

### 3.4 Complications

1. **Infection and sepsis** (immunoglobulin loss).
2. **Thrombosis** (loss of antithrombotic proteins).
3. **Acute kidney injury.**
4. **End-stage renal disease** if proteinuria does not resolve (prolonged protein exposure causes tubular atrophy and fibrosis).

## 4. Causes of nephrotic syndrome

| Disease | Key features |
|---|---|
| **Minimal change disease** | Commonest in children; steroid-sensitive |
| **FSGS** | Common in adults; resistant; progressive |
| **Membranous nephropathy** | Commonest primary cause in adults |
| Secondary causes | Diabetes mellitus, amyloidosis, MPGN, IgA nephropathy, SLE |

### 4.1 Minimal change disease (MCD)

- Main cause of nephrotic syndrome in children: **90% in under 10s**, over 50% in older children; 10 to 25% of adult cases.
- Likely **systemic T-cell dysfunction**.
- **Primary** (idiopathic) or **secondary**: drugs (NSAIDs, lithium, sulphasalazine, pamidronate, D-penicillamine, some antibiotics), neoplasia (Hodgkin and non-Hodgkin lymphoma, leukaemia), infections (TB, syphilis), allergy.
- **Histology:** light microscopy normal ("nil disease"); electron microscopy shows only **diffuse foot process effacement**. Any sclerosis (even in one glomerulus) means another disease, usually FSGS.
- **Clinical:** sudden onset (days) oedema; BP normal or slightly raised; heavy proteinuria, lipiduria, very low albumin, hyperlipidaemia; creatinine normal or slightly high and normalises with remission.
- **Diagnosis:** **adults need a biopsy**. **Children** respond to steroids in over 90%, so biopsy is usually avoided and treatment is empirical.
- **Treatment:** first line **corticosteroids**, given for 3 to 4 months then tapered over 6 months (adult schedule). Second line: oral **cyclophosphamide** or **ciclosporin**.

> **Check:** in children the usual schedule is prednisolone about 60 mg/m² daily (maximum 60 mg) for 4 to 6 weeks, then about 40 mg/m² on alternate days for a further 4 to 6 weeks (or per local protocol). Relapses are common; steroid-dependent or frequent relapsers are given steroid-sparing agents (cyclophosphamide, levamisole, mycophenolate, calcineurin inhibitors). Biopsy is considered if the child does not respond to steroids.

### 4.2 Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS)

- **Focal** = some glomeruli affected; **segmental** = only part of each affected glomerulus is sclerosed. **All** glomeruli show diffuse foot process effacement, hence nephrotic syndrome.
- A common cause in adults (12 to 35%); in a child it is usually secondary or genetic. Fair-to-poor prognosis; often steroid-resistant; renal insufficiency within 5 to 10 years; progresses to ESRD.
- The main difference from MCD is the **presence of sclerosis**.

| | Primary FSGS | Secondary FSGS |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | Probably a circulating factor (autoantibody) injuring podocytes (not tested for) | Reduced nephron number or injury: diabetes, obesity, nephron loss (more than 75% of renal mass, e.g. renal agenesis), reflux nephropathy, healed previous GN (e.g. IgA), severe pre-eclampsia, drugs (interferon, pamidronate, heroin), anabolic steroids, HIV, sickle cell disease |
| Presentation | Sudden heavy proteinuria and full nephrotic syndrome | Proteinuria less heavy (may be under 3.5 g/day), albumin not very low, **renal impairment common** (poor sign) |
| Treatment | Immunosuppression: first line corticosteroids; second line ciclosporin or tacrolimus | Not immunosuppression: treat the cause and protect kidneys (ACE inhibitor, BP control) |

The excess workload on a fixed number of nephrons causes glomerular hypertrophy, then fibrosis and sclerosis.

### 4.3 Membranous nephropathy

- Commonest **primary** nephrotic syndrome in adults (15 to 33%); mostly secondary in children (e.g. **hepatitis B** antigenaemia). Slowly developing nephrotic syndrome (weeks).
- **Histology:** diffuse thickening of the glomerular capillary wall throughout all glomeruli with IgG and C3 deposition (subepithelial deposits).
- Thrombosis (including renal vein thrombosis) is particularly frequent.

| | Primary | Secondary |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency | About 75% of adult cases | Others |
| Causes | Idiopathic | SLE (class V lupus nephritis, most important), other autoimmune disease (thyroiditis); drugs (penicillamine, IV gold, high-dose captopril, NSAIDs, anti-TNF); infections (hepatitis B and C, syphilis, schistosomiasis, **Plasmodium malariae**); **malignancy** (lung, GI, colon, stomach, breast, prostate; lymphoma) |
| Treatment | Corticosteroids plus cyclophosphamide or ciclosporin; rituximab possible | Treat the underlying disease |

> **Remember:** an older adult with membranous nephropathy and cancer risk factors should be screened for malignancy (chest X-ray, abdominal CT, colonoscopy); it may be the only manifestation. In Kenya also think of hepatitis B, malaria (quartan) and schistosomiasis.

### 4.4 Other secondary causes

**Diabetic nephropathy**, **MPGN**, **IgA nephropathy** and **amyloidosis**. Amyloidosis is a protein-folding disorder with extracellular deposition of insoluble fibrils (β-pleated sheets); the commonest forms are **AL** (light chain) and **AA** (serum amyloid A, from chronic inflammation, e.g. TB).

## 5. Nephritic syndrome

Nephritic describes a **clinical pattern** (not a single syndrome) that always indicates **glomerular inflammation**, with attraction of inflammatory mediators, cell proliferation and eventually permanent damage if untreated. The mesangium, endothelium and GBM are targeted because they lie next to the circulation.

### 5.1 Urine findings

- **Red cells.** Bleeding from a stone, tumour or bladder lesion gives **normal-shaped RBCs**. **Dysmorphic RBCs** (distorted when squeezed through gaps in inflamed capillaries) indicate glomerular bleeding.
- **RBC casts / cellular casts:** formed from Tamm-Horsfall mucoprotein in the distal tubule and collecting duct loaded with RBCs from the glomerulus.
- Dysmorphic RBCs and RBC casts together are the **active urinary sediment**: it signals active glomerular inflammation needing urgent attention.
- Proteinuria from subnephrotic to nephrotic range.

### 5.2 Clinical features

1. **AKI** (raised creatinine, electrolyte disturbance) and **reduced urine output**.
2. **Oedema** (low GFR activates RAAS, causing sodium and water retention).
3. **Hypertension.**
4. Features of **systemic vasculitis** where relevant (rash, pulmonary haemorrhage).
5. Positive immune markers depending on cause: ANA, anti-dsDNA, low complement, ANCA.

### 5.3 Histology

Proliferative (inflammatory) glomeruli. **Crescents** (proliferation of parietal cells of Bowman's capsule) mean severe inflammation: this is a **medical emergency in nephrology**. Without immunosuppression the patient may reach ESRD in days to weeks.

### 5.4 Causes

IgA nephropathy / HSP, PSGN, lupus nephritis, anti-GBM disease, ANCA vasculitis, MPGN. Alport syndrome is also in the nephritic group.

> **Remember (Alport):** X-linked or autosomal inheritance; defective type IV collagen; haematuria, proteinuria, high-frequency sensorineural hearing loss, eye abnormalities, progressive renal failure; no effective treatment.

### 5.5 IgA nephropathy (Berger's disease) and Henoch-Schonlein purpura

- Commonest primary GN in developed countries; children and young males. May be silent or active.
- **Cause:** altered mucosal immunity gives excess IgA, which deposits in the mesangium and causes local inflammation.
- **Presentation:** dark or cola-coloured urine **1 to 3 days after** (or at the same time as) an upper respiratory infection (**synpharyngitic haematuria**); recurrent macroscopic haematuria after URTI or GI infection; or incidental microscopic haematuria and proteinuria on screening.
- Recurrent macroscopic haematuria is paradoxically a good prognostic sign (possibly lead-time bias: it brings patients to attention earlier).
- **HSP** is the systemic form: IgA deposition in skin (purpura), bowel and kidney. IgA nephropathy is kidney-only.
- **Diagnosis:** biopsy shows mesangial IgA deposition and mesangial expansion.
- **Treatment:** no effective immunosuppression except in severe cases. Control **blood pressure** (reduces proteinuria); give an **ACE inhibitor or ARB** to all patients to cut proteinuria and preserve renal function.

### 5.6 Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis (PSGN)

- Usually follows **throat infection with group A beta-haemolytic streptococcus** (also skin infection in children); in adults can follow staphylococcal soft-tissue or bone infection.
- Mechanism: bacterial antigens cross-react with glomerular antigens or form immune complexes.
- **Presentation:** **1 to 3 weeks** after infection (a latent period, unlike IgA): frank haematuria with **cola-coloured urine**, periorbital oedema, hypertension, oliguria.
- **Diagnosis:** raised **ASO titre**; **low C3**, normal or slightly low C4; positive throat culture possible.
- **Treatment:** supportive (salt and fluid restriction, antihypertensive and diuretic as needed; treat any residual infection). Children recover better and faster than adults.

### 5.7 Lupus nephritis

- SLE ("the disease with a thousand faces") can cause both nephritis and membranous nephropathy. Severity ranges from silent to ESRD in weeks or months. Maintain a high index of suspicion and refer early.
- **Diagnosis:** **kidney biopsy is mandatory**. Low C3 and C4 (consumption), positive ANA and anti-dsDNA, abnormal urinalysis and renal function.
- **Treatment** depends on the biopsy class and usually needs strong immunosuppression. Long-standing SLE can leave only scarring (glomerulosclerosis) without active inflammation.

### 5.8 Anti-GBM disease (Goodpasture's)

- Autoantibody against the **alpha-3 chain of type IV collagen** in glomerular and alveolar basement membranes. Smokers are at risk because lung damage exposes the antigen.
- **Features:** GN, which may be the only feature, plus **pulmonary haemorrhage** (haemoptysis). GN plus lung haemorrhage = **Goodpasture's disease**.
- **Diagnosis:** serum anti-GBM antibodies; biopsy with **linear IgG and C3** staining on immunofluorescence.
- **Treatment:** start immediately: **plasmapheresis** to remove antibodies plus **corticosteroids and cyclophosphamide** to stop new antibody production.

### 5.9 ANCA vasculitis

- ANCA stimulates neutrophils to adhere to vessel walls, causing vasculitis.

| Type | Staining | Disease |
|---|---|---|
| **C-ANCA** | Cytoplasmic (anti-PR3) | Granulomatosis with polyangiitis (**Wegener's**) |
| **P-ANCA** | Perinuclear (anti-MPO) | Microscopic polyangiitis, Churg-Strauss (EGPA) |

- **Features:** upper airway (sinus) and lung involvement with GN; pulmonary haemorrhage (haemoptysis).
- **Diagnosis:** positive ANCA plus biopsy showing severe GN, often crescentic (RPGN), with **negative or scant immunofluorescence**: **pauci-immune** GN.
- Worst prognosis: pulmonary haemorrhage and severe renal failure at presentation. Earlier treatment gives better renal recovery.
- **Treatment:** high-dose corticosteroids plus cyclophosphamide; **rituximab** is equally effective for remission at 6 to 12 months and is an option when cyclophosphamide is not tolerated or relapse occurs.

### 5.10 Membranoproliferative GN (MPGN)

A histological pattern with many causes; may present as nephritic or nephrotic. Primary (idiopathic) type mainly in children; secondary type in adults from hepatitis B and C, endocarditis, lupus, Sjogren syndrome, cancer, complement deficiency.

## 6. Nephrotic versus nephritic

| | Nephrotic | Nephritic |
|---|---|---|
| Pathology | Podocyte disease, foot process effacement; usually no inflammation | Inflammation of GBM, endothelium or mesangium |
| Proteinuria | Above 3.5 g/day | Variable (hundreds of mg to grams) |
| Urine | No haematuria; lipiduria | Dysmorphic RBCs, RBC casts (active sediment) |
| Labs | Albumin below 30 g/L; high cholesterol | Low GFR; electrolyte disturbance |
| Clinical | Marked oedema; BP may be high | Oedema, hypertension, signs of renal impairment or vasculitis |
| Acute complications | Thrombosis, infection, AKI | RPGN (crescents), AKI |
| Chronic complications | Atherosclerosis; tubular atrophy and fibrosis; CKD | Glomerular sclerosis; CKD to ESRD (faster than in nephrotic) |

## 7. Worked examples from the lecture

1. Young man, painless haematuria 3 days after a throat infection, proteinuria: **IgA nephropathy**.
2. Older woman, sinus symptoms, oliguria, RBC casts, crescents, C-ANCA positive, anti-GBM negative: **Wegener's (granulomatosis with polyangiitis)**.
3. Hodgkin lymphoma with 10 g proteinuria: **minimal change disease** (lymphoma-associated).
4. Nephrotic syndrome with renal vein thrombosis, subepithelial deposits: **membranous nephropathy**.
5. Haematuria two weeks after sore throat, joint pain: **PSGN**.

## Key points to revise

1. Nephrotic syndrome: proteinuria above 3.5 g/day, albumin below 30 g/L, oedema, hyperlipidaemia, lipiduria; podocyte foot process effacement.
2. Nephritic syndrome: dysmorphic RBCs and RBC casts, AKI, hypertension, oedema; crescents are an emergency.
3. Child under 10 with nephrotic syndrome: MCD until proven otherwise; treat with steroids without biopsy.
4. FSGS: sclerosis is the difference from MCD; secondary FSGS is treated by treating the cause.
5. Membranous: commonest adult primary cause; screen for cancer; think SLE, hepatitis B, malaria.
6. PSGN: 1 to 3 weeks after strep infection, low C3, raised ASO, supportive care. IgA: 1 to 3 days after URTI, normal complement.
7. Anti-GBM: linear IgG on IF, plasmapheresis. ANCA: pauci-immune, C-ANCA with Wegener's.
8. Manage nephrotic oedema with salt restriction and diuretics; vaccinate against pneumococcus; consider anticoagulation and statin; ACEI/ARB for proteinuria.
