# Aspiration Syndromes in Children

**Year 4 · Paediatrics · Paediatric Respiratory Medicine**

> **In one line:** aspiration is the passage of material from the oropharynx (or stomach) into the tracheobronchial tree. The effects range from silent to life-threatening and depend on what was inhaled (gastric acid, hydrocarbon, foreign body, saliva or oil), its volume and how often it happens. Gravity decides where it lands, most often the right lower lobe.

## What you must know

- Material aspirated: **gastric contents, foreign bodies, hydrocarbons, excessive secretions**. Hydrocarbon agents (the **CHAMP** mnemonic) aspirate easily.
- **Predisposing factors:** anatomical (tracheo-oesophageal fistula, cleft palate), neuromuscular (altered consciousness, cerebral palsy), **GERD**, poor feeding technique and oral hygiene.
- **Right** lung is favoured: the right main bronchus is wider and more vertical. Supine: posterior segment of upper lobes and superior segment of lower lobes. Upright: bilateral basal segments, middle lobe, lingula.
- Gastric aspiration is severe at **volume above 0.8 mL/kg and pH below 2.5**; changes appear within **1 to 2 hours**.
- **Hydrocarbons: do not induce vomiting or empty the stomach** (usually); supportive care.
- Airway **foreign body**: rigid bronchoscopy **the same day**; 15 to 30% have a normal X-ray.
- Choking first aid: **under 1 year, 5 back blows then chest thrusts; over 1 year, 5 back blows then abdominal thrusts (Heimlich) or chest thrusts**.

## 1. Definition and causes

- Spectrum: asymptomatic to acute, life-threatening events. Aspiration may come from above (oropharynx, nose) or from below (stomach, reflux).
- **CHAMP:** **C**amphor, **H**alogenated carbons, **A**romatic hydrocarbons, **M**etals and **P**esticides.

### Predisposing and risk factors

| Group | Examples |
|---|---|
| Anatomical/mechanical | Tracheo-oesophageal fistula, cleft palate, oesophageal diverticulum (e.g. **Zenker's**) |
| Neuromuscular / CNS | Altered consciousness, cerebral palsy, CNS disorders, seizure disorders, swallowing disorders |
| GI | **Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease**, achalasia |
| Iatrogenic / toxic | Recent anaesthesia, intoxication or drug overdose |
| Miscellaneous | Poor feeding technique, poor oral care |

## 2. Where aspirate goes

- Location is **gravity-dependent** and depends on posture.
- The **right middle and lower lobes** are most often affected because the right main bronchus is larger in calibre and straighter.
- **Recumbent (supine):** posterior segments of the upper lobes and **superior segments of the lower lobes**.
- **Upright:** bilateral basal segments, middle lobe, lingula.
- **Prone:** right upper lobe, middle lobe, lingula. The right upper lobe is also a typical site in alcoholics aspirating supine.

> **Exam tip:** aspiration pneumonia in a bedridden child: think right lower lobe or posterior segments. A foreign body in a child more often goes into the right bronchus (58%).

## 3. Acute gastric aspiration (chemical pneumonitis)

- Large volumes after vomiting; complicates gastroenteritis, altered consciousness and **general anaesthesia**.
- Pathophysiology depends on **pH, volume, particulate matter and duration**. Severe if **volume above 0.8 mL/kg and pH below 2.5**.
- Clinical changes within **1 to 2 hours**. Massive aspiration causes hypoxaemia, haemorrhagic pneumonitis, atelectasis and intravascular fluid shifts.
- **Management:**
  - **Immediate airway suction.** Later suction and **bronchoscopy** if large particles were inhaled.
  - Monitor oximetry, blood gases and chest X-ray.
  - Recovery in **2 to 3 weeks** unless infection or barotrauma complicates.
  - Cover for infection if predisposing factors are present; **purely chemical pneumonitis may not need antibiotics at first**.
- **Prevention:** enteral feeding via tubes passed **beyond the pylorus**, head-up positioning, and **nil by mouth at least 4 hours before general anaesthesia** (the slides' figure; follow local fasting guidelines).

## 4. Hydrocarbon aspiration

- Includes **turpentine, gasoline (petrol), kerosene** (very relevant in Kenya, as kerosene is kept in households and often stored in drink bottles).
- Symptoms within **30 minutes**; severe pneumonitis possible. Lung changes at **2 to 8 hours**, peak at **48 to 72 hours**. Persistent pulmonary function abnormalities may remain for years. Liver, CNS and heart may also be affected.
- **Treatment:** **gastric emptying is nearly always contraindicated** (aspiration risk). Supportive: oxygen, fluids, ventilation if needed. Ingestions **above 30 mL** may justify gastric emptying; decide case by case.
- Avoid induced vomiting, avoid steroids and prophylactic antibiotics unless there is evidence of infection.

## 5. Chronic recurrent aspiration

- Repeated small aspirations of gastric, nasal or oral contents cause: **bronchiolitis, recurrent pneumonia, atelectasis, wheeze, cough, apnoea, laryngospasm.**
- Causes: tracheo-oesophageal fistula, cleft palate, GERD, neuromuscular disease, altered consciousness, poor feeding technique and oral hygiene, **Zenker's diverticulum, achalasia**, chronic reflux.
- **Lipoid pneumonia**: from mineral oil (laxative) or oily nose drops (no longer used).

### Diagnosis

- Assess feeding; look for predisposing factors.
- **Drooling** or pooled saliva suggests dysphagia.
- **Barium swallow with video fluoroscopy** to assess the swallowing mechanism.
- High index of suspicion needed. Chest X-ray, pH study or endoscopy as indicated.

### Management

- Treat the underlying problem.
- Mild: **positioning, thickened feeds, limit volume, small frequent feeds**, avoid overfeeding.
- GERD: **H2 blockers or proton pump inhibitors**.
- **Fundoplication** and **gastrostomy or jejunostomy** feeding.
- Reduce secretions: **glycopyrrolate, scopolamine, botulinum toxin** (side effects).
- Severe unresponsive cases: salivary gland excision, ductal ligation, laryngotracheal separation or oesophago-gastric disconnection.
- Suspected bacterial infection: **broad-spectrum antibiotics**: amoxicillin-clavulanic acid with metronidazole, clindamycin, meropenem or ampicillin-sulbactam (anaerobic cover is important).

### Complications

- Segmental or lobar pneumonia, bronchopneumonia, **lung abscess, empyema**; chronic lung disease and bronchiectasis.

## 6. Foreign body in the airway

- Choking or coughing with wheeze; history of eating nuts (groundnuts are classic in Kenya) or placing a small object in the mouth. Peak age is toddlers.

### Stages

1. **Initial event:** violent coughing, gagging, possible airway obstruction.
2. **Asymptomatic interval:** object lodges, reflexes fatigue, symptoms settle (a diagnostic trap).
3. **Complications:** obstruction, erosion, infection with fever, cough, haemoptysis, pneumonia, atelectasis.

### Features

- Most lodge in a bronchus (**right bronchus 58%**); laryngeal or tracheal in almost 10%.
- An **oesophageal foreign body can compress the trachea** and mimic an airway foreign body.
- Patient may be asymptomatic and **X-ray normal in 15 to 30%**. Take **PA and lateral** films (add expiratory or decubitus views).
- In expiration the bronchial foreign body acts as a ball valve, trapping air: **obstructive emphysema (air trapping)**. **Atelectasis is a late finding.**
- Presents as persistent cough or **unilateral wheeze not responding to bronchodilators**.

### Treatment

- **Prompt endoscopic removal with a rigid bronchoscope, on the same day**, in theatre or ICU.
- Oesophageal foreign bodies (e.g. coins, button batteries) are removed endoscopically; **button batteries are an emergency**.

## 7. First aid for choking

| Situation | Action |
|---|---|
| Conscious, can speak, cough and breathe | Encourage coughing |
| Unconscious | Start CPR (ABC sequence) |
| Conscious but cannot speak, cough or breathe, **under 1 year** | **5 back blows, then chest thrusts**; repeat until object is dislodged |
| Conscious but cannot speak, cough or breathe, **over 1 year** | **5 back blows, then Heimlich (abdominal thrusts) or chest thrusts** |

- Remove only what you can see (finger sweep). Do not perform blind finger sweeps in infants.

> **Remember:** abdominal thrusts are avoided in infants because of the risk of liver injury; use chest thrusts.

## Key points to revise

1. Aspiration is passage of oropharyngeal or gastric contents into the airways; risk groups are neurologically impaired children, those with GERD, cleft palate or TEF.
2. The right lower lobe is the commonest site; supine posture favours posterior upper and superior lower segments.
3. Gastric aspiration is severe at above 0.8 mL/kg and pH below 2.5; suction first, antibiotics only if infection is likely.
4. Hydrocarbon pneumonitis peaks at 48 to 72 hours; avoid gastric emptying; supportive care.
5. Chronic aspiration causes recurrent pneumonia, wheeze and cough; diagnose by video fluoroscopy; treat reflux and feeding, consider fundoplication and gastrostomy.
6. Complications include pneumonia, lung abscess and empyema.
7. Airway foreign body has three stages; unilateral wheeze or air trapping; rigid bronchoscopy the same day.
8. Choking first aid depends on age: back blows then chest thrusts under 1 year; back blows then abdominal or chest thrusts over 1 year.
