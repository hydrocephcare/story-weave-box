# Electrocardiogram (ECG): Physiology and Interpretation

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Cardiology (electrocardiography)**

> **In one line:** the ECG is a graphic recording of the heart's electrical activity, in which each wave matches a mechanical event of the cardiac cycle (P wave for atrial depolarisation, QRS for ventricular depolarisation, T wave for ventricular repolarisation). Read it by rate, rhythm, axis and then the waves and intervals, and use the lead groups to localise ischaemia.

## What you must know

- Standard paper speed **25 mm/s**: a large square (5 mm) = **0.20 s**, a small square (1 mm) = **0.04 s**. Standard calibration is **0.1 mV per mm** (10 mm = 1 mV).
- Rate = **1500 / number of small squares between R waves** (or 300 / large squares).
- **P** = atrial depolarisation, **QRS** = ventricular depolarisation, **T** = ventricular repolarisation. Atrial repolarisation is normally hidden in the QRS.
- Normal ECG: sinus rhythm (P before every QRS), **PR 0.12 to 0.20 s**, **QRS under 0.12 s**, axis **-30 to +90 degrees**.
- Lead groups: **anterior V1 to V4**, **lateral I, aVL, V5, V6**, **inferior II, III, aVF**.
- **ST depression of 1 mm or more** is the hallmark of myocardial ischaemia.
- The ECG shows electrical activity; **echocardiography** shows mechanical activity.

## 1. The cardiac cycle

The cardiac cycle is the electrical, pressure and volume changes in a functioning heart between successive heartbeats.

- **Diastole:** myocardium relaxed. **Systole:** myocardium contracts. Atrial systole is atrial contraction; ventricular systole is ventricular contraction.
- Electrical changes in the muscle cause the mechanical change (contraction), so changes in membrane potential in a part of the heart reflect mechanical events there.

| Mechanical phase | Phase of the cycle |
|---|---|
| Ventricular filling | Ventricular diastole, atrial systole |
| Isovolumetric contraction | Ventricular systole |
| Ventricular ejection | Ventricular systole |
| Isovolumetric relaxation | Ventricular and atrial diastole |

The full cycle coordinates electrical changes, pressure changes in the left atrium, left ventricle and aorta, ventricular volume changes, and the cardiac valves.

## 2. Electrophysiology

- **EKG** (from the German *Elektrokardiogramm*) and **ECG** are the same thing.
- The ECG records **electrical** activity; **mechanical** activity is sensed by echocardiography.
- A known electrode pattern gives a known tracing pattern, so the electrical pattern can reveal **blood supply problems** (ischaemia, infarction).
- **Direction rule:** depolarisation spreading **toward** the recording electrode gives an **upward (positive)** deflection; spreading **away** gives a **downward (negative)** deflection.
- When muscle is fully polarised or fully depolarised, no potential difference is recorded, giving a flat **isoelectric line**.
- After depolarisation, cells **repolarise** to return to the resting state.

### Conduction pathway

1. **Sinoatrial (SA) node** in the right atrium is the normal pacemaker.
2. Depolarisation spreads across the atria, producing the **P wave**. Three tracts within the atria carry the impulse to the AV node.
3. The **AV node** slows conduction, giving the atria time to empty into the ventricles before ventricular systole.
4. The **bundle of His** connects the AV node to the bundle branches.
5. **Purkinje fibres** are the terminal bundle branches.

## 3. Time and voltage on the ECG

- Machines run at **25 mm/s** (standard) or 50 mm/s.
- **Major lines** 5 mm apart = 0.20 s at 25 mm/s. **Minor lines** 1 mm apart = 0.04 s.
- **Voltage** is on the vertical axis; standard calibration 0.1 mV per mm.

## 4. The basic complexes

| Component | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **P wave** | Atrial depolarisation, causing atrial contraction. Normal amplitude 0.25 mV or less (2.5 mm) |
| Atrial repolarisation | Not normally visible (buried in the QRS) |
| PR interval | Includes conduction through the AV node; the bundle of His and bundle branches are excited in the middle of the PR interval |
| **Q wave** | First downward deflection after the P; marks the start of ventricular depolarisation |
| **R wave** | Positive deflection after the Q |
| **S wave** | Negative deflection after the Q or R |
| **QRS** | Ventricular depolarisation |
| **T wave** | Repolarisation of ventricular muscle fibres; follows the QRS |

## 5. The standard 12-lead ECG

Usually recorded with the patient **resting supine**.

| Group | Leads | Plane |
|---|---|---|
| Bipolar limb leads | I, II, III | Frontal |
| Augmented limb leads | aVR, aVL, aVF | Frontal |
| Chest (precordial) leads | V1 to V6 | Transverse (circle the heart) |

Each lead gives a different electrical view of the heart:

| View | Leads |
|---|---|
| **Anterior** | V1 to V4 |
| **Lateral** | I, aVL, V5, V6 |
| **Inferior** | II, III, aVF |

> **Remember:** lead II shows a large R wave because the left ventricular current vector runs parallel to that lead's axis, towards its positive electrode. Lead V1 shows a large S wave because the left ventricular vector points away from V1.

## 6. Interpretation

### Step 1: Rate

One PQRST is one complete cardiac cycle.

- At standard speed, **1500 divided by the number of small squares between two R waves**.
- Quick method: find an R wave on a heavy line, then count off **300, 150, 100, 75, 60** for each following heavy line; where the next R lands gives the rate.
- For irregular rhythms: count the cycles in a **6 second** strip (marked on the paper) and **multiply by 10**.

### Step 2: Rhythm

- A normal rhythm has a consistent R-R interval. Mild variation with breathing is normal (sinus arrhythmia).

| Rhythm | Rate | Other features |
|---|---|---|
| **Normal sinus rhythm** | 60 to 100/min | Regular; P upright in I, II and aVF; PR under 0.20 s; QRS under 0.10 s |
| **Sinus bradycardia** | Under 60/min | Regular |
| **Sinus tachycardia** | Over 100/min | Regular |

> **Check:** the slide on the normal ECG gives a rate of 50 to 100/min and QRS under 0.12 s, while the sinus rhythm slide says 60 to 100/min and QRS under 0.10 s. The usual textbook limits are rate 60 to 100/min and QRS under 0.12 s (under 0.10 s in many texts).

### Step 3: Axis

The normal axis lies between **-30 and +90 degrees**. Axis is determined from the limb leads in the frontal plane (a QRS that is mainly positive in leads I and aVF gives a normal axis).

### Step 4: Waves and intervals

- **P wave:** present before each QRS in sinus rhythm.
- **PR interval:** 0.12 to 0.20 s.
- **QRS:** under 0.12 s.
- **ST segment:** should be isoelectric.
- **T wave:** direction and size.

### The normal ECG in summary

Sinus rhythm (P before every QRS), rate 50 to 100 bpm (see Check above), axis +90 to -30 degrees, PR 0.12 to 0.20 s, QRS under 0.12 s.

**Classifying an abnormal rhythm:** first ask whether it is **sinus or not sinus**; if not sinus, is it **ventricular or supraventricular**; then look at the QRS **morphology**.

## 7. AV conduction disturbances (heart block)

Blockage or delay of the impulse at the AV node.

| Degree | Features |
|---|---|
| **First degree** | Every P wave is followed by a QRS, but conduction is delayed (long PR interval over 0.20 s) |
| **Second degree** | Some, but not all, P waves are followed by a QRS (dropped beats). Mobitz type I: PR lengthens until a beat is dropped. Mobitz type II: PR constant, beats dropped suddenly |
| **Third degree (complete)** | No relationship between P waves and QRS complexes; atria and ventricles beat independently |

> **Exam tip:** Mobitz II and complete heart block can cause syncope and need pacing; first degree block and Mobitz I are usually benign.

## 8. Arrhythmias

An **arrhythmia** is an irregular heartbeat.

- **Sinus arrhythmia:** a P wave precedes every QRS but the R-R interval varies, usually with respiration. Physiological, mainly in the young.
- **Premature atrial contraction (PAC):** an early beat arising from the atria; usually has a premature P wave with a narrow QRS.
- **Premature ventricular contraction (PVC):** an early beat from the ventricles; wide, bizarre QRS with no preceding P wave and a compensatory pause.

## 9. Myocardial ischaemia

- **ST segment depression** is the hallmark of ischaemia.
- Reduced oxygen-rich blood supply alters normal cell function and displaces the ST segment by **1 mm or more below the baseline**.
- The ST depression may be **upsloping, horizontal or downsloping**; horizontal and downsloping depression are the more significant.

> **Remember:** ST depression in ischaemia (angina, NSTEMI) versus ST elevation in transmural infarction (STEMI). Persistent ST elevation with a typical history is treated as STEMI.

## Key points to revise

1. The ECG records electrical activity; echocardiography records mechanical activity.
2. Depolarisation toward a lead gives an upward deflection; away gives a downward deflection.
3. P = atrial depolarisation, QRS = ventricular depolarisation, T = ventricular repolarisation.
4. At 25 mm/s: 1 small square = 0.04 s, 1 large square = 0.20 s; rate = 1500 / small squares between R waves.
5. Normal: PR 0.12 to 0.20 s, QRS under 0.12 s, axis -30 to +90 degrees, sinus rhythm 60 to 100/min.
6. Anterior V1 to V4, lateral I, aVL, V5, V6, inferior II, III, aVF.
7. Heart block: first degree has a long PR, second degree drops some beats, third degree has complete dissociation.
8. ST depression of 1 mm or more indicates myocardial ischaemia.
