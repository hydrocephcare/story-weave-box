# Cerebral Palsy

**Year 4 · Paediatrics · Paediatric neurology and orthopaedics**

> **In one line:** cerebral palsy is a **non-progressive** injury to the developing brain (before maturation, in the first 1-2 years) that causes lasting abnormalities of **movement, tone and posture**, most often spasticity. Brain damage is static, but the musculoskeletal deformities **evolve with growth**, so care is multidisciplinary and aims for the most independent child possible.

## What you must know

- Definition: **non-progressive** cerebral damage before brain maturation (1-2 years) causing muscle weakness, spasticity and other symptoms.
- Causes: **prenatal** (maternal disease, toxaemia, cerebral malformation or haemorrhage, inborn errors of metabolism), **perinatal** (labour and respiratory complications, infections), **postnatal** (infection, violence, convulsions).
- Topographic types: **diplegia, tetraplegia, hemiplegia**, plus bilateral hemiplegia, paraplegia, monoplegia, triplegia.
- Physiological types: **spastic** (commonest), **athetoid** (? kernicterus), **ataxic**, **rigid**, **mixed**.
- Surgery is best in **spastic hemiplegics and severe deformities**; **contraindicated in athetoid and ataxic** types.
- **Botulinum toxin** temporarily reduces dynamic spasticity.
- The degree of intellectual impairment is central in planning treatment.

## 1. Definition and incidence

**Cerebral palsy (CP)**: non-progressive cerebral damage occurring before brain maturation (1-2 years), resulting in muscle weakness, spasticity and other symptoms.

- Incidence quoted in the slides: **0.5-2 per 1000** in premature deliveries.

> **Check:** overall prevalence in most series is about **2-3 per 1000 live births**; the risk is much **higher in preterm and low birth weight infants**.

## 2. Causes

| Timing | Causes |
|---|---|
| **Prenatal** | Maternal disease, **toxaemia** (pre-eclampsia), cerebral malformation, intracranial haemorrhage, inborn errors of metabolism; also congenital infections (TORCH) |
| **Perinatal** | Labour complications, birth asphyxia, respiratory complications, perinatal infections; **kernicterus** (bilirubin encephalopathy) and prematurity-related periventricular leucomalacia |
| **Postnatal** | Infection (meningitis, encephalitis), **violence** (non-accidental injury), convulsions, hypoxic events |

In Kenya, birth asphyxia, neonatal jaundice and cerebral malaria are important preventable causes.

## 3. Classification

### 3.1 Topographic (which limbs)

| Type | Features |
|---|---|
| **Diplegia** | Arms and legs, **legs much more than arms**; most patients eventually walk |
| **Tetraplegia (quadriplegia)** | Arms, legs and trunk; **high mortality**, most unable to walk, **IQ low** |
| **Hemiplegia** | Upper and lower limbs on one side (**upper more than lower**), with spasticity; patients eventually walk |
| Others | **Bilateral hemiplegia, paraplegia (legs), monoplegia, triplegia** |

### 3.2 Physiological (type of tone or movement)

| Type | Frequency | Features | Surgery |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Spastic** | **50-60%** (commonest) | Increased tone (**clasp-knife / "Jack-knife" spasticity**), slow restricted movements, **increased reflexes, Babinski positive**. Most important to the orthopaedic surgeon | Yes |
| **Athetosis (dyskinetic)** | 20-25% | **? Kernicterus**; involuntary, uncontrolled slow movements; normal reflexes; +/- rigidity or tremors | **Not for surgery** |
| **Ataxia** | 1-5% | Inability to control or coordinate movement when starting; **intention tremor**, nystagmus, unbalanced gait | **Not for surgery** |
| **Rigidity** | 5-7% | **Lead-pipe rigidity** | |
| **Mixed** | | Spasticity plus athetosis with whole-body involvement | |

> **Check:** in most series spastic CP is 70-80% of cases; the slide figure is lower. Spasticity is an upper motor neurone sign (clasp-knife, hyper-reflexia, upgoing plantar), whereas athetosis comes from basal ganglia damage and ataxia from the cerebellum.

## 4. Clinical presentation

Typical presentation: a young child (for example a **3-year-old boy**) with **inability to stand or walk**, or delay in motor milestones. Look for:
- Persistent primitive reflexes, abnormal tone (hypotonia early, then spasticity), early hand preference (under 1 year suggests hemiplegia).
- Feeding problems, drooling, speech delay.
- Associated problems: **intellectual disability, epilepsy, visual and hearing impairment**, contractures and hip dislocation.

### 4.1 Characteristic deformities

| Region | Deformity |
|---|---|
| **Upper limb** | Shoulder adduction and internal rotation; elbow flexion; forearm pronation; wrist and finger flexion |
| **Lower limb** | Hip adduction, flexion and internal rotation; knee flexion; foot **equinus** with varus or valgus; **scissoring gait** |
| **Spine** | **Kyphoscoliosis** |

The hips are the key joint: spastic adductors and flexors pull the femoral head out (subluxation, then dislocation), causing pain and arthritis. Pelvic and hip radiographs are therefore the **two most important X-rays during follow-up** (hip and spine).

## 5. Management

### 5.1 Aims of treatment
- To make the child **as independent as possible**.
- **Avoid pain** (hip arthritis).
- Maintain **sitting posture**.
- Maintain **spinal stability**.
- **Social benefit** (education, integration, carer burden).

### 5.2 Approach
- Take a history, examine, investigate and treat; **the degree of intellectual disability is of great importance in treatment planning**.
- **Multidisciplinary team**: orthotics before and after surgery; physiotherapy and occupational therapy; orthopaedic surgery; neurosurgery and paediatric neurology; speech therapy.

### 5.3 Exercise and conservative care
- Start **early (first month) when suspected**.
- By a qualified physiotherapist **and parents**.
- Prevent contractures, develop coordination, include mental exercise.
- Use orthotics, plaster of Paris (POP) or casts if needed.

### 5.4 Botulinum toxin
Intramuscular **botulinum toxin** temporarily reduces **dynamic spasticity**. It is thought to promote normal muscle growth and avoid soft tissue contracture.

### 5.5 Surgery
- Best in **spastic hemiplegics and for severe deformities**.
- **Contraindicated in athetoid and ataxic** types.
- **Goals**: decrease spasm, release contractures, correct deformity, rebalance muscles, stabilise flail joints.

| Option | Example |
|---|---|
| Neurectomy | Obturator neurectomy for adductor spasm |
| Tenotomy | Division of a tendon |
| Tenoplasty / muscle lengthening (recession) | Tendo-Achilles lengthening, hamstring lengthening |
| Tendon transfer | Rebalance muscle pull |
| Bony surgery | Osteotomy, fusion |
| Spinal surgery | Scoliosis correction |

> **Exam tip:** the classic exam questions are "which types are not for surgery" (athetoid and ataxic) and "which X-rays at follow-up" (hip and spine).

## 6. Complications and prognosis

Contractures, hip dislocation, scoliosis, pressure sores, aspiration and recurrent chest infections, epilepsy, malnutrition. Prognosis depends on the type: diplegic and hemiplegic children usually walk; tetraplegia has the poorest outcome.

## Key points to revise

1. Cerebral palsy is a non-progressive brain injury before 1-2 years of age.
2. Causes are prenatal, perinatal and postnatal; kernicterus gives athetosis.
3. Topographic types: diplegia (legs more), tetraplegia (worst), hemiplegia (upper more).
4. Spastic CP is commonest; athetoid and ataxic are not for surgery.
5. Deformities: hip adduction and flexion, knee flexion, equinus, scissoring, kyphoscoliosis.
6. Goal: independence, no pain, sitting posture, spinal stability.
7. Early physiotherapy with parents; botulinum toxin for dynamic spasticity; surgery for fixed deformity.
