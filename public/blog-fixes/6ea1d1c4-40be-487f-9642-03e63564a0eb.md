# Febrile Seizures

**Year 4 · Paediatrics · Paediatric neurology**

> **In one line:** a febrile seizure is a convulsion with fever (38 °C or higher) in a child aged 6 to 60 months that is not due to CNS infection, metabolic disturbance or prior afebrile seizures. Most are simple, benign and need only reassurance and treatment of the fever's cause, but complex or prolonged seizures, meningitis risk, and a few epilepsy syndromes (Dravet, GEFS+) must be recognised.

## What you must know

- Age **6 to 60 months** (peak **12 to 18 months**), temperature **38 °C or higher**, no CNS infection, no metabolic cause, no prior afebrile seizures.
- **Simple:** generalised (usually tonic-clonic), **15 minutes or less**, **does not recur within 24 hours**.
- **Complex:** over 15 minutes, **and/or focal, and/or recurs within 24 hours**. **Febrile status epilepticus:** over 30 minutes.
- 2 to 5% of neurologically healthy children have at least one. **Recurrence about 30%** after a first, 50% after two or more, and 50% if onset is under 1 year.
- Only about **5%** go on to develop epilepsy.
- **Lumbar puncture** for infants under 6 months with fever and seizure, any ill-looking child, or with signs of concern.
- No routine EEG, blood tests or neuroimaging after a first simple febrile seizure.
- Rescue treatment if seizure lasts **over 5 minutes**; antipyretics do **not** prevent recurrence; long-term AEDs are generally not recommended.

## 1. Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Febrile seizure** | Seizure at 6 to 60 months (peak 12 to 18 months) with temperature of 38 °C (100.4 °F) or higher, not from CNS infection or metabolic imbalance, no history of afebrile seizures |
| **Simple febrile seizure** | Primary generalised, usually tonic-clonic, lasting a maximum of 15 minutes, not recurring within 24 hours |
| **Complex febrile seizure** | Prolonged (over 15 minutes), and/or focal, and/or recurring within 24 hours |
| **Febrile status epilepticus** | Febrile seizure lasting over 30 minutes |
| **FIRES** (febrile infection-related epilepsy syndrome) | A different disorder: older children (over 5 years), mostly male, with an encephalitis-like illness without an identifiable infectious agent; previously normal children then develop difficult-to-treat epilepsy |

- After a simple febrile seizure the postictal state is very short; the child returns to baseline within minutes.

## 2. Epidemiology and outcome

- **2 to 5%** of neurologically healthy infants and children have at least one, usually simple, febrile seizure.
- **Simple** febrile seizures carry **no increased mortality** and **no long-term adverse effects** on behaviour, scholastic performance, neurocognitive function or attention. (Children who later develop epilepsy may have such difficulties.)
- **Complex** febrile seizures may carry about a **2-fold increase in mortality** over the following 2 years, probably from coexisting pathology.
- **Recurrence:** about **30%** after a first episode, **50%** after two or more, and **50%** if onset is before 1 year of age.
- About **15%** of children with epilepsy had febrile seizures, but only **5%** (range 1 to 33% depending on risk factors) of children with febrile seizures later develop epilepsy.

> **Remember:** risk factors for later epilepsy are complex features, a neurodevelopmental abnormality, a family history of epilepsy, and febrile status epilepticus.

## 3. Aetiology and pathogenesis

- **Fever from infection** is the trigger: otitis media, roseola (HHV-6) and HHV-7, norovirus, enteroviruses, Shigella and similar agents. In febrile status epilepticus, HHV-6B (more often) and HHV-7 account for **30%**.
- **Genetic:** positive family history in many; some families show autosomal dominant inheritance, but most cases are **polygenic**. Genes include **SCN1A, SCN1B, SCN9A and CPA6**.
- **Inflammation:** imbalance between pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8) and the anti-inflammatory ILR-1A is associated with febrile status epilepticus. A **low ILR-1A/IL-8 ratio** predicts hippocampal abnormality on MRI after febrile status epilepticus and may be a biomarker of later **mesial temporal lobe epilepsy**.
- **Iron deficiency** is associated with an increased risk, so screen for and treat it.

### Epilepsy syndromes that start with febrile seizures

| Syndrome | Features |
|---|---|
| **GEFS+** (generalised epilepsy with febrile seizures plus) | Autosomal dominant, highly variable phenotype. Onset in early childhood, remission in mid-childhood. Multiple febrile seizures, then afebrile generalised seizures (tonic-clonic, absence, myoclonic, atonic, myoclonic-astatic). A focal variant exists |
| **Dravet syndrome (SMEI)** | Most severe of the febrile seizure-associated epilepsies; see below |
| **Temporal lobe epilepsy** | Many patients with mesial temporal sclerosis have earlier febrile seizures |

### Dravet syndrome
- Infantile onset. Febrile and afebrile **unilateral clonic seizures** recurring every 1 to 2 months; compared with ordinary febrile seizures they are **more prolonged, more frequent, focal and in clusters**.
- Later, seizures occur with lower fever and then without fever. In the second year: **myoclonus, atypical absences, focal seizures**, then **developmental delay**.
- Usually a **de novo mutation** (rarely autosomal dominant inheritance or from an unaffected carrier). **SCN1A** loss-of-function mutations cause about **80%** (the same gene is affected in GEFS+, but with milder effect). GABRG2, SCN1B and SCN2A rarely; 10 to 20% have no gene found. Milder forms: Dravet spectrum or SMEI-Borderland.
- Many children labelled as having "vaccine encephalopathy" (prolonged febrile seizures and regression after vaccination) turn out to have Dravet mutations, so the illness is caused by the mutation and not the vaccine.

## 4. Evaluation

Every child needs a **detailed history and thorough general and neurological examination**, looking for the source of fever (ears, throat, chest, urine, skin) and for signs of meningitis.

### Lumbar puncture
- **Meningitis must be considered.** LP is done for:
  - All infants **under 6 months** with fever and seizure.
  - Any **ill-looking** child, or at any age if there are concerning signs or symptoms.
- LP is an **option** for children **6 to 12 months** who are deficient in Hib and pneumococcal immunisation or whose status is unknown, and for children **pre-treated with antibiotics**.
- In febrile status epilepticus a non-traumatic LP rarely shows pleocytosis (**96% have under 3 nucleated cells**) with normal protein and glucose. Pleocytosis points to bacterial or viral infection.

### EEG
- Not needed after a first simple febrile seizure in a neurologically healthy child; it does **not predict recurrence or epilepsy**.
- Drowsiness spikes (especially over age 4) do not predict epilepsy. EEG within 2 weeks often shows non-specific posterior slowing, so delay or repeat after more than 2 weeks.
- Reserve for special cases where epilepsy is strongly suspected, to **define the epilepsy type**. If done, record **at least 20 minutes** in wakefulness and sleep.
- EEG helps distinguish ongoing seizure from a prolonged postictal state. After febrile status epilepticus, **focal temporal slowing** raises the chance of mesial temporal sclerosis.

### Blood tests and imaging
- **Not routine** after a first simple febrile seizure (electrolytes, calcium, phosphorus, magnesium, full blood count).
- **Blood glucose** initially and when there is prolonged postictal obtundation or poor oral intake.
- Do electrolytes if clinically indicated (for example dehydration); **low sodium** raises the risk of recurrence within 24 hours.
- **No CT or MRI** after a first simple febrile seizure. Complex seizures need individual work-up (EEG, imaging), especially if neurologically abnormal.
- About **10%** of children with febrile status epilepticus have acute hippocampal swelling, and about **71%** of these later show hippocampal atrophy.

## 5. Management

### Acute
- Protect the airway, lay the child on their side, do not put anything in the mouth, and treat the cause of the fever.
- If the seizure lasts **over 5 minutes**: **lorazepam, midazolam or diazepam**. Parents may be given **rectal diazepam** for home use, or **buccal or intranasal midazolam**.

### Prevention of recurrence
- **No continuous or intermittent antiepileptic therapy** is generally recommended after simple febrile seizures.
- For frequent recurrence, **intermittent oral clonazepam** (0.01 mg/kg every 8 to 12 hours, maximum 1.5 mg/day) or **oral diazepam** (0.33 mg/kg every 8 hours) during febrile illness. These reduce but do not eliminate recurrence.
- Continuous **phenobarbital or valproic acid** was used historically but is rarely justified (side effects, no proven long-term benefit).
- **Antipyretics** relieve discomfort but **do not reduce recurrence risk**.
- Chronic AEDs may be considered in children at high risk of later epilepsy; they do not change the chance of future epilepsy.
- Check for and treat **iron deficiency**.

### Counselling
- Explain recurrence versus epilepsy risks, teach how to handle a seizure at home, and give emotional support. Parents often fear the child is dying.

> **Exam tip:** antipyretics do not prevent febrile seizures. Say this clearly, but still treat distress and fever.

## Key points to revise

1. Febrile seizure: 6 to 60 months, fever 38 °C or more, no CNS infection or metabolic cause, no prior afebrile seizures.
2. Simple: generalised, 15 minutes or less, no recurrence in 24 hours. Complex: over 15 minutes, focal or recurrent.
3. Recurrence about 30%; epilepsy risk only about 5%.
4. LP for under 6 months, ill-looking children, or concerning signs; consider in 6 to 12 months with incomplete Hib or pneumococcal vaccination.
5. No EEG, imaging or routine bloods after a first simple febrile seizure; check glucose.
6. Rescue benzodiazepine if seizure lasts over 5 minutes; antipyretics do not prevent recurrence; long-term AEDs generally not advised.
7. Dravet syndrome (SCN1A, about 80%) and GEFS+ are epilepsies that begin with febrile seizures.
