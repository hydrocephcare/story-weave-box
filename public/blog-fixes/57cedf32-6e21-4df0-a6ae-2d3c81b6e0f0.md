## Part 1: Basic Virology — Classification and Structure

## 1. Introduction

Basic virology forms the foundation of everything else in the virology course. Without understanding what a virus is, how it is classified, and what its components do, it is impossible to understand pathogenesis, diagnosis, or treatment. At MKU, basic virology appears heavily in MCQs — testing viral classification, the Baltimore system, functions of viral components, and distinguishing features of specific virus families. Several essay questions on viral receptors and spikes and viral pathogenesis also draw directly from this foundational knowledge.

## 2. What is a Virus

A virus is an obligate intracellular parasite — it can only replicate inside a living host cell. It is not a cell — it has no organelles, no ribosomes, no cell membrane of its own, and no metabolic machinery. It hijacks the host cell's machinery to replicate.

**Key features that distinguish viruses from other microorganisms:**
- Contains only one type of nucleic acid — either DNA or RNA, never both
- Has no ribosomes — cannot synthesize proteins independently
- Has no mitochondria — cannot generate energy
- Replication by assembly of pre-formed components — not by binary fission
- Much smaller than bacteria — range from 20 nm (parvovirus) to 300 nm (poxvirus)

## 3. Viral Structure — Components and Functions

### The Genome

- Either DNA or RNA — never both
- Can be single-stranded (ss) or double-stranded (ds)
- Can be positive-sense (+) or negative-sense (-)
  - **Positive-sense RNA** — same sequence as mRNA; can be directly translated by host ribosomes
  - **Negative-sense RNA** — complementary to mRNA; must be converted to positive-sense first by viral RNA polymerase before translation
- Can be segmented (multiple separate pieces) or non-segmented (one continuous piece)
  - Segmented genomes — influenza (8 segments), rotavirus (11 segments)
  - Segmentation allows genetic reassortment — basis of antigenic shift in influenza

### The Capsid

- Protein coat that surrounds and protects the viral genome
- Made of repeating protein subunits called **capsomeres**
- Two main shapes:
  - **Icosahedral** — spherical, 20 triangular faces; most efficient use of protein; examples: adenovirus, HSV, poliovirus, papillomavirus
  - **Helical** — protein subunits arranged in a helix around the genome; examples: influenza (inside envelope), rabies, TMV
  - **Complex** — neither icosahedral nor helical; example: poxvirus (most complex animal virus)
- **Functions of capsid:**
  - Protects genome from nucleases and environmental damage
  - Mediates attachment to host cells (in non-enveloped viruses)
  - Determines antigenicity — capsid proteins are targets of antibodies
  - Facilitates entry into host cell

### The Envelope

- Lipid bilayer membrane derived from host cell membrane during budding
- Present in enveloped viruses — absent in non-enveloped (naked) viruses
- Contains viral glycoproteins (spikes) inserted into it
- **Functions:**
  - Mediates attachment to host cell receptors via glycoprotein spikes
  - Mediates fusion with host cell membrane
  - Target of neutralizing antibodies

**Clinical significance of envelope:**
- Enveloped viruses are sensitive to:
  - Drying — envelope desiccates
  - Detergents and soap — dissolve lipid envelope
  - Acid pH — destabilizes envelope
  - Heat — denatures envelope proteins
  - This is why handwashing with soap kills enveloped viruses (HIV, influenza, herpes)
- Non-enveloped viruses are resistant to:
  - Drying, acid, detergents — survive on surfaces, in gut, in water
  - This is why enteroviruses and adenoviruses spread easily via fomites and fecal-oral route

### The Tegument

- Layer of proteins between capsid and envelope — unique to herpesviruses
- Contains viral proteins that are immediately active upon cell entry — before viral gene expression begins
- Tegument proteins:
  - Shut down host protein synthesis immediately
  - Initiate viral gene transcription
  - Block host antiviral responses early

### Matrix Proteins

- Link the envelope to the capsid
- Important in viral assembly and budding
- Example: M protein in influenza, paramyxoviruses, rhabdoviruses

## 4. Enveloped vs Non-Enveloped Viruses

| Feature | Enveloped | Non-Enveloped (Naked) |
|---|---|---|
| Envelope | Present | Absent |
| Stability | Fragile — sensitive to heat, drying, detergents | Stable — resist heat, drying, acid, detergents |
| Transmission | Require close contact, moist secretions | Spread via fomites, fecal-oral, water |
| Soap and water | Kills virus | Less effective |
| Route of spread | Respiratory droplets, sexual, blood | Fecal-oral, respiratory, water, fomites |
| Examples | HIV, influenza, herpes, rabies, HBV, HCV | Poliovirus, adenovirus, parvovirus, HAV, rotavirus, HPV |

## 5. Viral Classification — The Baltimore System

The Baltimore classification system was devised by David Baltimore in 1971. It classifies viruses into seven groups based on the nature of their genome and the strategy used to produce mRNA — because all viruses must produce mRNA that can be translated by host ribosomes.

This is a high-yield MCQ topic at MKU.

| Group | Genome | Strategy | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Group I | dsDNA | Transcription directly to mRNA by host RNA polymerase | Herpesviruses, adenovirus, poxvirus, papillomavirus |
| Group II | ssDNA | Converted to dsDNA first, then transcribed | Parvovirus, anellovirus |
| Group III | dsRNA | Each segment transcribed to mRNA by viral RNA polymerase | Rotavirus, reovirus |
| Group IV | +ssRNA | Genome itself acts as mRNA — directly translated | Poliovirus, HAV, HCV, dengue, yellow fever, rhinovirus |
| Group V | -ssRNA | Viral RNA polymerase converts to +ssRNA (mRNA) first | Influenza, measles, mumps, rabies, RSV, Ebola |
| Group VI | +ssRNA with DNA intermediate | Reverse transcriptase converts RNA → DNA → integrated → mRNA | HIV, HTLV-1 |
| Group VII | dsDNA with RNA intermediate | Reverse transcriptase used in replication | Hepatitis B virus |

**Memory hook for Baltimore groups:**
- Groups I and II — DNA viruses
- Groups III, IV, V — RNA viruses (no reverse transcriptase)
- Groups VI and VII — use reverse transcriptase (retroviruses and hepadnaviruses)

## 6. ICTV Classification System

The International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV) is the official body that names and classifies viruses.

**Taxonomic hierarchy:**
- Order → Family → Subfamily → Genus → Species

**Naming conventions:**
- Family names end in **-viridae** (e.g., Herpesviridae, Retroviridae)
- Subfamily names end in **-virinae** (e.g., Alphaherpesvirinae)
- Genus names end in **-virus** (e.g., Simplexvirus)

**Basis of ICTV classification:**
- Type of nucleic acid (DNA or RNA)
- Strandedness (single or double)
- Sense (positive or negative)
- Presence or absence of envelope
- Capsid symmetry
- Size
- Mode of replication
- Host range

## 7. Atypical Viral Agents

### Viroids
- Smallest known infectious agents — smaller than viruses
- Consist of naked circular ssRNA only — no capsid, no envelope, no proteins
- Infect plants only — not humans
- Replicate using host RNA polymerase
- Cause diseases of agricultural plants

### Virusoids (Satellite RNAs)
- Similar to viroids but require a helper virus to replicate
- Associated with plant viruses

### Prions
- Covered in detail in Part 3

## Part 2: Viral Replication

## 1. Introduction

Viral replication is the process by which a virus produces copies of itself using the host cell's machinery. Understanding replication is essential for understanding pathogenesis — viral replication causes cell damage and death — and for understanding antiviral drug targets, since most antivirals work by blocking specific steps in replication. At MKU, replication appears in MCQs and the essay on stages of viral pathogenesis draws on knowledge of replication.

## 2. The Viral Replication Cycle — General Steps

All viruses, regardless of type, follow the same general sequence of replication steps:

### Step 1: Adsorption (Attachment)
- Viral spike or capsid protein binds specifically to receptor on host cell surface
- Highly specific — like a lock and key
- Determines host range and tissue tropism
- Irreversible binding — virus committed to infecting that cell

### Step 2: Penetration (Entry)
- Virus enters the cell after attachment
- Three mechanisms:
  - **Receptor-mediated endocytosis** — virus taken up in endosome; most common for enveloped viruses
  - **Direct fusion** — viral envelope fuses directly with plasma membrane; e.g., HIV, herpesviruses at neutral pH
  - **Translocation** — non-enveloped viruses inject genome directly through membrane; e.g., some bacteriophages

### Step 3: Uncoating
- Viral capsid and envelope removed — genome released into cytoplasm or nucleus
- Triggered by:
  - Low pH in endosome — conformational change in envelope glycoproteins
  - Viral enzymes
  - Host cell proteases
- For influenza — M2 ion channel allows H+ into virion → uncoating occurs in endosome
- For herpesviruses — capsid transported to nuclear pore → genome injected into nucleus

### Step 4: Early Gene Expression
- Viral genome transcribed to produce early mRNA
- Early proteins are mainly enzymes needed for genome replication:
  - DNA polymerases, RNA polymerases, reverse transcriptase, thymidine kinase
  - Proteins that shut down host cell functions
  - Proteins that evade immune response

### Step 5: Genome Replication
- Viral genome copied many times using viral polymerases
- DNA viruses — replicate in nucleus (exception: poxviruses replicate in cytoplasm)
- RNA viruses — replicate in cytoplasm (exception: influenza and orthomyxoviruses replicate in nucleus)
- Retroviruses — RNA → DNA (reverse transcription) → integrate → DNA replicated with host genome

### Step 6: Late Gene Expression
- Late mRNA transcribed and translated → structural proteins
- Capsid proteins, envelope glycoproteins, matrix proteins

### Step 7: Assembly
- New viral genomes packaged into newly synthesized capsids
- Occurs in nucleus (herpesviruses, adenovirus) or cytoplasm (picornaviruses, poxvirus)
- Enveloped viruses acquire their envelope during budding

### Step 8: Release (Budding or Lysis)
- **Non-enveloped viruses** — cell lyses → releases all virions at once → cell death
- **Enveloped viruses** — bud through host cell membrane, acquiring envelope; cell may survive longer
- Influenza neuraminidase cleaves sialic acid → virions released from cell surface

## 3. Lytic Cycle vs Lysogeny

This is primarily relevant to bacteriophages but the concepts apply to animal viruses.

### Lytic Cycle
- Virus infects host cell
- Takes over cell machinery immediately
- Replicates rapidly
- Lyses (kills) the host cell
- Releases large numbers of new virions
- Cell is destroyed
- Equivalent in animal viruses — acute cytolytic infection (e.g., poliovirus, influenza)

### Lysogeny (Lysogenic Cycle)
- Virus infects host cell
- Viral DNA integrates into host chromosome — called **prophage** in bacteria; **provirus** in animal viruses
- Viral DNA replicates with host DNA every time cell divides — all daughter cells carry viral genome
- No active viral replication — no virions produced
- Host cell is not destroyed — continues to live and divide
- Under appropriate stimulus (UV radiation, stress, chemicals) — **induction** occurs
- Prophage excised from chromosome → lytic cycle begins → cell eventually destroyed

**Effects of lysogeny on the host cell:**
- **Lysogenic conversion** — integration of prophage changes the phenotype of the host cell
- Most important examples in medicine:
  - **Corynebacterium diphtheriae** — only produces diphtheria toxin when infected by bacteriophage carrying the tox gene — a classic example of lysogenic conversion
  - **Clostridium botulinum** — botulinum toxin genes carried by bacteriophage
  - **Vibrio cholerae** — cholera toxin encoded by phage CTXφ
  - **Streptococcus pyogenes** — erythrogenic toxin (scarlet fever) encoded by phage
- **Superinfection immunity** — lysogenized cell resistant to infection by same phage type

**Equivalent in animal viruses:**
- HIV provirus in CD4+ cells
- Herpesvirus latency in neurons
- EBV latency in B cells

## 4. Viral Growth Curve — One-Step Growth Experiment

The one-step growth experiment demonstrates the kinetics of viral replication.

**Steps:**
1. Host cells infected simultaneously with a large inoculum of virus — synchronous infection
2. Cells washed — removes unattached virus
3. At intervals, cells and medium sampled — infectious virus titred
4. Results plotted — number of infectious units vs time

**Phases of the viral growth curve:**

**Eclipse Period:**
- Immediately after infection
- No infectious virus detectable inside or outside cells
- Input virus has been uncoated — genome released — no longer infectious
- Viral components being synthesized but not yet assembled into virions
- Length varies by virus — typically 2–12 hours

**Latent Period (Rise Period):**
- Includes eclipse period
- Ends when new infectious virions first appear inside cells
- Virions assembled but not yet released

**Burst (Rise):**
- Infectious virions released from cells
- Rapid rise in extracellular virus titre
- Continues until cell is lysed or no more virions released

**Burst Size:**
- Total number of infectious virions produced per infected cell
- Varies widely — poliovirus produces ~10,000; herpesvirus ~1,000; HIV ~10,000

## 5. Replication of Specific Virus Types

### DNA Virus Replication (Group I — e.g., Herpesviruses, Adenovirus)

1. Virus attaches to receptor → enters cell → transported to nucleus
2. Viral DNA enters nucleus through nuclear pore
3. Host RNA polymerase II transcribes viral DNA → early mRNA → early proteins (polymerases, immune evasion proteins)
4. Viral DNA polymerase replicates viral DNA genome
5. Late mRNA transcribed → late structural proteins (capsid proteins, glycoproteins)
6. Assembly in nucleus → capsids enveloped at nuclear membrane → transported to cell surface → released

**Exception — Poxviruses:**
- Replicate entirely in cytoplasm — carry their own RNA polymerase
- Form characteristic cytoplasmic inclusions — Guarnieri bodies

### RNA Virus Replication — Positive-Sense (Group IV — e.g., Poliovirus, HCV, Dengue)

1. Virus attaches → genome released into cytoplasm
2. Positive-sense RNA genome acts directly as mRNA
3. Host ribosomes translate it → large polyprotein
4. Viral protease cleaves polyprotein into individual proteins including RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp)
5. RdRp makes negative-sense RNA copy → template for new positive-sense RNA genomes
6. New positive-sense genomes serve as mRNA and as genomes for new virions
7. Assembly in cytoplasm → release by lysis

### RNA Virus Replication — Negative-Sense (Group V — e.g., Influenza, Measles, Rabies)

1. Virus brings its own RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) inside the virion — essential because host cannot read negative-sense RNA
2. On entry into cell, RdRp immediately transcribes negative-sense genome → positive-sense mRNA
3. mRNA translated → viral proteins including more RdRp
4. Negative-sense genome copies made for new virions
5. Assembly and release by budding

### Retrovirus Replication (Group VI — HIV)

1. gp120 binds CD4 + co-receptor → gp41 mediates fusion → core enters cytoplasm
2. Reverse transcriptase converts ssRNA genome → dsDNA (with RNase H degrading RNA strand)
3. dsDNA transported to nucleus
4. Integrase integrates dsDNA into host chromosome → provirus
5. Host RNA polymerase transcribes provirus → viral mRNA and full-length genomic RNA
6. Translation → Gag, Pol, Env polyproteins
7. Protease cleaves polyproteins → mature viral proteins
8. Assembly at cell membrane → budding → mature virion released

## Part 3: Prions and Slow Viruses

## 1. Introduction

Prions represent the most unconventional infectious agents in all of medicine. They are infectious proteins — they contain no nucleic acid whatsoever, yet they cause fatal progressive neurodegenerative diseases with very long incubation periods. They challenge the fundamental dogma that all infectious agents must contain nucleic acid. The diseases they cause — collectively called transmissible spongiform encephalopathies (TSEs) — are invariably fatal, have no treatment, and are extraordinarily resistant to normal sterilization methods. At MKU, prions appear in MCQs testing their unique properties and the diseases they cause.

## 2. What are Prions

**Prion** stands for **Pr**oteinaceous **i**nfectious particle.

- Consist entirely of protein — no DNA, no RNA
- The prion protein is a misfolded version of a normal host cell protein
- **Normal prion protein (PrPc)** — found on the surface of neurons; normal function unclear; alpha-helix rich conformation; soluble; non-infectious
- **Abnormal/infectious prion protein (PrPSc)** — misfolded version; beta-sheet rich conformation; insoluble; protease-resistant; infectious
- **Mechanism of propagation:**
  - PrPSc contacts normal PrPc → induces PrPc to refold into PrPSc conformation
  - Chain reaction — exponential accumulation of PrPSc in neurons
  - Neurons die → spongiform (spongy) appearance of brain — vacuolation
  - No inflammatory response — immune system cannot recognize prion protein as foreign (it is self protein)

## 3. Properties of Prions — High Yield

Prions are extraordinarily resistant to standard sterilization and deactivation methods:

| Method | Effect on Prions |
|---|---|
| Boiling (100°C) | Does NOT inactivate |
| Autoclaving (134°C, 18 min) | Partially inactivates |
| Formaldehyde | Does NOT inactivate |
| Ionising radiation (UV, gamma) | Does NOT inactivate |
| Nucleases (DNase, RNase) | No effect — no nucleic acid |
| Sodium hypochlorite (bleach) | Inactivates |
| NaOH (sodium hydroxide) | Inactivates |
| Incineration | Inactivates |

**Standard hospital sterilization does NOT kill prions** — special protocols required for neurosurgical instruments exposed to prion-infected tissue.

## 4. Transmissible Spongiform Encephalopathies (TSEs) — Prion Diseases

### Human TSEs

**1. Creutzfeldt-Jakob Disease (CJD)**

- Most common human prion disease
- Four forms:
  - **Sporadic CJD (sCJD)** — 85% of cases; spontaneous misfolding of PrPc; no known cause; age 50–70; rapidly progressive dementia; death within 1 year
  - **Familial/Genetic CJD** — 10–15%; autosomal dominant mutations in PRNP gene encoding PrPc
  - **Iatrogenic CJD** — transmitted by: corneal transplants, dural grafts, contaminated neurosurgical instruments, cadaveric pituitary growth hormone
  - **Variant CJD (vCJD)** — caused by eating BSE-contaminated beef; younger patients (average age 28); psychiatric symptoms early; peripheral sensory symptoms; longer course than sporadic CJD; link to mad cow disease

**Clinical features of CJD:**
- Rapidly progressive dementia
- Myoclonus — involuntary jerking movements; characteristic
- Ataxia
- Visual disturbances
- Psychiatric symptoms — depression, anxiety, personality change (especially vCJD)
- Akinetic mutism — late stage
- Death — average 6 months from onset (sporadic CJD)

**2. Kuru**
- Affected the Fore tribe of Papua New Guinea
- Transmitted by ritual cannibalism — eating brains of deceased tribe members
- Eliminated after cannibalism stopped
- Clinical features: cerebellar ataxia, tremor (kuru means trembling), dementia, death
- Incubation period: up to 30 years

**3. Gerstmann-Sträussler-Scheinker Syndrome (GSS)**
- Rare familial prion disease
- Autosomal dominant — PRNP mutation
- Cerebellar ataxia, dementia
- Slower progression than CJD — survival 2–10 years

**4. Fatal Familial Insomnia (FFI)**
- Autosomal dominant — PRNP mutation
- Progressive insomnia — inability to sleep — leads to hallucinations, dementia, death
- Affects thalamus primarily

### Animal TSEs

| Disease | Animal |
|---|---|
| Scrapie | Sheep and goats — oldest known TSE |
| Bovine Spongiform Encephalopathy (BSE) | Cattle — Mad Cow Disease |
| Chronic Wasting Disease (CWD) | Deer and elk |
| Feline Spongiform Encephalopathy | Cats |
| Transmissible mink encephalopathy | Mink |

## 5. Diagnosis of Prion Diseases

- **Brain MRI** — T2/FLAIR and DWI sequences; cortical ribboning, basal ganglia signal changes in CJD
- **EEG** — periodic sharp wave complexes in sporadic CJD (1 per second); characteristic
- **CSF 14-3-3 protein** — marker of rapid neuronal death; positive in CJD
- **RT-QuIC (Real-Time Quaking-Induced Conversion)** — highly sensitive and specific; detects PrPSc in CSF
- **Brain biopsy/autopsy** — definitive; histology shows spongiform change, astrogliosis, PrPSc deposits; immunohistochemistry
- **No serology** — immune system does not produce antibodies against prions

## 6. Slow Virus Infections

Slow virus infections are persistent viral infections with very long incubation periods (months to years) followed by progressive, fatal disease. The term was coined before prions were understood — some diseases originally called slow virus infections are now known to be caused by prions.

### True Slow Viral Infections (Caused by Conventional Viruses)

**1. Subacute Sclerosing Panencephalitis (SSPE)**
- Caused by: persistent measles virus in the brain — years after primary measles infection
- Incubation: 7–10 years after measles
- Occurs in children who had measles before age 2
- Pathogenesis: defective measles virus persists in neurons; cannot complete replication; slowly accumulates
- Clinical stages:
  - Stage 1: personality change, behavioural problems, school failure
  - Stage 2: myoclonic jerks, seizures, intellectual deterioration
  - Stage 3: spasticity, coma
  - Stage 4: vegetative state, death
- Diagnosis: very high measles antibody titres in CSF; EEG — Radermecker complexes (periodic high-amplitude bursts); brain biopsy — intranuclear inclusion bodies
- Treatment: none effective; isoprinosine and interferon-beta slow progression
- Prevention: measles vaccination — SSPE has virtually disappeared in vaccinated populations

**2. Progressive Multifocal Leukoencephalopathy (PML)**
- Caused by: JC virus (a polyomavirus) — reactivation in immunocompromised patients
- JC virus infects 70–80% of adults — latent in kidneys and lymphoid tissue
- Reactivates in AIDS, organ transplant, immunosuppressive therapy
- Destroys oligodendrocytes → progressive demyelination
- Clinical features: cognitive decline, visual loss, weakness, speech problems, ataxia; rapidly progressive
- Diagnosis: MRI — white matter lesions; PCR of CSF for JC virus DNA
- Treatment: restore immune function (ART for HIV); no specific antiviral

**3. HIV Dementia (HIV-Associated Neurocognitive Disorder — HAND)**
- HIV itself infects microglial cells in the brain
- Progressive cognitive decline, motor dysfunction, behavioural changes
- Improves with ART

### Originally Called Slow Virus — Now Known to be Prion Diseases
- Kuru
- CJD
- Scrapie
- GSS
- FFI

## Part 4: Poxviruses and Varicella-Zoster Virus

## 1. Introduction

Poxviruses and Varicella-Zoster Virus (VZV) represent two very different but clinically important groups of DNA viruses that both cause characteristic vesicular skin lesions. Smallpox, caused by Variola virus, was one of the most devastating diseases in human history — responsible for more deaths than any other infectious disease — before being eradicated in 1980 through one of the greatest achievements of modern medicine. VZV causes chickenpox (primary infection) and shingles (reactivation) — both common and clinically important diseases. At MKU these topics appear in MCQs and occasionally in essays on viral latency and reactivation.

## 2. Poxviruses

### Classification and Structure
- **Family:** Poxviridae
- **Genome:** Double-stranded DNA — largest and most complex viral genome
- **Non-enveloped** in terms of classification — though has complex outer membranes
- **Shape:** Brick-shaped or ovoid — most complex morphology of all animal viruses
- **Replication:** Entirely in the cytoplasm — carries its own RNA polymerase and all replication enzymes; does not need the nucleus
- **Cytoplasmic inclusions:** Guarnieri bodies — seen on histology of infected cells

**Unique features of poxviruses:**
- Largest animal viruses — visible under light microscope
- Most complex genome — encodes hundreds of proteins
- Replicate in cytoplasm — unlike most DNA viruses
- Carry their own RNA polymerase and DNA polymerase
- Encode multiple immune evasion proteins

### Smallpox (Variola)

**Causative agent:** Variola virus — two strains:
- Variola major — case fatality rate 20–30%
- Variola minor (Alastrim) — case fatality rate less than 1%

**Historical significance:**
- Killed hundreds of millions of people throughout history
- Edward Jenner developed first vaccine in 1796 — used cowpox virus (vaccinia)
- Global eradication campaign by WHO — declared eradicated in 1980
- Last natural case — Ali Maow Maalin, Somalia, 1977
- Only virus ever eradicated

**Pathogenesis:**
- Transmitted by respiratory droplets and direct contact with skin lesions
- Inhaled → infects oropharyngeal mucosa → regional lymph nodes → primary viremia → spleen, liver, bone marrow → secondary viremia → skin and mucous membranes
- Skin lesions go through characteristic evolution

**Clinical features:**
- **Incubation:** 7–17 days
- Prodrome: sudden high fever, severe headache, backache, myalgia — 2–4 days
- Rash:
  - Begins on face → spreads centrifugally to trunk and limbs
  - **Centrifugal distribution** — more lesions on face and extremities than trunk
  - All lesions at same stage of development — synchronous
  - Evolution: macule → papule → vesicle → pustule → crust → scar
  - Lesions are deep, firm, and feel embedded in skin
  - Oral and pharyngeal lesions — enanthem

**Distinguishing smallpox from chickenpox:**

| Feature | Smallpox | Chickenpox (VZV) |
|---|---|---|
| Distribution | Centrifugal — face and extremities more | Centripetal — trunk more |
| Lesion stage | All lesions at same stage | Lesions at different stages simultaneously |
| Depth | Deep, firm, embedded | Superficial, fragile |
| Palm and sole | Yes — lesions present | Rare |
| Fever pattern | Fever before rash | Rash with mild fever |
| Scarring | Severe, permanent | Usually none |

**Diagnosis:**
- Electron microscopy — brick-shaped virions
- PCR — detection of Variola DNA
- Culture — in BSL-4 laboratory (only 2 locations: CDC Atlanta and VECTOR Russia)

**Bioterrorism concern:**
- Variola virus stored in two WHO-approved repositories
- Concern about weaponization — especially as vaccination stopped in 1980 and most people now susceptible
- Smallpox vaccine (vaccinia) stockpiled by many governments

### Monkeypox (Mpox)

- **Causative agent:** Monkeypox virus — closely related to Variola
- **Reservoir:** Rodents in Central and West Africa
- **Transmission:** Zoonotic — animal to human; also human-to-human (close contact, respiratory droplets, skin lesions)
- **2022 global outbreak** — spread globally through close contact, predominantly in men who have sex with men
- **Clinical features:** Similar to smallpox but milder; fever, lymphadenopathy (distinctive), rash; case fatality lower
- **Lymphadenopathy** — distinguishes monkeypox from smallpox
- **Treatment:** Tecovirimat (TPOXX) — antiviral; blocks viral egress
- **Prevention:** Smallpox vaccine — 85% cross-protective

### Molluscum Contagiosum

- **Causative agent:** Molluscum contagiosum virus
- Common skin infection — especially in children and immunocompromised
- Transmitted by direct contact and fomites
- **Lesions:** Small, pearly, dome-shaped papules with central umbilication — pathognomonic
- Self-limiting in immunocompetent — resolves in months
- Extensive lesions in HIV/AIDS patients — marker of immunosuppression
- Treatment: cryotherapy, curettage, topical agents

## 3. Varicella-Zoster Virus (VZV)

### Classification
- **Family:** Herpesviridae
- **Subfamily:** Alphaherpesvirinae — same subfamily as HSV
- **Genome:** Double-stranded DNA
- **Enveloped**
- Causes two distinct diseases:
  - **Primary infection:** Varicella (chickenpox)
  - **Reactivation:** Herpes zoster (shingles)

### Epidemiology

- Highly contagious — one of the most contagious human viruses
- Spreads by: respiratory droplets, direct contact with vesicle fluid, airborne
- Primary infection almost universal in childhood in unvaccinated populations
- After primary infection — latency established in dorsal root ganglia and cranial nerve ganglia

## 4. Varicella (Chickenpox) — Primary VZV Infection

### Pathogenesis
- Inhaled virus infects respiratory tract → regional lymph nodes → primary viremia
- Replicates in liver and spleen → secondary viremia → skin
- Vesicular rash appears — skin lesions contain high titre of virus

### Clinical Features
- **Incubation:** 10–21 days
- **Prodrome:** Mild fever, malaise — 1–2 days (in children prodrome often absent)
- **Rash:**
  - Starts on trunk and face → spreads to limbs — **centripetal distribution**
  - **Pleomorphic** — lesions at different stages simultaneously — macules, papules, vesicles, crusts all visible at once
  - Vesicles described as **dewdrop on a rose petal** — clear vesicle on erythematous base
  - Intensely pruritic
  - Mucous membranes also affected — mouth, conjunctiva, genitalia
- Fever — low grade, correlates with extent of rash

### Complications

**In immunocompetent:**
- Secondary bacterial infection of skin lesions — most common complication; Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pyogenes
- Cerebellar ataxia — most common CNS complication; usually benign and self-limiting
- Varicella pneumonia — more common and severe in adults and smokers
- Encephalitis — rare

**In immunocompromised:**
- Disseminated varicella — multi-organ involvement; hepatitis, pneumonia, encephalitis; high mortality
- Progressive varicella — continued new lesion formation without healing

**In pregnancy:**
- First/second trimester — congenital varicella syndrome (skin scarring, limb hypoplasia, eye defects, brain defects)
- Near term — neonatal varicella — severe, potentially fatal if mother develops rash 5 days before to 2 days after delivery (neonatal immune system not yet primed)

**Reye's Syndrome:**
- Rare but serious — encephalopathy and liver failure
- Associated with aspirin use during varicella in children
- Aspirin must NOT be given to children with varicella

### Diagnosis
- Clinical — characteristic rash
- **Tzanck smear** — multinucleated giant cells; does not distinguish VZV from HSV
- **PCR** — from vesicle fluid; gold standard
- **DFA** (Direct Fluorescent Antibody) — from vesicle scraping; rapid and specific

### Treatment
- **Immunocompetent children:** Supportive — antipyretics (paracetamol, not aspirin), antihistamines for itch, keep nails short
- **Acyclovir** — indicated for:
  - Adults with varicella — more severe disease
  - Immunocompromised
  - Pregnant women
  - Newborns with neonatal varicella
  - Start within 24 hours of rash onset for maximum benefit

### Prevention
- **Varicella vaccine** — live attenuated (Oka strain); two doses; highly effective (>90%)
- **VZIG (Varicella-Zoster Immunoglobulin)** — passive immunization for post-exposure prophylaxis in high-risk susceptible individuals (immunocompromised, pregnant women, neonates)

## 5. Herpes Zoster (Shingles) — VZV Reactivation

### Pathogenesis
- After primary varicella — VZV establishes latency in dorsal root ganglia and cranial nerve ganglia
- Latent virus reactivates when cellular immunity declines — aging, immunosuppression, stress, malignancy
- Reactivated virus travels anterograde along sensory nerve → skin of the corresponding dermatome
- Causes vesicular rash confined to one or two dermatomes — unilateral

### Clinical Features
- **Prodrome (2–4 days):** Severe burning, shooting pain, paraesthesia along affected dermatome — before rash appears; often misdiagnosed at this stage
- **Rash:** Unilateral vesicular rash strictly following one dermatome
  - Most common dermatomes: thoracic (T3–L3) — most common; trigeminal (face)
  - Does not cross midline
  - Evolution: erythema → papules → vesicles → pustules → crusts
  - New lesions form over 3–5 days

### Special Forms of Herpes Zoster

**Herpes Zoster Ophthalmicus:**
- Reactivation in ophthalmic branch of trigeminal nerve (V1)
- Rash on forehead, tip of nose (Hutchinson's sign — nasal tip rash predicts eye involvement), periorbital area
- **Hutchinson's sign** — vesicles on tip of nose; indicates involvement of nasociliary branch → high risk of eye complications
- Complications: keratitis, uveitis, glaucoma, blindness
- Treat aggressively with systemic acyclovir + ophthalmology referral

**Ramsay Hunt Syndrome:**
- Reactivation in geniculate ganglion of facial nerve (CN VII)
- Triad: painful vesicles in ear canal and pinna + ipsilateral facial nerve palsy + hearing loss/tinnitus/vertigo
- Treat with acyclovir + prednisolone

### Complications of Herpes Zoster

**Postherpetic Neuralgia (PHN) — most common and important:**

- Persistent severe pain in the dermatome after the rash has healed
- Lasts months to years
- More common and severe in elderly
- Debilitating — significant impact on quality of life
- Management: tricyclic antidepressants (amitriptyline), gabapentin, pregabalin, topical lidocaine or capsaicin patches

**Disseminated Zoster:**
- In immunocompromised — lesions spread beyond the primary dermatome
- Multi-organ involvement — hepatitis, pneumonitis, encephalitis
- High mortality without treatment

### Diagnosis
- Clinical — characteristic dermatomal distribution
- PCR — from vesicle fluid; gold standard
- DFA — from vesicle scraping

### Treatment
- **Antivirals** — most effective when started within 72 hours of rash onset:
  - Acyclovir — 800 mg five times daily for 7 days
  - Valacyclovir — 1 g three times daily for 7 days (better bioavailability)
  - Famciclovir — 500 mg three times daily for 7 days
- **Steroids** — prednisolone combined with antiviral; reduces acute pain, may reduce PHN risk
- **Analgesics** — important; pain can be severe; paracetamol, NSAIDs, opioids if needed
- **PHN treatment:** Amitriptyline, gabapentin, pregabalin, topical capsaicin

### Prevention
- **Shingles vaccine:**
  - **Zostavax** — live attenuated; single dose; reduces zoster incidence by 51% and PHN by 67%
  - **Shingrix** — recombinant subunit vaccine; two doses; more effective (>90% against zoster); preferred in elderly and immunocompromised
- Recommended for adults over 50 years
