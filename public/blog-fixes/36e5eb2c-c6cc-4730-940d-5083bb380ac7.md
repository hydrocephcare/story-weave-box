This article provides a comprehensive overview of fundamental concepts in medical microbiology, covering microbial biochemistry, cellular structures, mechanisms of pathogenesis, and the principles behind antimicrobial therapies. Understanding these core areas is crucial for Year 2 MBChB students to grasp how microorganisms interact with the human host and how infectious diseases are managed.

## 1. Microbial Biochemistry and Energy Acquisition

Microorganisms exhibit a remarkable diversity in how they acquire energy and nutrients, forming the basis of their survival and ecological roles, including pathogenicity.

### Types of Trophs
Organisms are classified based on their carbon and energy sources:

*   **Autotrophs**: Synthesize their own organic compounds from inorganic carbon sources (e.g., CO₂).
*   **Heterotrophs**: Obtain organic compounds from their environment, requiring pre-formed organic molecules for nutrition.
*   **Photoautotrophs**: Use light energy to fix CO₂ into organic compounds (e.g., cyanobacteria, photosynthetic bacteria). They are primary producers.
*   **Chemoautotrophs (Chemolithotrophs)**: Obtain energy by oxidizing inorganic compounds (e.g., ammonia, hydrogen sulfide, iron) and use CO₂ as their carbon source. Important in nutrient cycling.
*   **Chemoheterotrophs**: Obtain both energy and carbon from organic compounds. Most medically relevant bacteria and all fungi, protozoa, and animals fall into this category.

### Energy Currencies
All cellular life relies on universal energy currencies:

*   **Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP)**: The primary energy currency, generated through substrate-level phosphorylation, oxidative phosphorylation, or photophosphorylation.
*   **Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide (NADH)** and **Flavin Adenine Dinucleotide (FADH₂) **: Electron carriers that transfer energy during metabolic reactions, particularly in respiration.

### Metabolic Strategies for Energy Acquisition
Microbes employ various pathways to generate ATP:

*   **Glycolysis**: The initial breakdown of glucose into pyruvate, producing a small amount of ATP and NADH. Occurs in both aerobic and anaerobic conditions.
*   **Krebs Cycle (Citric Acid Cycle)**: Further oxidizes pyruvate derivatives, generating ATP, NADH, and FADH₂. Requires aerobic conditions or specific anaerobic pathways.
*   **Electron Transport Chain (ETC) & Oxidative Phosphorylation**: The most efficient ATP-generating pathway, where electrons from NADH and FADH₂ are passed along a series of protein complexes, creating a proton gradient used by ATP synthase. This process is central to respiration.

**Key Metabolic Pathways:**

*   **Aerobic Respiration**: Uses oxygen as the final electron acceptor in the ETC, yielding a high amount of ATP. Many pathogenic bacteria (e.g., *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*) are obligate aerobes.
*   **Anaerobic Respiration**: Uses inorganic molecules other than oxygen (e.g., nitrate, sulfate) as final electron acceptors. Less efficient than aerobic respiration but allows growth in oxygen-depleted environments (e.g., some *Clostridium* species).
*   **Fermentation**: An anaerobic process that regenerates NAD⁺ from NADH by transferring electrons to an endogenous organic molecule (e.g., pyruvate). Produces much less ATP than respiration but allows glycolysis to continue in the absence of external electron acceptors. Examples include lactic acid fermentation (e.g., *Streptococcus*, *Lactobacillus*) and alcohol fermentation (e.g., yeasts).
*   **Phototrophy**: Utilizes light energy. The **Calvin Cycle** is a central pathway for **carbon fixation** in photoautotrophs (e.g., cyanobacteria, purple sulfur bacteria). It converts **CO₂ → glucose** using ATP and NADPH generated from light-dependent reactions. Key enzymes include RuBisCO (Ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase) and G3P dehydrogenase. The Calvin Cycle forms the foundation of photosynthetic energy storage, making it vital for global carbon cycling.
*   **Chemolithotrophy**: As mentioned, this involves oxidizing inorganic compounds for energy. Examples include nitrifying bacteria (oxidize ammonia to nitrite/nitrate) and sulfur-oxidizing bacteria.

## 2. Principles of Medical Microbiology

Medical microbiology is built upon foundational discoveries that elucidated the role of microorganisms in disease and established principles for their control.

### Historical Contributors of Medical Microbiology

*   **Louis Pasteur (1822-1895)**: Revolutionized understanding with his experiments disproving spontaneous generation, firmly establishing the **germ theory of disease**. Developed the first vaccines for rabies and anthrax, and introduced pasteurization to prevent food spoilage.
*   **Robert Koch (1843-1910)**: A key figure in identifying specific disease-causing microbes. Formulated **Koch’s Postulates**, a set of criteria to establish a causal relationship between a microbe and a disease. Isolated the bacterium causing tuberculosis (*Mycobacterium tuberculosis*) and cholera (*Vibrio cholerae*).
*   **Joseph Lister (1827-1912)**: Pioneered **antiseptic surgery** by using carbolic acid to sterilize surgical instruments and wounds, dramatically reducing post-operative infections and mortality. His work validated Pasteur's germ theory in a clinical setting.

### Basic Concepts in Medical Microbiology

*   **Pathogenicity**: The ability of a microorganism to cause disease.
*   **Virulence**: The degree of pathogenicity, often quantified by the severity of disease or the number of microbes required to cause infection.
*   **Sterilization**: The complete destruction or removal of all viable microorganisms, including bacterial spores, from an object or habitat.
*   **Disinfection**: The process of eliminating most pathogenic microorganisms (excluding spores) from inanimate objects.
*   **Antisepsis**: The reduction of microbial load on living tissue to prevent infection.

## 3. Prokaryotic Cell Structures

Bacteria, as prokaryotes, possess distinct cellular structures that contribute to their survival, virulence, and interaction with the host.

### External Structures

*   **Capsule**: A well-organized, outermost layer of polysaccharide or polypeptide, firmly attached to the cell wall. It is a significant **virulence factor**, primarily by being **anti-phagocytic**, allowing bacteria to evade host immune responses. It also aids in adherence to surfaces (e.g., *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Klebsiella pneumoniae*).
*   **Cell Wall**: A rigid layer providing structural support and protection against osmotic lysis. Its composition is a key differentiator between Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria.
    *   **Gram-positive bacteria**: Possess a **thick peptidoglycan layer** (murein) external to the cell membrane. This layer is extensively cross-linked and contains **teichoic acids** and lipoteichoic acids, which contribute to cell wall integrity and can act as virulence factors (e.g., *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Streptococcus pyogenes*).
    *   **Gram-negative bacteria**: Have a **thin peptidoglycan layer** sandwiched between an inner cytoplasmic membrane and an **outer membrane**. The outer membrane contains **Lipopolysaccharide (LPS)**, also known as endotoxin, which is a potent immune stimulant and a major virulence factor, causing fever, shock, and DIC in severe infections (e.g., *E. coli*, *Salmonella*, *Neisseria*).
*   **Flagella**: Long, filamentous appendages composed of flagellin protein, responsible for **motility** (swimming). Bacteria can be monotrichous (one flagellum), lophotrichous (tuft at one end), amphitrichous (at both ends), or peritrichous (all over). Flagella allow bacteria to move towards attractants or away from repellents (chemotaxis).
*   **Fimbriae/Pili**: Shorter, thinner, and more numerous than flagella. Primarily involved in **attachment** to host cells and surfaces, crucial for colonization and biofilm formation (e.g., *Neisseria gonorrhoeae*). Specialized pili, called **sex pili**, are involved in **conjugation**, the transfer of genetic material between bacteria.

### Internal Structures

*   **Plasmids**: Small, circular, extrachromosomal DNA molecules that replicate independently of the bacterial chromosome. They often carry genes for **antibiotic resistance** (R-plasmids) or **virulence factors** (e.g., toxin production), which can be readily transferred between bacteria via conjugation.
*   **Ribosomes**: Prokaryotic ribosomes are **70S** (composed of 30S and 50S subunits), distinct from eukaryotic 80S ribosomes. This difference is exploited by many antibiotics that selectively target bacterial protein synthesis.
*   **Nucleoid**: The region within the cytoplasm where the bacterial chromosome (a single, circular DNA molecule) is located. It is not membrane-bound like a eukaryotic nucleus.
*   **Endospores**: Highly resistant, dormant structures formed by some Gram-positive bacteria (e.g., *Bacillus*, *Clostridium*) in response to adverse conditions. They can survive extreme heat, radiation, and chemicals, making them medically significant for sterilization and disease transmission.

| **Peptidoglycan** | Thick (20-80 nm), multi-layered                      | Thin (2-7 nm), single-layered                        |
| **Outer Membrane**| Absent                                               | Present, external to peptidoglycan                   |

## 4. Antibiotics and Resistance

Antibiotics are crucial for treating bacterial infections, but the rise of antibiotic resistance poses a significant global health threat.

### Antibiotics Overview
Antibiotics are classified by their chemical structure and their mechanism of action, targeting essential bacterial processes:

*   **Cell Wall Synthesis Inhibitors**: Prevent the formation of the peptidoglycan layer, leading to osmotic lysis.
    *   **Beta-lactams**: Penicillins, Cephalosporins, Carbapenems, Monobactams. Inhibit transpeptidases (Penicillin-Binding Proteins, PBPs) involved in peptidoglycan cross-linking.
    *   **Glycopeptides**: Vancomycin. Binds to D-Ala-D-Ala termini of peptidoglycan precursors, preventing cross-linking and elongation.
*   **Protein Synthesis Inhibitors**: Target bacterial 70S ribosomes.
    *   **Aminoglycosides**: Gentamicin, Streptomycin. Bind to 30S subunit, causing misreading of mRNA.
    *   **Macrolides**: Erythromycin, Azithromycin. Bind to 50S subunit, inhibiting translocation.
    *   **Tetracyclines**: Doxycycline. Bind to 30S subunit, blocking tRNA binding.
    *   **Chloramphenicol**: Binds to 50S subunit, inhibiting peptide bond formation.
*   **Nucleic Acid Synthesis Inhibitors**: Interfere with DNA or RNA replication/transcription.
    *   **Quinolones/Fluoroquinolones**: Ciprofloxacin. Inhibit bacterial DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV.
    *   **Rifamycins**: Rifampicin. Inhibit bacterial RNA polymerase.
*   **Metabolic Pathway Inhibitors**: Block essential metabolic pathways.
    *   **Sulfonamides**: Inhibit dihydropteroate synthase, blocking folic acid synthesis.
    *   **Trimethoprim**: Inhibits dihydrofolate reductase, also blocking folic acid synthesis. Often used in combination with sulfonamides (co-trimoxazole).
*   **Cell Membrane Disruptors**: Polymyxins. Disrupt the integrity of the bacterial cell membrane.

### Mechanisms of Resistance
Bacteria develop resistance through various mechanisms, often encoded on plasmids or transposons:

*   **Genetic Transfer**: Resistance genes can spread rapidly among bacteria.
    *   **Transformation**: Uptake of naked DNA from the environment.
    *   **Conjugation**: Direct transfer of plasmids (often R-plasmids) via sex pili.
    *   **Transduction**: Transfer of bacterial DNA by bacteriophages.
*   **Enzymatic Inactivation**: Production of enzymes that degrade or modify the antibiotic. E.g., **β-lactamases** (penicillinases, cephalosporinases, carbapenemases) hydrolyze the β-lactam ring, rendering the antibiotic inactive.
*   **Target Modification**: Alteration of the antibiotic's target site, reducing its binding affinity. E.g., altered PBPs in **MRSA** (Methicillin-Resistant *Staphylococcus aureus*) reduce beta-lactam binding; altered ribosomal subunits reduce aminoglycoside or macrolide binding; altered D-Ala-D-Ala in **VRE** (Vancomycin-Resistant Enterococci) prevents vancomycin binding.
*   **Efflux Pumps**: Membrane proteins that actively pump antibiotics out of the bacterial cell, reducing intracellular concentration. These can confer multi-drug resistance.
*   **Reduced Permeability**: Alterations in outer membrane porins (Gram-negative bacteria) can prevent antibiotics from entering the cell.

### Types of Action

*   **Bacteriostatic**: Inhibit bacterial growth and replication, allowing the host immune system to clear the infection. Examples include tetracyclines, macrolides, chloramphenicol, sulfonamides. These are often suitable for patients with intact immune systems.
*   **Bactericidal**: Directly kill bacteria. Examples include penicillins, cephalosporins, aminoglycosides, quinolones, vancomycin. These are preferred for severe infections, immunocompromised patients, or infections in sites where host defenses are poor (e.g., endocarditis, meningitis).

## 5. Viruses and Pathogenesis

Viruses are obligate intracellular parasites that hijack host cellular machinery for replication, leading to diverse pathogenic outcomes.

### Basic Viral Structure and Replication

*   **Structure**: Consist of genetic material (DNA or RNA) encased in a protein coat called a **capsid**. Some viruses also have an outer **envelope** derived from host cell membranes, studded with viral glycoproteins (spikes).
*   **Replication Cycle**: Typically involves attachment, entry, uncoating, replication of genetic material, synthesis of viral proteins, assembly, and release (lysis or budding).

### Viral Pathogenesis and Types of Infection

*   **Productive (Lytic) Infection**: Virus replicates efficiently, leading to the lysis and death of the host cell and release of new virions (e.g., Influenza, Poliovirus).
*   **Abortive Infection**: Virus enters the cell, but replication is incomplete or blocked, resulting in no progeny virus and no cell death. Can occur in non-permissive cells.
*   **Latent Infection**: The viral genome persists in the host cell without active replication or production of infectious virions. The virus can reactivate later to cause productive infection (e.g., Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV), Varicella-Zoster Virus (VZV)).
*   **Persistent (Chronic) Infection**: Continuous production of viral particles without immediate cell lysis, often leading to long-term disease (e.g., Hepatitis B Virus, Hepatitis C Virus).
*   **Oncogenic Infection**: Some viruses can transform host cells, leading to uncontrolled proliferation and cancer (e.g., Human Papillomavirus (HPV), Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV)).

### Latency & Immune Evasion
Viruses employ sophisticated strategies to evade the host immune system:

*   **Latency**: As described above, the viral genome remains dormant within host cells, often in a non-replicating state, making it invisible to immune surveillance (e.g., HSV hides in neurons).
*   **Downregulating MHC I**: Many viruses reduce the expression of Major Histocompatibility Complex class I molecules on the surface of infected cells, preventing cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) from recognizing and killing the infected cells.
*   **Antigenic Variation**: Rapid mutation of surface antigens (e.g., spikes) allows the virus to escape pre-existing antibodies (e.g., Influenza virus).
*   **Molecular Mimicry**: Producing proteins that resemble host proteins, confusing the immune system.
*   **Interference with Immune Signaling**: Producing viral proteins that block interferon pathways or cytokine signaling.

### Importance of Viral Spikes

*   **Spikes** are surface glycoproteins embedded in the viral envelope (e.g., hemagglutinin and neuraminidase in influenza virus). They are critical for **host cell attachment and entry** by binding to specific host cell receptors.
*   Mutations in spike proteins are responsible for **antigenic drift** (minor changes due to point mutations, leading to seasonal epidemics) and **antigenic shift** (major changes due to reassortment of viral RNA segments, leading to pandemics), particularly in influenza virus. These changes necessitate annual vaccine updates.

## 6. Virology - Specific Topics

### Retroviruses

*   **Definition**: RNA viruses that utilize the enzyme **reverse transcriptase** to synthesize a DNA copy of their RNA genome. This DNA copy is then integrated into the host cell's genome, becoming a **provirus**.
*   **Life Cycle (HIV example)**: Entry via CD4 and co-receptor binding → reverse transcription of RNA to cDNA → integration of cDNA into host genome (provirus) by integrase → latency or active transcription of viral genes → assembly of new virions → budding.
*   **Viral Latency**: The integrated provirus can remain dormant for extended periods, making it difficult to eradicate and contributing to immune evasion.
*   **Immune Evasion**: HIV employs high mutation rates (antigenic variation), latency, and direct infection of immune cells (CD4+ T cells) to evade the immune system.

### Phages (Bacteriophages)

*   **Definition**: Viruses that specifically infect bacteria. They are abundant in nature and play significant roles in bacterial evolution and ecology.
*   **Life Cycles**:
    *   **Lytic cycle**: Phage replicates and lyses the host bacterium, releasing new phages.
    *   **Lysogenic cycle**: Phage DNA integrates into the bacterial chromosome (prophage) and replicates along with the host genome without causing immediate lysis. The prophage can later excise and enter the lytic cycle.
*   **Clinical Relevance**:
    *   **Transduction**: Phages can transfer bacterial genes, including **resistance genes** (e.g., antibiotic resistance) and **virulence genes** (e.g., toxin genes like diphtheria toxin, botulinum toxin), between bacteria. This is a major mechanism for the spread of antibiotic resistance and pathogenicity.
    *   **Phage Therapy**: The use of bacteriophages to treat bacterial infections, particularly those resistant to conventional antibiotics. This is an emerging area of research.

## 7. Antiviral & Antiretroviral Drugs

Developing antiviral drugs is challenging due to the intimate relationship between viruses and host cells, making it difficult to target viral processes without harming host cells.

### Antiviral Drugs
Antivirals target specific stages of the viral replication cycle:

*   **Entry/Fusion Inhibitors**: Block viral attachment to host cells or fusion of the viral envelope with the host membrane (e.g., Enfuvirtide for HIV, Maraviroc for HIV).
*   **Uncoating Inhibitors**: Prevent the release of the viral genome into the host cytoplasm (e.g., Amantadine for Influenza A, though resistance is common).
*   **Nucleoside/Nucleotide Analogues**: Mimic natural nucleosides/nucleotides, getting incorporated into the viral DNA/RNA during replication and terminating chain elongation or inhibiting viral polymerases. Examples include **Acyclovir** (HSV, VZV), Ganciclovir (CMV), Ribavirin (RSV, HCV), Tenofovir (HIV, HBV).
*   **Protease Inhibitors**: Block viral proteases, which are essential for cleaving viral polyproteins into functional individual proteins required for viral assembly (e.g., Saquinavir for HIV).
*   **Neuraminidase Inhibitors**: Block the neuraminidase enzyme on the surface of influenza virus, preventing the release of new virions from infected cells (e.g., Oseltamivir, Zanamivir).

### Antiretroviral Therapy (ART)

ART is a combination therapy used to treat **HIV infection**, typically involving multiple drugs from different classes to suppress viral replication and prevent resistance. This approach is known as **Highly Active Antiretroviral Therapy (HAART)**.

**Common Classes of Antiretroviral Drugs:**

*   **Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors (RTIs)**:
    *   **Nucleoside/Nucleotide Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors (NRTIs)**: Mimic nucleosides/nucleotides and are incorporated into the nascent viral DNA chain, causing chain termination (e.g., Tenofovir, Emtricitabine, Zidovudine).
    *   **Non-Nucleoside Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors (NNRTIs)**: Bind directly to reverse transcriptase, inhibiting its activity non-competitively (e.g., Efavirenz, Nevirapine).
*   **Protease Inhibitors (PIs)**: Block the HIV protease enzyme, preventing the cleavage of viral polyproteins into functional proteins, leading to the production of immature, non-infectious virions (e.g., Ritonavir, Lopinavir).
*   **Integrase Inhibitors (INSTIs)**: Block the HIV integrase enzyme, preventing the integration of viral DNA into the host cell's genome (e.g., Raltegravir, Dolutegravir).
*   **Entry/Fusion Inhibitors**: Prevent HIV from entering host cells by blocking attachment or fusion (e.g., Maraviroc, Enfuvirtide).

ART has transformed HIV from a rapidly fatal disease into a manageable chronic condition, significantly improving the quality of life and life expectancy for people living with HIV.
