# Neonatal Sepsis

**Year 4 · Paediatrics · Neonatology**

> **In one line:** neonatal sepsis is bloodstream (or other sterile-site) infection in a baby under 1 month, made likely by an immature immune system and breached barriers; **early-onset** disease comes from the mother (ascending or at birth, e.g. GBS and E. coli), while **late-onset** disease comes from the environment and hospital (e.g. coagulase-negative staphylococci, Klebsiella). Signs are non-specific, so treat promptly with IV antibiotics and supportive care.

## What you must know

- **Definition**: infant under 1 month, clinically ill, with a **positive blood culture** (the gold standard). Negative cultures with compatible signs = **clinical sepsis**.
- **Early-onset**: day 0 to 3 (about 85% present within 24 hours); maternal organisms: **GBS, E. coli**, *Listeria*, *H. influenzae*. **Pneumonia** is commoner.
- **Late-onset**: day 4 to 90, from the environment; **meningitis and bacteraemia** are commoner.
- Prematurity and very low birthweight raise the risk 10 to 20 times; onset is fastest in premature babies.
- Signs are **non-specific**: poor feeding, temperature instability, respiratory distress, apnoea, lethargy, seizures, jaundice, hypoglycaemia.
- Investigations: FBC, CRP, **blood, urine and CSF cultures**; treat with IV antibiotics following local paediatric protocols.
- Prevention: treat maternal GBS bacteriuria, clean delivery, **cord care**, early breastfeeding.

## 1. Definition and classification

- **Sepsis neonatorum**: disease of infants younger than 1 month, clinically ill, with positive blood cultures.
- **Clinical sepsis**: signs consistent with infection but blood and other sterile-site cultures negative.

| Feature | Early-onset | Late-onset |
|---|---|---|
| Timing | Day of life 0 to 3 | Day 4 to 90 |
| Source | **Mother** (transplacental haematogenous spread, ascending infection from the cervix, or colonised birth canal at delivery) | **Environment** (hospital, community, equipment, hands) |
| Commoner presentation | **Pneumonia** | **Meningitis and bacteraemia** |
| Timing detail | 85% within 24 hours, 5% at 24 to 48 hours, a smaller number at 48 to 72 hours | Later and more insidious |

- Onset is most rapid in premature neonates.
- Early-onset sepsis is **10 to 20 times** more likely in premature, very low birthweight infants.

## 2. Aetiology

| Onset | Organisms |
|---|---|
| **Early** | **Group B Streptococcus (GBS)**, ***Escherichia coli***, coagulase-negative *Staphylococcus*, ***Haemophilus influenzae***, ***Listeria monocytogenes*** |
| **Late** | Coagulase-negative staphylococci, ***Staphylococcus aureus***, *E. coli*, ***Klebsiella***, ***Pseudomonas***, *Enterobacter*, ***Candida***, GBS, *Serratia*, *Acinetobacter*, anaerobes |

> **Remember:** in low-resource settings, Gram-negative organisms (Klebsiella, E. coli) and *Staph aureus* dominate and are often resistant to first-line antibiotics. Know your unit's antibiogram.

## 3. Pathophysiology: why neonates are vulnerable

### 3.1 Cellular immunity

- **Neutrophils (PMNs)** are deficient in chemotaxis and killing.
  - Reduced adherence to endothelium, so poor margination and migration into tissues.
  - Fail to degranulate in response to chemotactic factors.
  - Less deformable, so cannot move through the extracellular matrix to reach the infection.
  - Phagocytosis and killing are even weaker in the sick infant.
  - **Neutrophil reserves are easily depleted** because bone marrow response is weak (hence neutropenia in severe sepsis).
- **Monocytes** are at adult levels but macrophage chemotaxis is impaired; macrophage numbers are reduced in lungs, liver and spleen; their bactericidal activity, antigen presentation and cytokine production are not fully competent.
- Memory function after primary infection is delayed; neonates lack **memory T cells**, which build up with antigen exposure.
- **NK cells** are few and immature, producing far less **interferon gamma**.

### 3.2 Humoral immunity

- Preformed IgG comes by **placental transfer**, mostly in late gestation (so preterm babies have low levels).
- The neonate can make antibody, but initial responses are small. **IgM is low** at birth.
- **IgA** comes from breast milk; the neonate does not secrete its own IgA until **2 to 5 weeks**.
- Response to **bacterial polysaccharide antigens** is poor for the first 2 years.
- **Complement** levels vary widely; the terminal cytotoxic components, needed to kill gram-negative bacteria, are deficient.

### 3.3 Barrier function

- Skin and mucous membranes are present but functionally weak and break down easily in premature infants.
- Invasive procedures (lines, catheters, intubation) breach barriers in ill or premature babies.

## 4. Risk factors

### 4.1 Early-onset sepsis

- **Maternal**: GBS colonisation (especially with inadequate prophylaxis), **PROM**, preterm and prolonged rupture of membranes, **chorioamnionitis**, maternal UTI, maternal fever above 38 C, poor prenatal care, poor nutrition, low socio-economic status, recurrent abortion, substance abuse.
- **Baby**: prematurity, low birthweight, **low Apgar (below 6 at 1 or 5 minutes)**, difficult delivery, birth asphyxia, meconium staining, congenital anomalies.
- The slides also list "black mother" as a risk factor; this reflects US data on disparities and is not a biological risk in itself. Poverty and access to care explain much of it.

### 4.2 Late-onset sepsis

- Prematurity.
- **Central venous catheter** (more than 10 days), urinary catheterisation.
- Chronic mechanical ventilation, nasal cannula or CPAP.
- Failure to advance enteral feeding.
- Use of **H2 blockers or proton pump inhibitors**.
- Gastrointestinal pathology.

## 5. Clinical features

Signs are **non-specific** and depend on the organism and the body's response.

| System | Features |
|---|---|
| General | Temperature instability (fever or hypothermia), poor feeding, lethargy |
| Respiratory | Cough, grunting, retractions, nasal flaring, tachypnoea or irregular breathing, crackles, reduced breath sounds, cyanosis, hypoxaemia, pulmonary hypertension; **central apnoea** or periodic breathing |
| Cardiovascular | Reduced cardiac output, progressive **bradycardia**, systemic hypotension, poor perfusion |
| Metabolic | Hypoglycaemia or hyperglycaemia, metabolic acidosis, **jaundice** |
| Neurological (meningitis) | Stupor with or without irritability, coma, **seizures**, **bulging anterior fontanelle**, extensor rigidity, focal cerebral signs, cranial nerve signs, nuchal rigidity (a late sign in neonates) |

> **Exam tip:** "the baby is just not right" (poor feeding, lethargy, temperature instability) is the commonest presentation. A low threshold for investigating and treating is essential; neonatal meningitis often has no neck stiffness.

## 6. Investigations

- **Full blood count with differential**: neutropenia, raised immature-to-total neutrophil ratio, thrombocytopenia.
- **CRP** and other infection markers (procalcitonin where available); serial CRP helps decide when to stop antibiotics.
- **Cultures of blood, urine and CSF** remain the gold standard. Obtain before antibiotics; do a lumbar puncture when the baby is stable.
- **Chest X-ray** if respiratory signs.
- **Cranial ultrasound** (for meningitis complications such as ventriculitis, hydrocephalus, abscess).
- Glucose, gases, electrolytes and clotting (looking for DIC) as clinically indicated.

## 7. Management

### 7.1 Supportive therapy

- **Temperature control** (warm environment, kangaroo care).
- **IV fluids** and **NGT feeds**; continue **breastfeeding** when possible.
- **Blood products** as needed (e.g. platelets, plasma for DIC).
- Respiratory support, correct hypoglycaemia and acidosis, treat seizures.

### 7.2 Definitive therapy

- **Intravenous antibiotics according to paediatric protocols.** In practice, start broad-spectrum cover as soon as cultures are taken, and narrow when the organism is known. A common empirical regimen is **ampicillin (or benzylpenicillin) plus gentamicin**; use a third-generation cephalosporin for meningitis or suspected resistant Gram-negatives. Follow Kenya MoH/local guidance.
- Duration: about 7 to 10 days for bacteraemia and 14 to 21 days for meningitis, depending on the organism (standard references).

## 8. Prognosis

- With **early diagnosis and treatment**, most term infants have no long-term health problems.
- If early signs or risk factors are missed, **mortality increases**.
- **Residual neurological damage in 15% to 30%** of neonates with septic meningitis (hydrocephalus, deafness, cerebral palsy, epilepsy, developmental delay).

## 9. Prevention

- Treat **GBS bacteriuria** during pregnancy when diagnosed and again during labour (intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis).
- Adequate **antenatal care**, **clean and safe delivery**, good postnatal care including **cord care**.
- **Early initiation of breastfeeding**.
- Hand hygiene, minimising invasive lines, and judicious acid-suppressant use reduce late-onset sepsis.

## Key points to revise

1. Neonatal sepsis: infant under 1 month, ill, with positive blood culture; clinical sepsis if culture-negative.
2. Early-onset is day 0 to 3 (85% within 24 hours), maternal source, pneumonia common; late-onset is day 4 to 90, environmental, meningitis common.
3. Early organisms: GBS, E. coli, Listeria; late: coagulase-negative staphylococci, S. aureus, Klebsiella, Pseudomonas, Candida.
4. Neonates have defective neutrophil function, low IgM and IgA, deficient terminal complement and weak barriers.
5. Risk factors: early-onset (PROM, chorioamnionitis, maternal fever, prematurity), late-onset (central lines, ventilation, acid suppressants).
6. Signs are non-specific; investigate with FBC, CRP, and blood, urine and CSF cultures.
7. Give supportive care and IV antibiotics per protocol; residual neurological damage follows 15% to 30% of septic meningitis.
8. Prevent with maternal GBS treatment, clean delivery, cord care and early breastfeeding.
