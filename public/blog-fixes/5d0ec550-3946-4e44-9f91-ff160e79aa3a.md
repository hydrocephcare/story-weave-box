# Abdominal Pain and Irritable Bowel Syndrome

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Gastroenterology**

> **In one line:** abdominal pain is a common complaint that is usually benign but can signal an acute surgical emergency (obstruction, perforation, peritonitis). The task is to decide whether it is acute or chronic, visceral or parietal, surgical or medical, and, if chronic, whether it is functional (most often **irritable bowel syndrome**) or organic, using the history, the alarm features and focused tests.

## What you must know

- Abdominal pain is reported by about 75% of healthy adolescents and about half of all adults.
- **Acute** pain is under 12 weeks and progressively worsening; **chronic** is over 12 weeks and unchanged. Pain in a sick or unstable patient is managed as **acute**, whatever its duration.
- **Obstruction and peritonitis (perforation)** are the two syndromes needing urgent surgical referral.
- **Visceral** pain is vague and midline; **parietal** (peritoneal) pain is sharp and localised, with guarding, rigidity and rebound.
- In surgical causes, **pain comes before vomiting**; in gastroenteritis, vomiting comes first.
- Every adult with acute abdominal pain needs an **abdominal X-ray (erect and supine)**: **air under the diaphragm** means perforation, **air-fluid levels** mean small bowel obstruction. In a woman of childbearing age, do a **beta-hCG**.
- The commonest cause of chronic abdominal pain is **IBS**, a functional diagnosis made only after excluding alarm features.
- **Rome III:** pain for at least 3 days a month over 3 months, improved by defecation and linked to change in stool frequency and form.

## 1. Classification of abdominal pain

### By duration

| | Acute | Chronic |
|---|---|---|
| Duration | under 12 weeks | over 12 weeks |
| Course | progressively worse | unchanged for months |

**Acute abdominal pain (the "surgical abdomen")** is a condition with a rapidly worsening prognosis without surgery. Pain is usually severe and may come with unstable vital signs, fever and dehydration. It accounts for about 50% of urgent admissions to general surgical units.

### By origin

| | Visceral | Parietal (somatic) |
|---|---|---|
| Source | hollow or solid organs; gut is insensitive to burning and cutting but sensitive to **distension, contraction, twisting and stretching** | parietal peritoneum (somatic nerves), involved by inflammation, infection or neoplasia |
| Character | vague discomfort to excruciating; poorly localised; unpaired structures give **midline** pain (bilateral innervation) | sharp, **well localised** and lateralised |
| Signs | non-specific | localised tenderness, then **guarding, rigidity, rebound** as peritonitis develops |

- **Typical progression** (e.g. appendicitis): visceral pain first, then parietal pain as localised peritonitis develops.
- **Visceral localisation:** epigastric (stomach, duodenum, biliary tract); periumbilical (small bowel, appendix, caecum); suprapubic (colon, sigmoid, genitourinary tract).

**Referred pain** (a symptom, not a sign; based on embryological development):

| Source | Referred to |
|---|---|
| Ureteric obstruction | testis |
| Subdiaphragmatic irritation | ipsilateral shoulder |
| Gynaecological disease | back or proximal lower limb |
| Biliary disease | right infrascapular region |
| Myocardial infarction | epigastrium, neck, jaw |

**Psychogenic pain:** cultural, emotional and psychosocial factors affect the pain experience; if no organic cause is found, depression or somatisation disorder may be responsible.

### By location (differential diagnosis)

| Region | Causes |
|---|---|
| **Epigastric** | myocardial infarction, peptic ulcer, acute cholecystitis, perforated oesophagus |
| **Right upper quadrant** | acute cholecystitis, duodenal ulcer, hepatitis, congestive hepatomegaly, pyelonephritis, appendicitis (retrocaecal/high), right pneumonia |
| **Left upper quadrant** | ruptured spleen, gastric ulcer, aortic aneurysm, perforated colon, pyelonephritis, left pneumonia |
| **Right lower quadrant** | appendicitis, salpingitis, tubo-ovarian abscess, ruptured ectopic pregnancy, renal or ureteric stone, irreducible hernia, mesenteric adenitis, Meckel's diverticulitis, Crohn's disease, perforated caecum, psoas abscess |
| **Left lower quadrant** | sigmoid diverticulitis, salpingitis, tubo-ovarian abscess, ruptured ectopic, irreducible hernia, perforated colon, Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis, renal or ureteric stone |

## 2. The acute abdomen: three mechanisms

| Mechanism | Pain pattern |
|---|---|
| **Inflammation** | develops gradually over hours; initially diffuse, then localised once the parietal peritoneum is involved; movement worsens it; guarding and rigidity |
| **Perforation** | **abrupt**, severe onset, leading to generalised peritonitis |
| **Obstruction** | **colicky** pain with spasms; patient writhes and doubles up; colicky pain that does **not** disappear between spasms suggests complicating inflammation (strangulation) |

## 3. History and examination

### History: key questions

- Type, location and radiation, character and severity.
- Onset (sudden?) and duration.
- Exacerbating and relieving factors.
- Associated symptoms (fever, vomiting).
- Medications, especially **aspirin and NSAIDs**.

### High-yield questions (surgical versus medical)

| Question | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Which came first, pain or vomiting? | pain first suggests a **surgical** disease (e.g. small bowel obstruction); vomiting first, then pain and fever suggests **gastroenteritis** |
| How long? | under 48 hours is more likely surgical |
| Constant or intermittent? | constant is more likely serious |
| History of cancer, diverticulosis, gallbladder disease, IBD? | a first episode is more likely something serious |
| Vascular history, hypertension, heart disease, atrial fibrillation? | consider **mesenteric ischaemia or aortic aneurysm** |

> **Remember:** advanced age raises the risk of a serious cause. Pain that wakes the patient from sleep is always significant.

### Examination

- **Vital signs:** abnormalities suggest an acute abdomen. Orthostatic changes are less reliable in diabetics, the elderly and those on beta-blockers. A **pulse rise of 30** or pre-syncope on standing is highly sensitive for loss of **1 L of blood or 3 L of fluid**; BP changes are less reliable. The patient must stand at least one minute before measurement.
- **Guarding:** voluntary (reduced if the patient flexes the knees) versus **involuntary** (reflex muscle spasm).
- **Rigidity:** board-like.
- **Rebound tenderness:** suggests peritoneal irritation (can be present in 25% of normal people).

## 4. Investigations in acute pain

| Test | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **CBC** | leucocytosis suggests infection |
| **LFTs** | raised bilirubin suggests biliary disease |
| **Renal function, urinalysis** | exclude UTI and renal causes |
| **Plain abdominal X-ray (erect and supine)** | **air under the diaphragm** = perforated viscus; **air-fluid levels** = small bowel obstruction |
| **Ultrasound** | best for gallstones; cholecystitis shows a thick gallbladder wall and stones |
| **CT** | not for everyone; use when a mass, renal stone, abscess or appendicitis needs confirmation |
| **Beta-hCG** | in every woman of childbearing age (exclude pregnancy and ectopic) |

- If the patient is unstable, **resuscitate first**, then image.
- Investigations for chronic pain depend on the clinical clues:
  - **Endoscopy and ultrasound** for epigastric pain, dyspepsia and gallbladder-type symptoms.
  - **Colonoscopy** for altered bowel habit, rectal bleeding or obstruction suggesting colonic disease.
  - **Angiography** when pain is provoked by food in a patient with widespread atherosclerosis (mesenteric ischaemia).
  - Simple tests (bloods, faecal calprotectin, sigmoidoscopy) suffice if there is no rectal bleeding, weight loss or abnormal findings.
  - **Ultrasound, CT and faecal elastase** for upper abdominal pain radiating to the back (pancreatitis).
  - Alcohol misuse, weight loss and diarrhoea suggest chronic pancreatitis or pancreatic cancer.
  - Recurrent loin-to-flank pain with urinary symptoms: look for renal or ureteric stones (X-ray, ultrasound, IVU).
  - Psychiatric history, repeated negative investigations and vague symptoms suggest a psychological origin.

## 5. General management of the acute abdomen

- Perforations are closed, inflammatory conditions treated with antibiotics or resection, and obstructions relieved. **The presence or absence of peritonitis** is the most important factor for urgency and need for surgery.

| Condition | Management |
|---|---|
| **Acute appendicitis** | early surgery (conventional right iliac fossa incision or laparoscopic) because of perforation risk and recurrence, even though non-operative treatment can work |
| **Acute cholecystitis** | early laparoscopic cholecystectomy preferred (high recurrence risk, low surgical morbidity) |
| **Acute diverticulitis** | conservative treatment; if perforated, resect; primary anastomosis is preferable to Hartmann's (oversew rectal stump, end colostomy) depending on contamination and patient state |
| **Small bowel obstruction** | early surgery if the cause is clear (e.g. external hernia); if adhesions, operate only if no resolution within **48 hours** or **strangulation** signs (colicky pain becomes constant, peritonitis, tachycardia, fever, leucocytosis) |
| **Large bowel obstruction** | pseudo-obstruction treated non-operatively (colonoscopic decompression may help); mechanical obstruction needs resection, usually with primary anastomosis; distinguish by **water-soluble contrast enema** |
| **Perforated peptic ulcer** | surgical closure is standard; selected patients without generalised peritonitis whose perforation has sealed (confirmed by water-soluble contrast meal) can be managed non-operatively; **aggressive resuscitation first** |

## 6. Worked case 1: appendicitis

A 24-year-old man with one day of generalised pain that moved to the right lower abdomen and groin, two episodes of vomiting, no diarrhoea, fever or dysuria. T 37.8, HR 95, RLQ tenderness with mild guarding, hypoactive bowel sounds.

- **Acute**, visceral (diffuse) then parietal (localised RLQ) pain.
- Absence of diarrhoea and urinary symptoms argues against gastroenteritis and UTI.
- Most likely **acute appendicitis**: refer to surgery; CT may confirm.

## 7. Peptic ulcer disease

**Definition:** PUD covers duodenal and gastric ulcers.

**Worked case 2:** a 72-year-old on aspirin and clopidogrel with several days of dull upper pain relieved by food, now worse after eating, diffuse pain, T 38.9, BP 90/45, RR 22, distended and diffusely tender with rebound and guarding. This is a **perforated duodenal ulcer**; erect X-ray shows **air under the diaphragm**.

### Causes

| Rank | Cause |
|---|---|
| Commonest | ***Helicobacter pylori*** |
| 2nd | **NSAIDs** (inhibit prostaglandins, which stimulate protective mucus) |
| 3rd | acid hypersecretion, e.g. **Zollinger-Ellison syndrome** |
| Others | smoking (ulcers twice as likely), alcohol and coffee (may worsen symptoms, causation unproven), stress and personality type (unproven), diet |

- **Stress ulcers** (burns, head injury): intense vasoconstriction of the gastric mucosal vessels leads to sloughing and ulceration.

### Clinical features

- Sharp, dull or aching, gnawing or burning **epigastric pain**, or a "hungry" feeling; nocturnal symptoms and the effect of food are variable; relieved by milk, food or antacids; epigastric tenderness.
- **Duodenal ulcer:** pain **relieved by food**, with weight gain.
- **Gastric ulcer:** pain **made worse by food**, with weight loss.
- Other: nausea, vomiting, early satiety, weight loss.

### Complications

| Complication | Warning sign |
|---|---|
| **Perforation** | sudden, severe, diffuse abdominal pain |
| **Pyloric outlet obstruction** | **vomiting** is the cardinal feature |
| **Haemorrhage** | nausea, haematemesis, melaena, dizziness |

### Diagnosis

- **Endoscopy** is the most accurate test. In gastric ulcers a **biopsy is essential** to exclude malignancy; duodenal ulcers do not need biopsy.
- Barium swallow (double-contrast preferred) is less reliable.
- ***H. pylori*:** biopsy (gold standard), **urea breath test** (most convenient; sensitivity and specificity above 95%), serology (lower specificity).
- **Serum gastrin** if Zollinger-Ellison syndrome is suspected.

### Treatment

- **Supportive:** stop aspirin/NSAIDs; restrict alcohol; stop smoking; reduce stress; avoid eating before bed; less coffee.
- **Acid suppression:** **PPIs** (most effective), H2 blockers (accelerate healing), antacids (symptom relief).
- **Eradicate *H. pylori*** (triple or quadruple therapy: PPI, bismuth, two antibiotics) if the test is positive; also start acid suppression.
- **Cytoprotection:** sucralfate (facilitates healing); misoprostol (commonly causes GI upset).
- Active NSAID-induced ulcer: stop the NSAID.
- **Surgery:** rarely needed; for complications (bleeding, perforation, gastric outlet obstruction).

## 8. Acute pancreatitis

**Worked case 3:** a 46-year-old man with alcohol abuse, 3 days of severe upper abdominal pain and vomiting, HR 115, BP 98/65, mildly distended, epigastric tenderness with voluntary guarding, no rebound. No signs of perforation, so the most likely diagnosis is **acute pancreatitis**.

**Definition:** inflammation from prematurely activated pancreatic enzymes causing autodigestion. Most cases are mild to moderate, but up to **25% are severe** (necrotising).

### Causes

1. **Gallstones (40%)**: stone blocks the ampulla of Vater; always rule it out, even with an alcohol history.
2. **Alcohol (40%).**
3. **Post-ERCP** (clinical pancreatitis in 2 to 8%; asymptomatic amylase rise is common).
4. Viral infections (mumps, Coxsackie B).
5. Drugs: sulfonamides, thiazides, NSAIDs, furosemide, oestrogens, HIV drugs.
6. Postoperative complications (high mortality).
7. Scorpion bites.
8. Pancreas divisum.
9. Pancreatic cancer.
10. Hypertriglyceridaemia, hypercalcaemia.
11. Uraemia.
12. **Blunt abdominal trauma** (commonest cause in children).

### Clinical features

- **Epigastric pain**, steady, dull and severe, radiating to the back in about 50%, worse when supine and after meals; nausea, vomiting, anorexia.
- Signs: low-grade fever, tachycardia, hypotension, leucocytosis, epigastric tenderness, distension, reduced bowel sounds (ileus).
- **Haemorrhagic pancreatitis**: **Grey Turner's sign** (flank ecchymosis), **Cullen's sign** (periumbilical ecchymosis), **Fox's sign** (inguinal ligament ecchymosis).

### Diagnosis

- **Serum amylase**: commonest first test, but non-specific and may be normal; over **5 times** the upper limit is highly specific.
- **Serum lipase**: more specific.
- LFTs (to find a gallstone cause); glucose, calcium, haematocrit, urea, blood gases, LDH, AST, WBC for **Ranson's criteria** (prognosis).
- Abdominal X-ray: limited; excludes perforation (free air); calcification suggests chronic pancreatitis.
- **Ultrasound**: finds gallstones, follows pseudocysts or abscesses.
- **CT**: most accurate test; indicated in severe disease and for complications.
- **ERCP**: severe gallstone pancreatitis with biliary obstruction, or recurrent disease of unknown cause.

### Complications

| Complication | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Pancreatic necrosis** | sterile (half resolve spontaneously, monitor in ICU) or infected (high mortality, multi-organ failure in 50%; débridement and antibiotics). Only **CT-guided aspiration** with Gram stain/culture separates them |
| **Pseudocyst** | encapsulated fluid collection 2 to 3 weeks after the attack, **no epithelial lining**; risks: rupture, infection, gastric outlet obstruction, fistula, haemorrhage, ascites. CT is best. Under 5 cm: observe; over 5 cm: drain (percutaneous or surgical) |
| **Haemorrhagic pancreatitis** | Cullen, Grey Turner and Fox signs; contrast CT |
| **ARDS** | life-threatening |
| **Pancreatic ascites or pleural effusion** | |
| **Ascending cholangitis** | stone in the ampulla |
| **Pancreatic abscess** | rare; develops over 4 to 6 weeks; less dangerous than infected necrosis |

### Treatment

- **Mild:** bowel rest (nil by mouth), IV fluids and electrolyte correction, analgesia, nasogastric tube if severe vomiting or ileus.
- **Severe:** ICU; **early enteral feeding within 72 hours** via nasojejunal tube; add parenteral nutrition if not resolved in a few days; consider **prophylactic antibiotics (imipenem) if more than 30% of the pancreas is necrotic**.

## 9. Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)

**Worked case 4:** a 23-year-old woman with 2 years of intermittent left lower quadrant pain relieved by defecation, constipation and bloating.

**Definition:** an idiopathic, chronic continuous or remittent **functional** disorder with intrinsic bowel motility dysfunction and no organic disease or specific cause. It is predominantly a **pain syndrome** often relieved by defecation.

### Epidemiology

- Affects 10 to 15% of adults (up to 20% in some estimates); about **70% are women** (the slide question gives three times commoner); usually **young**.
- About 50% of gastroenterology referrals; only about 25% seek care.
- Anxiety and depression are the commonest associated disorders; psychiatric symptoms often precede bowel symptoms; stress and luminal irritants worsen it.
- Benign with a favourable long-term prognosis; no cure but effective control.

### Aetiology

Incompletely understood; **biopsychosocial** factors, with luminal factors (diet, gut microbiota). Altered visceral sensitivity and motility.

### Clinical features

- **Recurrent cramping abdominal pain**, location varies but the **sigmoid colon (LLQ)** is common; **relieved by defecation**; **less at night**.
- Altered stool frequency and consistency: diarrhoea, constipation or alternating (about 20% constipation only; most have diarrhoea or alternating).
- Abnormal stool passage (straining, urgency, incomplete evacuation); mucus; bloating or distension.
- Long history with exacerbations triggered by life events or stress; symptoms in other systems (fatigue, headache, shortness of breath, dizziness); coexisting anxiety and depression; symptoms worsened by eating; patient's conviction of a "popular" cause (allergy, *H. pylori*).
- Symptoms must be present for **at least 3 months**.

**Supporting symptoms:** stool frequency over 3 per day or under 3 per week; hard/lumpy or loose/watery stool; straining, urgency or incomplete evacuation; mucus; bloating.

### Diagnosis

- **Clinical diagnosis, but also a diagnosis of exclusion.** All laboratory tests are normal and sigmoidoscopy shows no mucosal lesions.
- **Rome III criteria:** recurrent abdominal pain or discomfort on at least **3 days per month** in the last 3 months, with two or more of: (1) improved by defecation, (2) onset linked to a change in stool frequency, (3) onset linked to a change in stool form.
- **Alarm features** that point elsewhere: rectal bleeding, weight loss, family history of cancer, **onset after 45 years**, fever, anaemia, progressive deterioration, steatorrhoea, dehydration.
- Limited tests: CBC, renal panel, faecal occult blood, stool for ova and parasites, ESR, sometimes flexible sigmoidoscopy.
- First exclude **lactose intolerance, IBD, coeliac disease, carcinoid, giardiasis, anatomical bowel defects**. Endoscopy only to exclude other diseases.

### Management

- Often no specific treatment; reassurance; treat dominant symptoms.
- **Diet and fibre**; avoid dairy (if lactose intolerant) and excess caffeine.
- **Diarrhoea:** loperamide, diphenoxylate.
- **Constipation:** docusate (Colace), psyllium, osmotic laxatives.
- **Antispasmodics:** hyoscyamine, dicyclomine.
- **Tricyclic antidepressants** for resistant cases.
- Tegaserod (5-HT4 agonist) improved pain, bloating and constipation in women in short-term studies.

> **Check:** tegaserod and cisapride were withdrawn or restricted in many countries because of cardiac safety concerns. In practice use fibre, antispasmodics, loperamide, laxatives and low-dose tricyclics.

### Physician attitude

- Acknowledge the patient's distress, build trust, allow enough time, and explain that this is a chronic "sensitive gut" that reacts to food and mood, not a serious disease.

## 10. Differential of chronic abdominal pain

The great majority are functional (IBS). Organic causes include: **IBD, peptic ulcer disease** (which may present acutely or chronically), gastric, small bowel or large bowel cancer, **pancreatic cancer**, coeliac disease, reflux disease, functional dyspepsia.

**Features suggesting organic illness (alarm features):** unstable vital signs, weight loss, fever, dehydration, electrolyte abnormalities, signs of GI bleeding, anaemia, signs of malnutrition.

## 11. Self-test cases

1. **Dull epigastric pain, worse in the morning, relieved by meals, no weight loss:** duodenal ulcer (four times commoner than gastric ulcer; gastric ulcer worsens with food; reflux gives retrosternal burning on lying flat; carcinoma gives dramatic weight loss).
2. **80-year-old with epigastric pain and melaena, pulse 99, BP 98/69:** upper GI bleed, so the next step is **IV cannulation (two large-bore cannulae) and fluids**, with blood for CBC, U&Es, clotting, group and save; then keep nil by mouth and arrange endoscopy. The Rockall score assesses risk of rebleeding and death.
3. **32-year-old with bloating relieved by defecation, alternating bowel habit, no alarm features, normal CBC:** IBS; give fibre and antispasmodics and review in 2 months; no colonoscopy or small bowel studies. Consider lactose intolerance; investigate further if over 40 with new symptoms, weight loss or a family history of colon cancer.

## Key points to revise

1. Acute pain (under 12 weeks) with obstruction or peritonitis needs urgent surgical review; treat the unstable patient as acute.
2. Visceral pain is midline and vague; parietal pain is localised with guarding, rigidity and rebound.
3. Pain before vomiting suggests surgery; vomiting before pain suggests gastroenteritis.
4. Erect abdominal X-ray: air under the diaphragm means perforation; air-fluid levels mean obstruction; do beta-hCG in women of reproductive age.
5. Peptic ulcer: *H. pylori* then NSAIDs; duodenal ulcer pain eases with food, gastric ulcer worsens; endoscopy with biopsy for gastric ulcers; PPI plus eradication.
6. Acute pancreatitis: gallstones and alcohol (40% each); lipase and amylase, CT for complications; Cullen, Grey Turner and Fox signs; pseudocyst forms at 2 to 3 weeks.
7. IBS is the commonest cause of chronic abdominal pain; Rome III; diagnosis of exclusion; alarm features include onset after 45, weight loss, bleeding, anaemia and family history of cancer.
8. IBS management is reassurance, fibre and diet, antispasmodics, loperamide or laxatives, and tricyclics in resistant cases.
