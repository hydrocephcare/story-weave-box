# Encephalopathies in Children, with Cerebral Palsy

**Year 4 · Paediatrics · Paediatric neurology**

> **In one line:** encephalopathy is a generalised disorder of cerebral function that may be acute or chronic, progressive or static, with infectious, toxic, metabolic, genetic, ischaemic and other causes. **Cerebral palsy** is the commonest static encephalopathy of childhood: a non-progressive injury to the developing brain that produces a permanent disorder of movement and posture, managed by a multidisciplinary team.

## What you must know

- **Encephalopathy** = generalised brain dysfunction; causes include infection, toxins (carbon monoxide, drugs, lead), metabolic, genetic, ischaemic.
- **Cerebral palsy (CP):** permanent disorder of movement and posture from a **non-progressive** disturbance of the developing fetal or infant brain; incidence **3.6 per 1,000**, male : female **1.4 : 1**.
- In **80%** of CP there are antenatal factors; **fewer than 10%** have evidence of intrapartum asphyxia (higher in some low-resource regions).
- Preterm CP comes from **periventricular leukomalacia (PVL)** and intracerebral haemorrhage; PVL causes **spastic diplegia**.
- **Dyskinetic (athetoid) CP**: basal ganglia and thalamus; birth asphyxia or kernicterus (globus pallidus).
- **MRI** is more sensitive than CT; CT only to show calcification of congenital infection.
- Therapeutic cooling reduces CP after HIE; magnesium sulphate before 32 weeks may reduce it.
- Other encephalopathies: mitochondrial, HIV, lead, burn, hypertensive (PRES), radiation, acute necrotising.

## 1. Cerebral palsy: definition

- A group of **permanent disorders of movement and posture** causing activity limitation, attributed to **non-progressive disturbances in the developing fetal or infant brain**.
- Motor disorders are often accompanied by disturbances of **sensation, perception, cognition, communication and behaviour**, plus **epilepsy** and secondary musculoskeletal problems.
- Causes are developmental, genetic, metabolic, ischaemic, infectious and other acquired aetiologies producing a common group of neurological phenotypes.
- CP is historically a "static" encephalopathy, but movement disorders and orthopaedic complications (**scoliosis, hip dislocation**) can change or progress over time.
- Many children and adults with CP function at a high educational and vocational level with **no cognitive dysfunction**.

## 2. Epidemiology and aetiology

- CP is the **commonest and costliest chronic motor disability** starting in childhood: incidence **3.6 per 1,000**, M : F **1.4 : 1**.
- The Collaborative Perinatal Project (about 45,000 children followed from in utero to 7 years) found most children with CP were born **at term** after uncomplicated labour.
- **80%** had features pointing to **antenatal** abnormal brain development; many had congenital anomalies outside the CNS. **Under 10%** had evidence of intrapartum asphyxia.
- The European CP Study (over 400 children): more than half were born at term; under 20% had clinical or imaging indicators of intrapartum factors. Intrapartum contribution is higher in some low-resource regions.

### Risk factors

| Factor | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Maternal infection** | Chorioamnionitis, placental membrane or cord inflammation, foul-smelling liquor, maternal sepsis, temperature above 38 °C in labour, UTI: raised CP risk even at normal birth weight. In the European study 39.5% of mothers reported infection in pregnancy, 19% had UTI, 11.5% took antibiotics |
| **Inflammatory cytokines** | Raised in heel-prick blood at birth in children later diagnosed with CP; an **interleukin-6 gene polymorphism** is linked to more CP in term infants |
| **Prematurity** | Survival of very premature infants under 1,000 g raises prevalence; about **15 per 100** of these develop CP. Main lesions: **intracerebral haemorrhage** (declining) and **PVL** (still a major problem) |
| **Multiple pregnancy** | 12% of cases in the European study versus 1.5% of the population; risk in twins **5 to 8 times**, triplets **20 to 47 times**; in-utero death of a co-twin is about 8 times the risk of twins who both survive and about 60 times a singleton |
| **Infertility treatment** | Linked mainly through multiple pregnancy (24% of multiple pregnancies were after infertility treatment versus 3.4% of singletons) |
| **Sex and birth weight** | More common and more severe in boys, enhanced at extremes of weight: males with IUGR and birth weight under the 3rd centile are **16 times** more likely to have CP; above the 97th centile **4 times** |
| **Perinatal** | Hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy, kernicterus, neonatal sepsis, hypoglycaemia |
| **Thrombophilia** | Intrauterine or perinatal thromboembolic stroke (anticardiolipin antibodies, factor V Leiden) |

> **Remember:** PVL reflects the vulnerability of **immature oligodendroglia** in premature infants to oxidative stress from ischaemia or infection/inflammation. Periventricular white matter loss, cystic change, ventricular dilatation and a thin corpus callosum on MRI at 40 weeks corrected age predict later CP.

## 3. Clinical features: motor syndromes

CP is classified **physiologically** (the main motor abnormality) and **topographically** (limbs involved), and is associated with intellectual impairment, epilepsy, and visual, hearing, speech, cognitive and behavioural problems.

### Spastic hemiplegia

- Reduced spontaneous movement of the affected side; **early hand preference**.
- Arm usually more involved than leg; hand manipulation difficult by 1 year.
- Walking delayed to **18 to 24 months** with a **circumductive gait**; growth arrest of the hand and thumbnail (especially with contralateral parietal abnormality).
- **Spasticity** = tone that increases with speed of passive stretch, greatest in antigravity muscles; causes **equinovarus** foot and **toe-walking** (gastrocnemius); the arm flexes on running.
- Ankle clonus, Babinski sign, brisk reflexes, weak hand and foot dorsiflexors, poor selective motor control.
- About **one third** have seizures (usually in the first year or two); about **25%** have cognitive abnormalities.
- Causes (European study): **34%** white matter injury in utero, **27%** focal lesion (stroke); others malformations (CMV, lissencephaly, polymicrogyria, schizencephaly, cortical dysplasia). Test for inherited thrombophilia (and evaluate the mother) where stroke is suspected.

### Spastic diplegia

- Bilateral spasticity, **legs worse than arms**; linked to white matter damage between **20 and 34 weeks' gestation**. About 15% follow in-utero lesions in term infants.
- First sign is often at crawling: arms move normally but the legs drag like a rudder (**commando crawl**).
- Severe hip adduction makes nappies hard to apply; paraspinal involvement may prevent sitting.
- Brisk reflexes, ankle clonus, bilateral Babinski, **scissoring** when suspended by the axillae, delayed walking on tiptoe with equinovarus feet.
- Severe cases: disuse atrophy and impaired leg growth with a normally developed upper trunk.
- Intellect is usually good, seizures are uncommon; learning and visual deficits occur from damage to sensory as well as motor white matter pathways.
- **MRI shows PVL in over 70%**: scarring and shrinkage of periventricular white matter with compensatory ventricular enlargement. Diffusion tensor imaging shows thalamocortical sensory pathways are often as injured as corticospinal motor pathways.

### Spastic quadriplegia

- **Most severe form**: all limbs affected, high association with intellectual disability and seizures.
- **Swallowing difficulty** (supranuclear bulbar palsy) causing aspiration pneumonia and growth failure.
- Lesions: severe PVL and **multicystic encephalomalacia**.
- Increased tone in all limbs, reduced movement, brisk reflexes, extensor plantars; flexion contractures of knees, elbows and wrists by late childhood; speech and visual problems common. Children with athetosis are classed as **mixed CP**.

### Athetoid (dyskinetic, extrapyramidal) CP

- **15 to 20%** of CP.
- Infants are **hypotonic** with poor head control and head lag, then develop variable rigidity and **dystonia** over years (muscles rigid throughout range with involuntary flexor and extensor contractions giving fixed postures).
- **Upper limbs** more affected than lower (unlike diplegia).
- Feeding difficulty, tongue thrust, drooling, slurred or absent speech; usually **no upper motor neuron signs**, seizures uncommon, **intellect often preserved**.
- Type most associated with **birth asphyxia**. 76% have basal ganglia and thalamic lesions; near-total acute asphyxia gives symmetric lesions in the **posterior putamen and ventrolateral thalamus** (status marmoratus). **Kernicterus** gives bilateral **globus pallidus** lesions. Metabolic causes: mitochondrial disorders, glutaric aciduria.

| Type | Main features | Typical lesion or cause |
|---|---|---|
| Spastic hemiplegia | One side, arm worse, circumductive gait | White matter injury, stroke, malformation |
| Spastic diplegia | Legs worse than arms, scissoring | PVL in preterm |
| Spastic quadriplegia | All limbs, bulbar signs, ID, seizures | Severe PVL, cystic encephalomalacia |
| Athetoid / dyskinetic | Dystonia, choreoathetosis, intact intellect | Asphyxia, kernicterus, metabolic |

> **Exam tip:** DOPA-responsive dystonia (**Segawa disease**) mimics dyskinetic CP with a **normal MRI** and diurnal variation (worse later in the day). Test with low-dose L-dopa or CSF neurotransmitters.

### Comorbidities (dyskinetic series)
Pain 75%, cognitive disability 50%, hip displacement 30%, seizures 25%, behaviour disorders 25%, sleep disturbance 20%, visual impairment 19%, hearing impairment 4%.

## 4. Diagnosis

- A thorough history and examination to **exclude a progressive CNS disorder**: degenerative disease, metabolic disease, spinal cord tumour, muscular dystrophy.
- Consider **skull base anomalies** or cervical cord disease when arms and cranial nerves are little affected.
- **MRI brain** to define structural lesions and malformations; **MRI spinal cord** if cord pathology is possible. CT only for calcifications of congenital infection.
- **Hearing and visual function** tests.
- **Genetic evaluation** (chromosomes) for malformations; **metabolic tests** (amino acids, organic acids, MR spectroscopy) for metabolic suspicion.
- Thrombophilia tests if in-utero or neonatal stroke suspected.

### Mimics (differential)
Hereditary spastic diplegias, monoamine transmitter disorders, arginase deficiency (urea cycle disorder causing spastic diplegia), sulfite oxidase or molybdenum cofactor deficiency (can look like perinatal asphyxia), and treatable inborn errors (amino acids, creatine, fatty acid oxidation, lysosomal, mitochondrial, organic acids, vitamin cofactors).

## 5. Management of cerebral palsy

### Prevention
- **Magnesium sulphate IV** to mothers in imminent preterm labour before 32 weeks reduced CP at 2 years in controlled trials; one follow-up study showed no benefit at school age.
- **Therapeutic cooling** of term infants with HIE to **33.3 °C for 3 days, starting within 6 hours** of birth, reduces dyskinetic or spastic quadriplegic CP.

### Multidisciplinary care
Neurodevelopmental paediatrician, paediatric neurologist, rehabilitation specialist, physiotherapist, occupational therapist, speech pathologist, social worker, educator, developmental psychologist, ophthalmologist.

- **Parents** taught to feed, carry, dress, bathe and play in ways that limit abnormal tone, and to supervise exercises to prevent **contractures** (especially tight Achilles tendon).
- Physical and occupational therapy for mobility and arm use; speech therapists for communication and swallowing; adaptive equipment.

### By type
- **Spastic diplegia:** orthoses, walkers, poles, standing frames. For marked spasticity or hip dislocation: soft-tissue surgery (adductor tenotomy, psoas transfer and release). **Selective dorsal rhizotomy** helps selected severe cases with little basal ganglia involvement.
- **Hemiplegia:** tight heel cord treated by **Achilles tenotomy** or serial **botulinum toxin**; **constraint-induced movement therapy** (cast on the good side) improves the affected arm at any age.
- **Quadriplegia:** motorised wheelchairs, special feeding devices, customised seating.

### Drugs

| Drug | Use and dose from the lecture |
|---|---|
| **Diazepam** | Spasticity: 0.01 to 0.3 mg/kg/day in 2 to 4 divided doses; sedation |
| **Baclofen** | Spasticity: 0.2 to 2 mg/kg/day in 2 to 3 doses; may lower seizure threshold |
| **Dantrolene** | Spasticity: 0.5 to 10 mg/kg/day in 2 doses |
| **Levodopa** | Dystonia and DOPA-responsive dystonia: 0.5 to 2 mg/kg/day |
| **Trihexyphenidyl (Artane)** | Dystonia: 0.25 mg/day in 2 to 3 doses, titrated up; may improve arm use and vocalisation |
| **Reserpine** | Hyperkinetic movement (athetosis, chorea): 0.01 to 0.02 mg/kg/day in 2 doses, maximum 0.25 mg daily |
| **Tetrabenazine** | Hyperkinetic movement: 12.5 to 25 mg in 2 to 3 doses |
| **Carbamazepine** | May help dystonia |

> **Check:** the dantrolene range of 0.5 to 10 mg/kg/day and the diazepam range are the slide figures; titrate from low doses in practice and follow local formulary.

- **Intrathecal baclofen pump**: delivers the drug at the cord, avoiding CNS side effects of large oral doses; needs a team and follow-up for pump and infection complications.
- **Botulinum toxin** into spastic muscle groups; into **salivary glands** for drooling (10 to 30% of patients; traditionally anticholinergics).
- **Deep brain stimulation** in selected refractory cases. **Hyperbaric oxygen** has not been shown to help.
- **Communication aids:** Bliss symbols, speech-generating devices, adapted computers.
- Early management of **behaviour problems** (psychologist or psychiatrist); learning and attention disorders handled by psychologist and educator.
- **Ophthalmology:** strabismus, nystagmus and optic atrophy are common.
- Promptly assess **lower urinary tract dysfunction**.

## 6. Mitochondrial encephalomyopathies

- Heterogeneous syndromes from genetic defects impairing **oxidative phosphorylation**; organs with high energy needs (brain, muscle) are most vulnerable.
- Features: seizures, weakness, ptosis, external ophthalmoplegia, psychomotor regression, hearing loss, movement disorders, ataxia, with **lactic acidosis**; also cardiomyopathy and diabetes mellitus.
- Multifocal, intermittent or relapsing-remitting signs, often precipitated by intercurrent illness.
- **MELAS** presents with developmental delay, weakness, headaches and stroke-like focal signs; imaging injury **does not fit vascular territories**.

| Syndrome | Expansion |
|---|---|
| MELAS | Mitochondrial myopathy, encephalopathy, lactic acidosis, stroke-like episodes |
| MERRF | Myoclonic epilepsy and ragged red fibres |
| NARP | Neuropathy, ataxia, retinitis pigmentosa |
| LHON | Leber hereditary optic neuropathy |
| KSS | Kearns-Sayre syndrome |
| Others | Reversible infantile cytochrome C oxidase deficiency myopathy, Leigh disease (subacute necrotising encephalomyopathy), mtDNA depletion syndrome, Reye syndrome |

## 7. Other encephalopathies

- **HIV encephalopathy:** common in infants and children with HIV; causes developmental delay or regression, microcephaly and spasticity (standard teaching).
- **Lead encephalopathy:** toxic encephalopathy from lead poisoning.
- **Burn encephalopathy:** about **5%** of children with significant burns in the first weeks. Causes: anoxia (smoke inhalation, CO poisoning, laryngospasm), electrolyte abnormalities, bacteraemia and sepsis, cortical vein thrombosis, head injury, cerebral oedema, drug reactions, emotional distress. **Seizures** are commonest; also altered consciousness, hallucinations, coma. Treat the cause (hypoxaemia, seizures, electrolytes, oedema); recovery is generally excellent, especially if seizures are the main problem.
- **Hypertensive encephalopathy:** most often from renal disease (acute glomerulonephritis, chronic pyelonephritis, end-stage renal disease), and may be its first sign. Severe hypertension causes cerebral vasoconstriction, increased permeability, focal oedema and haemorrhage. Presents acutely (seizures, coma) or insidiously (headache, drowsiness, vomiting, blurred vision, transient cortical blindness, hemiparesis). Papilloedema and retinal haemorrhages may occur.
- **PRES (posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome):** T2 hyperintensity especially in the **occipital lobes**, can be confused with infarction; may occur without hypertension. Features: generalised seizures, headache, altered mental state, visual disturbance. **MRI is the study of choice** (CT may be normal). Treat by restoring normal blood pressure and controlling seizures.
- **Radiation encephalopathy:**
  - Acute: after large daily doses in young patients; endothelial injury causes oedema and haemorrhage; sudden irritability, lethargy, headache, focal signs, seizures, sometimes hemiparesis from infarct; **steroids** help.
  - Late: headaches and slowly progressive focal signs, hemiparesis, seizures.
  - Increased later risk of stroke, moyamoya, aneurysm, vascular malformations, mineralising microangiopathy, stroke-like migraines.
  - ALL treated with **intrathecal methotrexate plus cranial irradiation** may develop lethargy, cognitive loss, dementia, focal signs and seizures months or years later; CT shows white matter calcification and necrotising encephalopathy at post-mortem.
- **Acute necrotising encephalopathy (ANE):** rare, severe, commoner in Asia; viral trigger (influenza, HHV-6) in a genetically susceptible host; **raised liver enzymes without hyperammonaemia**; **symmetric thalamic lesions** on MRI; familial form **ANE1** (RANBP2 mutation); poor prognosis, some respond to steroids and IVIG.
- **Cystic leukoencephalopathy:** autosomal recessive (RNASET2 mutations); MRI resembles congenital CMV; static encephalopathy without megalencephaly.

## Key points to revise

1. Encephalopathy is generalised brain dysfunction with many causes; CP is the commonest static childhood encephalopathy.
2. CP: incidence 3.6 per 1,000, M : F 1.4 : 1; 80% antenatal origin; under 10% intrapartum asphyxia.
3. Risk factors: prematurity (PVL), maternal infection, multiple pregnancy, extremes of birth weight, thrombophilia and stroke.
4. Types: hemiplegia (stroke or white matter), diplegia (PVL, legs worse), quadriplegia (worst, bulbar, seizures), dyskinetic (asphyxia and kernicterus).
5. MRI is the imaging of choice; exclude progressive mimics, including Segawa disease.
6. Prevention: cooling for HIE (33.3 °C for 3 days within 6 hours), magnesium sulphate before 32 weeks.
7. Treat with a team: therapy, orthoses, baclofen, botulinum toxin, surgery, communication and feeding support.
8. Learn the other encephalopathies by their trigger: mitochondrial (lactic acidosis), hypertensive and PRES, radiation, burn, ANE.
