# Sickle Cell Anaemia

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Haematology**

> **In one line:** sickle cell anaemia is an autosomal recessive haemoglobinopathy in which a single amino acid change in the beta-globin chain (glutamic acid to valine at position 6) makes deoxygenated HbS polymerise. Red cells sickle, haemolyse and block small vessels, causing chronic haemolytic anaemia, painful vaso-occlusive crises, organ infarction and functional hyposplenism.

## What you must know

- **Point mutation at the 6th position of the beta chain: glutamic acid replaced by valine** (HbS). In HbC, glutamic acid is replaced by lysine.
- **Trait (HbAS):** about 40% HbS, usually asymptomatic. **Disease (HbSS):** over 80% HbS.
- Sickling is triggered by **hypoxia, acidosis, dehydration and raised MCHC**; **HbF inhibits sickling**, so babies are well until 5 to 6 months.
- Sickled red cells live **30 to 40 days** (normal 120); Hb is 6 to 8 g/dl.
- Crises: **vaso-occlusive** (commonest), aplastic (parvovirus B19), splenic sequestration, haemolytic, hepatic, **acute chest syndrome** (25% of deaths), dactylitis.
- Diagnosis: sickling or solubility test, then **Hb electrophoresis** (mostly HbS with 2 to 20% HbF).
- Management: analgesia (morphine), hydration, oxygen, transfusion, **hydroxyurea** to raise HbF, vaccination, penicillin prophylaxis, folic acid, genetic counselling.
- HbS carriers are relatively **protected against falciparum malaria**.

## 1. Background: normal and abnormal haemoglobin

- About **750 structural haemoglobin variants** are known. The alpha chain has 141 amino acids; the beta chain 146.

| Haemoglobin | Structure | Proportion |
|---|---|---|
| **HbA** | alpha2 beta2 | About 96% (main adult component) |
| **HbA2** | alpha2 delta2 | 2 to 3% |
| **HbF** | alpha2 gamma2 | Falls gradually after 2 years; only traces in adults |

- **HbF is raised** in sickle cell anaemia, sickle beta-thalassaemia, leukaemias (juvenile CML, erythroleukaemia), myeloproliferative disease and normal pregnancy.

### Haemoglobinopathies

- **Qualitative (structural):** abnormal globin chain, for example HbS, HbC, HbD.
- **Quantitative:** reduced globin synthesis, for example the thalassaemias.

## 2. Definition, genetics and epidemiology

- A hereditary haemoglobinopathy with production of defective haemoglobin; **autosomal recessive** inheritance.
- **HbS:** glutamic acid replaced by valine at position 6 of the beta chain. **HbC:** replaced by lysine.
- By 2015 about **4.4 million** people had sickle cell disease and **43 million** had sickle cell trait.
- Highest prevalence in **sub-Saharan Africa**, South Asia, the Middle East and the Mediterranean. The commonest genotype is homozygous **HbSS**. In Kenya, prevalence is highest around Lake Victoria and the coast, where malaria is endemic.

### Sickle cell syndromes

| State | Genotype | Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Sickle cell trait** | HbAS (one mutated gene) | About 40% HbS; asymptomatic carrier; sickling only in severe hypoxia |
| **Sickle cell anaemia** | HbSS | Over 80% HbS; full disease |
| **Double heterozygous** | Sickle-thalassaemia, HbSC, HbSD | Variable severity; HbSC milder haemolysis but retinopathy, avascular necrosis |

### Normal versus sickled red cells

| | Normal | Sickled |
|---|---|---|
| Lifespan | 120 days | 30 to 40 days |
| Oxygen-carrying capacity | Normal | Reduced |
| Hb | 12 to 14 g/dl | 6 to 9 g/dl |

## 3. Pathogenesis

1. Substitution of hydrophobic **valine** for glutamic acid creates a sticky patch.
2. On **deoxygenation**, HbS molecules aggregate and **polymerise** into long rods, deforming the cell into a sickle shape.
3. Early sickling is **reversible**: on oxygenation HbS depolymerises and the cell regains its shape.
4. Repeated cycles cause membrane damage and **irreversible sickling**, with cells that stay sickled even when fully oxygenated.
5. Rigid, sticky cells **adhere to endothelium** and occlude small vessels (ischaemia, infarction), and are destroyed (haemolysis).

### Factors influencing the rate and degree of sickling

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Amount of HbS and the other chains in the cell** | Heterozygotes sickle only in severe hypoxia; homozygotes get full disease. **HbF inhibits polymerisation**, so newborns are protected until 5 to 6 months. **HbC** aggregates with deoxy-HbS more readily than HbA does (HbSC disease) |
| **Intracellular Hb concentration (MCHC)** | Polymerisation depends strongly on concentration. Dehydration raises MCHC and sickling. **Sickle-thalassaemia** lowers globin synthesis and MCHC, so the disease is milder |
| **Low pH** | Lowers oxygen affinity (Bohr effect) and promotes sickling |
| **Duration of low oxygen tension** | Sickling occurs in sluggish microvascular beds such as **spleen and bone marrow**. Inflammation and red cell adhesion lengthen transit time and promote sickling |

### Malaria protection

- Carriers are relatively protected against **falciparum malaria**.
- The parasite lowers oxygen tension in the infected cell, which sickles quickly; sickled parasitised cells are removed (sequestered) in the spleen. This explains the high gene frequency in malarious regions.

## 4. Morphology and pathology

- **Chronic haemolysis, hyperbilirubinaemia, microvascular occlusion, infarction and thrombosis.**
- **Bone marrow expansion** causes bone resorption and secondary new bone formation: prominent cheekbones and skull changes (**crew-cut appearance** on X-ray).
- **Spleen:** early phase splenomegaly with marked congestion and trapped sickled cells in cords and sinuses. Repeated infarcts lead to progressive shrinkage and **autosplenectomy** by adolescence or even early childhood.
- **Peripheral smear:** sickle cells, target cells, features of splenic atrophy (**Howell-Jolly bodies**), irregularly contracted cells.

## 5. Clinical features

- Chronic **haemolytic anaemia**, jaundice, pallor.
- **Vaso-occlusive** episodes with hypoxic injury, infarction and severe pain.
- **Increased susceptibility to infection** because of markedly impaired splenic function and defects in the alternative complement pathway, especially **pneumococci** and ***H. influenzae*** (meningitis, sepsis); also *Salmonella* osteomyelitis.
- Dactylitis (hand-foot syndrome) in infants.
- Growth retardation, delayed puberty, leg ulcers.

### Sickle cell crises

| Crisis | Features |
|---|---|
| **Vaso-occlusive (painful)** | Pain in any region, often extremities, back, chest; fever may accompany. Triggered by infection, cold, dehydration, hypoxia, stress |
| **Splenic sequestration** | Acute painful splenic enlargement with trapping of red cells; sudden fall in Hb; risk of **hypovolaemic shock**; seen in early childhood before autosplenectomy |
| **Aplastic** | Sudden pallor and weakness with rapidly falling Hb and **reticulocytopenia**. Usual trigger **parvovirus B19**; self-limited, 7 to 10 days |
| **Haemolytic (hyperhaemolytic)** | Acute fall in Hb; common with coexistent **G6PD deficiency** |
| **Acute chest syndrome** | **25% of deaths**; follows vaso-occlusive crisis (hypoventilation, hypoxia) or **fat embolism** from bone. Fever, cough, tachypnoea, chest pain, hypoxia, wheeze, respiratory distress and failure |
| **Hepatic crisis** | Right upper quadrant pain, jaundice |
| **Dactylitis** | Painful swelling of hands and feet |

### Other acute complications

Pneumonia, meningitis, sepsis, osteomyelitis, **stroke**, avascular necrosis, **priapism**, venous thromboembolism.

> **Exam tip:** a sudden drop in Hb with reticulocytes low = aplastic crisis (parvovirus); with reticulocytes high and a big tender spleen = sequestration.

## 6. Diagnosis

- **Hb 6 to 8 g/dl**, reticulocytosis, raised bilirubin.
- **Peripheral blood film:** irregularly contracted cells, sickle cells, target cells, Howell-Jolly bodies.
- **Sickling test:** blood mixed with an oxygen-consuming reagent (**sodium metabisulphite**) induces sickling if HbS is present. It does not distinguish trait from disease.
- **Sickle solubility test:** shows presence of HbS.
- **Haemoglobin electrophoresis:** predominantly HbS with **2 to 20% HbF**; no HbA in HbSS (trait has HbA more than HbS).
- Screening of newborns and family studies; prenatal diagnosis by DNA analysis.

### Differential of a painful crisis

Acute appendicitis, acute pancreatitis, acute pyelonephritis, pelvic inflammatory disease, hepatobiliary disease; avascular necrosis and acute osteomyelitis when there is persistent local bone pain.

## 7. Management

### Vaso-occlusive crisis

- **Analgesia** by IV, intranasal or oral route depending on severity.
- **Morphine 0.1 mg/kg IV or SC every 20 minutes** until controlled, then maintain **0.05 to 0.1 mg/kg every 2 to 4 hours** (SC, IV or PO).
- Persistent pain benefits from a **patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) pump**.
- **Simple or exchange transfusion** if pain is intractable despite analgesia.
- LMWH (**tinzaparin**) has been found to shorten the course of pain.
- Adjuncts: hydroxyurea, antihistamines, anxiolytics, antiemetics.
- **Maintain hydration** and treat any trigger (infection); give oxygen if hypoxic.

### Other complications

- **Acute chest syndrome, splenic sequestration:** oxygen, judicious fluids, transfusion; close monitoring of oxygen saturation; consider empiric antibiotics for acute chest syndrome.
- **Aplastic crisis:** supportive care and simple transfusion as needed.

### Long-term management

- **Transfusion therapy** (indications: stroke, severe anaemia, chest syndrome; chronic transfusion needs iron chelation).
- **Hydroxyurea and erythropoietin increase HbF.** Hydroxyurea reduces crises and chest syndrome.
- Analgesia (morphine, meperidine).
- **Genetic counselling**, monitoring of vital signs, nutritional support (**folic acid** daily).
- **Immunisations** (pneumococcal, meningococcal, *H. influenzae* type b, hepatitis B, annual influenza) plus **penicillin prophylaxis** in children.
- Psychotherapy and psychosocial support.
- Curative option: **allogeneic stem cell transplant** in selected patients.

## 8. Complications

- Stroke (screen children with transcranial Doppler)
- Increased risk of severe bacterial infection
- Cholelithiasis (pigment gallstones)
- Osteomyelitis and avascular necrosis
- Hyposplenism / autosplenectomy
- Also: retinopathy, chronic kidney disease, pulmonary hypertension, leg ulcers, priapism

## Key points to revise

1. HbS is glutamic acid to valine at beta position 6; autosomal recessive; trait about 40% HbS, disease over 80%.
2. Hypoxia, acidosis, dehydration and high MCHC promote sickling; HbF protects.
3. Sickled cells live 30 to 40 days; Hb 6 to 8 g/dl; autosplenectomy and Howell-Jolly bodies.
4. Crises: vaso-occlusive, aplastic (parvovirus B19), sequestration, haemolytic, acute chest syndrome (25% of deaths).
5. Diagnose by sickling or solubility test and Hb electrophoresis (HbS with 2 to 20% HbF).
6. Painful crisis: morphine 0.1 mg/kg, fluids, oxygen, treat triggers; PCA if persistent.
7. Hydroxyurea raises HbF; give vaccines, penicillin prophylaxis and folic acid.
8. Encapsulated bacteria (pneumococcus, H. influenzae) cause the infections.
