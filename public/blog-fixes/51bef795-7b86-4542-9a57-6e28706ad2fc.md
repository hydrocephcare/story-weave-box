# Acquired Heart Disease in Children

**Year 4 · Paediatrics · Paediatric cardiology**

> **In one line:** acquired heart disease is cardiac disease that does not exist at birth and is usually secondary to an external agent; in children the key conditions are **Kawasaki disease** (coronary aneurysms), **rheumatic fever** (post-streptococcal autoimmune carditis), **pericarditis and tamponade**, **myocarditis and cardiomyopathy**, and **infective endocarditis**.

## What you must know

- Acquired heart disease: begins after birth, usually secondary to an extraneous agent.
- **Kawasaki disease**: fever 5+ days plus rash, conjunctivitis, oral changes, hand/foot changes, cervical lymphadenopathy; treat with **IVIG 2 g/kg** and aspirin to prevent coronary aneurysms.
- **Rheumatic fever** is a leading cause of acquired heart disease in children (peak 5-15 years); **Jones criteria**; **carditis is the only feature that causes permanent damage**.
- **Rheumatic prophylaxis**: benzathine penicillin 1.2 million units IM every 28 days until age 21-25.
- **Tamponade**: raised jugular venous pressure, muffled heart sounds, **pulsus paradoxus** (more than 10 mmHg fall in SBP on inspiration).
- **Endocarditis**: alpha-haemolytic (viridans) streptococci commonest; **blood cultures before antibiotics**; 4-6 weeks IV treatment.
- **Myocarditis** is mostly viral (Coxsackie); heart failure and arrhythmias.

## 1. Definition and types

**Acquired heart disease**: disease affecting cardiac tissue and function that does not have its inception at birth and is usually secondary to an extraneous agent.

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Ischaemic / hypoxic** | Coronary occlusion, atherosclerosis, **Kawasaki disease**, **sickle cell anaemia**, hypoperfusion (surgical ischaemic arrest, severe hypotension), asphyxia |
| **Hypertensive** | Systemic hypertension, pulmonary hypertension |
| **Infectious** | Pericarditis, myocarditis, endocarditis |
| **Inflammatory** | Post-pericardiotomy syndrome, rheumatic fever, collagen vascular diseases |
| **Metabolic** | Endocrine (adrenal, pituitary, pancreatic, thyroid, parathyroid); storage diseases (glycogen, mucopolysaccharides) |
| **Nutritional** | Deficiencies (starvation, vitamin, mineral, carnitine) and excesses (obesity, vitamin, mineral) |
| **Traumatic** | Penetrating, blunt |

## 2. Kawasaki disease

### 2.1 Epidemiology
- Recognised in the 1970s; **inflammatory disease of unknown aetiology**.
- About 9.2 per 100,000 per year; usually **under 4 years**.
- Winter and spring; 3-year "epidemics".
- Asians and black children more affected than white (quoted ratio 9 : 1.5 : 1).

### 2.2 Pathophysiology
Immunoregulatory anomalies, activation of T and B lymphocytes, production of immunoglobulins and cytokines, a widespread immune reaction, generalised **microvasculitis**, myocardial and pericardial inflammation and **coronary vasculitis**.

### 2.3 Clinical features
**Fever of 5 or more days** plus (principal features):
- **Polymorphous rash**
- **Non-purulent conjunctivitis**
- **Erythema of oral membranes**, including tongue (strawberry tongue), red cracked lips
- **Indurative oedema of hands and feet** (later periungual desquamation)
- **Cervical lymphadenopathy**
- Acute and often severe toxic presentation; multi-organ involvement.

> **Remember:** the full criteria are fever 5 days plus at least 4 of the 5 other features; incomplete (atypical) Kawasaki disease is common in infants and still needs treatment.

### 2.4 Laboratory and echocardiography
- Raised acute phase reactants, **raised ESR**, **raised platelets** (thrombocytosis in week 2-3).
- Myocardial dysfunction and pericardial effusion.
- **Coronary thickening progressing to dilatation and aneurysm**: acute-phase echo shows diffuse coronary wall thickening and dilatation in 30-50%; dilatation is defined as lumen **over 3 mm in a child under 5**; aneurysms are sacular or fusiform.

### 2.5 Cardiovascular stages

Coronary aneurysms occur in **20% of untreated and 2-4% of treated** children.

| Stage | Time | Findings |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Weeks 1-2 | Microvasculitis; peri-, myo- and endocarditis; perivasculitis of coronaries |
| 2 | Weeks 1.5-3 | Coronary vasculitis with **aneurysms and thrombi**; intimal proliferation; peri-, myo-, endocarditis |
| 3 | Weeks 4-5 | Scarring and intimal thickening of coronaries; **myocardial infarction** |
| 4 | Over 2 months | Advanced coronary artery disease, myocardial fibrosis |

### 2.6 Treatment
- **IVIG 2 g/kg over 24 hours** (ideally within 10 days of onset).
- **Aspirin** 20-25 mg/kg/dose every 6 hours until afebrile 2-3 days, then **3-5 mg/kg/day** for 6-8 weeks until ESR and platelet count are normal; **indefinitely if coronary artery anomalies**.

> **Exam tip:** Kawasaki disease is the commonest cause of acquired heart disease in children in developed countries; aspirin is an exception to the rule of avoiding aspirin in children (Reye syndrome).

## 3. Pericarditis and post-pericardiotomy syndrome

### 3.1 Pericarditis
Inflammation (often infective) of the pericardial space. Features: **chest pain**, **friction rub**, **pericardial effusion**, fever, raised ESR.

**Causes**: viral, purulent, tuberculous, rheumatic, Kawasaki, uraemic.

### 3.2 Post-pericardiotomy syndrome
- Occurs in about **30%** if the pericardium is opened, **1-2 weeks after surgery**.
- Aetiology uncertain: viral, autoimmune.
- Fever, chest pain, friction rub, pericardial effusion.
- **Treatment**: aspirin **50-75 mg/kg/day for 4-6 weeks**; steroids **2 mg/kg/day** tapered over 3-4 weeks; diuretics cautiously.

### 3.3 Cardiac tamponade

- **Pathophysiology**: increased pericardial fluid raises filling pressures, impedes ventricular filling and decreases cardiac output. A **rapid small volume** increase is more dangerous than a large, slowly accumulating chronic volume.
- **Physical findings**: **decreased heart sounds**, **distended jugular veins**, **pulsus paradoxus** (fall in SBP of **over 10 mmHg** on inspiration; blood pools in the pulmonary bed so LV filling falls).
- **ECG**: low voltage, ST-T changes, **electrical alternans**.
- **CXR**: "water-bottle" heart if large volume; normal if acute.
- **Echo**: space between heart and pericardium, swinging heart, inspiratory variation in Doppler flows.
- **Treatment**: urgent **pericardiocentesis** (echo guided) or surgical drainage (not in slides; standard).

## 4. Myocarditis and congestive cardiomyopathy

**Myocarditis**: infection of the myocardium with lymphocytic infiltration. **Cardiomyopathy**: degenerative process affecting myocytes. Both impair myocardial function.

### 4.1 Aetiology

| Myocarditis | Congestive (dilated) cardiomyopathy |
|---|---|
| **Viral**: Coxsackie, ECHO, adenovirus | Infectious (viral) |
| **Bacterial**: TB, streptococcus | Familial (Duchenne muscular dystrophy) |
| Fungal (unusual) | Metabolic (glycogen storage) |
| **Protozoal**: Chagas disease (T. cruzi), malaria, toxoplasmosis | Ischaemic (Kawasaki) |
| Rickettsial, spirochaetal | Toxic (**anthracyclines**) |
| Metazoal: trichinosis, echinococcosis | Nutritional (carnitine deficiency) |

### 4.2 Clinical features
General malaise or viral syndrome, **low cardiac output (shock)**, gallop rhythm, mitral regurgitation, ST-T changes. Echo: reduced shortening fraction, segmental wall motion abnormalities, valvar insufficiency.

### 4.3 Course
Most (70-80%) have mild to moderate heart failure and 20-30% severe heart failure. Of all cases about 60-70% recover, 10-20% progress to dilated cardiomyopathy and about 10% die or need transplant.

### 4.4 Diagnosis
Clinical findings, identification of the agent, **endomyocardial biopsy** (lymphocytic infiltrate, necrosis, the agent, "staging").

### 4.5 Treatment
- **Symptomatic**: inotropes, diuretics, afterload reduction.
- Correct the cause.
- **Immunosuppression**: steroids, antivirals, ciclosporin, interferon.
- **Transplantation** for refractory disease.

## 5. Infective endocarditis

### 5.1 Epidemiology
- Microbial infection of the endocardial surface (valves or wall), **acute (virulent)** or **subacute (prolonged)**.
- 1 in 1,800 to 1 in 4,500 paediatric admissions; any age; peak in the fifth decade (adults).
- No change between the pre- and post-antibiotic eras; influenced by better diagnosis, drug abuse and treatment modalities.

### 5.2 Organisms
| Organism | Comment |
|---|---|
| **Alpha-haemolytic streptococci** (viridans) | Commonest (over 60%); prolonged course |
| **Staphylococcus aureus** | 2nd commonest (20%); virulent |
| Beta-haemolytic streptococci | Uncommon |
| Coagulase-negative staphylococci | Increasing |
| Candida | Fungal |

### 5.3 Risk factors
| High risk | Moderate risk |
|---|---|
| Prosthetic valves, surgical shunts, indwelling catheters, previous endocarditis | PDA, VSD, ASD (not secundum), bicuspid aortic valve, rheumatic heart disease, mitral valve prolapse with MR |

### 5.4 Clinical features
Fever (**high with Staphylococcus, low with Streptococcus**), "viral syndrome", **new murmur**, heart failure, petechiae, raised ESR, anaemia, haematuria (also splinter haemorrhages, Osler nodes, Janeway lesions, Roth spots, splenomegaly).

### 5.5 Diagnosis and treatment
- **Blood cultures**: positive off antibiotics; 5-8% culture negative; take **2-3 sets over 24 hours**.
- **Echo**: vegetations, valve insufficiency.
- **Treatment**: specific antimicrobials **4-6 weeks IV** (quoted 2 weeks with or without oral); surgery, especially for prosthetic valves.

### 5.6 Prophylaxis (AHA guidelines)
- **Amoxicillin** orally for upper respiratory procedures; **clindamycin** if penicillin allergic.
- **Ampicillin and gentamicin, or vancomycin**, for GU or GI procedures.

> **Check:** current AHA and ESC guidance limits prophylaxis to the highest-risk patients (prosthetic valves, previous endocarditis, certain congenital heart disease) and dental procedures; GU/GI prophylaxis is no longer routine. Good dental hygiene is the best prevention.

## 6. Rheumatic fever

### 6.1 Epidemiology and aetiology
- A leading cause of acquired heart disease in children (**5-15 years, peak 8 years**).
- USA incidence 0.5-3.0 per 100,000 (1900: 100-200 per 100,000); still common in low-resource settings, including Kenya.
- **Post-infectious connective tissue response** in a susceptible host after **group A beta-haemolytic streptococcal pharyngitis**.
- Risk: family history of RHD, low socio-economic status (overcrowding).

### 6.2 Pathophysiology
In 1960 Kaplan and co-workers showed antigenic similarity between streptococcal cell wall and myocardium: an **autoimmune response to group A streptococcus with cross-reaction to myocardium** (molecular mimicry).

### 6.3 Jones criteria

| Major | Frequency |
|---|---|
| **Carditis** | 40-50% |
| **Arthritis** | 60-85% |
| **Chorea** | 15% |
| **Erythema marginatum** | 10% |
| **Subcutaneous nodules** | 2-10% |

| Minor | |
|---|---|
| Clinical | Arthralgia, fever, history of RF or RHD |
| Laboratory | Raised ESR, CRP, prolonged PR interval |

**Plus evidence of preceding streptococcal infection** (raised ASO, positive culture, or recent scarlet fever). Diagnosis: 2 major, or 1 major + 2 minor, with evidence of strep infection.

### 6.4 Manifestations

- **Arthritis**: commonest manifestation; monoarticular/oligoarticular, usually large joints; **migratory or fleeting**; good response to aspirin; **no residual effect**.
- **Carditis**: 1-2 weeks after strep (may be delayed). Endocardium (valves), myocardium (tachycardia, cardiomegaly, heart failure), pericardium (rub, rarely effusion). A prior attack predisposes to recurrence. **The only feature that causes permanent damage.**
  - **Mitral**: regurgitation, mild to severe (**Carey-Coombs murmur**), CHF, stenosis later.
  - **Aortic**: regurgitation; less common but more severe.
- **Sydenham chorea** (St Vitus' dance): prepubertal girls (8-12 years); emotional lability and personality change, then loss of motor coordination with spontaneous purposeless movements and weakness; often isolated.
- **Erythema marginatum**: non-pruritic serpiginous or annular rash, prominent on the trunk and inner proximal limbs.
- **Subcutaneous nodules**: hard, painless, non-pruritic, freely mobile, 0.2-2.0 cm, symmetrical, single or in clusters, on extensor surfaces of joints, scalp or spine.

### 6.5 Investigations
Raised ESR; **ASO above 333 Todd units**; throat culture; leucocytosis; hypochromic microcytic anaemia; ECG (first-degree heart block, arrhythmia); CXR (progressive cardiac enlargement); echocardiography.

### 6.6 Treatment
- Cardiac supportive care; **bed rest 1-2 weeks**; immobilise inflamed joints.
- **Aspirin 100 mg/kg/day** (target level 20 mg/100 mL) after the diagnosis is made (watch for toxicity).
- **Benzathine penicillin G 0.6-1.2 million units** to eradicate the organism.
- **Prednisone 2 mg/kg/day** for severe carditis (2-4 weeks, then taper).
- Treat heart failure (digoxin: watch toxicity).

### 6.7 Prevention (secondary prophylaxis)
Any patient with documented rheumatic fever: prophylaxis after the attack until **21-25 years of age** with **benzathine penicillin 1.2 million units IM every 28 days**, or **erythromycin 250 mg twice daily** if penicillin allergic.

> **Exam tip:** arthritis responds to aspirin and leaves no sequelae, whereas carditis can damage valves; chorea and erythema marginatum may appear late and need no ASO elevation.

## Key points to revise

1. Acquired heart disease starts after birth: ischaemic, hypertensive, infectious, inflammatory, metabolic, nutritional, traumatic.
2. Kawasaki disease: fever 5+ days with rash, conjunctivitis, mucosal changes, hand/foot oedema, lymphadenopathy; treat with IVIG 2 g/kg and aspirin; coronary aneurysm in 20% untreated.
3. Pericarditis causes chest pain, rub, effusion; tamponade gives pulsus paradoxus over 10 mmHg and electrical alternans.
4. Myocarditis is mostly viral; treatment is supportive; 60-70% recover.
5. Endocarditis: viridans streptococci commonest, Staph aureus second; 2-3 blood cultures before antibiotics; 4-6 weeks IV.
6. Rheumatic fever follows group A strep pharyngitis; Jones criteria; carditis is the only permanent damage.
7. Treat rheumatic fever with aspirin, penicillin and steroids for severe carditis; prophylaxis with benzathine penicillin 1.2 MU every 28 days to age 21-25.
