# Chronic Diarrhoea and Malabsorption

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Gastroenterology**

> **In one line:** chronic diarrhoea (more than 4 weeks) is classified by the mechanism: watery (osmotic or secretory), fatty (maldigestion or malabsorption) or inflammatory. A history, stool tests (osmotic gap, fat, leucocytes) and targeted biopsy then separate the causes, which include coeliac disease, Whipple disease, tropical sprue, small bowel bacterial overgrowth, bile acid diarrhoea and exocrine pancreatic insufficiency.

## What you must know

- **Chronic diarrhoea = more than 4 to 6 weeks.** Acute is under 2 weeks (usually infective); subacute is 2 to 4 weeks.
- Four patterns: **osmotic, secretory, fatty (steatorrhoea), inflammatory.**
- **Fecal osmotic gap** = 290 − 2 × (stool Na + stool K). **Over 100 mOsm/kg = osmotic; under 50 = secretory.** Osmotic diarrhoea stops with fasting; secretory does not.
- **Coeliac disease:** best initial test is **IgA anti-tissue transglutaminase (anti-tTG)**; diagnosis is confirmed by **duodenal biopsy** (villous atrophy); treat with a **gluten-free diet**.
- **Whipple disease:** *Tropheryma whipplei*; **PAS-positive foamy macrophages**; ceftriaxone then co-trimoxazole for 1 year.
- **SBBO:** B12 deficiency with **normal or high folate**; jejunal culture is the gold standard; treat the cause plus antibiotics.
- **Bile acid diarrhoea** (after ileal resection or cholecystectomy) responds to **cholestyramine**.
- Pancreatic insufficiency (usually alcohol-related chronic pancreatitis) responds to **enzyme replacement**.

## 1. Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Diarrhoea** | Change in stool consistency (loose), volume or frequency |
| **Acute** | Under 2 weeks, usually infectious |
| **Subacute** | 2 to 4 weeks |
| **Chronic** | More than 4 to 6 weeks |
| **Maldigestion** | Inadequate breakdown of large molecules (e.g. lack of enzymes or bile) |
| **Malabsorption** | Inadequate mucosal transport of digested products into the blood |

## 2. Normal fluid handling and pathophysiology

- About **9 to 10 L** of fluid (diet plus secretions) enter the jejunum each day. The **small bowel absorbs about 90%**.
- The colon receives about 900 mL per day, absorbs about 90% of it, and **80 to 100 mL** is lost in stool.
- A healthy colon can compensate for extra load by absorbing **3000 to 4000 mL** per day, but cannot cope with more than about 4 L.
- Therefore **small bowel disease** gives **large-volume** diarrhoea, because the small bowel normally handles most of the fluid.

> **Remember:** an acute onset of frequency with cramps and fever points to infection; loose blood-stained stool points to inflammation; pale, offensive stool that floats, with anorexia and weight loss, points to steatorrhoea.

## 3. Classification

Chronic diarrhoea is grouped as **non-inflammatory** (watery: osmotic or secretory; and fatty) or **inflammatory**.

### 3.1 Osmotic diarrhoea

- Poorly absorbed solutes stay in the lumen and hold water by osmosis.
- **Relieved by fasting.**
- **Fecal osmotic gap over 50 mOsm/kg suggests osmotic diarrhoea; over 100 mOsm/kg is definitive.** Stool pH is low in carbohydrate malabsorption because colonic bacteria ferment the sugars to acids.

| Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|
| Ingestion of poorly absorbed agents | Carbohydrate malabsorption (diets high in fructose or sugar alcohols); diuretic mannitol; osmotic laxatives (magnesium, phosphate, sulphate, sorbitol, lactulose) |
| Loss of a nutrient transporter | Lactose intolerance (lactase deficiency); other disaccharidase deficiency; glucose-galactose malabsorption |

### 3.2 Secretory diarrhoea

- There is **active secretion** of fluid and electrolytes and reduced absorption.
- **Does not stop with fasting**; osmotic gap **under 50 mOsm/kg**.

| Cause group | Examples |
|---|---|
| Infection | Most commonly **bacterial toxins** (e.g. cholera, *E. coli*) |
| Pancreatic | Pancreatic insufficiency |
| Inflammation and dysregulation | Diverticulitis, vasculitis, microscopic colitis, gastroenteritis |
| Ion-transport defects | Absent transport mechanism; congenital chloridorrhoea |
| Reduced mucosal surface | Surgery or disease |
| Bile acids | Ileal bile acid malabsorption |
| Idiopathic | Epidemic (Brainerd), sporadic |
| Drugs and toxins | NSAIDs, colchicine, stimulant laxative abuse |
| Neoplasia | Colon cancer, lymphoma, rectal villous adenoma |
| Abnormal motility | Diabetic neuropathy, IBS, post-vagotomy |
| Circulating secretagogues | Addison disease, hyperthyroidism, carcinoid syndrome, phaeochromocytoma, gastrinoma (Zollinger-Ellison), VIPoma, somatostatinoma |

> **Exam tip:** to separate osmotic from secretory diarrhoea use (1) a **diagnostic fast** and (2) the **stool osmotic gap**. Fasting cures osmotic only.

### 3.3 Fatty diarrhoea (steatorrhoea)

- Bloating, flatulence, **greasy, foul-smelling stool that is hard to flush**.
- Weight loss and signs of fat-soluble vitamin deficiency (peripheral neuropathy, easy bruising), iron-deficiency anaemia, coagulopathy, hypoalbuminaemia, osteopenia.

| Maldigestion (inadequate breakdown of triglycerides) | Malabsorption (inadequate mucosal transport) |
|---|---|
| Pancreatic exocrine insufficiency (chronic pancreatitis, cystic fibrosis) | Mucosal disease (coeliac disease, Whipple disease) |
| Inadequate luminal bile acids (e.g. advanced primary biliary cirrhosis) | Mesenteric ischaemia |
| Small bowel bacterial overgrowth (bile salt deconjugation) | Structural: short bowel syndrome, post-resection diarrhoea |
| | Lymphatic obstruction (radiation, infection, lymphoma) |

### 3.4 Inflammatory diarrhoea

- **Small-volume** diarrhoea with **blood, mucus and pus**, abdominal pain and fever.
- Findings: faecal leucocytes, gross or occult blood, raised ESR/CRP, leucocytosis. **Persists on fasting.**

| Group | Examples |
|---|---|
| Inflammatory bowel disease | Ulcerative colitis, Crohn disease, diverticulitis, ulcerative jejunoileitis |
| Other | Ischaemic colitis, radiation colitis |
| Neoplasia | Colon cancer, lymphoma |
| Infection, bacterial | **Pseudomembranous colitis (*C. difficile*)**; invasive bacteria (some *E. coli*, *Shigella*, TB, MAC, *Yersinia*) |
| Infection, viral | CMV, herpes simplex |
| Infection, parasitic | Amoebiasis, strongyloides |

> **Remember (SEECSY):** invasive bacterial causes of colitis: *Salmonella*, *E. coli*, *Entamoeba*, *Campylobacter*, *Shigella*, *Yersinia enterocolitica*.

**Clostridium difficile**

- Diarrhoea starts in the first days of antibiotics or **up to 6 weeks after stopping**.
- First line: **metronidazole 500 mg orally three times daily for 10 days** or **vancomycin 125 mg orally four times daily for 7 to 10 days**.
- Vancomycin works better for hypervirulent strains (ribotype 027), but is costlier and may drive resistance in enterococci and *Staph. aureus*; some reserve it for relapse (15 to 30%), failure of first treatment or severe infection.

## 4. Drugs and toxins that cause diarrhoea

Acid-reducing agents (H2 blockers, PPIs); magnesium antacids; antiarrhythmics (digoxin, quinidine); antibiotics; antineoplastics; antiretrovirals; beta blockers; colchicine; levothyroxine; SSRIs; furosemide; metformin; NSAIDs and aspirin; misoprostol; theophylline; amphetamines; caffeine; alcohol; and **opioid withdrawal** (opioids themselves constipate, diarrhoea follows withdrawal).

## 5. Malabsorption

### 5.1 Where nutrients are absorbed

| Site | Nutrients |
|---|---|
| **Duodenum / jejunum** | Carbohydrates, fats, amino acids, iron, fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K), calcium, magnesium, minerals |
| **Ileum** | **Vitamin B12, bile salts**, magnesium |
| **Colon** | Short-chain fatty acids; vitamin K and biotin (B7) made by gut bacteria |

> **Remember:** disease of the **terminal ileum** gives **B12 deficiency**; disease of the **colon** may reduce vitamin K (coagulopathy). Iron, calcium and folate are absorbed proximally.

### 5.2 Clinical features by nutrient

| Malabsorbed | Clinical features | Laboratory findings |
|---|---|---|
| Calories | Weight loss with normal appetite | |
| Fat | Pale, bulky stool, steatorrhoea | Stool fat over 6 g/day |
| Protein | Oedema, muscle wasting, amenorrhoea | Low albumin and total protein |
| Carbohydrate | Watery diarrhoea, flatulence, milk intolerance | Stool pH under 6; osmotic gap over 100; raised breath hydrogen |
| Vitamin B12 | Anaemia, subacute combined degeneration (paraesthesia, ataxia, loss of position and vibration sense), hypersegmented neutrophils | Macrocytic anaemia, low B12, raised MMA and homocysteine, abnormal Schilling test |
| Folate | Anaemia | Macrocytic anaemia; low serum and red cell folate; raised homocysteine |
| Iron | Anaemia, glossitis, pica (pagophagia) | Microcytic anaemia; low iron and ferritin; raised TIBC |
| Calcium and vitamin D | Paraesthesia, tetany, pathological fractures, positive Chvostek and Trousseau signs, osteoporosis | Low calcium; abnormal DEXA; raised ALP |
| Vitamin A | Follicular hyperkeratosis, night blindness | Low serum carotene |
| Vitamin K | Easy bruising, bleeding | Raised INR; low factors II, VII, IX, X |
| B vitamins (general) | Cheilosis, painless glossitis, angular stomatitis, acrodermatitis | |

## 6. Approach to chronic diarrhoea

### History

- **Onset** (congenital, abrupt, gradual), **pattern** (continuous or intermittent), **duration**.
- **Stool**: watery, bloody or fatty; faecal incontinence; weight loss.
- **Abdominal pain**: IBD, IBS, ischaemia.
- **Aggravating and relieving factors**: diet, stress; effect of fasting.
- **Iatrogenic**: drugs, radiation, surgery, laxatives (factitious diarrhoea).
- **Systemic disease**: hyperthyroidism, diabetes, collagen vascular disease, AIDS, immunoglobulin deficiency, neoplasm.
- **Epidemiology**: travel, food, water.

### Examination

General (fluid balance, nutrition), skin (flushing, rashes, dermatitis herpetiformis), thyroid (masses), chest (wheeze), heart (murmur), abdomen (hepatomegaly, ascites, masses, tenderness), extremities (oedema), and **anorectal** (sphincter competence, rectal mass, faecal occult blood).

### Investigations

**Routine blood tests**

- **FBC:** anaemia (nutritional deficiency or GI bleeding); leucocytosis in inflammatory causes.
- **Chemistry:** fluid and electrolytes, nutritional status, serum protein and globulin.

**Stool analysis**

- Weight; electrolytes and **osmotic gap**; pH; faecal occult blood; **faecal leucocytes** (or lactoferrin or calprotectin); **fat** (Sudan stain or 48 to 72 hour quantitative collection); **laxative screen** (if positive, repeat before further work-up).
- Stool culture is mainly useful in acute diarrhoea. Ova and parasites, *Giardia* antigen, *C. difficile*, coccidia, microsporidia and cryptosporidium as indicated.

**Imaging and endoscopy**

- Small bowel series; CT/MRI or CT/MR enterography.
- **Upper endoscopy with small bowel biopsy** and aspirate for quantitative culture (especially for coeliac disease).
- **Colonoscopy with random biopsies** (especially for IBD and microscopic colitis).

## 7. Coeliac disease

### Definition

- Small intestinal malabsorption after ingesting **gluten** (wheat) or related proteins of rye, barley and oats.
- Characteristic, though not specific, **villous atrophy**.
- Prompt clinical and histological improvement on a strict **gluten-free diet**, and relapse on reintroduction.
- It is an **autoimmune** disease with positive serology; it differs from simple gluten intolerance, which has no antibodies or microscopic change.

### Pathology

1. Loss of normal villous structure (flattening); crypts are elongated and open onto a flat surface.
2. **Leaky mucosa**: tight-junction changes between damaged absorptive cells increase permeability.
3. Marked increase in cells in the lamina propria (plasma cells and lymphocytes).

### Who is at risk

- **First-degree relatives** (about 10% affected; about 75% concordance in monozygotic twins). Screen relatives of any index case.
- **IgA deficiency** (2 to 3%), **type 1 diabetes** (3 to 6%), **autoimmune thyroid disease** (6 to 8%), connective tissue disease, Down syndrome.
- **HLA-DQ2** in 90 to 95% of patients and **HLA-DQ8** in about 8%.

### Clinical presentation

| Domain | Features |
|---|---|
| Non-specific GI | Diarrhoea (commonest), bloating, dyspepsia |
| Nutritional | Anaemia (iron, folate); CBC may be microcytic or macrocytic and show **hyposplenism** (target cells, spherocytes, Howell-Jolly bodies) |
| Fat-soluble vitamins | A: poor night vision, follicular hyperkeratosis. D: hypocalcaemia, osteoporosis. K: bruising, bleeding, raised INR |
| Skin | **Dermatitis herpetiformis** (clears with a strict gluten-free diet; some need dapsone) |
| Liver | Raised ALT and AST |
| Malignancy | Enteropathy-associated **T-cell lymphoma**, small bowel adenocarcinoma |
| Other | Osteoporosis; may be **asymptomatic** |

> **Exam tip:** think of coeliac disease in anyone with unexplained **iron-deficiency anaemia, diarrhoea or dyspepsia**.

### Serology

| Test | Performance | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| **IgA anti-tTG** | Sensitivity 95%, specificity 97.5% | **Best initial test** |
| IgA endomysial antibody | Sensitivity 85 to 98% (about 93); specificity 97 to 100% (about 99) | |
| IgA anti-gliadin | Sensitivity 75 to 90% (about 80); specificity 82 to 95% (about 85) | |
| IgG anti-gliadin | Sensitivity 69 to 85% (about 75); specificity 73 to 90% (about 80) | Use in **IgA-deficient** patients |

- Adults with positive serology must **stay on gluten** until duodenal biopsy. High-titre serology in children can be diagnostic without biopsy. Antibody titres fall with successful treatment.

### Diagnosis

- **Duodenal biopsy is the gold standard.** Endoscopy often looks normal, so biopsy regardless. Changes are patchy: take **more than four biopsies from the second part of the duodenum plus one from the bulb**.
- Findings (Marsh classification): loss of mucosal folds, crypt hyperplasia, villous flattening, scalloping.

### Management

- **Education**: the commonest cause of poor response is accidental or unrecognised gluten ingestion.
- **Gluten-free diet**: avoid wheat, barley, rye (and oats as on the slides).
- Correct deficiencies (iron, calcium, vitamin D, folate).
- Anticipate complications (lymphoma, carcinoma, osteoporosis, hyposplenism).

## 8. Whipple disease

- **Chronic systemic infection** by *Tropheryma whipplei*, an intracellular gram-positive bacillus. Affects GI tract, CNS, heart, joints and skin.
- **Mechanism of diarrhoea**: macrophage-rich infiltrate of the small bowel mucosa and **obstruction of mesenteric lymphatics**, causing malabsorption (watery or fatty diarrhoea; fatty because of lymphatic obstruction), weight loss and pain.

| System | Features |
|---|---|
| Joints | Seronegative oligo- or polyarthralgia (often the **first** manifestation) |
| Neurological | Progressive dementia, **supranuclear ophthalmoplegia**, altered consciousness, oculo-masticatory and oculo-facial skeletal myorhythmia. CNS involvement is the most serious |
| Cardiac | Culture-negative endocarditis (mitral valve commonest), myocarditis, pericarditis |
| Skin | Hyperpigmentation (common) |
| General | Low-grade fever |

**Diagnosis**

- Small bowel biopsy: **foamy macrophages** on H&E and numerous **PAS-positive granules** (lysosomes full of the bacterium). PAS is very specific. Confirm with **PCR**.
- Endoscopy: whitish-yellow plaque-like patches in 75% (lipid droplets or lymphangiectasia).

**Treatment**

- 2 weeks of an antibiotic that crosses the blood-brain barrier: **ceftriaxone 2 g IV daily**, then **co-trimoxazole (TMP/SMX) double-strength twice daily for 1 year**.
- Or **penicillin G 2 MU IV 4-hourly plus streptomycin**, then co-trimoxazole for 1 year.
- Long-term follow-up: relapse in up to one-third, often in the CNS; repeat the same therapy or use **doxycycline plus hydroxychloroquine**.

> **Remember:** "PAS-positive foamy whipped cream in a CAN" (PAS stain, foamy macrophages).

## 9. Small bowel bacterial overgrowth (SBBO, blind loop syndrome)

### Normal flora

| Site | Bacteria (CFU/mL) |
|---|---|
| Stomach | 0 to 10³ |
| Jejunum | 0 to 10⁴ (gram-positive aerobes: oral streptococci, lactobacilli) |
| Ileum | 10⁵ to 10⁹ |
| Colon | 10¹⁰ to 10¹² (anaerobes: *Bacteroides*, peptostreptococcus, lactobacillus, bifidobacterium, clostridium; facultative aerobes: *E. coli*, enterococcus, staphylococcus) |

### Barriers that prevent SBBO and what breaks them

| Barrier | Disrupted by |
|---|---|
| Gastric acid and pancreatic enzymes | Chronic pancreatitis, low HCl (surgery, pernicious anaemia, partial gastrectomy, long-term PPI) |
| Intact bowel structure | Surgery, diverticula (stasis) |
| Motility (migrating motor complex) | Systemic sclerosis, diabetic autonomic neuropathy, pseudo-obstruction |
| Secretory IgA | Immunodeficiency (hypogammaglobulinaemia) |
| Ileocaecal valve | Scarring from inflammation, surgery |
| Competing bacteria | Antibiotics |

### Effects of overgrowth

1. **Bile acids deconjugated** so fat absorption is poor.
2. Bilirubin deconjugated.
3. **Carbohydrate** fermented to CO₂, H₂, D-lactic acid and short-chain fatty acids.
4. Lipid and protein metabolism altered (short-chain fatty acids, amines, ammonia).
5. Bacteria use **B12**, so deficiency occurs; they make **folate**, so folate is **normal or high** (unlike extensive coeliac disease or resection).

### Presentation

Bloating, watery diarrhoea, abdominal pain, weight loss, neuropathy; vitamin A, D, E, K deficiency (bruising, night blindness, bone disease); iron deficiency; hypoproteinaemia; **B12 deficiency**; fat and carbohydrate malabsorption.

### Investigations

- **Jejunal aspirate culture** is the **gold standard** (abnormal above 10⁴).
- **Breath tests**: hydrogen breath test, ¹⁴C-xylose breath test, ¹⁴C-bile acid breath test.
- **Schilling test** (see below).

### Management

1. **Treat the underlying cause** (stop acid suppression, treat Crohn disease, remove strictures): the most important step.
2. **Nutrition**: replace fat-soluble vitamins and B12; low-carbohydrate diet to limit bacterial substrate.
3. **Antibiotics for 7 to 10 days**: metronidazole, amoxicillin-clavulanate, tetracycline, doxycycline or amoxicillin. Cycle antibiotics to avoid resistance. If breath tests show high methane, add neomycin.
4. Bowel cleansing with PEG and prokinetics; low-dose octreotide has a promotility effect (higher doses cause hypomotility). Probiotics have minimal evidence.

### Schilling test (B12 absorption)

Oral radiolabelled B12 is given at every stage with an extra item added, and urinary B12 is measured.

| Stage | Added | Normal result means |
|---|---|---|
| I | IM unlabelled B12 | Low intake was the cause |
| II | Intrinsic factor | Lack of intrinsic factor (pernicious anaemia, gastric resection) |
| III | Oral antibiotics | Bacterial overgrowth |
| IV | Pancreatic enzymes | Pancreatic insufficiency |

If it is still abnormal after all stages, think of **ileal disease**.

| Cause | Co-labelled B12 | With intrinsic factor | With pancreatic enzymes | After 5 days antibiotics |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pernicious anaemia | Reduced | Normal | Reduced | Reduced |
| Chronic pancreatitis | Reduced | Reduced | Normal | Reduced |
| Bacterial overgrowth | Reduced | Reduced | Reduced | Normal |
| Ileal disease | Reduced | Reduced | Reduced | Reduced |

> **Check:** the Schilling test is rarely available now; B12 and intrinsic factor antibodies, breath tests and a therapeutic antibiotic trial are used in practice.

## 10. Tropical sprue

- **Villous atrophy and crypt hyperplasia** in people living in or returning from the tropics (Asia, Africa, Middle East, Caribbean, Central and South America). **Acquired**, not present in newborns; travellers from industrialised countries may develop it.
- Cause unknown, probably continuous **bacterial contamination of the upper small bowel** plus nutritional deficiency.
- **Features**: chronic diarrhoea often with steatorrhoea, anorexia, cramps, bloating; **B12 and folate deficiency** (unlike SBBO) with megaloblastic anaemia.
- **Diagnosis**: jejunal aspirate and biopsy (gold standard) if suspicion is high; history of prolonged tropical residence.
- **Treatment**: **tetracycline for 3 to 6 months** (treatment of choice) plus folate and B12. Resuscitate severely ill patients with fluids and electrolytes; give **B12 1000 µg** to all acute cases.

## 11. Bile acid diarrhoea

- Bile acids are made in the liver; **95% are reabsorbed in the terminal ileum** and only 5% reach the colon. When ileum is resected or diseased, excess bile salts spill into the colon and stimulate **water and electrolyte secretion**, causing diarrhoea. Fat malabsorption also increases **oxalate absorption**, causing renal oxalate stones.
- **Types**: type 1 idiopathic; type 2 after small bowel (ileal) resection or **cholecystectomy**. Most commonly seen in **Crohn disease** after ileal resection.
- **Features**: urgent watery diarrhoea or mild steatorrhoea.
- **Diagnosis**: **SeHCAT** (⁷⁵Se-homocholic acid taurine) retention test; contrast studies; B12 absorption; raised serum **7α-hydroxycholestenone** (non-invasive marker).
- **Treatment**: **bile acid sequestrants** such as **cholestyramine**.

## 12. Exocrine pancreatic insufficiency

- Mainly **chronic pancreatitis** (alcohol is the commonest cause); steatorrhoea; history of abdominal pain, long alcohol use and diarrhoea.
- Improves with **pancreatic enzyme replacement** (lipase and protease).

| Enzyme | Action |
|---|---|
| Pancreatic amylase | Starch to maltose |
| Trypsin, chymotrypsin | Protein to peptides |
| Pancreatic lipase | Fats to fatty acids and glycerol |

| Cause | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Chronic pancreatitis, cystic fibrosis, diabetes, sequelae of necrotising pancreatitis, hereditary pancreatitis | Loss of pancreatic tissue (most cases) |
| Periampullary tumours, IPMN, pancreatic head carcinoma, benign tumours | Obstruction of the main duct |
| Coeliac disease, Crohn disease, Shwachman-Diamond syndrome | Reduced pancreatic stimulation |
| Zollinger-Ellison syndrome | Acid inactivates enzymes |
| Gastrectomy, gastric bypass, extensive small bowel surgery | Surgical loss of mixing and stimulation |

## Key points to revise

1. Chronic diarrhoea is more than 4 weeks; classify as osmotic, secretory, fatty or inflammatory.
2. Osmotic gap over 100 = osmotic (stops with fasting); under 50 = secretory.
3. Coeliac: IgA anti-tTG first, duodenal biopsy confirms, gluten-free diet; screen first-degree relatives.
4. Whipple: PAS-positive foamy macrophages; ceftriaxone then co-trimoxazole for 1 year.
5. SBBO: B12 low, folate normal or high; jejunal culture; treat the cause plus antibiotics.
6. Tropical sprue: tropical exposure, B12 and folate low, tetracycline for 3 to 6 months.
7. Bile acid diarrhoea after ileal resection: cholestyramine.
8. Pancreatic insufficiency (alcohol, chronic pancreatitis): steatorrhoea, give enzymes.
