# Haemolytic Anaemias

**Year 4 · Paediatrics · Haematology**

> **In one line:** haemolysis is premature destruction of red cells (normal survival 110 to 120 days); anaemia results when destruction outstrips marrow production. Look for reticulocytosis, indirect hyperbilirubinaemia, raised LDH and low haptoglobin, then find the cause: a defect in the cell (membrane, enzyme, haemoglobin) or something acting on it (antibodies, mechanical, toxins, infection).

## What you must know

- Normal red cell survival is **110 to 120 days** (half-life 55 to 60 days). In haemolysis the marrow can raise output **2 to 3 fold acutely and 6 to 8 fold in chronic haemolysis**.
- Markers of haemolysis: **reticulocytosis, raised indirect (unconjugated) bilirubin, raised LDH, low haptoglobin**; free haemoglobin in plasma proves **intravascular** haemolysis.
- First tests: FBC with indices, **peripheral smear, reticulocyte count and direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test**.
- **Hereditary spherocytosis (HS):** autosomal dominant membrane defect (ankyrin, spectrin); spherocytes, **raised MCHC (over 35 g/dL)**, splenomegaly, gallstones; treated by folate and, if needed, splenectomy after age 6.
- Crises in HS: **aplastic (parvovirus B19)**, hypoplastic, megaloblastic (folate deficiency) and sequestration.
- **Hereditary elliptocytosis** is usually asymptomatic; **pyropoikilocytosis** is severe with extreme microcytosis; **Southeast Asian ovalocytosis** protects against cerebral malaria.
- Splenectomy gives lifelong risk of **sepsis from encapsulated organisms**; vaccinate and give prophylactic penicillin.

## 1. Definition and pathophysiology

- **Haemolysis** = premature destruction of RBCs. **Anaemia** develops when the rate of destruction exceeds the rate of production.
- RBC survival shortens, the red cell count falls, **erythropoietin rises**, and the marrow responds with **compensatory erythroid hyperplasia** and a rise in **reticulocytes**.

### Consequences of haemolysis

| Effect | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Reticulocytosis | Compensatory erythroid hyperplasia |
| Bone changes | Expansion of the marrow spaces at the expense of cortical bone, seen in severe chronic haemolysis such as **thalassaemia**: skull and long-bone changes on examination or X-ray; higher risk of long-bone fracture |
| Jaundice and gallstones | Increased haemoglobin breakdown gives **indirect hyperbilirubinaemia**, more biliary excretion of haem pigments and **pigment (bilirubinate) gallstones** |
| Altered plasma haem-binding proteins | Haemoglobin binds **haptoglobin** and **haemopexin** (cleared faster as complexes). Oxidised haem binds albumin to form **methaemalbumin**. When capacity is exceeded, **free haemoglobin appears in plasma**, which is prima facie evidence of **intravascular haemolysis** |
| Haemoglobinuria and iron loss | Free Hb dissociates into dimers and is filtered. When tubular reabsorption is exceeded, Hb appears in urine. Even without haemoglobinuria, iron is lost via renal tubular cell shedding (iron stored as **haemosiderin**), so chronic intravascular haemolysis can cause **iron deficiency** |
| Vascular damage | Persistent free Hb and haemin are linked to **pulmonary hypertension, thrombosis, inflammation** and impaired renal function |

## 2. Classification

| Basis | Categories |
|---|---|
| Site of defect | **Intrinsic (intracorpuscular):** membrane, enzyme or haemoglobin defect. **Extrinsic (extracorpuscular):** antibodies, mechanical factors, plasma factors |
| Origin | Inherited or acquired |
| Mechanism | **Immune** or non-immune |
| Time course | Acute or chronic |
| Site of destruction | **Intravascular** (within vessels) or **extravascular** (reticuloendothelial system: spleen and liver) |

- Most **intrinsic** defects are **inherited**: membrane disorders, metabolic (enzyme) defects, haemoglobinopathies. The exception is **paroxysmal nocturnal haemoglobinuria (PNH)**, which is acquired.
- Most **extrinsic** defects are **acquired**: immune (warm and cold agglutinin haemolysis), and non-immune (systemic disease, drugs and toxins, mechanical destruction).

| Group | Examples |
|---|---|
| Membrane | Hereditary spherocytosis, elliptocytosis, pyropoikilocytosis, ovalocytosis, stomatocytosis |
| Enzyme | G6PD deficiency, pyruvate kinase deficiency |
| Haemoglobin | Sickle cell disease, thalassaemias |
| Immune | Autoimmune haemolytic anaemia (warm, cold), haemolytic disease of the newborn, transfusion reaction |
| Non-immune acquired | Malaria, sepsis, microangiopathic (HUS, TTP, DIC), drugs and toxins, burns |

> **Remember:** in Kenya, always consider **malaria, sickle cell disease, G6PD deficiency (drug-induced) and thalassaemia** in a child with haemolysis.

## 3. Evaluation

- **History:** current illness, co-existing diagnoses, past history, **family history** (anaemia, jaundice, splenectomy, gallstones), medications and recent exposures.
- **Tests:** FBC with indices, **peripheral smear**, **reticulocyte count**, **direct antiglobulin test (DAT)**.
- **Evidence of haemolysis:** raised bilirubin (indirect), raised **LDH**, low **haptoglobin**.
- Further tests as indicated: G6PD assay, haemoglobin electrophoresis, osmotic fragility or EMA binding, abdominal ultrasound for spleen and gallstones.

## 4. Hereditary spherocytosis (HS)

### 4.1 Overview

- A common inherited haemolytic anaemia: prevalence about **1 in 2,000 to 5,000**. It is the commonest inherited red cell abnormality causing haemolysis in people of **Northern European** origin.
- Very variable clinically: from asymptomatic with compensated anaemia to severe anaemia needing regular transfusions.

### 4.2 Pathophysiology

- Two parts: an **intrinsic membrane defect** plus an **intact spleen** that selectively retains, damages and removes the abnormal cells.
- Qualitative or quantitative defects of membrane proteins (most often **ankyrin or spectrin**) cause loss of membrane surface area without a proportional loss of volume. The cell becomes a sphere with **reduced deformability**, cannot pass from the splenic cords to the sinuses, and is trapped and destroyed.
- **Splenectomy greatly improves red cell survival.**
- HS is a defect of the **vertical interactions** coupling the membrane skeleton to the lipid bilayer.

### 4.3 Genetics

- Usually **autosomal dominant**. About **25% have no family history** (recessive inheritance or de novo mutation).

### 4.4 Clinical features

- **Neonatal period:** a significant cause of haemolysis; anaemia and or hyperbilirubinaemia severe enough to need phototherapy, transfusion or exchange transfusion. Haemolysis is worse in newborns because **HbF binds 2,3-DPG poorly**, and the excess free 2,3-DPG destabilises the spectrin-actin-protein 4.1 interaction. Transfusion need in infancy does **not** predict severe disease later, as infants mount a reticulocyte response slowly in the first months.
- **Severity classes:**

| Class | Share of HS | Features |
|---|---|---|
| Mild | 20 to 30% | Asymptomatic into adulthood; mild well-compensated anaemia |
| Moderate ("typical") | 60 to 70% | Partially compensated haemolysis with reticulocytosis; fatigue, pallor, intermittent jaundice |
| Severe | 3 to 5% | Life-threatening anaemia, transfusion dependent |

- **Splenomegaly** is common after infancy and present in almost all by young adulthood (uncommon in neonates).
- **Gallstones** (bilirubin stones) increase with age: as early as 4 to 5 years, and present in most adults with HS.

### 4.5 Complications

- **Aplastic crisis:** mainly **parvovirus B19**. High turnover plus marrow failure can cause profound anaemia (haematocrit under 10%), **high-output heart failure**, collapse and death; white cells and platelets may also fall.
- **Hypoplastic crises** with other infections; **megaloblastic crisis** from folate deficiency.
- Rare: splenic sequestration crisis, gout, cardiomyopathy, priapism, leg ulcers, neurological or muscular problems (e.g. spinocerebellar degeneration).

### 4.6 Diagnosis

- Haemolytic anaemia with **reticulocytosis** and indirect hyperbilirubinaemia.
- **MCV low-normal or slightly low; MCHC raised (over 35 g/dL).** MCHC over 35.4 g/dL with RDW under 14% has been suggested as a screening test.
- **Smear:** spherocytes (smaller, dense, hyperchromic from cell dehydration, **no central pallor**) and polychromatic reticulocytes. More spherocytes generally means more severe disease.
- Low haptoglobin, raised LDH. **DAT negative.**
- Special tests: osmotic fragility (incubated), EMA binding by flow cytometry.

> **Exam tip:** HS is the leading cause of **Coombs-negative** haemolytic anaemia needing transfusion in the first months of life.

### 4.7 Differential diagnosis of spherocytes

- Neonates need a high index of suspicion, especially de novo or recessive cases without family history. Jaundice is frequent and **kernicterus** can occur.
- **ABO incompatibility** (isoimmune haemolytic disease) closely mimics HS; a **positive DAT** establishes immune haemolysis.
- **Autoimmune haemolytic anaemia** also gives spherocytes, but there is a previous normal Hb, haematocrit and reticulocyte count and the DAT is positive.
- HS may be masked by iron, folate or B12 deficiency, cholestatic liver disease, HbSC disease or beta-thalassaemia.
- Rare causes of transient spherocytic haemolysis: thermal injury, haemolytic transfusion reaction, clostridial sepsis, severe hypophosphataemia, Wilson disease, congenital dyserythropoietic anaemia type II, hereditary stomatocytosis, snake, bee or wasp envenomation.

### 4.8 Management

**Supportive care**

- Infants of a parent with HS must be watched closely: hyperbilirubinaemia may **peak several days after birth**. Counsel parents about neonatal anaemia, jaundice and possible phototherapy, transfusion or exchange transfusion.
- A few infants stay transfusion dependent until adequate erythropoiesis develops, usually by **6 to 12 months**. Dependence after that is rare and usually means recessive HS.
- Once baseline severity is known, an annual haematology visit is usually enough.
- Monitor growth, exercise tolerance and spleen size; keep vaccinations up to date.
- **Screen for gallbladder disease from about age 4**, repeating every 3 to 5 years or as indicated.
- Document parvovirus B19 immunity in new patients; document HIV and hepatitis serology in those transfused.
- **Folic acid** supplementation for moderate and severe HS.
- Anticipatory guidance on aplastic crisis (parvovirus) and hypoplastic crises with other infections.

**Splenectomy**

- Curative in most: haemolysis, anaemia, hyperbilirubinaemia and gallstones are greatly reduced or eliminated.
- Risks: operative risks and **lifelong increased risk of sepsis** from encapsulated bacteria (pneumococcus, meningococcus, *Haemophilus influenzae* type b), not fully removed by vaccination. Extra risk if far from medical care, or living in or travelling to areas with **malaria or babesiosis**.
- Decide jointly with the family after weighing risks and benefits.

| Point | Guidance |
|---|---|
| Mild HS | Splenectomy generally **not** recommended |
| Timing | After **age 6 years** if possible, to avoid the high risk of overwhelming post-splenectomy infection in younger children |
| Technique | **Laparoscopic** is preferred (less morbidity) |
| Partial (subtotal) splenectomy | Removes 85 to 95% of spleen volume; reduces haemolysis (less than total) while keeping some phagocytic function; attractive in severe HS needing frequent early transfusion |
| Gallstones | Do **cholecystectomy** at the same time if stones present |
| Platelets | Post-splenectomy thrombocytosis is common, needs no treatment and resolves |
| Prevention | Vaccinate patient, household and close contacts; prophylactic antibiotics at least until age 5 or at least 2 years after splenectomy; continue folate if Hb and reticulocytes do not normalise |

- **Splenectomy failure** (haemolysis returns, Howell-Jolly bodies disappear from the smear): accessory spleen, splenic autotransplantation into the peritoneum, wrong diagnosis, or another co-inherited haemolytic disorder. Confirm with **radionuclide studies**.

> **Remember:** Howell-Jolly bodies on the smear confirm functional asplenia after splenectomy.

## 5. Hereditary elliptocytosis (HE), pyropoikilocytosis (HPP) and ovalocytosis (SAO)

All show abnormal shapes on smear. HS affects **vertical** interactions; HE and related disorders affect **horizontal** interactions (spectrin-spectrin and junctional complexes), leaving the cell vulnerable to shear stress.

| Feature | HE | HPP | SAO |
|---|---|---|---|
| Smear | Elliptocytes, normochromic normocytic | Microspherocytes, fragments, occasional elliptocytes; MCV 50 to 65 fL | Ovalocytes (plumper than elliptocytes), some with 1 or 2 transverse ridges |
| Genetics | Autosomal dominant (occasional de novo); mutations in alpha or beta spectrin (impaired dimer-to-tetramer assembly); less often protein 4.1 or glycophorin C | Two abnormal spectrin alleles: self-association mutation from a parent with mild HE plus a spectrin-deficient allele from the other | Autosomal dominant; in-frame 9 amino-acid deletion in **band 3** |
| Clinical | Most have no significant haemolysis (incidental finding); about 10% have haemolytic HE; worse in infancy | Severe, chronic haemolysis; mostly in people of African descent | Neonatal hyperbilirubinaemia; little to no haemolysis later |
| Geography | Worldwide, commoner where malaria is endemic | African descent | New Guinea, Malaysia, Indonesia, Philippines |
| Malaria | Spectrin-mutant cells resist malaria in vitro | | Protects against malaria, especially **cerebral malaria** |

- HE is much commoner than HS but far less symptomatic. HE and HPP co-segregate in families.
- In the newborn, elliptocytes may be absent in the first months; even in hemolytic HE the smear shows bizarre poikilocytes and pyknocytes. HbF binding 2,3-DPG poorly again destabilises the spectrin-actin-protein 4.1 complex.
- Chronic haemolytic HE: anaemia, jaundice, splenomegaly; later gallstones; occasional aplastic crises.
- **Diagnosis of HE:** smear, autosomal dominant inheritance, and exclusion of other causes (iron, folate, B12 deficiency, thalassaemia, myelodysplasia, pyruvate kinase deficiency). No diagnostic cut-off for percentage of elliptocytes. Use reticulocytes, bilirubin, LDH and haptoglobin to judge severity; EMA binding or incubated osmotic fragility in haemolytic HE and HPP; ultrasound for spleen and gallstones.
- **Treatment:** typical HE needs none. Chronic HE and HPP sometimes need transfusion; splenectomy criteria are like HS; give folic acid if haemolysis continues after splenectomy. SAO needs no treatment beyond the newborn period.

## 6. Hereditary stomatocytosis syndromes

Dominantly inherited disorders of red cell cation permeability, changing intracellular water. Net gain of Na and K draws in water (stomatocytes, **hydrocytes**); net loss draws water out (dehydrated cells, **xerocytes**).

| | Hereditary xerocytosis (HX) | Hereditary hydrocytosis |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency | Commonest stomatocytosis | Very rare |
| Defect | **PIEZO1** mutation (delayed channel inactivation); rarely Gardos channel. Net K loss without compensating Na gain, causing dehydration | Increased Na and K permeability, with high intracellular Na and water; sometimes RhAG mutation |
| Features | May present as hydrops fetalis with perinatal anaemia and ascites (transient, unexplained) | **Most severe** volume disorder: moderate to severe haemolysis, macrocytosis (110 to 150 fL), low MCHC (24 to 30%), 10 to 30% stomatocytes, raised osmotic fragility; jaundice, splenomegaly, gallstones |
| Treatment | Supportive | Supportive, occasional transfusion, folate daily, ultrasound for gallstones. **Avoid splenectomy** (post-splenectomy major thromboses) |

## Key points to revise

1. Haemolysis shortens red cell survival from 110 to 120 days; the marrow compensates up to 6 to 8 fold with reticulocytosis.
2. Markers: reticulocytosis, indirect hyperbilirubinaemia, high LDH, low haptoglobin; free plasma Hb = intravascular haemolysis; DAT separates immune from non-immune.
3. Intrinsic defects are inherited (except PNH); extrinsic defects are mostly acquired.
4. HS: dominant ankyrin or spectrin defect, spherocytes, MCHC over 35, DAT negative, gallstones, splenomegaly.
5. Parvovirus B19 causes aplastic crisis in haemolytic anaemias; give folate for chronic haemolysis.
6. Splenectomy for HS: after age 6, laparoscopic, with cholecystectomy if stones, vaccines and prophylactic antibiotics; not for mild HS.
7. HE is usually benign; HPP is severe with MCV 50 to 65 fL; SAO is a band 3 defect protecting against cerebral malaria.
8. Hydrocytosis and xerocytosis: avoid splenectomy because of thrombotic risk.
