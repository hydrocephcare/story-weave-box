# Addison Disease (Primary Adrenal Insufficiency in Children)

**Year 4 · Paediatrics · Paediatric Endocrinology**

> **In one line:** Addison disease is failure of the whole adrenal cortex, so the child loses both **cortisol** (glucocorticoid) and **aldosterone** (mineralocorticoid). The result is hypotension, hyponatraemia, hyperkalaemia, hypoglycaemia and, because ACTH is no longer suppressed, hyperpigmentation. Minor illness can tip the child into a life-threatening adrenal crisis, which is treated at once with IV hydrocortisone and saline with glucose.

## What you must know

- Symptoms appear when **90% or more** of both adrenal cortices are destroyed or dysfunctional.
- In **infants** the commonest cause is **salt-losing congenital adrenal hyperplasia** (CAH). Overall, the commonest cause is **autoimmune destruction**.
- Cortisol deficiency: hypotension, hypoglycaemia, ketosis, fatigue. Aldosterone deficiency: hypovolaemia, **hyponatraemia, hyperkalaemia**, salt craving.
- **High ACTH** causes hyperpigmentation (skin creases, scars, gums, buccal mucosa). It is usually absent in infants.
- Hyponatraemia is present in about 90% at diagnosis; hyperkalaemia in only about half of older children. **A normal potassium never excludes Addison disease.**
- Diagnosis: low cortisol, high ACTH, high renin, and a **failed response to the ACTH (cosyntropin) stimulation test**.
- Crisis: **5% glucose in 0.9% saline** plus **IV hydrocortisone**. Avoid hypotonic fluids.
- Long-term: hydrocortisone 10 mg/m2/day in 3 divided doses, fludrocortisone if aldosterone is deficient, and **stress doses** during illness.

## 1. Definition and background

- **Addison disease** is adrenocortical insufficiency due to destruction or dysfunction of the **entire adrenal cortex**, affecting glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid function.
- The medulla is usually spared (so adrenaline and noradrenaline secretion is preserved).
- Normal regulation: hypothalamus (CRH) stimulates pituitary ACTH, which stimulates cortisol from the cortex. Cortisol feeds back negatively on both hypothalamus and pituitary. Aldosterone is controlled mainly by the renin-angiotensin system and potassium, not ACTH.
- **Primary** insufficiency (adrenal gland failure) gives high ACTH. **Secondary** (pituitary) and **tertiary** (hypothalamic or steroid withdrawal) give low ACTH, and aldosterone is preserved.
- In children, primary insufficiency is mostly **genetic**, often (but not always) evident in infancy, and less often **acquired**, e.g. autoimmune. Autoimmunity itself has a genetic basis, so the distinction is not absolute.

## 2. Causes

### 2.1 Inherited causes

| Cause | Key features |
|---|---|
| **Salt-losing CAH** | Commonest cause of adrenal insufficiency in infancy. About **75% of infants with 21-hydroxylase deficiency**, almost all with **lipoid adrenal hyperplasia** and most with **3-beta-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase deficiency** have salt loss in the newborn period (cannot make cortisol or aldosterone). |
| **Adrenal hypoplasia congenita (AHC)** | Relatively frequent cause of adrenal failure in males. Failure of the **definitive zone**; the fetal zone may be normal. Adrenal failure shows as the fetal zone involutes: infancy or first 2 years, occasionally later. Aldosterone deficiency may precede cortisol deficiency. **DAX1 (NR0B1) mutation, Xp21.** Same gene causes **hypogonadotropic hypogonadism** (no puberty). Cryptorchidism may occur, but infant testicular function is often normal, with a normal or prolonged testosterone surge in the first month. Part of a **contiguous gene deletion** with Duchenne muscular dystrophy, glycerol kinase deficiency, cognitive impairment. |
| **SF-1 (NR5A1) mutations** | SF-1 is needed for adrenal and gonadal development. Heterozygous males have impaired testis development and may look female (like lipoid CAH). Adrenal insufficiency is rare. |
| **Pallister-Hall syndrome** | GLI3 mutation; occasional adrenal hypoplasia. |
| **X-linked adrenoleukodystrophy (ALD)** | See below. |
| **Neonatal ALD** | Autosomal recessive, subset of **Zellweger (cerebrohepatorenal) syndrome**. Peroxisomes fail to develop (PEX1, PEX5, PEX10, PEX13, PEX26). Neurological deterioration plus adrenal dysfunction; severe progressive cognitive impairment, death before 5 years. |
| **Familial glucocorticoid deficiency** | Autosomal recessive, both sexes. **Isolated cortisol deficiency, high ACTH, normal aldosterone** (salt loss occasionally). Hypoglycaemia, seizures, hyperpigmentation in the first decade. Marked cortical atrophy with relative sparing of the zona glomerulosa. Genes: **MC2R (ACTH receptor)** about 25%; **MRAP** about 20%; also MCM4 (growth failure, chromosome breakage, NK cell deficiency) and NNT (antioxidant defence). |
| **Triple A (Allgrove) syndrome** | ACTH resistance with **achalasia of the gastric cardia** and **alacrima**. Progressive neurological disease: autonomic dysfunction, intellectual disability, motor neuropathy, occasional deafness. Autosomal recessive, AAAS gene (12q13), protein aladin. |
| **Cholesterol synthesis/metabolism disorders** | Abetalipoproteinaemia and homozygous familial hypercholesterolaemia: mildly impaired adrenal function. Heterozygous FH: normal, unaffected by statins. **Smith-Lemli-Opitz** (sterol delta-7-reductase, 11q12-q13; facial anomalies, microcephaly, limb anomalies, developmental delay; high 7-dehydrocholesterol, low cholesterol). **Wolman disease** (lysosomal acid lipase, 10q23.2-23.3; cholesteryl ester accumulation; hepatosplenomegaly, steatorrhoea, distension, failure to thrive; **bilateral adrenal calcification**; death in first year). |
| **CBG deficiency** | Low total plasma cortisol but **normal urinary free cortisol and ACTH**. Hypotension and fatigue reported in some adults. |

### X-linked adrenoleukodystrophy

- Adrenocortical deficiency plus **CNS demyelination**; **very-long-chain fatty acids (VLCFA)** accumulate because of impaired peroxisomal beta-oxidation.
- Gene **ABCD1 (Xq28)**, encoding a peroxisomal transporter of VLCFA.
- Commonest picture: childhood or adolescent degenerative neurological disease progressing to severe dementia, loss of vision, hearing, speech and gait, death within a few years.
- Early signs are subtle: **behaviour change, falling school performance**. Generalised incomplete alopecia (like chemotherapy) is characteristic but inconsistent.
- Milder form: **adrenomyeloneuropathy** (late adolescence or early adulthood).
- Adrenal insufficiency can precede, coincide with, or follow neurological symptoms by years. **No correlation** between neurological and adrenal severity.
- Female heterozygous carriers may develop symptoms in midlife; adrenal insufficiency is rare.
- Prenatal DNA diagnosis and family screening (VLCFA assay, mutation analysis) are available.

### 2.2 Acquired causes

- **Autoimmune Addison disease (commonest overall).** Glands are tiny, with only remnants on microscopy, **lymphocytic infiltration**, medulla spared. Early on, isolated cortisol deficiency can occur. Most have **anti-adrenal cytoplasmic antibodies**; **21-hydroxylase (CYP21)** is the commonest autoantigen (also CYP17, CYP11A1). Most common in middle-aged women, can run in families. Also seen with coeliac disease and mitochondrial gene mutations. Polymorphisms (CIITA, CLEC16A, PTPN22) are inconsistently associated.
- **Infection.**
  - **Tuberculosis**: once common, now much less so (still relevant in Kenya, so consider it).
  - **Meningococcaemia**: the commonest infectious cause; adrenal haemorrhage and crisis is **Waterhouse-Friderichsen syndrome**.
  - **HIV/AIDS**: subclinical HPA axis abnormalities common, frank insufficiency rare; drugs used may alter adrenal hormones.
- **Drugs.** **Ketoconazole** (inhibits adrenal enzymes); **mitotane** (cytotoxic to adrenal cortex; used for adrenocortical carcinoma and refractory Cushing syndrome); **etomidate** (inhibits 11-beta-hydroxylase; a single induction dose blocks cortisol synthesis for 4 to 8 hours or more, a problem in stressed or ICU patients); **abiraterone** (prostate cancer, not seen in paediatrics). **Rifampicin, phenytoin and phenobarbital** are not causes but induce liver enzymes and **reduce the effect of replacement steroids**.
- **Adrenal haemorrhage.** Neonatal, after difficult labour (especially breech), about 3 per 100,000 live births; can be fetal. May cause death from exsanguination or hypoadrenalism. Presents with abdominal mass, anaemia, unexplained jaundice or **scrotal haematoma**; sometimes silent and found later as adrenal calcification. Postnatally, mostly in patients on **anticoagulants**, or from **child abuse**.

### 2.3 Autoimmune polyendocrinopathy syndromes

| Feature | APS-1 (APECED) | APS-2 |
|---|---|---|
| Inheritance | Autosomal recessive (mendelian) | Complex; HLA-DR3/DR4 (HLA-D3, D4) and MICA/MICB associations |
| Components | **Chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis** (usually first), then **hypoparathyroidism**, then **Addison disease** (early adolescence). Also gonadal failure, alopecia, vitiligo, keratopathy, enamel hypoplasia, nail dystrophy, malabsorption, chronic active hepatitis. Hypothyroidism and type 1 diabetes in under 10% | Addison disease with **autoimmune thyroid disease (Schmidt syndrome)** or **type 1 diabetes (Carpenter syndrome)**. Gonadal failure, vitiligo, alopecia, atrophic gastritis +/- pernicious anaemia |

- Components of APS-1 can keep appearing until the 5th decade.
- Antibodies to CYP21, CYP17 and CYP11A1 predict Addison disease, or ovarian failure in girls.
- Adrenal failure can evolve **rapidly** in APS-1; deaths and unexplained sibling deaths are reported. Monitor closely any child with APS-1 (or **hypoparathyroidism of unknown cause**) and screen apparently unaffected siblings.

## 3. Pathophysiology and clinical features

### 3.1 Why the features occur

- **Cortisol deficiency**: lower cardiac output and vascular tone; catecholamines lose their inotropic and pressor effect. Gives **orthostatic hypotension** in older children and **shock** at any age. Also hypoglycaemia (less gluconeogenesis), and ketosis as fat is used for fuel (worse with anorexia and vomiting).
- **Aldosterone deficiency**: less sodium resorption in the distal nephron gives hypovolaemia (worsening hypotension), hyponatraemia and **hyperkalaemia** (less potassium excretion). Cortisol deficiency alone does **not** cause hyperkalaemia.
- **Hyponatraemia** is multifactorial: hypotension and low cardiac output reduce GFR and free water excretion; **ADH** rises (response to hypotension, and loss of cortisol inhibition); aldosterone loss adds to it.
- **Raised ACTH** (loss of negative feedback) and gamma-MSH from the precursor **pro-opiomelanocortin** cause **hyperpigmentation**: bronze skin in fair children; skin creases, mucosa and scars; gums and buccal mucosa in dark-skinned children.

### 3.2 Presentation by age

| | Infants | Older children |
|---|---|---|
| Aldosterone need | Relatively greater (immature kidney, low sodium in breast milk and formula) | Less |
| Main findings | **Hyperkalaemia, hyponatraemia, hypoglycaemia** | Muscle weakness, malaise, anorexia, vomiting, weight loss, orthostatic hypotension (often insidious) |
| Ketosis | Not consistent (infants make ketones poorly) | Common |
| Pigmentation | Usually absent (takes weeks to months) | Often but not always |
| Course | Can deteriorate within days (reduced activity, anorexia, vomiting) | May decompensate in a minor infection |

- In older children, hyponatraemia is present in about 90%, hyperkalaemia in about 50% at diagnosis.
- Other features: **salt craving** (mineralocorticoid deficiency); with glucocorticoid deficiency fatigue, myalgia, fever, **eosinophilia, lymphocytosis, hypercalcaemia, anaemia**.
- Usually presents or is precipitated by **stress**: infection, trauma, surgery, vomiting, diarrhoea or **missed replacement steroids** (**adrenal crisis**).
- Frequently misdiagnosed as chronic fatigue, post-mononucleosis syndrome, chronic Lyme disease, depression or anorexia nervosa, or as gastroenteritis.

> **Exam tip:** Think of Addison disease in any child with orthostatic hypotension, hyponatraemia, hypoglycaemia and ketosis. Never use a normal potassium to rule it out.

## 4. Investigations

- **Urgent bedside:** glucose; **ECG** for hyperkalaemia.
- **Biochemistry:** low sodium, high potassium, acidosis, raised urea if dehydrated, low glucose, hypercalcaemia possible.
- **Cortisol:** may be low-normal, but is invariably too low for the degree of illness.
- **ACTH:** high in primary insufficiency (result may be delayed).
- **Aldosterone:** low or inappropriately normal; **plasma renin activity raised**.
- **ACTH (cosyntropin) stimulation test** (the definitive test): cortisol before and 30 or 60 minutes after **0.25 mg cosyntropin (ACTH 1-24) IV**. Resting cortisol is low and fails to rise. Normal resting level with no rise means no adrenal reserve. Low baseline with a good rise suggests **secondary** insufficiency. Aldosterone transiently rises and can also be measured. The **low-dose test (1 microgram/1.73 m2)** is more sensitive for reserve but less specific (more false positives).
- **Imaging:** ultrasound (needs an experienced operator), CT or MRI to assess adrenal size (small in autoimmune disease; calcified in TB, haemorrhage or Wolman).
- **Aetiological tests:**
  - **17-hydroxyprogesterone** with cortisol in the stimulation test if CAH is suspected.
  - **VLCFA**: elevated is diagnostic of ALD.
  - **Anti-adrenal (21-hydroxylase) antibodies**: autoimmune cause.
  - Genetic testing (results can take many weeks).
  - Screen for other autoimmunity; **hypoparathyroidism** is the commonest associated disorder in children (low calcium, high phosphate).

> **Remember:** Take blood for electrolytes, glucose, ACTH, cortisol, aldosterone and renin **before** giving steroids if the diagnosis is not established, but do not delay treatment waiting for results.

## 5. Differential diagnosis

- Gastroenteritis with dehydration, sepsis, other acute infections (the commonest confusions).
- CAH (especially in neonates), secondary adrenal insufficiency (hypopituitarism, steroid withdrawal), pyloric stenosis in infants (vomiting, but alkalosis and hypokalaemia).
- Diabetic ketoacidosis, renal tubular disorders and pseudohypoaldosteronism (salt loss with high renin and high aldosterone).
- Chronic fatigue, depression and anorexia nervosa in the insidious form.

## 6. Management

### 6.1 Acute adrenal insufficiency (crisis): immediate and vigorous

1. **Resuscitate (ABC)** and take blood for baseline tests as above; do an ACTH stimulation test during fluid resuscitation if the patient is stable enough.
2. **Fluids:** IV **5% glucose in 0.9% saline** to correct hypoglycaemia, hypovolaemia and hyponatraemia. **Avoid hypotonic fluids** (5% glucose in water, 0.2% saline) which worsen hyponatraemia.
3. **Hydrocortisone sodium succinate IV** (water-soluble): bolus of about **10 mg infants, 25 mg toddlers, 50 mg older children, 100 mg adolescents**, then a similar total amount in divided doses **6-hourly for the first 24 hours**; reduce over the next 24 hours if improving. High-dose hydrocortisone has a mineralocorticoid effect, helping salt repletion.
4. **Severe hyperkalaemia:** IV calcium and/or bicarbonate, rectal sodium polystyrene sulfonate (Kayexalate), or IV glucose with insulin.
5. Treat the precipitant (infection) and monitor glucose, electrolytes and ECG.

> **Exam tip:** In a patient with both adrenal insufficiency and hypothyroidism, give glucocorticoid **before** thyroxine. Thyroxine increases cortisol clearance and can precipitate crisis.

### 6.2 Chronic replacement

| Drug | Dose and notes |
|---|---|
| **Hydrocortisone** (oral) | **10 mg/m2/24 h in 3 divided doses**; some need 15 mg/m2/24 h to reduce morning fatigue |
| Prednisone/prednisolone | 20 to 25% of the hydrocortisone dose, divided twice daily |
| **Fludrocortisone** (if aldosterone deficient) | **0.05 to 0.2 mg daily** |

- **Monitoring:** ACTH in primary insufficiency (aim for a morning level from high-normal up to 3 to 4 times normal); precursor hormones in CAH; **plasma renin activity** for mineralocorticoid adequacy. Sample at a consistent time in relation to the hydrocortisone dose. Symptoms alone are not a safe guide; under-treated children decompensate in minor illness.
- **Sick-day rules:** increase hydrocortisone **2 to 3 fold** during infections and minor procedures. **Major surgery** under general anaesthesia needs high IV hydrocortisone as for crisis. Give injectable hydrocortisone if vomiting, and teach parents; provide a steroid alert card or bracelet.
- **Overdose:** glucocorticoids cause obesity, short stature and osteoporosis; fludrocortisone causes hypertension and occasionally hypokalaemia.
- Enzyme-inducing drugs (rifampicin, phenytoin, phenobarbital) reduce steroid effect; doses may need to rise.
- Screen over time for other autoimmune disease; follow up family members in APS-1 and ALD.

## 7. Complications and prognosis

- Adrenal crisis, shock, hypoglycaemic seizures, death if untreated.
- Complications of long-term steroids (growth, bone, weight) if overtreated.
- Prognosis is good with lifelong replacement and good education. In ALD, the neurological course determines outcome; neonatal ALD and Wolman disease are fatal early.

## Key points to revise

1. Addison disease is destruction of the whole adrenal cortex; symptoms appear after 90% is lost.
2. Infants: salt-losing CAH (21-hydroxylase deficiency). Overall commonest cause: autoimmune (anti-21-hydroxylase antibodies). Remember TB, meningococcal (Waterhouse-Friderichsen), ALD and AHC (DAX1).
3. Cortisol lack: hypotension, hypoglycaemia, ketosis. Aldosterone lack: hyponatraemia, hyperkalaemia, hypovolaemia, salt craving.
4. High ACTH causes hyperpigmentation; it is usually absent in infants.
5. Low cortisol, high ACTH and renin, and a failed cosyntropin test confirm the diagnosis; take samples before treatment.
6. Crisis: 5% glucose in 0.9% saline, IV hydrocortisone (10, 25, 50, 100 mg by age), treat hyperkalaemia, avoid hypotonic fluids.
7. Maintenance: hydrocortisone 10 mg/m2/day in 3 doses, fludrocortisone 0.05 to 0.2 mg daily, double or triple doses in illness.
8. Look for APS-1 (candidiasis, hypoparathyroidism, Addison) and APS-2 (thyroid, type 1 diabetes).
