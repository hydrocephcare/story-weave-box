# Malaria in Children

**Year 4 · Paediatrics · Tropical and infectious diseases**

> **In one line:** malaria is a protozoal infection of red cells by *Plasmodium* species, transmitted by the bite of the female **Anopheles** mosquito; it causes periodic fever, anaemia and splenomegaly, and **falciparum malaria** can progress within hours to cerebral malaria, severe anaemia, hypoglycaemia, acidosis and death, which is why prompt diagnosis and treatment matter.

## What you must know

- Malaria is the world's most devastating human parasitic infection; the WHO estimates hundreds of millions of cases a year, with the great burden in sub-Saharan Africa and in **children under 5**.
- Humans are the **intermediate host** (asexual cycle, schizogony); the female Anopheles is the **definitive host** (sexual cycle, sporogony). The infective form is the **sporozoite**.
- ***P. vivax* and *P. ovale*** form **hypnozoites** in the liver and cause **relapses**; ***P. falciparum*** and ***P. malariae*** do not (falciparum recrudesces).
- Fever pattern: **tertian** (48 h) for vivax/ovale, **quartan** (72 h) for malariae, **irregular** for falciparum.
- Diagnosis: **thick and thin blood smear** (gold standard), **RDT** (HRP2 for falciparum), PCR.
- Treatment: **artemether-lumefantrine** for uncomplicated falciparum; **IV artesunate** for severe malaria; **chloroquine + primaquine** for vivax, ovale and malariae (check G6PD).
- Severe malaria is a medical emergency: correct hypoglycaemia, convulsions, anaemia, acidosis and shock, and monitor blood glucose.

## 1. Epidemiology

Malaria affects over 40% of the world's population. In Kenya it is endemic around Lake Victoria and the coast, with epidemic-prone highland areas; most severe disease occurs in children under 5 and pregnant women. (The slide figure for India is 2 million cases and 1000 deaths a year.)

## 2. Life cycle

Two phases in two hosts:

| Phase | Where | Name |
|---|---|---|
| **Asexual** (endogenous) | Human | **Schizogony** (human is the intermediate host) |
| **Sexual** (exogenous) | Female Anopheles | **Sporogony** (mosquito is the definitive host) |

### Human cycle

1. **Pre-erythrocytic schizogony.** Sporozoites from the mosquito's salivary gland enter the blood at the bite and invade liver cells. Multiple nuclear divisions give **schizonts**, each holding **20,000 to 50,000 merozoites**. The liver cell ruptures and releases merozoites into the blood.

| Species | Pre-erythrocytic period |
|---|---|
| *P. falciparum* | 6 days |
| *P. vivax* | 8 days |
| *P. ovale* | 9 days |
| *P. malariae* | 13 to 16 days |

2. **Erythrocytic schizogony.** Merozoites (pear-shaped, 1 to 5 microns) attach to glycoprotein receptors on red cells and invade. Three stages: **trophozoite, schizont, merozoite**. Red cell rupture releases new merozoites (and toxins) and causes the fever.

| Species | Red cells infected |
|---|---|
| *P. vivax* | Young red cells (reticulocytes) |
| *P. malariae* | Old red cells |
| *P. falciparum* | Red cells of all ages (so very high parasitaemia) |

3. **Exo-erythrocytic (tissue) phase.** *P. vivax* and *P. ovale* sporozoites may become dormant **hypnozoites** in the liver and reactivate months to years later (relapse). *P. malariae* and *P. falciparum* sporozoites do not form hypnozoites; they develop straight into pre-erythrocytic schizonts.
4. **Gametogony.** Some merozoites become male (micro-) and female (macro-) **gametocytes** inside red cells; they appear in the peripheral blood. Macrogametocytes are larger.

### Mosquito cycle (sporogony)

The mosquito takes up gametocytes with its blood meal. Fertilisation (microgametocyte enters macrogametocyte) gives a **zygote**, which becomes an **ookinete**, then an **oocyst**, which matures and releases hundreds to thousands of **sporozoites** that migrate to the salivary glands.

## 3. Clinical features

**Incubation:** about 9 to 14 days (10 to 15 days quoted for first symptoms). First symptoms: fever, headache, chills, vomiting. Untreated, malaria can become severe and fatal.

### The paroxysm

| Stage | Features |
|---|---|
| **1. Cold stage** | Chills for 15 minutes to 1 hour as merozoites burst from red cells; nausea, vomiting, headache |
| **2. Hot stage** | Fever up to about 40 °C lasting hours, as newly released merozoites invade fresh cells |
| **3. Sweating stage** | Profuse sweating ends the episode |

Cycles are often asynchronous early on. In *P. malariae*, fever may last 8 hours or more and exceed 41 °C. Other symptoms: body aches, general malaise.

> **Remember (periodicity):** *malaria tertiana* = 48 h between fevers (*P. vivax*, *P. ovale*); *malaria quartana* = 72 h (*P. malariae*); *malaria tropica* = irregular high fever (*P. falciparum*). In children, especially in endemic areas, fever may be the only sign and the pattern is rarely classical.

### Duration of infection

- Untreated *P. falciparum* clears in less than a year.
- *P. vivax* and *P. ovale* persist as hypnozoites after parasites vanish from blood and can relapse for up to about 5 years.
- *P. malariae* can persist for up to 40 years at low level (recrudescence).
- **Relapse** = reactivation of liver hypnozoites. **Recrudescence** = return of parasites that survived in red cells.

## 4. Severe (complicated) falciparum malaria

Defined by detection of *P. falciparum* in the blood **plus** any of the following. Typically confusion or drowsiness with extreme weakness (prostration).

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Altered consciousness | Drowsiness to deep coma |
| **Cerebral malaria** | Unrousable coma not due to any other cause |
| Respiratory distress | Metabolic acidosis (bicarbonate below 15 mEq/L) |
| Multiple convulsions | 2 or more in 24 hours |
| Shock | Circulatory collapse, septicaemia ("algid malaria") |
| Pulmonary oedema | |
| Abnormal bleeding | DIC |
| Jaundice | |
| **Haemoglobinuria** | Black water fever |
| Acute renal failure | Oliguria or anuria |
| **Severe anaemia** | Hb below 5 g/dL or haematocrit below 15% |
| High fever | |
| **Hypoglycaemia** | Blood glucose below 2.2 mmol/L |

> **Check:** the management slide uses glucose below 2.8 mmol/L and Hb below 7 g/dL. The WHO definition of severe malaria uses glucose below 2.2 mmol/L (treat below 3 mmol/L in practice) and Hb below 5 g/dL (haematocrit below 15%) in children; treat anaemia by transfusion at Hb below 5 or below 7 with signs of decompensation.

### Why falciparum is dangerous

Fatal complications: cerebral malaria, hyperpyrexia, gastrointestinal disorders, algid malaria (shock), and black water fever. **Pernicious malaria** is the life-threatening form of acute falciparum infection due to heavy parasitisation, presenting as:

1. **Cerebral malaria**: hyperpyrexia, coma and paralysis; the brain is congested.
2. **Algid malaria**: clammy skin and peripheral circulatory failure.

### Pathogenesis of cerebral malaria

- High **cytokine** levels (TNF) may be toxic and also enhance **sequestration** of infected red cells.
- Infected cells adhere to capillary endothelium (**cytoadherence**) and to other red cells (**rosetting**) and to each other (**clumping**), blocking the microvasculature of the brain.

### Black water fever

Massive intravascular haemolysis in falciparum malaria; haemoglobin appears in the urine, giving dark, **cola-coloured** urine.

## 5. Laboratory diagnosis

| Test | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Blood film (thick and thin) with Giemsa stain** | **Gold standard.** Thick film finds parasites; thin film identifies the species and parasite density |
| **QBC (quantitative buffy coat)** | Rapid, sensitive; also detects babesiosis, trypanosomiasis, filariasis, relapsing fever |
| **Rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs)** | Immunochromatographic; result in **2 to 15 minutes**. **HRP2** is specific for *P. falciparum*; **pLDH** (OptiMAL) or **aldolase** can distinguish falciparum from non-falciparum |
| **PCR** | Detects parasite nucleic acids; more accurate than microscopy but expensive and needs a specialised laboratory |

> **Check:** the slide ranks antibody detection as most sensitive. Antibody tests show past exposure and are not used for acute diagnosis; PCR is the most sensitive test for current infection.

Other findings: anaemia, thrombocytopenia, raised bilirubin and LDH, hypoglycaemia, acidosis.

## 6. Pharmacology of antimalarials

| Class | Action | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Blood schizonticides** | Kill erythrocytic stage, end clinical illness | Quinine, artemisinins, amodiaquine, chloroquine, lumefantrine, atovaquone, sulphadoxine; (slow-acting, not used alone: tetracycline, clindamycin, proguanil) |
| **Tissue schizonticides** | Act on liver forms, block further development | Primaquine, pyrimethamine, proguanil, tetracycline |
| **Gametocytocidal** | Destroy sexual forms, prevent transmission | Primaquine, artemisinins; quinine (weak) |
| **Hypnozoitocidal** | Kill dormant liver stages of vivax/ovale | Primaquine, tafenoquine |
| **Sporontocidal** | Stop gametocyte development in the mosquito | Primaquine, proguanil, chlorguanil |

## 7. Treatment

### 7.1 Severe falciparum malaria

| | Preferred | Alternative |
|---|---|---|
| Drug | **IV artesunate**: 2.4 mg/kg on admission, then at 12 h, 24 h, then once daily for 7 days (or until oral therapy is tolerated) | **IV quinine**: loading 20 mg/kg over 4 hours, then 10 mg salt/kg 8-hourly (slide also gives a 7 mg salt/kg loading over 1 hour, then 10 mg/kg over 4 hours) |
| Follow-on | When the patient can take oral drugs, complete treatment with a full course of **artemether-lumefantrine** (3 days) | Plus doxycycline 3.5 mg/kg once daily (adults and children over 8 years) or clindamycin 10 mg/kg twice daily (pregnant women and children under 8 years); both for 7 days |

Artesunate 60 mg: reconstitute with 5% sodium bicarbonate, shake 2 to 3 minutes until clear, then add 5 mL of 5% dextrose or 0.9% saline (total 6 mL); give slow IV at 3 to 4 mL/min, or IM into the anterior thigh. Prepare fresh each time. Quinine: dilute in 250 mL of 5% dextrose and infuse over 4 hours; do not exceed 5 mg salt/kg per hour.

> **Exam tip:** quinine is never given as a rapid IV bolus (hypotension, arrhythmia) and requires glucose monitoring because it causes hypoglycaemia.

### 7.2 Uncomplicated *P. falciparum*

**Preferred: artemether-lumefantrine (AL, Riamet/Coartem)**; each tablet 20 mg artemether and 120 mg lumefantrine. Six doses over 3 days: first dose stat, second at 8 hours, then twice daily on days 2 and 3.

| Weight | Tablets per dose |
|---|---|
| 5 to 14 kg | 1 |
| 15 to 24 kg | 2 |
| 25 to 34 kg | 3 |
| Above 34 kg | 4 |

Give with a meal or drink containing at least 1.2 g fat (milk) to improve absorption.

**Alternative:** quinine sulphate (300 mg tablets) 10 mg salt/kg orally 8-hourly for 7 days, plus doxycycline 3.5 mg/kg daily (children over 8 years) or clindamycin 10 mg/kg twice daily (children under 8 years), for 7 days.

**Infants under 5 kg or under 4 months** should not get AL; use artesunate (IM or oral) or quinine as per local protocol (the slide gives IM artesunate or artemether on day 1, then oral artesunate to day 7, or quinine 15 to 20 mg/kg three times daily).

### 7.3 *P. knowlesi* and mixed infections

Treat as *P. falciparum*.

### 7.4 *P. vivax*, *P. ovale*, *P. malariae*

| Drug | Dose |
|---|---|
| **Chloroquine** (250 mg phosphate = 150 mg base) | 25 mg base/kg over 3 days: 10 mg/kg on day 1, 5 mg/kg on day 2, 5 mg/kg on day 3 (dose is calculated on **base**) |
| **Primaquine** (7.5 mg base/tab) | 0.5 mg base/kg daily for 2 weeks, started with chloroquine, with food |

**Check G6PD first.** In mild to moderate G6PD deficiency give primaquine 0.75 mg base/kg once weekly for 8 weeks. In **severe G6PD deficiency primaquine is contraindicated.** Primaquine clears the liver hypnozoites to prevent relapse.

### 7.5 Special groups

| Group | Management |
|---|---|
| **Pregnancy** | Quinine plus clindamycin for 7 days; artesunate plus clindamycin for 7 days if first line fails |
| **Lactation** | Standard antimalarials including ACTs; withhold dapsone, primaquine and tetracyclines |
| **Hepatic impairment** | Chloroquine (30 to 50% hepatic metabolism): adjust dose, monitor. Quinine: no change in mild to moderate; monitor. Artemisinins: no adjustment |
| **Renal impairment** | Chloroquine: 50% dose if CrCl below 10 mL/min or on haemodialysis/peritoneal dialysis; 100% on CRRT. Quinine: 8 to 12-hourly if CrCl 10 to 50; 24-hourly if below 10; severe CRF not on dialysis: 600 mg then 300 mg 12-hourly. Artemisinins: no adjustment |

## 8. Management of complications

Severe malaria needs **intensive care**: monitor fluid, electrolytes, glucose and acid-base; record intake and output.

| Complication | Immediate management |
|---|---|
| **Coma (cerebral malaria)** | Monitor Glasgow Coma Scale, temperature, respiration, BP and vital signs; protect airway |
| **Hyperpyrexia** (rectal above 40 °C) | Sponging, fanning, antipyretic; **rectal paracetamol** preferred over nephrotoxic NSAIDs |
| **Convulsions** | Slow IV **diazepam 0.15 mg/kg** (maximum 20 mg adults) |
| **Hypoglycaemia** | **50% dextrose** (as infusion fluid; in children use 10% dextrose 5 mL/kg bolus); check glucose every 4 to 6 hours for the first 48 hours |
| **Severe anaemia** | Packed red cell transfusion, with a small dose of frusemide 20 mg as needed to avoid circulatory overload |
| **Pulmonary oedema** | Sit upright at 45 degrees, oxygen, IV diuretic (poor response), stop IV fluids; early mechanical ventilation |
| **Acute renal failure** (urine below 400 mL/24 h in adults, or 0.5 mL/kg/hour; creatinine above 265 µmol/L) | Exclude pre-renal cause and obstruction; IV normal saline; if established, haemofiltration, haemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis |
| **DIC** | Packed cells, clotting factors or platelets: cryoprecipitate 10 units, platelets 4 to 8 units, FFP 10 to 15 mL/kg; vitamin K 10 mg slow IV for prolonged PT |
| **Metabolic acidosis** | Sodium bicarbonate 8.4% infusion over 30 minutes, repeat if needed; haemodialysis if severe |
| **Shock** | Suspect septicaemia: blood cultures, parenteral broad-spectrum antibiotics, correct fluid deficit |

## 9. Monitoring and follow-up

- Repeat the blood smear **daily** (twice daily in severe infection).
- Patients are usually afebrile and clinically better within 48 to 72 hours, except complicated cases.
- Repeat blood film **one month after recovery** to exclude recrudescence.

## 10. Prevention

- **Avoid mosquito bites:** long sleeves and trousers, repellent creams or sprays, **insecticide-treated bed nets**.
- **Chemoprophylaxis for travellers** to endemic areas: **mefloquine 250 mg weekly** (up to 1 year) or **doxycycline 100 mg daily** (up to 3 months), starting 1 week before and continuing 4 weeks after leaving.

| Mefloquine | Tablets per week |
|---|---|
| Under 5 kg / under 3 months | Not recommended |
| 5 to 12 kg (3 to 23 months) | 1/4 |
| 13 to 24 kg (2 to 7 years) | 1/2 |
| 25 to 35 kg (8 to 10 years) | 3/4 |
| 36 kg and above (11 years +) | 1 |

| Doxycycline | Tablets daily |
|---|---|
| Under 25 kg / under 8 years | Contraindicated |
| 25 to 35 kg (8 to 10 years) | 1/2 |
| 36 to 50 kg (11 to 13 years) | 3/4 |
| Over 50 kg (14 years +) | 1 |

In Kenya also: IPTp with sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine for pregnant women, indoor residual spraying, and the **RTS,S malaria vaccine** being introduced for children in high-burden counties.

## Key points to revise

1. Female Anopheles transmits sporozoites; liver (pre-erythrocytic) then red cell (erythrocytic) cycles; gametocytes infect the mosquito.
2. Hypnozoites in vivax and ovale cause relapse; treat with primaquine after G6PD check.
3. Periodicity: tertian 48 h (vivax, ovale), quartan 72 h (malariae), irregular (falciparum).
4. Severe malaria: cerebral malaria, severe anaemia, hypoglycaemia, acidosis, convulsions, shock, renal failure, black water fever.
5. Diagnosis: thick and thin film is the gold standard; RDT with HRP2 detects falciparum.
6. Uncomplicated falciparum: artemether-lumefantrine for 3 days, with fatty food; severe: IV artesunate.
7. Treat hypoglycaemia, convulsions and anaemia immediately; use rectal paracetamol, not NSAIDs.
8. Prevention: ITNs, repellents, prophylaxis for travellers; follow-up film at one month.
