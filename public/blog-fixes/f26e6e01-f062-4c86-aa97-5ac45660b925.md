# Chronic Kidney Disease (Chronic Kidney Failure)

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Nephrology**

> **In one line:** chronic kidney disease is the irreversible, progressive loss of nephrons. Surviving nephrons hyperfilter and hypertrophy to keep the GFR up, but this raises glomerular pressure and causes further sclerosis. Late disease brings fluid overload, anaemia (low erythropoietin), mineral-bone disease (secondary hyperparathyroidism), hyperkalaemia, acidosis and uraemia, and ends in dialysis or transplantation.

## What you must know

- CKD is staged by **GFR (K/DOQI)**: stage 1 over 90 (with kidney damage), 2 = 60 to 89, 3 = 30 to 59, 4 = 15 to 29, **stage 5 (ESRD) under 15 mL/min**.
- Commonest causes: **diabetic nephropathy** and **hypertensive nephropathy**, then glomerulonephritis, polycystic kidneys, obstruction and tubulointerstitial disease.
- Patients are **asymptomatic until GFR is below about 30 mL/min**; urea and creatinine rise significantly only after GFR halves.
- Anaemia is **normochromic normocytic** (low erythropoietin, no reticulocyte response); treat with **erythropoietin** (target Hb 11 to 12 g/dL) after correcting iron.
- Mineral-bone disease: **phosphate retention, low calcitriol, hypocalcaemia, secondary hyperparathyroidism**, renal osteodystrophy.
- Slow progression: **ACE inhibitors, strict BP and glucose control, avoid nephrotoxins** (NSAIDs, aminoglycosides, contrast).
- Firm indications for renal replacement: uraemic **pericarditis, encephalopathy, peripheral neuropathy**, refractory fluid overload, hyperkalaemia or acidosis.

## 1. Definitions and staging

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Chronic renal failure (CRF) | Progressive destruction of renal mass and function from irreversible sclerosis and nephron loss; used more specifically when GFR is **under 30 mL/min** |
| Chronic renal insufficiency (CRI) | Mild to moderate impairment, GFR **30 to 90 mL/min** |
| End-stage renal disease (ESRD) | Usually with uraemic symptoms, GFR under 10 to 15 mL/min |

**K/DOQI classification**

| Stage | Description | GFR (mL/min/1.73 m2) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Kidney damage with normal or raised GFR | Over 90 |
| 2 | Mild reduction | 60 to 89 |
| 3 | Moderate reduction | 30 to 59 |
| 4 | Severe reduction (CRF) | 15 to 29 |
| 5 | ESRD | Under 15 |

> **Check:** current KDIGO guidance splits stage 3 into 3a (45 to 59) and 3b (30 to 44) and adds albuminuria category; CKD requires abnormality for 3 months or more.

## 2. Pathophysiology

- Each kidney has about **1.2 million nephrons**, each contributing to GFR.
- Remaining nephrons maintain GFR by **hyperfiltration and compensatory hypertrophy**. Urea and creatinine rise significantly only when total GFR falls to **50%**; uraemic symptoms begin when GFR falls below **about 20%** of normal.
- Hyperfiltration is beneficial at first but becomes harmful: **raised glomerular capillary pressure** damages capillaries, causing **focal segmental glomerulosclerosis** and finally global glomerulosclerosis (a vicious cycle).

**Factors that accelerate glomerular injury**

- Systemic hypertension
- Acute insults (nephrotoxins, reduced perfusion)
- **Proteinuria**
- Increased renal ammoniagenesis with interstitial injury
- Hyperlipidaemia
- Hyperphosphataemia with calcium-phosphate deposition
- Reduced nitric oxide (the slide says "nitrous oxide")

**Ageing:** after age 30 there is physiological glomerulosclerosis; GFR falls linearly by about **8 mL/min/1.73 m2 per year** from a maximum of about 140. Muscle mass also falls, so daily creatinine excretion falls and **serum creatinine may stay constant despite falling GFR** in the elderly (creatinine overestimates GFR).

## 3. Epidemiology and prognosis

- Highest ESRD incidence is in those over 65, the fastest growing group.
- **5-year survival on chronic dialysis is about 35%, and about 20% in diabetics.**

## 4. Causes

| Group | Examples |
|---|---|
| 1. Diabetic nephropathy | Commonest cause |
| 2. Hypertensive nephropathy | Hypertensive nephrosclerosis |
| 3. Vascular | Renal artery stenosis; C-ANCA, P-ANCA and ANCA-negative vasculitides |
| 4. Primary glomerular disease | Membranous nephropathy, IgA nephropathy, FSGS, minimal change disease, membranoproliferative GN, rapidly progressive (crescentic) GN |
| 5. Secondary glomerular disease | Post-infectious GN (endocarditis, hepatitis B and C, syphilis, HIV, parasites, TB); amyloidosis, light-chain deposition; lymphoma; TTP; HUS; Henoch-Schonlein purpura; Alport syndrome; reflux nephropathy |
| 6. Connective tissue disease | SLE, rheumatoid arthritis, mixed connective tissue disease, scleroderma, Goodpasture syndrome, granulomatosis with polyangiitis (Wegener) |
| 7. Tubulointerstitial | Drugs (sulfonamides, allopurinol, gold, penicillamine, **chronic NSAIDs**), chronic infection, Sjogren's, chronic hypokalaemia or hypercalcaemia, sarcoidosis, **myeloma cast nephropathy**, heavy metals, radiation, cystinosis |
| 8. Polycystic kidney disease | |
| 9. Urinary tract obstruction | Stones, BPH, tumours, retroperitoneal fibrosis, urethral stricture, neurogenic bladder |

> **Kenyan context:** also consider HIV-associated nephropathy (HIVAN), schistosomiasis, chronic pyelonephritis, herbal remedies and late-presenting hypertension.

## 5. Clinical presentation

**History**

- Symptoms are **conspicuously absent** early: a high index of suspicion is needed. Patients may only report fatigue or feeling unwell, or present with the underlying disease (diabetes, reflux, infections), or with uraemic symptoms.
- Establish the cause: hypertension, diabetes, systemic infection, inflammatory or metabolic disease, **drugs and toxins** (NSAIDs, which patients under-report; gold, penicillamine, antimicrobials, lithium, ACE inhibitors), family history of renal or urological disease.
- Review of uraemic symptoms by system: GIT (appetite, nausea, vomiting, hiccups), CVS (dyspnoea, oedema, weight change), muscle cramps, pruritus, CNS (mental acuity, daily activities), pulmonary oedema.

**Examination**

- General: sick-looking, propped up in bed (pulmonary oedema); facial puffiness, wasting, or obesity (diabetes).
- Oedema; **uraemic frost**, dry skin, hyperpigmentation; dialysis scars (fistula).
- Blood pressure; fundoscopy (hypertensive or diabetic retinopathy); precordial exam (pericardial rub); abdomen for **bruits** and **palpable renal masses**.
- Neurology: **asterixis**, muscle weakness, neuropathy.
- Prostate size in men and pelvic masses in women (obstruction).

> **Remember:** patients with GFR 70 to 100 and those with CRI (GFR over 30) are generally asymptomatic with no clinical water, electrolyte or endocrine disturbance. Disturbances appear in CRF (under 30) and ESRD (under 10). Uraemic features are attributed to accumulated toxins (urea, hippurates, nucleic-acid end products, polyamines, myoinositol, phenols, benzoates, indoles).

## 6. Complications and manifestations

### Metabolic and haematological

| Problem | Mechanism and features |
|---|---|
| **Fluid overload** | Failure to excrete sodium and free water, usually GFR under 10 to 15; peripheral and pulmonary oedema, hypertension |
| **Normochromic normocytic anaemia** | Mainly low erythropoietin; worsens with falling GFR; no reticulocyte response; shortened red-cell survival |
| **Bleeding tendency** | Uraemic platelet dysfunction (abnormal platelet factor III activity, aggregation and adhesion, impaired prothrombin consumption); prolonged bleeding time, bruising |
| **Hyperkalaemia** | Usually GFR under 20 to 25. Earlier with high-potassium diet, low aldosterone, type IV RTA, diabetic nephropathy, **ACE inhibitors, NSAIDs**; worsened by acidaemia and insulin lack (extracellular shift) |
| **Metabolic acidosis** | Mixed non-anion-gap and anion-gap; the anion gap is usually not above 20 mEq/L; reduced ammonium excretion; late accumulation of phosphates, sulphates and organic anions |
| **Hyponatraemia** | In ESRD with free-water excess |

### Mineral and bone disorder (CKD-MBD)

1. Falling GFR means less phosphate filtered. Serum phosphate stays normal early because **PTH rises** to increase phosphaturia.
2. In moderate to severe CRF, **hyperphosphataemia** develops. It suppresses renal 1-alpha-hydroxylation of 25-hydroxyvitamin D, so **calcitriol is low** when GFR is under 30.
3. Low calcitriol lowers intestinal calcium absorption, and calcium binds phosphate, giving **hypocalcaemia**.
4. Low calcitriol, hypocalcaemia and hyperphosphataemia each independently drive PTH synthesis: **secondary hyperparathyroidism**. High PTH worsens hyperphosphataemia through bone resorption.
5. Persistently high PTH produces **osteitis fibrosa** (high turnover). Together these are **renal osteodystrophy**.

| Bone lesion | Notes |
|---|---|
| Osteitis fibrosa | High turnover; high PTH |
| Osteomalacia | Mainly from aluminium accumulation; less common |
| **Adynamic bone disease** | Low turnover; predominant lesion in chronic peritoneal dialysis; increasing |
| Dialysis-related amyloidosis | Beta-2-microglobulin, after 8 to 10 years of dialysis; cysts at ends of long bones, carpal tunnel |

- **Calciphylaxis:** skin necrosis with subcutaneous, vascular, joint and visceral calcification in poorly controlled calcium-phosphate product.

### Systemic

| System | Features |
|---|---|
| Cardiovascular | **Uraemic pericarditis** (PR depression and diffuse ST elevation; effusion often haemorrhagic, may cause tamponade); heart failure, ischaemic heart disease, hypertensive heart disease, vascular calcification, atherosclerosis |
| Neurological | **Encephalopathy** (poor memory, concentration, sleep disturbance; can progress to coma). **Peripheral neuropathy**: sensory before motor, legs before arms, distal before proximal; **restless legs syndrome**; later weakness and loss of reflexes. Asterixis, myoclonus, chorea, seizures, coma in terminal uraemia |
| GI | Anorexia, nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, uraemic ulcers, hiccups |
| Skin | Pallor, hyperpigmentation, **uraemic frost**, dryness, pruritus, ecchymoses |
| Musculoskeletal | Fatigue, somnolence, cramps, fasciculations, failure to thrive |
| Reproductive | Men: erectile dysfunction, low libido. Women: low oestrogen, amenorrhoea, inability to carry pregnancy to term |

> **Exam tip:** evidence of peripheral neuropathy (and pericarditis or encephalopathy) is a firm indication for renal replacement therapy.

## 7. Investigations

**Blood**

- FBC: normochromic normocytic anaemia; WBC (chronic infection).
- **Raised urea and creatinine**.
- Electrolytes: **hyperkalaemia, low bicarbonate, hypocalcaemia, hyperphosphataemia**, hyponatraemia (ESRD with water excess).
- LFTs: hypoalbuminaemia (nephrotic or malnourished).
- Fasting glucose, lipids, uric acid.

**Urinalysis**

- **RBC casts:** proliferative glomerulonephritis.
- Pyuria or WBC casts: interstitial nephritis (especially with eosinophiluria) or UTI.
- Dipstick proteinuria: glomerular or tubulointerstitial.
- **24-hour urine protein:** over 2.0 g is in the glomerular range, over 3.0 to 3.5 g is **nephrotic range**, under 2.0 g suggests tubulointerstitial disease.
- Persistent **albumin over 17 mg/g creatinine in men and over 25 mg/g in women** signals chronic renal damage regardless of GFR; use to monitor, especially in diabetes, hypertension and GN.

**Aetiological tests**

- Complement (low in some GN), serum and urine protein electrophoresis (myeloma), ANA and anti-dsDNA (SLE), **C-ANCA** (granulomatosis with polyangiitis), **P-ANCA** (polyarteritis nodosa, microscopic polyangiitis), **anti-GBM** (Goodpasture), hepatitis B and C, HIV, VDRL, PSA in older men.

**Imaging**

| Test | Role |
|---|---|
| Plain abdominal X-ray | Radio-opaque stones, nephrocalcinosis |
| **Renal ultrasound** | **Small echogenic kidneys** in advanced CKD. Kidneys remain normal or large in **diabetic nephropathy, HIVAN, polycystic kidney disease and amyloid**. Screens for hydronephrosis (may be absent in early obstruction), retroperitoneal fibrosis, tumour; Doppler for renal arteries; retrograde pyelogram if obstruction is strongly suspected |
| IV pyelogram | Rarely used (contrast toxicity); renal stones |
| Voiding cystourethrogram | Gold standard for vesicoureteric reflux |
| Radionuclide scan | Residual function in severe hydronephrosis |
| CT | Best for masses, cysts and **most sensitive for stones**. Avoid IV contrast in renal impairment (risk of acute kidney injury, higher in moderate to severe CRF and dehydration) |

**Cockcroft-Gault (creatinine clearance), use routinely to estimate residual function**

- Male CrCl = [(140 - age) x weight (kg)] / [serum creatinine (mg/dL) x 72]
- Female: multiply the result by 0.85.

**Renal biopsy**

- Ultrasound-guided; indicated for renal impairment or near-nephrotic proteinuria with unclear diagnosis after other work-up.
- **Not indicated in small echogenic kidneys** (severely scarred, irreversible).
- Commonest complication: bleeding (rarely life-threatening).

## 8. Management

### Slowing progression

1. **Blood pressure control** to target; **ACE inhibitors or ARBs** as tolerated (monitor creatinine and potassium; avoid in advanced renal failure, bilateral renal artery stenosis, or stenosis in a solitary kidney).
2. **Strict glycaemic control** in diabetes.
3. Treat hyperlipidaemia.
4. **Avoid nephrotoxins:** IV contrast, NSAIDs, aminoglycosides.
5. **Diet:** protein restriction early is controversial, but it is recommended as ESRD approaches to delay uraemic symptoms. A CKD diet is low in protein, sodium, potassium and phosphate.

### Treating complications

| Problem | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Anaemia | Check iron stores (transferrin saturation, ferritin). If iron deficient (TSat under 20%, ferritin under 100 ug/L): **IV iron 50 to 100 mg twice weekly for 5 weeks**, repeat if still low. **Erythropoietin 50 to 150 units/kg per week IV or SC** (once to three times a week). Hb should rise 1 to 2 g/dL over 4 weeks; **target Hb 11 to 12 g/dL** |
| Hyperphosphataemia | Dietary phosphate restriction and **phosphate binders** |
| Hypocalcaemia | Calcium supplements with or without calcitriol |
| Secondary hyperparathyroidism | Calcitriol or vitamin D analogues |
| Volume overload | Loop diuretics or ultrafiltration |
| Metabolic acidosis | Oral alkali (sodium bicarbonate) |
| Bleeding/coagulopathy | Desmopressin, cryoprecipitate, conjugated oestrogens, transfusion, EPO |
| Thromboembolism risk | Appropriate anticoagulant prophylaxis; avoid or adjust doses of anticoagulants such as low-molecular-weight heparin |
| Uraemia | **Renal replacement therapy**: haemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, transplantation |

**Phosphate binders and vitamin D**

| Drug | Notes |
|---|---|
| Calcium carbonate / calcium acetate | Bind dietary phosphate in the gut as insoluble calcium phosphate (risk of hypercalcaemia) |
| **Sevelamer** (Renagel) | Non-calcium binder; 800 to 1600 mg (1 to 2 tablets) three times daily with meals; fewer hypercalcaemic episodes than calcium acetate |
| Lanthanum carbonate (Fosrenal) | Non-calcium, non-aluminium binder in the upper GI tract |
| **Calcitriol** | 0.25 microgram daily; suppresses PTH and raises intestinal calcium absorption |
| Doxercalciferol (Hectorol) | Vitamin D analogue (1-alpha-hydroxyergocalciferol) that does not need renal activation |

> **Exam tip:** avoid aluminium-containing binders (osteomalacia, neurotoxicity). Phosphate binders must be taken **with meals**.

## 9. Practice questions (answers)

1. Circulatory overload in a patient with CKD: **weight gain, frothy sputum, jugular venous distension (B)**.
2. Statement most likely true of ESRD: the slide's key is **A (pyelonephritis is a recognised cause)**. The other options are wrong (ESRD gives hyperkalaemia and raised urea/creatinine, and reduced erythropoietin).
> **Check:** the commonest causes are diabetes and hypertension, not pyelonephritis; the answer works by elimination.
3. GFR 55 with uncontrolled hypertension and coronary disease: **lisinopril (A)**, an ACE inhibitor that lowers BP and protects the kidney.
4. Stage 4 CKD diet: **low protein, low sodium, low potassium, low phosphate (A)**.

## Key points to revise

1. CKD stages 1 to 5 are defined by GFR; stage 5 is under 15 mL/min.
2. Diabetes and hypertension are the leading causes; glomerular hyperfiltration drives progression.
3. Symptoms appear late; uraemia begins when GFR falls under about 20% of normal.
4. Anaemia is normocytic normochromic from low erythropoietin; give EPO (target Hb 11 to 12 g/dL) and correct iron.
5. Phosphate retention and low calcitriol cause hypocalcaemia and secondary hyperparathyroidism (renal osteodystrophy).
6. Hyperkalaemia, acidosis, fluid overload, pericarditis, neuropathy and encephalopathy are the key complications and dialysis indications.
7. Ultrasound shows small echogenic kidneys (except diabetes, HIVAN, polycystic and amyloid); avoid biopsy in small scarred kidneys.
8. Slow progression with ACE inhibitors, BP and glucose control, protein restriction near ESRD, and avoidance of NSAIDs, contrast and aminoglycosides.
