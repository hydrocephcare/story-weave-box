## WEEK 1: INTRODUCTION TO ONCOLOGY**History of cancer treatment:**- Ancient era – surgery only (Egyptian/Greek records of tumor excision)
- Early 1900s – radiotherapy introduced (discovery of X-rays/radium)
- 1940s – first chemotherapy (nitrogen mustard, derived from WWI chemical warfare research)
- 1990s–2000s – targeted therapy era (imatinib, trastuzumab)
- 2010s–present – immunotherapy/checkpoint inhibitors, personalized/precision oncology
-
**Cellular and genetic basis of cancer:**

- Cancer = uncontrolled cell proliferation from accumulated genetic mutations
- **Oncogenes** – mutated/overactive genes that drive proliferation (e.g. RAS, HER2/neu, MYC)
- **Tumor suppressor genes** – normally halt proliferation/induce apoptosis; loss of function drives cancer (e.g. p53, BRCA1/2, RB)
- **DNA repair gene defects** – allow mutations to accumulate (e.g. Lynch syndrome – mismatch repair genes)
-
**Mechanisms of cancer (Hallmarks of Cancer):**

- Sustained proliferative signaling
- Evasion of growth suppressors
- Resistance to apoptosis (cell death evasion)
- Replicative immortality (telomerase reactivation)
- Angiogenesis induction (new blood vessel formation)
- Invasion and metastasis
- Reprogrammed energy metabolism (Warburg effect)
- Evasion of immune destruction
-
**Cancer biology basics:**

- Tumor = clonal expansion of a single mutated cell
- Benign vs malignant – malignant cells invade locally and metastasize
- Grading (differentiation) vs Staging (extent of spread, e.g. TNM system)
-
**Immuno-oncology introduction:**

- Immune system can recognize and destroy abnormal cells (immune surveillance)
- Tumors evade this via immune checkpoints (PD-1/PD-L1, CTLA-4) – basis for modern immunotherapy
- Tumor microenvironment can be immunosuppressive (Tregs, myeloid-derived suppressor cells)
-
## WEEK 2: TREATMENT APPROACHES IN ONCOLOGY**Surgery:**
- Curative (complete tumor removal, early-stage solid tumors)
- Palliative (relieve symptoms, debulking)
- Diagnostic (biopsy for histology)
- Reconstructive (post-resection)
-
**Radiotherapy:**

- Uses ionizing radiation to damage DNA of rapidly dividing cells
- **External beam** vs **brachytherapy** (internal source)
- Can be curative, adjuvant (post-surgery), neoadjuvant (pre-surgery to shrink tumor), or palliative (pain/bleeding control)
- Adverse effects: local skin reactions, fatigue, organ-specific damage (e.g. pneumonitis, fibrosis)
-
**Pharmacotherapy (overview):**

- **Chemotherapy** – cytotoxic drugs targeting dividing cells
- **Hormonal therapy** – for hormone-sensitive cancers (breast, prostate)
- **Targeted therapy** – molecularly targets specific cancer pathways
- **Immunotherapy** – harnesses immune system against tumor
- Often used in combination (multimodal therapy) for best outcomes
-
## WEEK 3: CLASSIFICATION OF CHEMOTHERAPY DRUGS**By cell-cycle specificity:**
- **Cell-cycle-specific (CCS)** – act only on actively dividing cells in a particular phase
-
G1 phase: corticosteroids

- S phase: antimetabolites (methotrexate, 5-FU)
- M phase: vinca alkaloids, taxanes
- More effective against rapidly growing tumors (high growth fraction)
- **Cell-cycle-nonspecific (CCNS)** – act on cells in any phase, including resting (G0)
-
Alkylating agents, antitumor antibiotics, platinum compounds

- Effective against slow-growing tumors too; often more dose-dependent toxicity
-
**Cell cycle phases (for reference):**

- G1 (growth) → S (DNA synthesis) → G2 (preparation for division) → M (mitosis) → back to G1 or G0 (resting)
-
**Clinical implication:**

- CCS drugs are scheduled based on cell cycle timing (e.g. repeated dosing)
- CCNS drugs follow a linear dose-response (more drug = more cell kill) regardless of timing
-
## WEEK 4: ALKYLATING AGENTS, ANTIMETABOLITES, ANTHRACYCLINES**Alkylating agents (CCNS):**
- **MOA** – form covalent bonds (alkyl groups) with DNA → cross-linking of DNA strands → prevents replication/transcription → apoptosis
- **Examples:**
-
Cyclophosphamide – prodrug, activated in liver; used in lymphomas, leukemias, autoimmune disease

- Carmustine – crosses BBB, used in brain tumors
- Cisplatin – also classified as platinum compound (see Week 5)
- **Toxicities:** myelosuppression, hemorrhagic cystitis (cyclophosphamide – prevented with mesna + hydration), secondary leukemias (long-term risk)
-
**Antimetabolites (CCS – S phase):**

- **MOA** – structurally mimic normal metabolites (folate, purines, pyrimidines) → block DNA/RNA synthesis
- **Examples:**
-
Methotrexate – folate antagonist (inhibits dihydrofolate reductase); rescue with leucovorin (folinic acid) to reduce toxicity

- 5-Fluorouracil (5-FU) – pyrimidine analog, blocks thymidylate synthase
- Gemcitabine – pyrimidine analog, used in pancreatic, lung cancers
- **Toxicities:** mucositis, myelosuppression, hepatotoxicity (methotrexate), hand-foot syndrome (5-FU)
-
**Anthracyclines (CCNS):**

- **MOA** – inhibit topoisomerase II + intercalate into DNA + generate free radicals → DNA strand breaks
- **Examples:** Doxorubicin, Daunorubicin, Epirubicin
- **Toxicities:** **dose-dependent cardiotoxicity** (cumulative dose limit, monitor ejection fraction), myelosuppression, alopecia, "red urine" (harmless discoloration)
- **Cardioprotection:** dexrazoxane; liposomal formulations reduce cardiotoxicity
-
## WEEK 5: TOPOISOMERASE INHIBITORS, MITOTIC INHIBITORS, PLATINUM COMPOUNDS**Topoisomerase inhibitors:**
- **MOA** – inhibit enzymes that relieve DNA supercoiling during replication → DNA strand breaks accumulate
- **Topoisomerase I inhibitors:** Irinotecan, Topotecan
- **Topoisomerase II inhibitor:** Etoposide
- **Toxicities:** myelosuppression (especially neutropenia), diarrhea (irinotecan – cholinergic/delayed types)
-
**Mitotic inhibitors (CCS – M phase):**

- **MOA** – disrupt microtubule dynamics needed for mitotic spindle formation
- **Vinca alkaloids** (Vinblastine, Vincristine) – inhibit microtubule **polymerization**
- **Taxanes** (Paclitaxel, Docetaxel) – **stabilize** microtubules, preventing depolymerization
- **Toxicities:**
-
Vincristine – peripheral neuropathy, constipation/ileus (minimal myelosuppression)

- Vinblastine – more myelosuppressive
- Taxanes – peripheral neuropathy, hypersensitivity reactions, myelosuppression
-
**Platinum compounds (CCNS):**

- **MOA** – form DNA adducts/cross-links (similar mechanism to alkylating agents, but distinct class)
- **Examples:**
-
Cisplatin – nephrotoxicity (hydrate aggressively), ototoxicity, severe nausea/vomiting

- Carboplatin – less nephrotoxic, more myelosuppressive
- Oxaliplatin – peripheral neuropathy (cold-induced), used in colorectal cancer
-
## WEEK 6: ANTITUMOR ANTIBIOTICS, MISCELLANEOUS AGENTS**Antitumor antibiotics (CCNS):**
- **MOA** – DNA intercalation, free radical generation, strand breakage
- **Examples:**
-
Bleomycin – causes strand breaks via free radicals; unique toxicity = **pulmonary fibrosis** (monitor pulmonary function, minimal myelosuppression)

- Mitomycin C – DNA cross-linking; used in bladder cancer (intravesical), GI cancers
- Dactinomycin – intercalates DNA; used in Wilms tumor, gestational trophoblastic disease
-
**Miscellaneous agents:**

- **Asparaginase** – depletes asparagine (an amino acid leukemic cells can't synthesize themselves) → starves leukemic cells; used in ALL; risk of hypersensitivity, pancreatitis, coagulopathy
- **Mitotane** – adrenolytic agent, used in adrenocortical carcinoma
- **Procarbazine** – alkylating-like agent, used in Hodgkin lymphoma (MOPP regimen); disulfiram-like reaction with alcohol, MAOI-like interactions
-
## WEEK 7: TARGETED THERAPY, HORMONAL THERAPY, IMMUNOTHERAPY**Targeted therapy:**
- Designed to act on specific molecular abnormalities driving the cancer (more selective than classic chemo)
- **Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs):** Imatinib (BCR-ABL, CML), Erlotinib (EGFR, lung cancer)
- **Monoclonal antibodies:** Trastuzumab (HER2, breast cancer), Bevacizumab (VEGF/anti-angiogenic), Rituximab (CD20, lymphoma)
- **Toxicities:** generally better tolerated than classic chemo, but specific risks (e.g. trastuzumab – cardiotoxicity; bevacizumab – hypertension, bleeding, GI perforation)
-
**Hormonal therapy:**

- Used for hormone-sensitive tumors
- **Breast cancer (ER+):** Tamoxifen (SERM – blocks estrogen receptor), Aromatase inhibitors (anastrozole, letrozole – block peripheral estrogen synthesis, used post-menopause)
- **Prostate cancer:** GnRH agonists/antagonists (leuprolide), antiandrogens (flutamide)
- **Toxicities:** Tamoxifen – endometrial cancer risk, thromboembolism; Aromatase inhibitors – osteoporosis, joint pain
-
**Immunotherapy:**

- **Checkpoint inhibitors:**
-
Anti-PD-1 (nivolumab, pembrolizumab) – blocks PD-1/PD-L1 interaction, "releases the brakes" on T-cells

- Anti-CTLA-4 (ipilimumab)
- **CAR-T cell therapy** – genetically engineered T-cells targeting tumor antigens (e.g. CD19 in leukemia/lymphoma)
- **Toxicities:** immune-related adverse events (colitis, pneumonitis, endocrinopathies) due to generalized immune activation; CAR-T → cytokine release syndrome, neurotoxicity
-
## WEEK 8: ONCOLOGY PATIENT CARE — ADVERSE/TOXIC EFFECTS & MANAGEMENT**Common toxicities across chemo classes:**
- **Myelosuppression** – neutropenia (infection risk), anemia, thrombocytopenia (bleeding risk)
-
Management: G-CSF (filgrastim) for neutropenia, transfusions as needed, dose delays/reductions

- **Nausea/vomiting (CINV)** – classified as acute, delayed, or anticipatory
-
Management: 5-HT3 antagonists (ondansetron), NK1 antagonists (aprepitant), dexamethasone, combination regimens for highly emetogenic drugs (e.g. cisplatin)

- **Mucositis** – oral/GI mucosal damage; management = oral hygiene, cryotherapy, analgesics
- **Alopecia** – common, usually reversible after treatment ends
- **Extravasation injury** – vesicant drugs (e.g. doxorubicin, vincristine) can cause severe tissue damage if leaked outside vein; requires immediate management protocols
-
**Organ-specific toxicities (recap + management):**

- Cardiotoxicity (anthracyclines) → baseline + monitoring echo/ejection fraction, dexrazoxane, cumulative dose limits
- Nephrotoxicity (cisplatin) → aggressive hydration, avoid other nephrotoxins
- Pulmonary toxicity (bleomycin) → monitor pulmonary function tests, avoid high oxygen concentrations during anesthesia
- Neurotoxicity (vincristine, taxanes, oxaliplatin) → dose modification, neuroprotective strategies, symptomatic management
-
**General principles of oncology patient care:**

- Pre-treatment workup: baseline organ function (renal, hepatic, cardiac), counseling, fertility preservation discussion
- Supportive care: nutrition, psychosocial support, pain management
- Patient education: recognizing fever/signs of infection (neutropenic sepsis = medical emergency), when to seek urgent care
-
## WEEK 9: INFECTIONS IN CANCER PATIENTS**Why cancer patients are high risk:**
- Chemotherapy-induced neutropenia → impaired innate immunity
- Mucosal barrier breakdown (mucositis) → portal of entry for pathogens
- Indwelling catheters/central lines → line infections
- Disease itself (e.g. hematologic malignancies) impairs immune function
-
**Febrile neutropenia:**

- Defined as fever (≥38.3°C single reading or ≥38.0°C sustained) + neutrophil count <500/mm³ (or <1000 with expected decline)
- **Medical emergency** – requires immediate empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics (e.g. anti-pseudomonal β-lactam like piperacillin-tazobactam or cefepime) before culture results
- Risk stratification tools guide inpatient vs outpatient management
-
**Common pathogens in cancer patients:**

- Bacterial – gram-negatives (Pseudomonas), gram-positives (coagulase-negative staph from central lines)
- Fungal – Candida, Aspergillus (especially in prolonged neutropenia) → may need antifungal prophylaxis/empiric therapy
- Viral – reactivation of latent viruses (HSV, CMV, VZV) due to immunosuppression
-
**Prevention strategies:**

- Prophylactic antimicrobials in high-risk patients (e.g. fluoroquinolone prophylaxis, antifungal prophylaxis)
- G-CSF to shorten neutropenia duration
- Strict hand hygiene, neutropenic precautions, vaccination where appropriate (avoid live vaccines during active immunosuppression)
-
## WEEK 10: CHALLENGES IN THE PRACTICE OF ONCOLOGY (Kenya context)**Challenges for oncology in Kenya / LMIC settings:**
- Late-stage presentation (limited screening/awareness) → poorer prognosis at diagnosis
- Limited access to radiotherapy machines and specialized oncologists (geographic + financial barriers)
- High cost of targeted therapy/immunotherapy – often unaffordable or unavailable
- Shortage of trained oncology nurses/pharmacists for safe chemo administration
- Limited palliative care infrastructure
- Cultural beliefs/stigma affecting care-seeking behavior
-
**Life of an oncologist (general themes, likely discussion-based):**

- Multidisciplinary teamwork (surgeons, radiologists, pathologists, pharmacists)
- Emotional demands of breaking bad news, end-of-life care
- Balancing curative vs palliative goals based on resource availability
-
**Success stories:**

- Improved survival with early detection + access to standard regimens (e.g. childhood ALL, testicular cancer – highly curable even in resource-limited settings if treated properly)
- Growing local capacity – cancer treatment centers expanding access to chemo/radiotherapy in Kenya
-

## WEEK 1: PRINCIPLES OF RATIONAL ANTIMICROBIAL PRESCRIBING**Acute infections vs autoimmune disease (distinguishing fevers):**- Acute infection – sudden onset, identifiable pathogen, often with localizing signs, responds to antimicrobials
- Autoimmune disease – can mimic infection (fever, malaise) but no organism isolated; responds to immunosuppression, not antibiotics
- Key clinical skill: don't reflexively treat every fever as infectious — consider autoimmune/inflammatory causes (e.g. lupus flare, vasculitis) if cultures are repeatedly negative
-
**Site-specific signs/symptoms & host factors:**

- Localizing symptoms point to the source (e.g. dysuria → UTI, neck stiffness → meningitis, productive cough → pneumonia)
- **Host factors affecting choice/dose:**
-
Age (neonate vs elderly – different pathogen profiles, renal/hepatic maturity)

- Immune status (HIV, chemo, transplant → think opportunistic organisms)
- Pregnancy (avoid teratogenic/fetotoxic agents – e.g. tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones)
- Renal/hepatic function (dose adjustment)
- Allergy history
-
**Empirical antimicrobial therapy:**

- Started before culture results, based on most likely pathogen(s) for that clinical syndrome + local resistance patterns
- Broad-spectrum initially → de-escalate once organism/sensitivity known ("start broad, narrow smart")
-
**Isolation of an organism:**

- Gram stain → rapid presumptive ID (gram +/- , cocci/bacilli)
- Culture & sensitivity – gold standard, guides definitive therapy (takes 24–72h)
- Rapid diagnostics (PCR, antigen tests) – increasingly used for faster turnaround
-
**Antimicrobial dosing, failure, and toxicity:**

- Dosing based on PK/PD principles (see Week 7), renal/hepatic function, site of infection (e.g. needs to cross BBB for meningitis)
- **Causes of treatment failure:** wrong diagnosis, resistant organism, poor adherence, inadequate dose/duration, abscess needing drainage (antibiotics alone won't work), drug interactions
- **Toxicity monitoring:** therapeutic drug monitoring for narrow-TI agents (e.g. vancomycin, aminoglycosides)
-
**Prevention & control of AMR:**

- Antimicrobial stewardship programs (right drug, dose, duration)
- Infection prevention (hand hygiene, isolation precautions)
- Surveillance of resistance patterns
- Public education on completing courses, not self-medicating
-
## WEEK 2: PROPHYLAXIS, CNS INFECTIONS, RESPIRATORY INFECTIONS, TB**Antimicrobial prophylaxis:**
- **Surgical prophylaxis** – single dose before incision (e.g. cefazolin), covers likely contaminating organisms, not full treatment course
- **Medical prophylaxis** – e.g. TMP-SMX for PCP prevention in HIV, isoniazid for latent TB, penicillin for rheumatic fever recurrence
- Key principle: prophylaxis is narrow, short-duration, and targeted — overuse drives resistance
-
**CNS infections:**

- **Bacterial meningitis** – medical emergency; empiric therapy must cross BBB
-
Neonates: ampicillin + cefotaxime/gentamicin (covers GBS, E. coli, Listeria)

- Adults: ceftriaxone + vancomycin (covers S. pneumoniae, N. meningitidis) ± ampicillin if Listeria risk (elderly, immunocompromised)
- Add dexamethasone (reduces inflammatory complications, especially in pneumococcal meningitis)
- **Viral meningitis/encephalitis** – mostly supportive; HSV encephalitis → IV acyclovir (empirically, while awaiting PCR)
- **Brain abscess** – often polymicrobial, needs drainage + prolonged antibiotics that penetrate CNS
-
**Respiratory tract infections:**

- **Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP):** outpatient – amoxicillin or macrolide; inpatient – β-lactam + macrolide or respiratory fluoroquinolone
- **Hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP):** broader coverage (anti-pseudomonal β-lactam ± MRSA coverage)
- **Pharyngitis** – mostly viral; if Group A Strep (bacterial) → penicillin/amoxicillin to prevent rheumatic fever
- **Sinusitis/otitis media** – often self-limiting/viral; bacterial → amoxicillin (± clavulanate)
-
**Tuberculosis:**

- **Standard regimen (RIPE):** Rifampin + Isoniazid + Pyrazinamide + Ethambutol × 2 months, then Rifampin + Isoniazid × 4 months (total 6 months)
- **Key toxicities:**
-
Isoniazid – hepatotoxicity, peripheral neuropathy (prevent with pyridoxine/B6)

- Rifampin – hepatotoxicity, strong CYP450 inducer (drug interactions), orange body fluids
- Pyrazinamide – hepatotoxicity, hyperuricemia
- Ethambutol – optic neuritis (monitor visual acuity)
- **Latent TB treatment:** isoniazid monotherapy (6–9 months) or rifampin-based shorter regimens
- Directly Observed Therapy (DOT) used to ensure adherence and prevent resistance (MDR-TB)
-
## WEEK 3: GI, INTRA-ABDOMINAL, UTI, STI INFECTIONS**Infectious diarrhea:**
- Most cases viral/self-limiting → supportive care (oral rehydration) is mainstay
- Bacterial causes warranting antibiotics: Shigella, Campylobacter (severe), C. difficile, traveler's diarrhea (severe)
- **C. difficile colitis** – associated with prior antibiotic use; treat with oral vancomycin or fidaxomicin (NOT metronidazole as first-line per current guidelines, though still used in resource-limited settings)
- Avoid antimotility agents (loperamide) in suspected invasive/bloody diarrhea (risk of toxic megacolon)
-
**Intra-abdominal infections:**

- Often polymicrobial (gram-negatives + anaerobes) – e.g. appendicitis, diverticulitis, peritonitis
- Empiric coverage needs anaerobic coverage: e.g. piperacillin-tazobactam, or ceftriaxone + metronidazole
- Source control (drainage/surgery) is essential — antibiotics alone often insufficient (e.g. abscess)
-
**Urinary tract infections:**

- **Uncomplicated cystitis:** nitrofurantoin, TMP-SMX, or fosfomycin (first-line); avoid nitrofurantoin in renal impairment
- **Pyelonephritis:** fluoroquinolones or ceftriaxone, more aggressive treatment, consider hospitalization if severe
- **Complicated UTI** (catheter, structural abnormality, male, pregnancy) – broader workup, longer duration
- Asymptomatic bacteriuria – generally NOT treated except in pregnancy or before urologic procedures
-
**Sexually transmitted diseases:**

- **Gonorrhea** – ceftriaxone (rising resistance to older agents like fluoroquinolones)
- **Chlamydia** – azithromycin or doxycycline; often co-treated with gonorrhea empirically
- **Syphilis** – benzathine penicillin G (stage-dependent dosing)
- **Trichomoniasis** – metronidazole
- Partner treatment and screening for other STIs (HIV, hepatitis) important in management
-
## WEEK 4: SKIN/SOFT TISSUE, CANCER-PATIENT INFECTIONS, VIRAL/FUNGAL/PARASITIC**Skin and soft tissue infections:**
- **Cellulitis** – usually Strep/Staph; treat with cephalexin or dicloxacillin (non-purulent); add MRSA coverage (e.g. clindamycin, TMP-SMX) if purulent/abscess
- **Abscess** – incision and drainage is primary treatment; antibiotics adjunctive
- **Necrotizing fasciitis** – surgical emergency + broad-spectrum antibiotics (including coverage for Strep, Staph, anaerobes, gram-negatives)
- **Impetigo** – topical mupirocin or oral antibiotics for widespread disease
-
**Infections in cancer patients:** *(recap from Trimester 3 Week 9 — same core concept)*

- Neutropenic fever = medical emergency, empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics immediately
- Higher risk of fungal (Candida, Aspergillus) and viral reactivation (HSV, CMV) due to immunosuppression
-
**Viral infections (general):**

- Influenza – neuraminidase inhibitors (oseltamivir) if started early (<48h)
- HSV – acyclovir/valacyclovir
- VZV (shingles) – acyclovir/valacyclovir, started early to reduce postherpetic neuralgia risk
-
**Fungal infections:**

- **Candidiasis** – fluconazole (mucocutaneous), echinocandins/amphotericin B (invasive/severe)
- **Aspergillosis** – voriconazole first-line
- **Cryptococcal meningitis** (especially in HIV) – amphotericin B + flucytosine induction, then fluconazole maintenance
-
**Parasitic infections:**

- **Malaria** – artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) for uncomplicated; IV artesunate for severe
- **Helminths** – albendazole/mebendazole (broad-spectrum anthelmintics)
- **Amoebiasis** – metronidazole + luminal agent (e.g. paromomycin) to eradicate cysts
-
## WEEK 5: PHARMACOTHERAPY OF HIV & OPPORTUNISTIC INFECTIONS**HIV pharmacotherapy — drug classes:**
- **NRTIs** (Nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors) – tenofovir, lamivudine, abacavir; competitively inhibit reverse transcriptase
- **NNRTIs** (Non-nucleoside RTIs) – efavirenz, nevirapine; bind reverse transcriptase at a different site
- **Protease inhibitors (PIs)** – ritonavir, lopinavir; block viral protein cleavage/maturation
- **Integrase strand transfer inhibitors (INSTIs)** – dolutegravir, raltegravir; block integration of viral DNA into host genome (current first-line backbone in most guidelines)
- **Entry/fusion inhibitors** – maraviroc (CCR5 antagonist), enfuvirtide (less commonly used now)
-
**General principle:** combination ART (usually 3 drugs from ≥2 classes) to prevent resistance — never monotherapy

**Opportunistic infections in HIV (by CD4 count — classic teaching points):**
- CD4 <200 – **Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PCP)** → prophylaxis/treatment with TMP-SMX
- CD4 <100 – **Toxoplasmosis** → prophylaxis/treatment with sulfadiazine + pyrimethamine; **Cryptococcal meningitis** → amphotericin B + flucytosine
- CD4 <50 – **Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC)** → prophylaxis with azithromycin; **CMV retinitis** → ganciclovir
- TB can occur at any CD4 count but risk increases as CD4 falls
-
## WEEK 6: VIRAL HEPATITIS A, B & C**Hepatitis A:**
- Fecal-oral transmission, acute self-limiting illness, no chronic carrier state
- Management: supportive care; vaccine available for prevention (no specific antiviral treatment)
-
**Hepatitis B:**

- Blood/body fluid/perinatal transmission; can become chronic (especially if acquired in infancy)
- **Chronic HBV treatment:** nucleos(t)ide analogs — tenofovir, entecavir (suppress viral replication, don't cure)
- Vaccine available (and is part of routine immunization)
- Monitor for cirrhosis/hepatocellular carcinoma risk in chronic carriers
-
**Hepatitis C:**

- Primarily bloodborne (IV drug use, transfusions historically)
- High rate of chronicity if untreated
- **Treatment:** Direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) – e.g. sofosbuvir + velpatasvir; cure rates >95%, much shorter duration than old interferon-based regimens
- No vaccine currently available
-
**Key shared concept:** all three can cause acute hepatitis (jaundice, elevated transaminases, malaise) — distinguished by serology (HAV IgM, HBsAg/anti-HBc, HCV antibody + RNA)

## WEEK 7: PHARMACODYNAMICS OF ANTIMICROBIAL AGENTS**Time-dependent killing:**- Efficacy depends on **duration** that drug concentration stays above the MIC (minimum inhibitory concentration), not peak concentration
- Examples: β-lactams (penicillins, cephalosporins), vancomycin
- Dosing strategy: frequent dosing or continuous/extended infusions to maximize time above MIC
-
**Concentration-dependent killing:**

- Efficacy depends on **peak concentration** relative to MIC (Cmax/MIC ratio) — higher peaks = more killing
- Examples: aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones
- Dosing strategy: larger, less frequent doses (e.g. once-daily aminoglycoside dosing) to maximize peak while allowing "drug-free" intervals (reduces toxicity, exploits post-antibiotic effect)
-
**Other PD concepts:**

- **Post-antibiotic effect (PAE)** – continued suppression of bacterial growth even after drug concentration falls below MIC (more bactericidal effect, important for dosing interval design)
- **AUC/MIC ratio** – relevant for some drugs (e.g. vancomycin, fluoroquinolones) – combines both time and concentration effects
-

## WEEK 8: TREATMENT & MANAGEMENT OF HIV**Clinical guidelines overview:**- ART recommended for **all** HIV-positive individuals regardless of CD4 count (current WHO/most national guidelines — "treat all" approach)
- Goal: undetectable viral load, CD4 recovery, prevention of transmission ("U=U" – undetectable = untransmittable)
-
**HAART (Highly Active Antiretroviral Therapy) review:**

- Standard first-line: 2 NRTIs + 1 INSTI (e.g. tenofovir + lamivudine + dolutegravir — common in many national guidelines including Kenya)
- Regular monitoring: viral load, CD4 count, adherence counseling
-
**Major side effects of ARTs (by class):**

- **NRTIs** – tenofovir: nephrotoxicity, ↓ bone density; abacavir: hypersensitivity reaction (test for HLA-B*5701 before starting)
- **NNRTIs** – efavirenz: CNS effects (vivid dreams, dizziness), hepatotoxicity, teratogenicity concerns (historically)
- **Protease inhibitors** – metabolic effects (dyslipidemia, insulin resistance, lipodystrophy), GI upset
- **INSTIs** – generally well-tolerated; dolutegravir: weight gain, rare neuropsychiatric effects
-
**Key counseling points:**

- Strict adherence essential — poor adherence → resistance development
- Drug interactions are common (especially with PIs, NNRTIs as CYP450 substrates/inducers/inhibitors)
- Lifelong therapy — not curative, but fully suppressive when adherent
-
## WEEK 9: EMERGING COMMUNICABLE DISEASES — COVID-19**Pharmacological management (general principles):**
- **Antivirals:** nirmatrelvir/ritonavir (Paxlovid) – protease inhibitor combination, given early in high-risk patients; remdesivir – RNA polymerase inhibitor, used in hospitalized patients
- **Anti-inflammatory:** dexamethasone – reduces mortality in patients requiring supplemental oxygen (by dampening cytokine-driven lung injury)
- **Monoclonal antibodies** – used against specific variants (efficacy varies as virus mutates)
- **Anticoagulation** – prophylactic dosing in hospitalized patients (COVID associated with increased thrombotic risk)
-
**Vaccines:**

- mRNA vaccines (Pfizer, Moderna) – deliver genetic instructions for spike protein → immune response
- Vector-based vaccines (AstraZeneca, J&J) – use a modified virus to deliver spike protein gene
- Key teaching point: vaccination reduces severity/hospitalization even though breakthrough infections occur
-
**General principles for emerging diseases (broader exam-relevant takeaway):**

- Importance of surveillance, rapid diagnostics, and adaptable treatment guidelines as evidence evolves
- Repurposing of existing antivirals/anti-inflammatories during outbreaks before disease-specific drugs are developed
-
## WEEK 10: MEDICATIONS IN DERMATOLOGY — ACNE**Acne pathophysiology (brief):**
- Increased sebum production + follicular hyperkeratinization + *Cutibacterium acnes* colonization + inflammation
-
**Topical treatments:**

- **Benzoyl peroxide** – antibacterial, comedolytic
- **Topical retinoids** (tretinoin, adapalene) – normalize follicular keratinization, first-line for comedonal acne
- **Topical antibiotics** (clindamycin) – often combined with benzoyl peroxide to reduce resistance

**Systemic treatments:**

- **Oral antibiotics** (doxycycline, minocycline) – for moderate-severe inflammatory acne; anti-inflammatory + antibacterial effect
- **Hormonal therapy** – combined oral contraceptives, spironolactone (anti-androgen) – useful in females with hormonal acne pattern
- **Isotretinoin (oral retinoid)** – for severe/nodulocystic/treatment-resistant acne
-
**Major toxicity:** highly teratogenic (absolute contraindication in pregnancy, requires strict contraception monitoring), mucocutaneous dryness, hyperlipidemia, mood changes (monitor for depression)

**Retinoid drug therapy (general class concept):**
- Vitamin A derivatives – regulate epithelial cell growth/differentiation
- All retinoids (topical and oral) carry photosensitivity risk and teratogenicity concerns (oral especially)
-
**Sunscreen preparations:**

- **Physical/mineral (zinc oxide, titanium dioxide)** – reflect/scatter UV rays, broad-spectrum, less irritating
- **Chemical (avobenzone, oxybenzone)** – absorb UV radiation, convert to heat
- Important counseling point especially for patients on retinoids/photosensitizing antibiotics (doxycycline) — increased sun sensitivity

## WEEK 1: INTRODUCTION TO PHARMACOLOGY**Branches of pharmacology:**- **Pharmacodynamics** – what the drug does to the body
- **Pharmacokinetics** – what the body does to the drug
- **Pharmacotherapeutics** – clinical use of drugs to treat disease
- **Toxicology** – study of poisons/adverse effects
- **Pharmacogenomics** – genetic influence on drug response
- **Clinical pharmacology** – application of pharm principles in patient care
-
**Drug discovery (overview):**

- Target identification → lead compound discovery → preclinical testing (animal) → clinical trials (Phase I–IV) → regulatory approval → post-marketing surveillance
-
**Sources of drugs:**

- **Natural** – plants (morphine, digoxin), animals (insulin, heparin), minerals (lithium)
- **Synthetic** – fully lab-made (sulfonamides)
- **Semi-synthetic** – natural base, chemically modified (semi-synthetic penicillins)
- **Biotechnology/recombinant** – genetically engineered (recombinant human insulin, monoclonal antibodies)
- **Microbial** – antibiotics derived from fungi/bacteria (penicillin from *Penicillium*)
-
**Nomenclature of drugs:**

- **Chemical name** – describes molecular structure (precise, technical)
- **Generic (nonproprietary) name** – official, non-trademarked (e.g. paracetamol)
- **Brand/trade name** – manufacturer's proprietary name (e.g. Panadol)
- Generic names preferred in prescribing/teaching to avoid ambiguity
-
**Drug information sources:**

- Pharmacopoeias (official drug standards)
- Formularies (e.g. national/hospital formulary)
- Package inserts/SmPCs
- Peer-reviewed journals, clinical guidelines, drug information databases
-
**Drug classifications:**

- By chemical structure, mechanism of action, therapeutic use, or physiological system affected
- Examples of classification systems: ATC (Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical) classification, pregnancy categories (A/B/C/D/X)
-
**History of pharmacology (key milestones):**

- Ancient herbal medicine traditions (Egyptian, Chinese, Ayurvedic)
- 19th century – isolation of active compounds (morphine 1804, quinine 1820)
- Early 20th century – Paul Ehrlich's "magic bullet" concept (selective toxicity) → birth of modern chemotherapy
- 1928 – discovery of penicillin (Fleming)
- Mid-20th century onward – rational drug design, receptor theory, regulatory frameworks (post-thalidomide tragedy → stricter drug safety testing)
-
## WEEK 2: PK/PD INTRO, PHARMACEUTICS, DRUG INTERACTIONS, TOXICOLOGY, PRESCRIBING**Intro to PK & PD (foundational definitions):**
- PK = ADME (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion) — concentration vs time
- PD = drug-receptor interaction → effect — concentration vs effect
-
**Pharmaceutics:**

- Study of drug formulation and dosage form design (tablets, capsules, injections, etc.)
- Affects bioavailability, stability, and route of administration choice
-
**Pharmacotherapeutics:**

- Application of pharmacological knowledge to actual disease treatment — choosing the right drug for the right condition
-
**Drug interactions (categories):**

- **Pharmacokinetic interactions** – one drug alters ADME of another (e.g. enzyme induction/inhibition affecting metabolism)
- **Pharmacodynamic interactions** – additive, synergistic, or antagonistic effects at the receptor/effect level (e.g. two CNS depressants combined)
- **Pharmaceutical (physical/chemical) interactions** – incompatibility before administration (e.g. precipitation when mixed in IV line)
-
**Drug discovery, targets of therapy & clinical trials:**

- **Common drug targets:** receptors, enzymes, ion channels, transporters, nucleic acids
- **Clinical trial phases:**
-
Phase I – small healthy volunteer group, safety/dosing

- Phase II – small patient group, efficacy + side effects
- Phase III – large patient population, confirms efficacy/safety vs standard treatment
- Phase IV – post-marketing surveillance (real-world, long-term safety)
-
**Basic principles in toxicology:**

- **Dose-response relationship** – "the dose makes the poison" (Paracelsus)
- **Types of toxicity:** acute vs chronic; local vs systemic
- **LD50** – dose lethal to 50% of test population (measure of acute toxicity)
- Antidotes/management principles: supportive care, decontamination (activated charcoal), specific antidotes where available (e.g. naloxone for opioids, N-acetylcysteine for paracetamol)
-
**Medication Therapy Management (MTM) & prescribing principles:**

- MTM = structured process to optimize therapeutic outcomes: medication review, identifying drug therapy problems, developing a care plan, follow-up
- **Principles of a good prescription:**
-
Correct patient identification, drug name (generic preferred), dose, route, frequency, duration

- Clear, legible, signed, dated
- Consider patient-specific factors: age, renal/hepatic function, allergies, pregnancy status, concurrent medications
- Avoid unnecessary polypharmacy; review regularly
-
## WEEK 3: ANS & CNS PHARMACOLOGY PART 1 — PERIPHERAL NERVOUS SYSTEM**Sympathomimetics (adrenergic agonists):**
- **Direct-acting** – act directly on adrenergic receptors (epinephrine – α/β; norepinephrine – mainly α1/β1; phenylephrine – α1 selective; albuterol/salbutamol – β2 selective)
- **Indirect-acting** – cause NE release or block reuptake (amphetamine, ephedrine)
- **Clinical uses:** anaphylaxis (epinephrine), hypotension/shock (norepinephrine), asthma/bronchospasm (β2 agonists), nasal decongestion (α1 agonists)
-
**Adrenergic blockers (antagonists):**

- **α-blockers:** prazosin (α1-selective, used in BPH/hypertension), phentolamine (non-selective, used in pheochromocytoma)
- **β-blockers:** propranolol (non-selective), metoprolol/atenolol (β1-selective, "cardioselective")
- **Clinical uses:** hypertension, angina, arrhythmias, BPH, migraine prophylaxis
- **Caution:** non-selective β-blockers can worsen bronchospasm in asthma/COPD (β2 blockade)
-
**Cholinomimetics (cholinergic agonists):**

- **Direct-acting** – bethanechol (muscarinic agonist, urinary retention), pilocarpine (glaucoma, miosis)
- **Indirect-acting (AChE inhibitors)** – neostigmine (reverses neuromuscular blockade, myasthenia gravis), physostigmine (crosses BBB, used in anticholinergic toxicity), donepezil (Alzheimer's)
- **Toxicity (organophosphate poisoning – AChE inhibition):** SLUDGE syndrome (Salivation, Lacrimation, Urination, Defecation, GI distress, Emesis); antidote = atropine + pralidoxime
-
**Cholinergic antagonists (anticholinergics):**

- **Muscarinic antagonists:** atropine (↑HR, ↓secretions, mydriasis, used in bradycardia/organophosphate poisoning), ipratropium (bronchodilation, COPD/asthma), oxybutynin (overactive bladder)
- **Effects mnemonic ("blind as a bat, dry as a bone, red as a beet, mad as a hatter"):** mydriasis/blurred vision, dry mouth/secretions, flushing, CNS effects (confusion, especially in elderly)
-
**Neuromuscular blockers:**

- **Depolarizing** – succinylcholine (rapid onset/short duration, used in intubation; risk of malignant hyperthermia, hyperkalemia)
- **Non-depolarizing (competitive)** – rocuronium, vecuronium (longer acting, reversed with neostigmine + anticholinergic, or sugammadex for rocuronium/vecuronium specifically)
-
**Local anesthetics:**

- **MOA** – block voltage-gated Na+ channels → prevent nerve conduction
- **Types:** esters (procaine, short-acting, metabolized by plasma cholinesterase) vs amides (lidocaine, bupivacaine, longer-acting, hepatically metabolized — easy memory trick: amides have 2 "i"s in name, e.g. lidocaine)
- **Toxicity:** CNS excitation→seizures, cardiotoxicity (especially bupivacaine); epinephrine often co-administered to prolong action/reduce systemic absorption (vasoconstriction) — except in areas with limited collateral circulation (fingers, toes, nose, ears, penis)
-
## WEEK 4: ANS & CNS PHARMACOLOGY PART 2 — CENTRAL NERVOUS SYSTEM**Neurotransmitters of the brain:**
- **Excitatory:** glutamate (main excitatory NT)
- **Inhibitory:** GABA (main inhibitory NT), glycine
- **Monoamines:** dopamine, norepinephrine, serotonin — implicated in mood, psychosis, movement disorders
- **Acetylcholine:** memory/cognition (relevant in Alzheimer's, Parkinson's balance)
-
**Antidepressants:**

- SSRIs (fluoxetine) – first-line, fewer side effects than older classes
- TCAs (amitriptyline) – effective but more anticholinergic/cardiotoxic side effects
- MAOIs (phenelzine) – reserved for treatment-resistant depression, dietary tyramine restriction
- SNRIs (venlafaxine) – block both serotonin and NE reuptake
-
**Psychomotor stimulants:**

- Amphetamines, methylphenidate – ↑ dopamine/NE release, used in ADHD, narcolepsy
- Caffeine – adenosine receptor antagonist, mild CNS stimulant
- Risk: abuse potential, cardiovascular effects (↑HR, ↑BP)
-
**Antipsychotic drugs:**

- **Typical (first-gen):** haloperidol, chlorpromazine – block D2 receptors; extrapyramidal side effects (EPS) common (akathisia, dystonia, tardive dyskinesia)
- **Atypical (second-gen):** risperidone, olanzapine, clozapine – block D2 + serotonin (5-HT2A); fewer EPS but more metabolic side effects (weight gain, dyslipidemia, diabetes risk)
- Clozapine – reserved for treatment-resistant schizophrenia; risk of agranulocytosis (requires regular blood monitoring)
-
**Antiemetics:**

- 5-HT3 antagonists (ondansetron) – chemo-induced/postoperative nausea
- D2 antagonists (metoclopramide, prochlorperazine) – also prokinetic; risk of EPS
- Antihistamines (diphenhydramine) – motion sickness
- NK1 antagonists (aprepitant) – used adjunctively in highly emetogenic chemo
-
**Anti-parkinson drugs:**

- **Levodopa + carbidopa** – dopamine precursor + peripheral decarboxylase inhibitor (reduces peripheral side effects, ↑ CNS availability) — mainstay of treatment
- **Dopamine agonists** (pramipexole) – directly stimulate dopamine receptors
- **MAO-B inhibitors** (selegiline) – ↓ dopamine breakdown
- **Anticholinergics** (benztropine) – help with tremor, used in younger patients
- Key pathology: loss of dopaminergic neurons in substantia nigra → relative ACh excess
-
**Central analgesics (opioids):**

- **MOA** – agonists at μ-opioid receptors → analgesia, euphoria, respiratory depression
- Morphine (prototype), fentanyl (highly potent, fast-acting), codeine (weaker, prodrug needing CYP2D6 conversion)
- Adverse effects: respiratory depression (most dangerous), constipation, sedation, dependence/tolerance
- Antidote: naloxone (competitive μ-antagonist)
-
**General anesthetics:**

- **Inhalational** – sevoflurane, isoflurane, nitrous oxide
- **IV** – propofol (rapid onset/recovery, common induction agent), ketamine (dissociative anesthesia, also analgesic, used in resource-limited settings)
- Stages of anesthesia: analgesia → excitement/delirium → surgical anesthesia → medullary depression (danger zone — avoid)
-
**Anti-anxiety agents:**

- Benzodiazepines – enhance GABA-A activity, fast-acting, risk of dependence
- Buspirone – non-benzodiazepine anxiolytic, no sedation/dependence, slower onset (partial 5-HT1A agonist)
-
**Anticonvulsants:** *(same core content as CNS section already covered — quick recap)*

- Na+ channel blockers (phenytoin, carbamazepine) – generalized tonic-clonic/partial seizures
- GABA enhancers (valproate, benzodiazepines, phenobarbital)
- T-type Ca2+ channel blockers (ethosuximide) – absence seizures specifically
-
## WEEK 5: AUTACOID PHARMACOLOGY**What are autacoids:** locally produced, locally acting biological substances ("local hormones") with rapid, short-lived effects.
**a) Histamine and antihistamines:**
- **Histamine receptors:**
-
H1 – smooth muscle contraction, vasodilation, ↑capillary permeability (allergy/inflammation)

- H2 – gastric acid secretion (stomach parietal cells)
- H3/H4 – mainly CNS/immune modulation (less clinically emphasized at this level)
- **H1 antagonists (antihistamines):**
-
First-gen (diphenhydramine, chlorpheniramine) – sedating, cross BBB, also anticholinergic

- Second-gen (loratadine, cetirizine) – less sedating, preferred for allergic rhinitis/urticaria
- Uses: allergies, urticaria, motion sickness (first-gen), mild sedation
- **H2 antagonists (ranitidine, famotidine):** reduce gastric acid secretion, used in peptic ulcer disease/GERD (largely surpassed by PPIs but still relevant)
-
**b) 5-Hydroxytryptamine (serotonin) and its antagonists:**

- Serotonin (5-HT) – multiple receptor subtypes (5-HT1 to 5-HT7); roles in mood, GI motility, vascular tone, platelet aggregation
- **5-HT3 antagonists** (ondansetron) – antiemetic, block serotonin in gut/CTZ
- **5-HT1 agonists (triptans – sumatriptan)** – used in acute migraine treatment (vasoconstriction of cranial vessels)
- **5-HT2 antagonists** – some atypical antipsychotics work partly via this mechanism
-
**c) Lipid-derived autacoids and PAF:**

- **Eicosanoids** (from arachidonic acid):
-
Prostaglandins – inflammation, pain, fever, gastric protection (PGE2), uterine contraction

- Thromboxane A2 – platelet aggregation, vasoconstriction
- Leukotrienes – bronchoconstriction, chemotaxis in inflammation (relevant in asthma)
- **Platelet-activating factor (PAF):** potent phospholipid mediator → platelet aggregation, bronchoconstriction, vasodilation, key role in inflammatory/allergic responses
- Clinical relevance: basis for NSAID/leukotriene-inhibitor/antiplatelet drug mechanisms (expanded further in Week 10)

## WEEK 6: ANTI-INFECTIVE STRATEGIES & "THE ENEMIES"**Basic strategies of antimicrobial therapy:**
- Selective toxicity – target structures/processes unique to the pathogen, not the host (e.g. bacterial cell wall, which human cells lack)
- Empiric vs definitive therapy (start broad based on likely pathogen, narrow once identified)
- Combination therapy rationale (broaden coverage, synergy, prevent resistance — see Trimester 2 Week 1)
-
**Managing antimicrobial resistance (AMR) — strategies:**

- Antimicrobial stewardship programs
- Avoiding unnecessary/inappropriate prescribing
- Surveillance of local resistance patterns
- Infection control measures to prevent spread of resistant organisms
- Development of new agents/combination products (e.g. β-lactam + β-lactamase inhibitor)
-
**The Enemies — Gram-positive pathogens:**

- *Staphylococcus aureus* (incl. MRSA), *Streptococcus* species, *Enterococcus*
- Cell wall structure: thick peptidoglycan layer, retains Gram stain (purple)
-
**Gram-negative pathogens:**

- *E. coli*, *Klebsiella*, *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, *Neisseria*
- Thin peptidoglycan + outer membrane with LPS (endotoxin) — harder to penetrate, intrinsic resistance to many agents
-
**Anaerobes:**

- *Bacteroides fragilis* (GI/intra-abdominal), *Clostridium* species
- Often found in deep tissue/abdominal infections; require specific anaerobic coverage (metronidazole, clindamycin, carbapenems)
-
**Mycobacteria:**

- *M. tuberculosis* – acid-fast, slow-growing, requires prolonged multi-drug therapy (RIPE regimen)
- *M. leprae* – causes leprosy, treated with multidrug therapy (dapsone, rifampin, clofazimine)
-
**Viruses:**

- Obligate intracellular parasites — antivirals target viral-specific enzymes (reverse transcriptase, protease, polymerase) to achieve selective toxicity
-
**Fungi:**

- Eukaryotic — fewer unique targets vs bacteria, hence fewer antifungal classes; target ergosterol (unique to fungal membranes) or fungal cell wall components
-
**Protozoans and helminths:**

- Protozoans (malaria, amoebiasis, toxoplasmosis) – target unique metabolic pathways
- Helminths (worms) – target neuromuscular function unique to the parasite (e.g. albendazole disrupts microtubule function)
-
## WEEK 7: CELL WALL & PROTEIN SYNTHESIS INHIBITORS, QUINOLONES**Bacterial cell wall synthesis inhibitors:**
- **β-lactams** – bind penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs), inhibit peptidoglycan cross-linking
-
Penicillins (penicillin G, amoxicillin) – narrow to extended spectrum

- Cephalosporins (1st–5th generation – increasing gram-negative coverage with each generation, 5th gen covers MRSA)
- Carbapenems (meropenem, imipenem) – broadest spectrum β-lactams, reserved for serious/resistant infections
- Monobactams (aztreonam) – gram-negative only, safe in penicillin allergy
- **Vancomycin** – inhibits cell wall precursor synthesis (different mechanism, binds D-Ala-D-Ala); gram-positive only (including MRSA)
- **Resistance mechanism:** β-lactamase enzymes (hydrolyze the β-lactam ring) → combat with β-lactamase inhibitors (clavulanate, tazobactam)
-
**Bactericidal protein synthesis inhibitors:**

- **Aminoglycosides** (gentamicin, tobramycin) – bind 30S ribosomal subunit, cause misreading of mRNA; concentration-dependent killing; nephrotoxic + ototoxic
-
**Bacteriostatic protein synthesis inhibitors:**

- **Tetracyclines** (doxycycline) – bind 30S, block tRNA attachment; photosensitivity, teeth discoloration (avoid in pregnancy/children)
- **Macrolides** (azithromycin, erythromycin) – bind 50S, block translocation; GI upset, QT prolongation
- **Clindamycin** – binds 50S; good anaerobic coverage; risk of C. difficile colitis
- **Chloramphenicol** – binds 50S; aplastic anemia, gray baby syndrome (limited modern use)
- **Linezolid** – binds 50S; used for resistant gram-positives (VRE, MRSA); risk of serotonin syndrome with SSRIs, thrombocytopenia
-
**Quinolones and urinary tract antiseptics:**

- **Fluoroquinolones** (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin) – inhibit DNA gyrase/topoisomerase IV; broad spectrum incl. gram-negatives/Pseudomonas; risk of tendon rupture, QT prolongation, avoid in children/pregnancy (cartilage toxicity)
- **Nitrofurantoin** – urinary antiseptic, concentrates in urine (not for systemic infections), used for uncomplicated cystitis
- **Fosfomycin** – single-dose option for uncomplicated UTI, inhibits early cell wall synthesis step
-
## WEEK 8: ANTIMETABOLITES, ANTIVIRALS, ANTIRETROVIRALS, ANTIFUNGALS**Antimetabolites (antibacterial context — distinct from cancer antimetabolites):**
- **Sulfonamides** (sulfamethoxazole) – inhibit dihydropteroate synthase (folate synthesis pathway)
- **Trimethoprim** – inhibits dihydrofolate reductase (next step in same pathway)
- Combined as **TMP-SMX (co-trimoxazole)** – sequential blockade = synergistic bactericidal effect; used in UTIs, PCP prophylaxis/treatment, some GI infections
- Adverse effects: hypersensitivity (Stevens-Johnson syndrome risk), hyperkalemia, hemolysis in G6PD deficiency
-
**Antiviral therapy (Herpes):**

- **Acyclovir, valacyclovir, famciclovir** – guanosine analogs, require viral thymidine kinase for activation (selective toxicity — only works in infected cells) → inhibit viral DNA polymerase
- Uses: HSV (oral/genital), VZV (chickenpox/shingles)
- Adverse effects: generally well-tolerated; nephrotoxicity with rapid IV infusion (crystallization in renal tubules) — ensure hydration
-
**Antiretroviral therapy (HIV & Hepatitis):** *(same core classes as Trimester 2 Week 5 — quick recap)*

- NRTIs, NNRTIs, PIs, INSTIs, entry inhibitors for HIV
- Hepatitis B: tenofovir, entecavir (nucleos(t)ide analogs)
- Hepatitis C: direct-acting antivirals (sofosbuvir-based regimens)
-
**Antifungal drugs:**

- **Azoles** (fluconazole, itraconazole, voriconazole) – inhibit fungal cytochrome P450 enzyme (14-α-demethylase) → block ergosterol synthesis; significant drug interactions (CYP450 inhibitors themselves)
- **Polyenes** (amphotericin B) – binds ergosterol directly, forms pores in fungal membrane; broad-spectrum but highly nephrotoxic, infusion reactions
- **Echinocandins** (caspofungin) – inhibit fungal cell wall β-glucan synthesis; good safety profile, used for invasive Candida/Aspergillus
- **Terbinafine** – inhibits squalene epoxidase (ergosterol synthesis pathway); used for dermatophyte infections (nail/skin)
-
## WEEK 9: BASIC IMMUNOLOGY & VACCINES**Basic principles of immunology:**
- **Innate immunity** – non-specific, immediate (skin barrier, phagocytes, complement, inflammation)
- **Adaptive immunity** – specific, has memory
-
**Humoral immunity** – B cells → antibodies (immunoglobulins: IgG, IgM, IgA, IgE, IgD)

- **Cell-mediated immunity** – T cells (helper CD4+, cytotoxic CD8+)
- **Antigen presentation** – MHC class I (presents to CD8+ T cells) vs MHC class II (presents to CD4+ T cells)
-
**Vaccines:**

- **Live attenuated** – weakened pathogen, strong/long-lasting immunity, contraindicated in immunocompromised/pregnancy (e.g. MMR, BCG, oral polio)
- **Inactivated/killed** – safer but generally needs booster doses (e.g. inactivated polio, hepatitis A)
- **Subunit/conjugate** – specific antigenic component only (e.g. HPV vaccine, pneumococcal conjugate vaccine)
- **Toxoid** – inactivated bacterial toxin, stimulates antitoxin antibodies (tetanus, diphtheria)
- **mRNA vaccines** – deliver genetic code for antigen production by host cells (COVID-19 vaccines)
- **Herd immunity concept** – sufficient population immunity protects unvaccinated/immunocompromised individuals indirectly
-
## WEEK 10: ANTI-INFLAMMATORY AGENTS — EICOSANOIDS, COX, NSAIDS, CORTICOSTEROIDS**Eicosanoids (recap + expansion from Week 5):**
- Arachidonic acid metabolism → two main pathways:
-
**COX pathway** → prostaglandins, thromboxane

- **Lipoxygenase pathway** → leukotrienes
-
**Cyclooxygenase enzymes:**

- **COX-1** – constitutive, "housekeeping" (gastric mucosal protection, platelet thromboxane production, renal blood flow)
- **COX-2** – induced during inflammation (mediates pain, fever, inflammation)
- This distinction explains why selective COX-2 inhibitors cause less GI toxicity than non-selective NSAIDs
-
**NSAIDs:**

- **MOA** – inhibit COX enzymes → ↓ prostaglandin synthesis → anti-inflammatory, analgesic, antipyretic
- **Non-selective** (ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac) – inhibit both COX-1 and COX-2
- **COX-2 selective** (celecoxib) – spares COX-1, less GI toxicity, but ↑ cardiovascular risk
- **Aspirin** – irreversibly inhibits COX (unique among NSAIDs); low-dose used for antiplatelet effect (cardioprotection)
- **Adverse effects:** GI ulceration/bleeding (COX-1 inhibition reduces protective mucus/bicarbonate), nephrotoxicity (reduced renal prostaglandins → reduced renal blood flow), cardiovascular risk (especially COX-2 selective agents), bleeding risk (antiplatelet effect)
- **Avoid in:** peptic ulcer disease, renal impairment, asthma (aspirin-sensitive asthma), late pregnancy (premature closure of ductus arteriosus)
-
**Corticosteroids:**

- **MOA** – broad anti-inflammatory/immunosuppressive action via glucocorticoid receptor → inhibit phospholipase A2 → ↓ arachidonic acid release → ↓ both prostaglandins AND leukotrienes (broader than NSAIDs)
- **Examples:** prednisone, dexamethasone, hydrocortisone
- **Uses:** asthma, autoimmune disease, allergic reactions, adrenal insufficiency replacement, many oncologic/inflammatory conditions
- **Adverse effects (long-term use):** adrenal suppression (HPA axis), osteoporosis, hyperglycemia, weight gain/Cushingoid features, increased infection risk, mood changes, skin thinning
- **Key counseling point:** never stop abruptly after prolonged use — taper to avoid adrenal crisis
-
## WEEK 11: GOUT, RHEUMATOID ARTHRITIS, IMMUNOSUPPRESSION**Gout pathophysiology (brief):**
- Hyperuricemia → monosodium urate crystal deposition in joints → acute inflammatory arthritis
-
**Colchicine:**

- **MOA** – inhibits microtubule polymerization → ↓ neutrophil migration/phagocytosis of urate crystals → ↓ inflammation
- Used for acute gout flares and prophylaxis
- Adverse effects: GI upset (diarrhea common), narrow therapeutic index
-
**Uricosuric agents:**

- **Probenecid** – inhibits renal tubular reabsorption of uric acid → ↑ excretion
- Used for chronic gout management (not acute flares); avoid in renal impairment/active stones
-
**Allopurinol:**

- **MOA** – xanthine oxidase inhibitor → ↓ uric acid production (prevents purine breakdown to uric acid)
- First-line for chronic gout prevention (not for acute attacks — can initially worsen flares if started during one)
- Adverse effects: hypersensitivity reactions (rare but severe — Stevens-Johnson syndrome/DRESS), drug interactions (↑ azathioprine/6-mercaptopurine levels, since these are also metabolized by xanthine oxidase)
-
**Immunosuppressive & immunomodulating agents (for RA and transplant/autoimmune contexts):**

- **Methotrexate (low-dose)** – first-line DMARD in RA, anti-inflammatory at low doses (different mechanism emphasis than oncology dosing)
- **Biologic DMARDs:**
-
TNF-α inhibitors (infliximab, etanercept, adalimumab) – block tumor necrosis factor, key inflammatory cytokine in RA

- Other biologics – rituximab (anti-CD20, depletes B cells), tocilizumab (IL-6 receptor antagonist)
- **Calcineurin inhibitors** (cyclosporine, tacrolimus) – inhibit T-cell activation, used in transplant rejection prevention and severe autoimmune disease
- **Azathioprine** – purine analog, immunosuppressive, used in transplant/autoimmune disease; interacts with allopurinol (see above)
- **Adverse effects across class:** increased infection risk (general immunosuppression theme), specific risks per agent (TNF inhibitors → reactivation of latent TB, screen before starting; methotrexate → hepatotoxicity, requires folate supplementation)
