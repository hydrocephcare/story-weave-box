# Antihypertensive Drugs

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Cardiology and Clinical Pharmacology**

> **In one line:** hypertension is persistently raised arterial pressure (blood pressure = cardiac output × peripheral resistance), a "silent killer" that damages the heart, brain, kidney and eyes. Drugs lower BP by reducing blood volume (diuretics), blocking the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (ACE inhibitors, ARBs), relaxing vessels (calcium channel blockers, vasodilators, alpha-blockers), slowing the heart (beta-blockers) or reducing sympathetic outflow (central agents). Choice depends on efficacy, safety, suitability (compelling indications) and cost.

## What you must know

- Hypertension affects **25 to 30% of adults**; only about **6% of treated patients reach goal BP** (as quoted in the lecture). It is usually asymptomatic.
- **Primary (essential)** has no identifiable cause; **secondary** follows kidney disease, adrenal tumours or Cushing syndrome, and some drugs.
- First step: **lifestyle modification** (weight loss, exercise, salt reduction, stop smoking). Drug therapy for BP of **140/90 mmHg or above** (target below 130/80 in diabetics).
- **Thiazide diuretics** (chlorthalidone, hydrochlorothiazide) are the preferred initial therapy in most uncomplicated cases (ALLHAT: chlorthalidone was superior to ACE inhibitor, calcium channel blocker and alpha-blocker for preventing cardiovascular events).
- **ACE inhibitors**: cough, angioedema, hyperkalaemia, first-dose hypotension; **contraindicated in pregnancy and bilateral renal artery stenosis**. **ARBs** are the same without cough or angioedema.
- **Never combine drugs of the same class** or with the same mechanism.
- **Beta-blockers**: stop gradually; non-selective ones (propranolol) must not be given to asthma/COPD patients.
- **Sodium nitroprusside** is for hypertensive emergency; watch for **cyanide toxicity**.

## 1. Background

- Hypertension is a common condition in which the long-term force of blood on artery walls is high enough to cause eventual damage. It persists for years without symptoms ("silent killer") and ends in **end-organ failure** (stroke, heart failure, renal failure) and death; it is a leading cause of death.
- Objectives of this topic: know the factors controlling BP, the drug classes, their mechanism, use, adverse effects and contraindications, and how to pick a drug for a particular patient.

### Types

| Type | Features |
|---|---|
| **Primary (essential)** | Mostly no identifiable cause; develops gradually over years |
| **Secondary** | Due to another disease (kidney disease, adrenal tumours, Cushing syndrome); tends to appear suddenly and cause higher BP than primary |
| **Drug-induced** | Alcohol, cocaine, antidepressants, caffeine, corticosteroids, ciclosporin, erythropoietin, oestrogens, nasal decongestants, **NSAIDs** |
| **Rebound hypertension** | BP rises after stopping or lowering the dose of a drug (typically **clonidine**) |

### Treatment principles

1. **Lifestyle modification:** weight loss, physical activity, sodium reduction, smoking cessation.
2. **Drug therapy** when BP is 140/90 mmHg or higher (below 130/80 mmHg is the goal in diabetics).

> **Check:** the slide writes "target BP = > 140/90"; this is the treatment threshold, not the target. Treatment aims to bring BP below 140/90 (below 130/80 in diabetes and other high-risk patients).

**Why combine drugs?**

1. Synergism: better effect with lower individual doses, so fewer side effects.
2. The side effects of one drug can oppose those of another.

> **Exam tip:** **do not combine drugs of the same class or with the same mechanism**; this causes resistance and more side effects.

## 2. Classification of antihypertensive drugs

| Class | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Diuretics** | Hydrochlorothiazide, chlorothiazide, chlorthalidone (thiazides); furosemide (loop); amiloride, spironolactone (potassium-sparing) |
| **Drugs acting on the RAAS** | **ACE inhibitors:** captopril, lisinopril, enalapril, ramipril. **ARBs:** losartan, valsartan, candesartan, telmisartan, irbesartan |
| **Calcium channel blockers** | Verapamil, diltiazem, nifedipine (also amlodipine) |
| **Direct vasodilators** | Hydralazine, minoxidil, diazoxide, sodium nitroprusside |
| **Beta-blockers** | Nadolol, bisoprolol, atenolol, metoprolol, propranolol; labetalol and carvedilol (alpha and beta) |
| **Alpha-1 blockers** | Prazosin, doxazosin, terazosin |
| **Centrally acting sympatholytics** | Clonidine, alpha-methyldopa |

## 3. Diuretics

| Group | Examples | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| **Thiazides** | Hydrochlorothiazide, chlorothiazide, chlorthalidone | **Best for hypertension without other complications.** Used as initial therapy in mild to moderate hypertension |
| **Loop diuretics** | Furosemide | More potent diuresis but smaller BP effect. Used in hypertension with renal impairment, heart failure and oedema |
| **Potassium-sparing** | Amiloride, aldosterone antagonists (mainly spironolactone) | Minimal BP-lowering alone; used with thiazides or loop diuretics to reduce the potassium loss they cause |

**Mechanism (thiazides):**

- They increase sodium and water excretion, reducing blood volume, cardiac output and renal blood flow. This **initial diuresis lasts 4 to 6 weeks**.
- With long-term treatment, plasma volume returns toward normal, but the **hypotensive effect persists** because of **reduced peripheral vascular resistance** (a fall in vessel wall sodium leads to reduced calcium in smooth muscle through sodium-calcium exchange).

**Adverse effects and cautions:** hypokalaemia, hyponatraemia, hyperuricaemia and **gout**, hyperglycaemia, hypercalcaemia; **contraindicated in gout and in hypokalaemia/hyponatraemia**.

## 4. Drugs acting on the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system

### 4.1 ACE inhibitors (captopril, lisinopril, enalapril, ramipril)

**Mechanism:**

- Decrease **angiotensin II** (vasoconstrictor) and increase **bradykinin** (vasodilator) by preventing its breakdown by ACE.
- BP falls mainly by **vasodilatation** (lower peripheral resistance) **without reflex tachycardia** or raised cardiac output. A fall in aldosterone adds to the effect.
- Especially effective when hypertension is **renin-dependent** (renovascular hypertension; young white patients).
- **Black and elderly patients** have low renin, so ACE inhibitors work less well alone; use calcium channel blockers or thiazides.

**Pharmacokinetics:**

- Polar, excreted in urine, do not cross the blood-brain barrier.
- Rapid oral absorption; **food reduces bioavailability** (take on an empty stomach).
- Long half-life, so **once daily** (captopril 3 to 4 times daily).
- **Enalapril and ramipril are prodrugs** activated in the liver. **Enalaprilat** is the active form of enalapril and can be given IV in hypertensive emergency.
- Slow onset: **2 to 4 weeks** for the full effect.

**Uses:**

- Essential hypertension.
- Hypertension with chronic renal disease, ischaemic heart disease or diabetes (renoprotective).
- **Heart failure**: reduce preload and afterload, lower cardiac work, inhibit remodelling.

**Adverse effects:**

- **Dry cough** (bradykinin).
- **Angioneurotic oedema** (nose, tongue, throat, larynx; bradykinin accumulation).
- **First-dose hypotension** (give at bedtime, start low, increase gradually).
- **Hyperkalaemia.**
- Acute renal failure, especially in **bilateral renal artery stenosis**.
- Severe hypotension in hypovolaemic patients.
- **Fetal renal failure and oligohydramnios**.
- **Captopril only** (sulfhydryl group): skin rash, fever, loss of taste (dysgeusia), proteinuria, neutropenia.

**Contraindications:** bilateral renal artery stenosis; hypovolaemia; **pregnancy (2nd and 3rd trimester: fetal hypotension, anuria, renal failure, malformation)**; hyperkalaemia; potassium-sparing diuretics (hyperkalaemia); **NSAIDs** (reduce the hypotensive effect by blocking bradykinin-mediated vasodilatation).

### 4.2 Angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs)

- Examples: losartan (has a potent active metabolite, so a long effect), valsartan, candesartan, telmisartan, irbesartan. Effective orally once daily; do not cross the blood-brain barrier.
- **Mechanism:** selective block of **AT1 receptors**. They block the receptor itself, not just ACE, so inhibition of angiotensin is **more complete** (other enzymes besides ACE also generate angiotensin II).
- **No effect on bradykinin**, so **no cough and no angioedema** (bradykinin is broken down by ACE). They can be used in asthmatic patients.
- **Uses:** first-line, especially with compelling indications: **diabetes, heart failure, chronic kidney disease**; also when ACE-inhibitor cough develops.
- Adverse effects and contraindications: as ACE inhibitors, except cough and angioedema.

> **Remember:** ACE inhibitor + ARB combination offers no benefit and raises the risk of hyperkalaemia and renal failure; avoid.

## 5. Calcium channel blockers (CCBs)

| Drug | Characteristic |
|---|---|
| **Nifedipine** (dihydropyridine) | Acts mainly on **vascular smooth muscle**: most selective vasodilator; best for hypertension. No active metabolite |
| **Verapamil** | Acts more on the **myocardium** (cardiac depressant). Active metabolites |
| **Diltiazem** | **Intermediate** effect on heart and vessels. Active metabolites |

- **Mechanism:** block calcium entry through L-type channels, causing **peripheral arteriolar vasodilatation** and **reduced cardiac contractility**.
- **Pharmacokinetics:** well absorbed orally (onset 0.5 to 2 hours); IV injection for emergencies (onset 1 to 3 minutes). Short half-life, so **sustained-release preparations** are used for once-daily dosing. Verapamil and nifedipine are over 90% protein-bound; diltiazem 70 to 80%.
- **Uses:** chronic hypertension, especially with **diabetes or angina**; **nicardipine IV** for hypertensive emergency. They are particularly useful in **black and elderly patients**, who have low renin and respond poorly to ACE inhibitors.

| Adverse effects | Drug |
|---|---|
| **Reflex tachycardia**, peripheral (ankle) oedema | Nifedipine |
| **Constipation**, peripheral oedema | Verapamil and diltiazem |
| Headache, hypotension, flushing (all from vasodilatation) | All |

> **Exam tip:** verapamil and diltiazem (non-dihydropyridines) should **not be combined with beta-blockers** because of the risk of heart block and bradycardia.

## 6. Beta-blockers

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Non-cardioselective** | Propranolol (never in asthma or COPD) |
| **Cardioselective (beta-1)** | Metoprolol, atenolol, bisoprolol |
| Mixed alpha and beta | Labetalol, carvedilol |

**Mechanism of BP lowering:** reduced cardiac output (beta-1 block), **reduced renin release** (beta-1), and a central effect.

**Use:**

- Mild to moderate hypertension; in severe cases combined with other drugs.
- They should **not be the primary agent for primary prevention** but are effective as **add-on therapy**.
- Take about **2 weeks** to reach optimal effect.
- Good evidence of benefit when **coronary artery disease** coexists.
- **Withdraw gradually** (abrupt withdrawal causes rebound tachycardia and angina).

**Adverse effects:** raised triglycerides, fatigue, **hypoglycaemia and masking of its symptoms in diabetics** (monitor), worsening of **peripheral arterial disease/Raynaud's**, erectile dysfunction, depression, insomnia, bronchoconstriction.

## 7. Sympatholytics

### Alpha-1 blockers (prazosin, doxazosin, terazosin)

- Block alpha-1 receptors in arterioles and veins, causing vasodilatation. Useful when hypertension coexists with **benign prostatic hyperplasia**. Not first-line (the doxazosin arm of ALLHAT showed more heart failure).
- Adverse effects: **first-dose postural hypotension** (take at night), dizziness, headache.

### Centrally acting drugs

| Drug | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Clonidine** | Direct **alpha-2 agonist** in the brainstem, reducing sympathetic outflow |
| **Alpha-methyldopa** | Indirect alpha-2 agonist: converted to **alpha-methylnorepinephrine** |

- Methyldopa is a standard drug for hypertension in **pregnancy**. Adverse effects: sedation, dry mouth, depression, and Coombs-positive haemolytic anaemia; abrupt clonidine withdrawal gives **rebound hypertension**.

## 8. Direct vasodilators

| Drug | Site and mechanism | Use | Specific adverse effects |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hydralazine** | Arteriolar dilator | Moderate to severe hypertension (with a diuretic and beta-blocker); hypertension in pregnancy (not first-line) | **Drug-induced lupus-like syndrome** |
| **Minoxidil** | Opens potassium channels (active metabolite is minoxidil sulphate); arteriolar dilator | Severe hypertension; **baldness** (topical) | **Hypertrichosis** (excess hair); contraindicated in women |
| **Diazoxide** | Opens potassium channels; arteriolar dilator | Hypertensive emergency (IV); **hypoglycaemia from insulinoma** | **Hyperglycaemia** (inhibits insulin release); contraindicated in diabetics |
| **Sodium nitroprusside** | Releases **nitric oxide**; arterio- and venodilator; IV infusion | **Hypertensive emergency**; severe heart failure | Severe hypotension; headache and palpitations; **methaemoglobinaemia**; **cyanide toxicity** (metabolic acidosis, arrhythmias, severe hypotension, death); thiocyanate toxicity |

**Common adverse effects of arteriolar vasodilators** (hydralazine, minoxidil): hypotension, **reflex tachycardia**, palpitation, angina, **salt and water retention (oedema)**. These come from sympathetic and RAAS activation, so give them with a **diuretic and a beta-blocker**.

## 9. Choosing a drug

- **Uncomplicated hypertension:** thiazide diuretic (preferred initial therapy for most), or CCB; ACE inhibitor/ARB.
- **Diabetes, CKD, heart failure:** ACE inhibitor or ARB.
- **Angina or post-myocardial infarction:** beta-blocker (+ ACE inhibitor).
- **Black and elderly patients:** CCB or thiazide.
- **Pregnancy:** methyldopa, labetalol, nifedipine; **never ACE inhibitors or ARBs**.
- **Hypertensive emergency:** IV labetalol, nicardipine, nitroprusside or enalaprilat.
- **ALLHAT** compared amlodipine, lisinopril and doxazosin against chlorthalidone; chlorthalidone was at least as good and cheapest, hence thiazides as preferred initial therapy.

## Key points to revise

1. Hypertension is a silent killer; start with lifestyle change, then drugs at 140/90 or higher (130/80 in diabetes).
2. Thiazides are preferred first-line in uncomplicated hypertension; avoid in gout, hypokalaemia and hyponatraemia.
3. ACE inhibitors: cough, angioedema, hyperkalaemia, first-dose hypotension; contraindicated in pregnancy and bilateral renal artery stenosis. ARBs have no cough or angioedema.
4. Dihydropyridine CCBs (nifedipine): vasodilators with reflex tachycardia and ankle oedema; verapamil and diltiazem: constipation and heart block risk.
5. Beta-blockers are add-on drugs; withdraw gradually; avoid non-selective ones in asthma and COPD; they mask hypoglycaemia.
6. Vasodilators (hydralazine, minoxidil) need a diuretic and a beta-blocker; hydralazine causes lupus-like syndrome; minoxidil causes hypertrichosis; diazoxide causes hyperglycaemia.
7. Nitroprusside (IV) treats hypertensive emergency; watch for cyanide toxicity.
8. Never combine drugs of the same class.
