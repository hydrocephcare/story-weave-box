Q24. Explain the pathogenesis of cervical squamous cell carcinoma.
- Persistent infection with high-risk HPV (16, 18) is the essential cause
- Viral oncoproteins **E6** degrades **p53** (loses apoptosis/DNA repair control); **E7** degrades **RB** (loses G1/S checkpoint control)
- Result: unchecked proliferation of cervical squamous cells at the transformation zone
- Progression: normal epithelium → low-grade CIN I → high-grade CIN II/III (carcinoma in situ) → invasion through basement membrane → invasive SCC
- Cofactors that promote progression: smoking, multiple sexual partners, early coitarche, immunosuppression (HIV), multiparity, long-term OCP use
- Without HPV clearance/screening (Pap smear detects dysplasia early), progression to invasive cancer can take 10-20 years
Q25. State 5 theories in the pathogenesis of endometriosis.
- **Retrograde menstruation (Sampson's theory)** — menstrual endometrium flows backward through fallopian tubes and implants on pelvic peritoneum/ovaries
- **Coelomic metaplasia** — peritoneal mesothelium undergoes metaplastic transformation into endometrial-type tissue
- **Vascular/lymphatic dissemination** — endometrial cells spread via blood or lymphatics to distant sites (explains rare extrapelvic endometriosis, e.g. lung)
- **Direct implantation** — surgical transplantation of endometrial tissue (e.g. into a caesarean scar)
- **Induction theory** — hormonal/immune factors induce undifferentiated peritoneal cells to differentiate into endometrial tissue
- *(Bonus)* Stem cell theory — bone-marrow-derived stem cells differentiate into ectopic endometrial tissue
Q26. State 5 degenerative changes uterine leiomyomas can undergo.
- **Hyaline degeneration** — most common, homogenous pink hyaline change
- **Cystic degeneration** — liquefaction creating cystic spaces
- **Calcification** — especially in postmenopausal, poorly vascularized fibroids
- **Red (carneous) degeneration** — haemorrhagic infarction, classically in pregnancy
- **Myxoid degeneration** — gelatinous, mucoid change
- *(Bonus)* Fatty change and necrosis (in rapidly growing or twisted pedunculated fibroids)
Q27. Classify ovarian neoplasms with examples.

Q28. Discuss the pathogenesis of toxaemia of pregnancy (pre-eclampsia/eclampsia).
- Root cause: **abnormal trophoblastic invasion** of maternal spiral arteries — cytotrophoblasts fail to remodel the spiral arteries into low-resistance vessels
- Result: persistently high-resistance, narrow spiral arteries → **placental hypoperfusion/ischemia**
- Ischemic placenta releases anti-angiogenic factors: increased **sFlt-1** (soluble Flt-1, a VEGF/PlGF antagonist) and decreased **PlGF** (placental growth factor)
- These circulate systemically → widespread **endothelial dysfunction**
- Endothelial dysfunction → vasospasm, increased vascular permeability, activation of coagulation
- Clinical result: maternal hypertension, proteinuria, oedema (pre-eclampsia); severe cases → seizures (eclampsia), HELLP syndrome (Haemolysis, Elevated Liver enzymes, Low Platelets)
Q29. Write short notes on Uterine Endometriosis.
- Definition: presence of endometrial glands and stroma outside the uterine cavity, most commonly ovaries, uterosacral ligaments, and pelvic peritoneum
- Pathogenesis: see Q25 theories (retrograde menstruation most widely accepted)
- Morphology: blue-black "powder-burn" lesions; ovarian involvement forms "chocolate cysts" (endometriomas) from cyclic bleeding of trapped old blood
- Clinical features: chronic pelvic pain, dysmenorrhea, dyspareunia, infertility
- Diagnosis: laparoscopy with biopsy showing endometrial glands + stroma + haemosiderin-laden macrophages
- Complications: infertility, adhesions, rarely malignant transformation (endometrioid or clear cell carcinoma)
Q30. Outline the classification of Primary Malignant Ovarian neoplasms.
- **Malignant epithelial tumours**
 — Serous cystadenocarcinoma (most common malignant ovarian tumour, often bilateral, psammoma bodies)
 — Mucinous cystadenocarcinoma
 — Endometrioid carcinoma (may coexist with endometriosis)
 — Clear cell carcinoma
 — Malignant Brenner tumour
- **Malignant germ cell tumours**
 — Dysgerminoma (most common malignant germ cell tumour)
 — Yolk sac tumour (AFP marker)
 — Embryonal carcinoma
 — Choriocarcinoma (β-hCG marker)
 — Immature teratoma
- **Malignant sex cord-stromal tumours**
 — Granulosa cell tumour (malignant potential, low-grade)
 — Sertoli-Leydig cell tumour (malignant forms)
Q31. Outline the morphologic features of Uterine Leiomyoma.
- Gross: well-circumscribed, round, firm, greyish-white mass with a characteristic **whorled cut surface**; often multiple, variable in size
- Location: intramural (within muscle wall, most common), submucosal (bulging into cavity — causes most bleeding symptoms), or subserosal (bulging outward, may become pedunculated)
- Microscopic: interlacing bundles/whorls of uniform, benign-appearing spindle-shaped smooth muscle cells with elongated, cigar-shaped nuclei
- Low mitotic activity, no significant nuclear atypia (distinguishes from leiomyosarcoma, which shows high mitotic rate + atypia + necrosis)
- Susceptible to degenerative changes (see Q26)
Q32. Write short notes on Paget's disease of the Vulva.
- Definition: intraepithelial adenocarcinoma of vulvar skin, arising from apocrine gland-bearing epithelium
- Clinical: erythematous, eczematous, scaly/crusted plaque, often pruritic, mimics dermatitis (delayed diagnosis common)
- Histology: large, pale "Paget cells" with abundant clear/pale cytoplasm scattered singly or in nests within the epidermis; PAS-positive, mucin-positive
- Key distinguishing point from breast Paget's disease: vulvar Paget's is usually a **primary intraepithelial adenocarcinoma confined to the epidermis** and is **less often** associated with an underlying invasive adenocarcinoma (though this should still be excluded), whereas breast Paget's is almost always associated with underlying DCIS or invasive ductal carcinoma
- Management: wide local excision; must exclude underlying invasive carcinoma or associated visceral malignancy

**Q33. Discuss Cervical Carcinoma: Risk Factors, Pathogenesis, Morphology, Grading & Staging** *(highest-yield essay — likely 20 marks)*

**Risk factors:**
- High-risk HPV infection (16, 18) — essential cause
- Multiple sexual partners / early coitarche
- Smoking
- Immunosuppression (HIV/AIDS, transplant patients)
- Multiparity
- Low socioeconomic status / lack of screening
- Long-term oral contraceptive use

**Pathogenesis:**
- HPV E6 protein degrades p53 → loss of apoptosis/DNA damage response
- HPV E7 protein degrades RB → loss of G1/S checkpoint control
- Combined effect: unregulated proliferation of infected basal squamous cells at the transformation zone
- Progresses through cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) stages before becoming invasive

**Morphology:**
- Precursor lesions (CIN) confined to epithelium, graded by depth of dysplastic cell involvement
- Invasive carcinoma: most commonly squamous cell carcinoma (~80%), arising at the squamocolumnar transformation zone; less commonly adenocarcinoma
- Gross: exophytic (fungating) or ulcerative/infiltrative mass; may be clinically inapparent (microinvasive)

**Grading (CIN system — based on depth of dysplastic epithelium):**
- CIN I — dysplasia confined to lower 1/3 of epithelium (mild dysplasia; often regresses)
- CIN II — dysplasia extends to lower 2/3 (moderate dysplasia)
- CIN III — full-thickness dysplasia without invasion (severe dysplasia/carcinoma in situ)
- Invasive carcinoma — dysplastic cells breach the basement membrane

**Staging (FIGO, simplified):**
- Stage 0 — carcinoma in situ (CIN III, no invasion)
- Stage I — confined to the cervix
- Stage II — extends beyond cervix but not to pelvic wall or lower 1/3 of vagina
- Stage III — extends to pelvic wall and/or lower 1/3 of vagina, and/or causes hydronephrosis
- Stage IV — extends beyond the true pelvis, or involves bladder/rectal mucosa, or distant metastasis

**Screening/prevention:** Pap smear, HPV DNA testing, HPV vaccination (Gardasil) targeting types 16, 18, 6, 11

## MALE REPRODUCTIVE & BREAST PATHOLOGY (standard curriculum — revise alongside textbook)

**5 causes of testicular infertility**
- Cryptorchidism (undescended testis)
- Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY)
- Varicocele
- Testicular torsion (if untreated/delayed)
- Post-infectious (mumps orchitis)
- *(Bonus)* Chemotherapy/radiation-induced germ cell damage

**Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH)**
- Arises in the **transition zone** of the prostate (surrounds urethra)
- Driven by **DHT** (dihydrotestosterone, via 5α-reductase conversion of testosterone)
- Causes nodular hyperplasia of glandular and stromal tissue → urethral compression → urinary symptoms (hesitancy, frequency, weak stream)
- Does **not** increase risk of prostate cancer (different zone, different pathogenesis)

**Prostate carcinoma**

- Arises in the **peripheral zone** (posterior lobe) — palpable on digital rectal exam
- Graded by the **Gleason grading system** (architectural pattern-based, most common + second most common pattern scored 1-5 each, summed for total score)
- **PSA** used for screening (not fully specific — also raised in BPH/prostatitis)
- Often multifocal; spreads to bone (osteoblastic metastases) characteristically

**Cryptorchidism**
- Failure of testicular descent into the scrotum
- Associated with **increased infertility risk** (impaired spermatogenesis from higher intra-abdominal temperature)
- Associated with **increased malignancy risk** (germ cell tumours) — risk persists even after surgical correction (orchiopexy), though correction reduces the risk somewhat and allows earlier tumour detection

**Testicular tumours**
- **Seminoma** — most common testicular germ cell tumour, **radiosensitive**, good prognosis, uniform cells resembling primordial germ cells (same lineage as ovarian dysgerminoma)
- **Non-seminomatous germ cell tumours** — more aggressive:
 — Embryonal carcinoma
 — Yolk sac tumour (AFP marker; most common testicular tumour in infants/children)
 — Choriocarcinoma (β-hCG marker, early haematogenous spread)
 — Teratoma

**Varicocele**
- Dilation of the pampiniform venous plexus
- Classically **left-sided** — due to the left testicular vein draining at a right angle into the left renal vein (higher pressure), versus the right testicular vein draining obliquely and directly into the IVC
- Causes a "bag of worms" scrotal swelling; associated with subfertility due to increased scrotal temperature

**Testicular torsion**
- Surgical emergency — twisting of the spermatic cord cuts off blood supply
- Sudden severe scrotal pain, **absent cremasteric reflex** is a key clinical sign
- Time-critical: irreversible ischemic damage within hours; requires emergency surgical detorsion

**Fibroadenoma**
- Most common **benign** breast tumour, typically in young women (teens-30s)
- Well-circumscribed, mobile, painless mass ("breast mouse")
- Biphasic proliferation of stromal and glandular epithelial elements

**Fibrocystic change**
- Most common benign breast condition overall (not a true neoplasm)
- Cystic dilation of ducts, apocrine metaplasia, fibrosis; fluctuates with menstrual cycle
- Some subtypes (e.g. with atypical hyperplasia) carry increased future cancer risk

**Invasive ductal vs invasive lobular carcinoma**

- **Invasive ductal carcinoma** — most common type of breast cancer overall; forms a firm, irregular mass
- **Invasive lobular carcinoma** — characteristically **bilateral**, grows in a **single-file ("Indian file") pattern**, associated with **loss of E-cadherin** expression (unlike ductal, which retains E-cadherin)

**Paget's disease of the breast**
- Eczematous, crusted change of the **nipple/areola**
- Almost always associated with an underlying **ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS)** or invasive ductal carcinoma
- Paget cells (large, clear, malignant cells) infiltrate the epidermis of the nipple, spreading from the underlying carcinoma via the lactiferous ducts

**Breast cancer risk factors**
- BRCA1/2 mutations (impaired homologous recombination DNA repair)
- Early menarche / late menopause (prolonged oestrogen exposure)
- Nulliparity
- Family history
- Obesity (postmenopausal), alcohol use, prior chest radiation

**Receptor status and treatment implications**
- **ER/PR-positive** → responsive to hormonal therapy (e.g. tamoxifen, aromatase inhibitors)
- **HER2-positive** → responsive to targeted anti-HER2 therapy (e.g. trastuzumab/Herceptin)
- **Triple-negative** (ER-, PR-, HER2-) → most aggressive subtype, no targeted hormonal/HER2 therapy available, relies on chemotherapy

Q34. Discuss Prostate Carcinoma: Risk Factors, Pathogenesis, Morphology, Grading & Staging
**Risk factors:**
- Age >50 (strongest factor)
- Black race — higher incidence and mortality
- Family history / genetics (BRCA2, HOXB13)
- Androgen dependence (testosterone/DHT drive growth)
- High animal fat diet
- Geographic factors (higher in Western countries)
**Pathogenesis:**
- Arises in the **peripheral zone** (vs BPH — transition zone)
- Precursor lesion: high-grade **Prostatic Intraepithelial Neoplasia (PIN)**
- **TMPRSS2-ERG gene fusion** in ~50% of cases → overexpression of ERG oncogene
- **PTEN loss** → activates PI3K/AKT pathway → uncontrolled proliferation/survival
- **MYC amplification**
- **Androgen receptor (AR) signalling** drives growth throughout the disease course, including castration-resistant stage
- Progression: PIN → localized carcinoma → local invasion (periprostatic fat, seminal vesicles) → metastasis
- Often multifocal
**Morphology:**
- Gross: firm, irregular, gritty yellow-white areas, poorly demarcated from normal parenchyma
- Micro: small, crowded glands, back-to-back pattern, **loss of basal cell layer** (key diagnostic feature), prominent nucleoli
**Grading:**
- **Gleason system** — primary and secondary architectural patterns each scored 1–5, summed for total (e.g. 3+4=7); newer Grade Groups 1–5 derived from this
**Staging:**
- TNM system — T (local extent: confined/capsule invasion/seminal vesicle invasion/adjacent structures), N (regional nodes), M (distant, especially bone)
**Spread:**
- Local: seminal vesicles, bladder base
- Distant: **osteoblastic (sclerotic) bone metastases** — spine, pelvis, femur — via Batson's venous plexus
**Screening/Diagnosis:**
- PSA (not fully specific — also raised in BPH/prostatitis), DRE, biopsy with Gleason scoring

Q35. Discuss Breast Carcinoma: Risk Factors, Pathogenesis, Morphology, Grading & Staging
**Risk factors:**
- BRCA1/2 mutations
- Early menarche / late menopause (prolonged oestrogen exposure)
- Nulliparity
- Family history
- Obesity (postmenopausal), alcohol use, prior chest irradiation
**Pathogenesis:**
- Progression model: normal epithelium → atypical ductal hyperplasia → **DCIS** (confined by intact basement membrane) → invasive ductal carcinoma
- Loss of **E-cadherin** underlies lobular carcinoma's discohesive, single-file growth (ductal carcinoma retains E-cadherin)
- Prolonged unopposed oestrogen exposure promotes proliferative drive
- HER2 gene amplification drives a distinct aggressive subtype
**Morphology:**
- **Invasive ductal carcinoma** (most common, ~70-80%) — firm, irregular, stellate/spiculated mass, gritty cut surface
- **Invasive lobular carcinoma** — bilateral, single-file ("Indian file") infiltration, less often forms a discrete mass
- **Paget's disease of the breast** — eczematous nipple/areola change; Paget cells (large, clear, malignant) infiltrating epidermis, almost always with underlying DCIS/invasive carcinoma
**Grading:**
- **Nottingham (modified Bloom-Richardson) grading** — tubule formation, nuclear pleomorphism, mitotic count, each scored 1-3, summed into Grade I-III
**Staging:**
- TNM — tumour size, nodal status (axillary), metastasis (bone, lung, liver, brain)
**Receptor status (guides treatment, often asked as part of this essay):**
- ER/PR+ → hormonal therapy (tamoxifen, aromatase inhibitors)
- HER2+ → trastuzumab (Herceptin)
- Triple-negative → most aggressive, chemotherapy-only
