## Musculoskeletal Examination — Knee & Shoulder

Complete study guide adapted from Charlie Goldberg, M.D. (UCSD School of Medicine). Original slide-deck source; content below preserves every clinical point from the source, reorganized into a clean, readable structure.

## General Principles

- A musculoskeletal exam is performed when there are symptoms (injury, pain, decreased function) — this is different from a routine "screening exam."
- Focus the exam on the symptomatic area.
- Musculoskeletal complaints are common and frequently examined in clinical practice.

### Historical clues to gather first

- What is the functional limitation?
- Are symptoms in a single joint or multiple joints?
- Acute onset or slowly progressive?
- If due to injury — what was the mechanism?
- Any prior problems with this area?
- Any systemic symptoms?

### Keys to evaluating any joint

- Fully expose the area — no shirts, pants, etc.; use gowns as needed.
- Inspect the joint(s) in question for signs of inflammation or injury (swelling, redness, warmth), deformity, and compare with the opposite side.
- Understand the normal functional anatomy.
- Observe normal activity — what can't the patient do? Specific limitations? A discrete event (e.g. trauma)? Mechanism of injury?
- Palpate the joint — warmth? Point tenderness? Over what structure(s)?
- Assess range of motion, both active (patient moves it) and passive (you move it).
- Assess strength and perform a neuro-vascular assessment.
- Perform specific provocative maneuvers.
- If there is an acute injury with pain, the patient may "protect" the joint, limiting movement and making examination difficult — examine the unaffected side first to build confidence and a sense of the patient's normal range.

### Terminology: flexion/extension, abduction/adduction

- Flexion: moving forward out of the frontal plane of the body (except at the knee and foot).
- Extension: movement in the direction opposite to flexion.
- Abduction: movement that brings a structure away from the body (along the frontal plane).
- Adduction: movement that brings a structure towards the body (along the frontal plane).

## The Knee Exam

### Knee anatomy — observation and identification of landmarks

- The knee is a hinge-type joint that tolerates significant force and weight.
- Its anatomy is straightforward, which makes the exam logical.
- Fully expose the knee — pants off, use a gown or shorts.
- Surface landmarks: patella (kneecap), patellar tendon, medial joint line, lateral joint line, quadriceps muscle, hamstring muscle group, tibia, anterior tibial tuberosity (insertion of the patellar tendon), and femur.

### Observation

- Obvious pain with walking?
- Landmarks visible/palpable?
- Scars from past surgery?
- Swelling — fluid in the joint (effusion)?
- Atrophic muscles (e.g. from chronic disuse)?
- Bowing of the legs (inward = valgus, outward = varus)?

### Range of motion (ROM)

1. Assess active, then passive range of motion (you move the joint).
2. With a hand on the patella during extension and flexion, osteoarthritis may produce a palpable grinding sensation (crepitus).
- Normal range of motion: full flexion 140°, full extension 0°.

### Assessing for a large effusion — ballottement

An effusion is fluid within the joint space; large effusions are usually obvious.

1. Flex the knee.
2. Place a hand on the suprapatellar pouch (above the patella), which communicates with the joint space.
3. Push down and towards the patella to move fluid toward the center of the joint.
4. Push down on the patella with your thumb.
5. If there is a large effusion, the patella "floats" and bounces back up when pushed down.

### Menisci — normal function and anatomy

- The medial and lateral menisci sit on top of the tibia, providing a cushioned articulating surface between the femur and tibia.
- They provide joint stability, distribute force, and protect the underlying articular cartilage.
- Menisci are damaged by trauma or degenerative changes with age.
- Symptoms when a torn piece interrupts normal joint movement: pain, instability ("giving out"), locking, and/or swelling.

### Evaluating for meniscal injury — joint line palpation

Joint line tenderness suggests medial or lateral meniscal injury (or osteoarthritis).

1. Slightly flex the knee.
2. Find the joint space along the lateral and medial margins — the joint line runs perpendicular to the long axis of the tibia.
3. Palpate along the medial, then lateral, margins.
4. Pain suggests underlying meniscus damage or osteoarthritis.

### Additional tests for meniscal injury

**McMurray's test — medial meniscus**
1. Place your left hand with middle, index and ring fingers on the medial joint line.
2. Grasp the heel with your right hand and fully flex the knee.
3. Turn the ankle so the foot points outward (everted), directing the knee to point outward.
4. Holding the foot in the everted position, extend and flex the knee.
5. If there is a medial meniscal injury, you may feel a "click" under your hand on knee extension, possibly with pain.

**McMurray's test — lateral meniscus**
1. Return the knee to fully flexed, turn the foot inward (inverted).
2. Direct the knee so it points inward.
3. Keep a hand on the knee with fingers along the joint line.
4. Extend and flex the knee.
5. If there is a lateral meniscal injury, feel a "click" with fingers on the joint line, possibly with pain.

*Note: the medial and lateral McMurray's tests are typically performed together.*

**Appley grind test**
1. Patient lies prone (on their stomach).
2. Grasp the ankle and foot with both hands, flex the knee to 90 degrees.
3. Hold the leg down with your leg across the back of the patient's thigh.
4. Push down while rotating the ankle.
5. This puts direct pressure on the menisci — if injured, this causes pain.
6. Test the opposite leg for comparison.

### Ligaments — normal anatomy and function

- Four bands of tissue connect the femur to the tibia, providing joint stability.
- Ligamentous injury (requiring significant force, e.g. the leg struck from the side with the foot planted) causes acute pain, swelling, and often a reported "pop" (the sound of the ligament tearing).
- After the acute swelling and pain settle, patients may report pain and instability (a sensation of the knee "giving out") with maneuvers that stress the damaged ligament.

**Testing the medial collateral ligament (MCL)**
1. Flex the knee to about 30 degrees.
2. Place your left hand on the lateral aspect of the knee.
3. Place your right hand on the ankle or calf.
4. Push inward with the left hand while supplying opposing force with the right.
5. If the MCL is torn, the joint "opens up" along the medial aspect.
6. Direct palpation over the ligament may also elicit pain.
- Always compare with the unaffected side, since normal ligamentous laxity varies between patients.

**Testing the lateral collateral ligament (LCL)**
1. Flex the knee to about 30 degrees.
2. Place your right hand on the medial aspect of the knee.
3. Place your left hand on the ankle or calf.
4. Push steadily with the right hand while supplying opposing force with the left.
5. If the LCL is torn, the joint "opens up" on the lateral aspect.
6. Direct palpation of the injured ligament may elicit pain.

**Alternative method for assessing the LCL and MCL**
1. Flex the knee to about 30 degrees, cradling the heel between your arm and body.
2. Place index fingers across the joint lines.
3. Using your body and index fingers, provide medial then lateral stress to the joint.

**Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) — Lachman's test**
1. Grasp the femur with your left hand, the tibia with your right.
2. Flex the knee slightly.
3. Pull up sharply (towards the belly button) with the right hand while stabilizing the femur with the left.
4. An intact ACL limits the amount of distraction, giving a "firm end point."
5. If the ACL is torn, the tibia feels unrestrained in forward movement.

**Drop Lachman's test** (for patients with large legs and/or examiners with small hands)
1. The patient's leg hangs off the table.
2. Place the ankle between your legs to stabilize it, holding the knee at about 30 degrees flexion.
3. Place a hand on the femur, holding it down on the table.
4. Grasp the tibia with the other hand and pull forward.

**Posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) — posterior drawer test**
1. The patient lies down with the knee flexed to about 90 degrees.
2. Sit on the patient's foot; grasp below the knee with both hands, thumbs meeting at the front of the tibia.
3. Push backward, noting the movement of the tibia relative to the femur — an intact PCL gives a discrete end point.
4. If the PCL is torn, the tibia feels unrestrained in backward movement.

### Anterior knee pain — patellofemoral problems and chondromalacia

A common source of anterior knee pain is patellar articulation with the femur.

1. Slightly flex the knee.
2. Push down on the patella with both thumbs — this elicits pain if chondromalacia (osteoarthritis of the undersurface of the patella) is present.
3. Move the patella side to side, palpating its undersurface — may elicit pain if chondromalacia is present.
4. Hold the patella in place with a hand and direct the patient to contract the quadriceps, forcing the inferior surface of the patella onto the femur — this elicits pain if chondromalacia is present.

### Summary of knee-exam maneuvers

| Maneuver | Clinical interpretation |
|---|---|
| Observe knee, identify surface anatomy, palpate | Variety of pathologic processes |
| Range of motion with palpation | Abnormal with a variety of pathologic processes; crepitus with degenerative joint disease (DJD) |
| Ballottement (pushing down on patella) | Patella "springs" upward — suggests a large effusion |
| Joint line tenderness | Meniscal injury, DJD |
| McMurray's test | Pain or palpable click with hand on joint line suggests meniscal injury |
| Appley grind test | Pain suggests meniscal injury on the side being palpated |
| Medial and lateral joint line stress | Excessive laxity suggests MCL or LCL tear |
| Lachman's test (or Drop Lachman's for small hands/large legs) | Excessive laxity suggests ACL tear |
| Posterior drawer test | Excessive laxity suggests PCL tear |
| Patellar manipulation (push down, palpate undersurface) | Pain suggests chondromalacia patellae |

## The Shoulder Exam

### Overview of anatomy

- The shoulder is formed by three bony structures: scapula, humerus, and clavicle.
- Held together by ligaments and an intricate web of muscles.
- Has a tremendous range of motion — often described as a "golf ball on a tee" structure.
- Compared with the knee, shoulder anatomy is more complex, and the exam involves more eponymous tests.

### Observation and palpation

- Expose both shoulders.
- Compare sides, noting: swelling, discoloration, deformity, atrophy.
- Remember that problems elsewhere (e.g. neck, abdomen) can cause referred pain appreciated in the shoulder — this should be uncovered via a good history and physical exam.
- Identify and palpate each bony surface landmark: acromion, clavicle, scapula, deltoid muscle, supraspinatus region, infraspinatus region, teres minor region.

### Active range of motion — flexion/extension and abduction/adduction

1. Trace an arc while reaching forward with the elbow straight (forward flexion). The patient should be able to move the hand to a position over the head — normal range is 0 to 180 degrees.
2. Reverse direction and trace the arc backwards (extension). The patient should be able to position the hand behind their back.
3. Direct the patient to abduct the arm to a position with the hand above the head — movement should be smooth and painless, with a normal range of 0 to 180 degrees.

### Range of motion — internal/external rotation

**Abduction and external rotation**
1. Direct the patient to place a hand behind the head.
2. Have them reach as far down the spine as possible.
3. Note the extent of reach in relation to the cervical spine.
4. They should be able to reach roughly the C7 level (C7 has the most prominent posterior "bump" on the cervical spine and is easily palpated).

**Adduction and internal rotation**
1. Direct the patient to place a hand behind their back.
2. Instruct them to reach as high up the spine as possible.
3. Note the extent of reach in relation to the scapula or thoracic spine.
4. They should be able to reach the lower border of the scapula (roughly T7 level).

### Passive range of motion

- If there is pain with active ROM, assess the same movements passively.
1. Grasp the humerus and move the shoulder through the ROMs described above.
2. Feel for crepitus (suggestive of arthritis) with a hand placed on the shoulder.
- Note which movements precipitate pain. Pain/limitation on active ROM but not passive ROM suggests a structural problem with muscles/tendons (they are actively firing during active ROM but not during passive ROM).
- Note limitations in movement: where exactly in the arc does it occur? Due to pain or weakness? How does it compare with the other side?

### The rotator cuff

- Four major muscles make up the rotator cuff.
- They allow a wide range of arm movement at the shoulder and keep the humerus in close contact with the scapula.
- Rotator cuff muscles and their function:
  - **Supraspinatus** — abducts the shoulder (up to roughly 80 degrees).
  - **Infraspinatus** — external rotation.
  - **Teres minor** — external rotation.
  - **Subscapularis** — internal rotation.

**Testing supraspinatus — the "empty can" test**

Anatomy: connects the top of the scapula to the humerus; firing abducts the shoulder. This is the most commonly damaged of the rotator cuff muscles.

1. The patient abducts the shoulder 30 degrees, with 30 degrees of forward flexion and full internal rotation (turned so the thumb points downward).
2. Forward flex the shoulder without resistance.
3. Repeat with resistance.
4. Note that the deltoid is responsible for abduction beyond roughly 70 degrees.
5. A partial supraspinatus tear causes pain and some weakness with the above maneuver; a complete tear prevents any abduction.

**Testing infraspinatus and teres minor**

Anatomy: both muscles connect the scapula to the humerus; firing externally rotates the arm.

1. The patient slightly abducts (20–30 degrees) the shoulder, elbows at 90 degrees.
2. Place your hands on the outside of their forearms.
3. Direct the patient to push their arms outward (externally rotate) while you resist.
4. Tears in the tendon cause weakness and/or pain.

**Testing subscapularis — Gerber's lift-off test**

Anatomy: connects the scapula (anterior surface origin) to the humerus; firing produces internal rotation.

1. The patient places a hand behind the back, palm facing out.
2. The patient lifts the hand away from the back.
3. If the tendon is partially torn, movement is limited or causes pain; complete tears prevent movement in this direction.

### Impingement, rotator cuff tendonitis, and sub-acromial bursitis

- The four rotator-cuff tendons pass underneath the acromion/coraco-acromial ligament en route to their insertions on the humerus.
- The space between the acromion/coracoacromial ligament and the tendons can become narrowed.
- This causes the tendons (particularly supraspinatus) to become "impinged upon," and the resulting friction inflames the tendons and the subacromial bursa (between the tendons and the acromion).
- The net result is shoulder pain, particularly when raising the arm over the head (e.g. swimming, reaching onto a top shelf, arm positioning during sleep).

**Neer's test for impingement**
1. Place one hand on the patient's scapula and grasp the forearm with the other; the arm is internally rotated (thumb pointed downward).
2. Forward flex the arm, positioning the hand over the head.
3. Pain suggests impingement.

**Hawkin's test for impingement, and sub-acromial palpation**

*Hawkin's test:*
1. Raise the patient's arm to 90 degrees of forward flexion.
2. Rotate internally (thumb pointed down) — this places the greater tubercle of the humerus in a position that further compromises the space beneath the acromion.
3. Pain suggests impingement.

*Sub-acromial palpation:*
1. Identify the acromion by following the scapular spine laterally to its tip.
2. Palpate the sub-acromial space region — pain suggests inflamed tendons/bursa.

### Biceps tendon — anatomy and function

- The long head of the biceps tendon runs in the bicipital groove of the humerus, inserting at the top of the glenoid.
- Subject to the same forces/stresses as the rotator-cuff tendons.
- The biceps flexes and supinates the forearm, and also assists flexion.
- Inflammation (tendonitis) causes pain at the top and anterior shoulder, particularly with flexion or supination.

**Biceps tendon testing**

*Palpation:*
1. Palpate the biceps tendon in the bicipital groove — pain suggests tendonitis.
2. Confirm you are on the tendon by having the patient supinate while you palpate.

*Resisted supination — Yergason's test:*
1. Elbow flexed 90 degrees, shoulder adducted (elbow bent at a right angle, arm against the body).
2. Grasp the patient's hand and direct them to rotate the arm so the palm faces up (supinate) while you resist.
3. Pain suggests tendonitis.
- *Note: a ruptured long head of biceps produces the classic "Popeye muscle" deformity.*

### Acromio-clavicular (A-C) joint pathology

- The A-C joint is minimally mobile; inflammation and degeneration cause shoulder pain.

**Specifics of testing**
1. Palpate the point where the end of the clavicle articulates with the acromion.
2. Push on the area — does it cause pain?
3. Ask the patient to move the arm across the chest — this stresses the A-C joint and causes pain in the setting of degenerative joint disease.
4. A-C joint separation causes swelling and pain on cross-arm testing or palpation.

### Summary of shoulder-exam maneuvers

| Maneuver | Clinical interpretation |
|---|---|
| Observation, palpation | General orientation |
| Range of motion (flexion/extension, abduction/adduction, internal/external rotation), with palpation | Decreased with a variety of shoulder pathology; crepitus on palpation with DJD |
| "Empty can" test (arm abducted 60°, forward flexed ~30°, thumb down, resistance to further flexion) | Pain/weakness suggests supraspinatus tear |
| Resisted external rotation | Pain/weakness suggests infraspinatus or teres minor tear |
| Resisted internal rotation / lift-off from back (Gerber's test) | Pain/weakness suggests subscapularis tear |
| Sub-acromial palpation | Pain suggests bursitis/impingement |
| Hawkin's test (elbow 90°, arm forward flexed 90°, examiner internally rotates) | Pain suggests bursitis/impingement |
| Neer's test (thumb down, elbow straight, examiner raises arm through forward flexion) | Pain suggests bursitis/impingement |
| Long head biceps palpation | Pain suggests biceps tendonitis |
| Yergason's test (elbow 90°, arm adducted, patient attempts supination while examiner resists) | Pain suggests biceps tendonitis |
| A-C joint tenderness, cross-arm test (reach across towards opposite shoulder) | Pain suggests A-C joint pathology (DJD, dislocation) |
