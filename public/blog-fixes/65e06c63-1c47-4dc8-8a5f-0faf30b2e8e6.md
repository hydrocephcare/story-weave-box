### Question 1
Discuss the pathogenesis of cervical squamous cell carcinoma, including the mechanism of action of HPV E6 and E7 oncoproteins.

**Model answer**
- Cause: persistent infection with high-risk HPV, mainly types 16 and 18. The virus infects basal cells of the squamous epithelium at the transformation zone through micro-abrasions.
- Integration of viral DNA into the host genome often breaks the E2 gene. E2 normally represses E6 and E7, so E6 and E7 are overexpressed.
- **E6** binds p53 and, with the ubiquitin ligase E6AP, tags it for degradation. Loss of p53 means no cell-cycle arrest, no DNA repair and no apoptosis. E6 also activates telomerase (hTERT), giving the cell immortality.
- **E7** binds the hypophosphorylated retinoblastoma protein (Rb) and releases the E2F transcription factor, which pushes the cell into S phase. E7 also inactivates the CDK inhibitors p21 and p27.
- Result: uncontrolled proliferation, genomic instability and accumulation of further mutations.
- Natural history: HPV infection → low-grade lesion (CIN 1) → high-grade lesion (CIN 2 and 3, carcinoma in situ) → invasive squamous cell carcinoma, over 10 to 20 years.
- Co-factors: smoking, HIV, long-term oral contraceptives, high parity, other sexually transmitted infections.

### Question 2
Give an outline of the classification of ovarian neoplasms with examples of each category.

**Model answer**
- **Surface epithelial tumours (about 65 to 70%):** serous (most common), mucinous, endometrioid, clear cell and Brenner tumours. Each can be benign, borderline or malignant.
- **Germ cell tumours (15 to 20%):** dysgerminoma, mature teratoma (dermoid cyst), immature teratoma, yolk sac tumour, embryonal carcinoma, choriocarcinoma.
- **Sex cord–stromal tumours (5 to 10%):** granulosa cell tumour (oestrogen-secreting), Sertoli–Leydig cell tumour (androgen-secreting), thecoma and fibroma.
- **Metastatic tumours:** Krukenberg tumour (signet-ring cells, usually from the stomach), also colon and breast primaries.

### Question 3
Compare and contrast Type 1 and Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus under the following headings:
- Aetiology
- Pathogenesis
- Clinical features

**Model answer**
- **Aetiology**
  - Type 1: autoimmune destruction of beta cells in genetically susceptible people (HLA-DR3/DR4), possibly triggered by a virus.
  - Type 2: strong genetic component plus obesity, physical inactivity and ageing.
- **Pathogenesis**
  - Type 1: T-cell mediated loss of beta cells leads to an absolute lack of insulin. Autoantibodies (GAD65, islet cell, insulin) are present.
  - Type 2: insulin resistance in muscle, liver and fat, followed by beta-cell dysfunction and relative insulin deficiency. Islet amyloid (amylin) is deposited.
- **Clinical features**
  - Type 1: usually children and young adults, rapid onset, thin, polyuria, polydipsia, weight loss, ketoacidosis. Needs insulin.
  - Type 2: usually adults, slow onset, often overweight, may be found by chance or present with complications (infections, neuropathy, visual loss). Hyperosmolar state rather than ketoacidosis. Treated first with lifestyle and oral drugs.

### Question 4
Discuss the pathophysiological categories of oedema, giving an example of each.

**Model answer**
- **Raised hydrostatic pressure:** heart failure, deep vein thrombosis, venous obstruction.
- **Reduced plasma oncotic pressure (hypoalbuminaemia):** nephrotic syndrome, cirrhosis, malnutrition (kwashiorkor).
- **Lymphatic obstruction:** filariasis, tumour invasion of nodes, after surgery or radiotherapy.
- **Sodium and water retention:** acute renal failure, excess aldosterone, post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis.
- **Increased vascular permeability:** acute inflammation, burns, allergy.
- Oedema may be localised (pitting of one leg) or generalised (anasarca).

### Question 5
Describe the immune mechanisms involved in glomerular injury.

**Model answer**
- **In situ immune complex formation:** antibodies bind antigens already in the glomerulus. Examples: anti-PLA2R in membranous nephropathy, anti-GBM antibodies against type IV collagen in Goodpasture syndrome.
- **Circulating immune complex deposition:** complexes formed elsewhere lodge in the glomerulus. Examples: post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis, lupus nephritis.
- **Cell-mediated injury:** T cells and macrophages (some crescentic and minimal change disease).
- **ANCA-mediated injury:** neutrophils activated by ANCA damage vessels (pauci-immune crescentic glomerulonephritis).
- **Complement activation:** C5b-9 membrane attack complex and C5a recruit neutrophils; alternative pathway abnormalities cause C3 glomerulopathy.
- Mediators of damage: neutrophils, monocytes, platelets, oxygen free radicals, proteases, and coagulation in crescent formation.

### Question 6
Outline the major risk factors for atherosclerosis, distinguishing between modifiable and non-modifiable factors.

**Model answer**
- **Non-modifiable:** increasing age, male sex (women are protected before the menopause), family history, genetic disorders such as familial hypercholesterolaemia.
- **Modifiable (major):** hyperlipidaemia (high LDL, low HDL), hypertension, cigarette smoking, diabetes mellitus.
- **Modifiable (others):** obesity and the metabolic syndrome, physical inactivity, high-fat diet, chronic inflammation (raised CRP), raised homocysteine and lipoprotein(a).

### Question 7
Discuss the aetiology, pathogenesis, and morphology of liver cirrhosis.

**Model answer**
- **Definition:** diffuse fibrosis of the liver with conversion of normal architecture into regenerative nodules.
- **Aetiology:** alcohol, chronic hepatitis B and C, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, autoimmune hepatitis, primary biliary cholangitis and primary sclerosing cholangitis, haemochromatosis, Wilson disease, alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, drugs, and unknown causes.
- **Pathogenesis:** chronic hepatocyte injury and inflammation activate hepatic stellate cells, which become myofibroblasts and lay down type I and III collagen. Sinusoids lose their fenestrae (capillarisation). Surviving hepatocytes regenerate as nodules. The fibrosis and nodules distort blood flow and cause portal hypertension.
- **Morphology:** a hard liver with a nodular surface. Micronodular (nodules under 3 mm, typical of alcohol), macronodular (over 3 mm, typical of viral hepatitis) or mixed. Microscopically, nodules are surrounded by fibrous septa with loss of normal lobular architecture.
- **Complications:** portal hypertension (varices, ascites, splenomegaly), hepatic encephalopathy, hepatorenal syndrome, hepatocellular carcinoma.

### Question 8
Classify chemical carcinogenic agents, giving examples of each class and the cancers they are associated with.

**Model answer**
- **Direct-acting (no activation needed):** alkylating agents such as cyclophosphamide (leukaemia, bladder cancer), beta-propiolactone.
- **Indirect-acting (need metabolic activation by cytochrome P450):**
  - polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, e.g. benzo[a]pyrene in tobacco smoke (lung cancer)
  - aromatic amines, e.g. beta-naphthylamine (bladder cancer)
  - azo dyes (liver cancer)
  - aflatoxin B1 from Aspergillus flavus (hepatocellular carcinoma, p53 mutation)
  - nitrosamines (stomach cancer)
- **Others:** vinyl chloride (hepatic angiosarcoma), asbestos (mesothelioma, lung cancer), arsenic (skin, lung), nickel and chromium (nasal and lung cancer), benzene (leukaemia).
- **Promoters (not carcinogenic alone):** phorbol esters, oestrogen, phenobarbital.

### Question 9
Compare and contrast autosomal dominant and autosomal recessive inheritance patterns, giving two examples of disorders for each.

**Model answer**
- **Autosomal dominant:** one mutant allele is enough. Both sexes affected, appears in every generation (vertical), 50% risk to each child of an affected parent. Often structural or receptor proteins, variable expression and incomplete penetrance, later onset. Examples: Huntington disease, familial hypercholesterolaemia, Marfan syndrome, neurofibromatosis.
- **Autosomal recessive:** two mutant alleles are needed. Parents are healthy carriers, 25% of children affected, appears in siblings (horizontal), more common with consanguinity. Often enzyme defects, early onset, more uniform. Examples: cystic fibrosis, sickle cell disease, phenylketonuria, thalassaemia.

### Question 10
Describe the stages and biological processes involved in fracture healing, including the role of callus formation.

**Model answer**
- **Haematoma (first hours to days):** bleeding fills the gap. Platelets and inflammatory cells release growth factors (PDGF, TGF-beta); necrotic bone is removed by macrophages.
- **Soft (fibrocartilaginous) callus (about 1 to 3 weeks):** granulation tissue, fibroblasts and chondroblasts bridge the gap with collagen and cartilage. This splints the fracture.
- **Hard (bony) callus (about 3 to 12 weeks):** osteoblasts convert the soft callus to woven bone by endochondral and intramembranous ossification. The fracture becomes stable.
- **Remodelling (months to years):** osteoclasts and osteoblasts replace woven bone with lamellar bone and restore the original shape, following mechanical stress (Wolff's law).
- Healing is helped by good blood supply, immobilisation and nutrition. It is delayed by infection, movement, smoking, steroids, diabetes and soft-tissue interposition. Complications: delayed union, non-union, malunion.

### Question 11
Give an outline of the aetiology, characteristics, and examples of the major categories of diarrhoeal illness.

**Model answer**
- **Secretory:** toxin-driven chloride and water secretion. Large watery stool that continues with fasting. Examples: cholera, enterotoxigenic *E. coli*.
- **Osmotic:** unabsorbed solutes pull water into the gut. Stops with fasting. Examples: lactase deficiency, magnesium laxatives.
- **Exudative (inflammatory):** mucosal damage with blood and pus. Examples: Shigella, Salmonella, Campylobacter, amoebiasis, inflammatory bowel disease.
- **Malabsorptive:** fatty, pale, foul stool (steatorrhoea). Examples: coeliac disease, giardiasis, tropical sprue.
- **Motility disorders:** irritable bowel syndrome, hyperthyroidism, diabetic neuropathy.
- Viral causes such as rotavirus and norovirus produce watery diarrhoea by damaging villi.

### Question 12
Write short notes on Rheumatic Heart Disease under the following headings:
- Aetiology and pathogenesis
- Morphological changes
- Complications

**Model answer**
- **Aetiology and pathogenesis:** follows pharyngitis by group A beta-haemolytic *Streptococcus pyogenes*. An immune cross-reaction (molecular mimicry) between streptococcal M protein and heart antigens, driven by antibodies and CD4 T cells, damages the heart. Usually in children aged 5 to 15 years.
- **Morphology:**
  - acute rheumatic fever: pancarditis. Aschoff bodies (central fibrinoid necrosis, Anitschkow "caterpillar" cells, giant cells), small vegetations (verrucae) along the closure lines of the mitral valve, MacCallum plaques in the left atrium, "bread and butter" pericarditis
  - chronic: thickened, fibrous leaflets, fused commissures, shortened and thickened chordae, giving a "fish-mouth" mitral stenosis
- **Complications:** mitral stenosis and regurgitation, atrial fibrillation, left atrial thrombus with embolism, heart failure, infective endocarditis, pulmonary hypertension.

### Question 13
Outline the classification of vasculitis based on vessel size, giving one example and its key features for each category.

**Model answer**
- **Large vessel:** giant cell (temporal) arteritis. Age over 50, headache, jaw claudication, raised ESR, granulomatous inflammation, risk of blindness. Also Takayasu arteritis (young women, aortic arch, absent pulses).
- **Medium vessel:** polyarteritis nodosa. Segmental fibrinoid necrosis and microaneurysms, no glomerulonephritis, linked to hepatitis B. Also Kawasaki disease (children, coronary aneurysms).
- **Small vessel:**
  - ANCA-associated: granulomatosis with polyangiitis (c-ANCA, upper airway, lung and kidney), microscopic polyangiitis (p-ANCA, no granulomas), eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (asthma, eosinophilia)
  - immune-complex: IgA vasculitis (Henoch–Schönlein; children, purpura, abdominal pain, arthritis, nephritis)

### Question 14
Discuss the pathogenesis of pre-eclampsia/toxaemia of pregnancy, including its effects on maternal organ systems.

**Model answer**
- **Definition:** new hypertension (140/90 or more) after 20 weeks, with proteinuria or other organ dysfunction.
- **Pathogenesis:** inadequate invasion of the spiral arteries by trophoblast leaves them narrow and muscular, so the placenta is poorly perfused and hypoxic. The placenta releases anti-angiogenic factors (soluble Flt-1, soluble endoglin) that lower VEGF and PlGF. This causes widespread maternal endothelial dysfunction: vasoconstriction (more thromboxane, less prostacyclin and nitric oxide), hypertension and increased permeability.
- **Kidney:** glomerular endotheliosis and proteinuria.
- **Liver:** periportal haemorrhage and necrosis; HELLP syndrome (haemolysis, raised liver enzymes, low platelets).
- **Brain:** headache, visual disturbance, seizures (eclampsia), haemorrhage.
- **Blood:** thrombocytopenia and DIC.
- **Placenta and fetus:** infarcts, abruption, growth restriction.

### Question 1
Discuss cervical carcinoma under the following headings:
a) Risk factors
b) Aetiology
c) Pathogenesis
d) Morphological changes
e) Clinical features and staging
*(20 Marks)*

**Model answer**
- **a) Risk factors:** early first intercourse, multiple partners, HIV infection and other immunosuppression, smoking, long-term oral contraceptives, high parity, other sexually transmitted infections, low socio-economic status, and no screening.
- **b) Aetiology:** persistent high-risk HPV infection (types 16 and 18 cause about 70% of cases; also 31, 33, 45, 52, 58).
- **c) Pathogenesis:** E6 degrades p53 and E7 inactivates Rb, so cells escape cell-cycle control (details as in Section A, Question 1). Progression: low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (CIN 1), then high-grade (CIN 2 and 3), then invasive carcinoma.
- **d) Morphology:** arises at the transformation zone. Gross: exophytic, ulcerative or infiltrative growth. Microscopy: squamous cell carcinoma (about 75 to 80%) with nests of malignant squamous cells and keratin pearls in the keratinising type; adenocarcinoma and adenosquamous carcinoma are less common.
- **e) Clinical features:** early disease is asymptomatic and found by screening. Later: post-coital or abnormal vaginal bleeding, foul discharge, pelvic pain. Advanced: ureteric obstruction with hydronephrosis and renal failure (the commonest cause of death), vesicovaginal or rectovaginal fistula.
- **Staging (FIGO):** I confined to the cervix; II beyond the cervix but not to the pelvic wall or lower third of the vagina; III pelvic wall, lower third of the vagina or hydronephrosis; IV bladder or rectum, or distant spread.
- **Prevention:** HPV vaccination and screening (Pap smear, VIA, HPV DNA test).

### Question 2
Discuss the three major pathways of breast cancer development and their resultant immunohistochemical staining patterns, including the clinical and prognostic significance of each pathway.
*(20 Marks)*

**Model answer**
- **1. Luminal (oestrogen receptor-positive) pathway:** ER positive, usually PR positive, HER2 negative. Luminal A has low Ki-67 and the best prognosis; luminal B has a higher Ki-67 and may be HER2 positive. Treated with endocrine therapy (tamoxifen, aromatase inhibitors).
- **2. HER2-enriched pathway:** ERBB2 gene amplification. HER2 is 3+ on immunohistochemistry (or amplified by FISH); ER and PR are negative. Aggressive but responds to trastuzumab and other anti-HER2 drugs, which improved its prognosis a lot.
- **3. Basal-like or triple-negative pathway:** ER, PR and HER2 all negative; stains for basal cytokeratins (CK5/6) and EGFR. Often high grade, p53 mutated, strongly associated with BRCA1. Occurs in younger women. Poor prognosis, no targeted therapy, but sensitive to chemotherapy.
- Prognostic markers used with these: tumour grade, size, lymph node status, Ki-67.

### Question 3
Discuss the following aspects of acute inflammation:
a) Cellular events in acute inflammation
b) Cell-derived mediators of inflammation
c) Plasma-derived mediators of inflammation
d) Local and systemic effects of acute inflammation
*(20 Marks)*

**Model answer**
- **a) Cellular events:** vasodilation and increased permeability; margination of neutrophils; rolling (selectins); firm adhesion (integrins binding ICAM-1); transmigration through the vessel wall (PECAM-1); chemotaxis along gradients (C5a, LTB4, IL-8, bacterial products); phagocytosis (recognition, engulfment, killing by oxygen radicals, nitric oxide and lysosomal enzymes).
- **b) Cell-derived mediators:** histamine and serotonin (vasodilation, permeability), prostaglandins and leukotrienes, platelet-activating factor, cytokines (IL-1, TNF, IL-6) and chemokines, nitric oxide, lysosomal enzymes, reactive oxygen species.
- **c) Plasma-derived mediators:** the complement system (C3a and C5a anaphylatoxins, C3b opsonin, membrane attack complex); the kinin system (bradykinin: pain and permeability); the coagulation and fibrinolytic systems (thrombin, fibrin, plasmin), activated by Hageman factor (XII).
- **d) Effects:** local signs are redness, heat, swelling, pain and loss of function. Outcomes: resolution, scarring, abscess formation or progression to chronic inflammation. Systemic effects: fever (IL-1, TNF, PGE2), leucocytosis, acute-phase proteins (CRP, fibrinogen), and in severe cases sepsis, septic shock and DIC.

### Question 4
Discuss Tuberculosis under the following headings:
a) Pathogenesis
b) Morphological events (primary and secondary)
c) Clinical presentation and complications
*(20 Marks)*

**Model answer**
- **a) Pathogenesis:** inhaled droplets carry *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* to the alveolar macrophages. The bacterium blocks phagosome–lysosome fusion and multiplies. After 2 to 4 weeks CD4 Th1 cells release interferon-gamma, which activates macrophages, and TNF helps form granulomas. This type IV hypersensitivity causes caseous necrosis.
- **b) Primary TB:** Ghon focus (subpleural, lower part of the upper lobe or upper part of the lower lobe) plus enlarged hilar nodes is the Ghon complex. Usually heals by fibrosis and calcification (Ranke complex). It can progress in immunosuppressed people, or spread to cause miliary TB.
- **Secondary TB (reactivation or reinfection):** begins at the apex of the upper lobe (Assmann focus). Caseation and cavitation, spread through the airways, fibrocaseous disease or miliary spread.
- **c) Clinical presentation:** cough for more than 2 weeks, haemoptysis, fever, night sweats, weight loss and anorexia.
- **Complications:** haemoptysis, pleural effusion and empyema, pneumothorax, bronchiectasis, aspergilloma, miliary TB, TB meningitis, spinal TB (Pott disease), TB lymphadenitis, intestinal, renal and adrenal (Addison disease) TB, amyloidosis.
- **Diagnosis:** sputum smear (Ziehl–Neelsen), GeneXpert, culture, chest X-ray.

### Question 5
Discuss the pathogenesis and morphological events in the development of atherosclerosis, including its major complications — myocardial infarction, stroke, and peripheral arterial disease.
*(20 Marks)*

**Model answer**
- **Pathogenesis:**
  - endothelial injury and dysfunction from hyperlipidaemia, hypertension, smoking and turbulent flow raises permeability and adhesion molecules (VCAM-1)
  - monocytes enter the intima and LDL is oxidised
  - macrophages take up oxidised LDL through scavenger receptors and become foam cells
  - smooth muscle cells migrate from the media (PDGF) and proliferate, laying down collagen
  - inflammation continues and the plaque grows
- **Morphology:** fatty streak (earliest lesion, foam cells) → fibrous plaque with a fibrous cap and a necrotic lipid core with cholesterol clefts → complicated plaque (calcification, ulceration, haemorrhage, thrombosis, aneurysm).
- **Favourite sites:** abdominal aorta, coronary arteries, popliteal arteries, internal carotid and circle of Willis.
- **Myocardial infarction:** plaque rupture with thrombus blocks a coronary artery.
- **Stroke:** carotid plaque thrombosis or embolism, or plaques in cerebral vessels cause ischaemic infarcts.
- **Peripheral arterial disease:** intermittent claudication, critical ischaemia and gangrene of the legs.
- **Other complications:** aortic aneurysm, mesenteric ischaemia, renal artery stenosis.

### Question 6
Compare wound healing by primary and secondary intention under the following headings:
a) Mechanism and stages of each
b) Factors affecting wound healing
c) Complications of wound healing
*(20 Marks)*

**Model answer**
- **a) Primary intention (clean incised wound with edges apposed):** clot seals the wound; neutrophils within 24 hours and macrophages by day 3; granulation tissue and new collagen by days 3 to 7; epithelium regenerates; remodelling of collagen over weeks. Minimal scar.
- **Secondary intention (large tissue loss, infection or edges apart):** more clot and debris, a stronger inflammatory reaction, a large amount of granulation tissue filling the defect, and wound contraction by myofibroblasts. Slower, with a larger scar.
- **b) Factors:**
  - local: infection, foreign body, poor blood supply, tension and movement, wound size, irradiation
  - systemic: age, protein malnutrition, vitamin C and zinc deficiency, diabetes, corticosteroids, chemotherapy, anaemia, smoking, uraemia
- **c) Complications:** infection, dehiscence, incisional hernia, hypertrophic scar and keloid, excessive granulation tissue, contracture, adhesions, neuroma, and (rarely) cancer in a long-standing scar (Marjolin ulcer).

### Question 1
A 45-year-old male presents with fatigue, early satiety, and massive splenomegaly. Full blood count shows WBC 120×10⁹/L with neutrophilia, left shift, and prominent basophilia.
a) What is the most likely diagnosis? (1 mark)
b) Outline the laboratory investigations you would request to confirm this diagnosis, stating the expected findings for each. (9 marks)

**Model answer**
- **a)** Chronic myeloid leukaemia (chronic phase).
- **b) Investigations:**
  - full blood count and film: high white cell count with neutrophils at every stage of maturation (myelocytes, metamyelocytes), basophilia and eosinophilia, often raised platelets, mild normocytic anaemia
  - leucocyte alkaline phosphatase score: low
  - bone marrow aspirate and biopsy: hypercellular, myeloid hyperplasia, less than 10% blasts in the chronic phase
  - cytogenetics: Philadelphia chromosome, t(9;22)(q34;q11)
  - FISH or RT-PCR: BCR-ABL1 fusion gene (p210 transcript), which confirms the diagnosis
  - serum uric acid, LDH and vitamin B12: raised

### Question 2
A 32-year-old woman with a history of heavy menstrual bleeding presents with progressive fatigue, pallor, and pica.
a) What is the most likely diagnosis? (1 mark)
b) State three clinical signs you would expect to find on examination. (3 marks)
c) List four confirmatory laboratory tests and state the expected result for each. (6 marks)

**Model answer**
- **a)** Iron deficiency anaemia.
- **b) Signs:** pallor of the conjunctivae and palms, koilonychia (spoon nails), angular stomatitis or glossitis, tachycardia with a flow murmur.
- **c) Tests:**
  - full blood count: low haemoglobin, low MCV and MCH (hypochromic microcytic), raised RDW
  - blood film: hypochromic microcytes and pencil cells
  - serum ferritin: low (the most specific test)
  - serum iron low, total iron-binding capacity high, transferrin saturation low
  - bone marrow iron stain (Perls): absent stores (rarely needed)

### Question 3
A 10-year-old child presents with a rapidly growing jaw mass, fever, and night sweats. Biopsy reveals a starry sky pattern on histology.
a) What is the most likely diagnosis? (1 mark)
b) Describe the laboratory investigations needed to confirm this diagnosis, including histological, immunophenotypic, and molecular findings. (9 marks)

**Model answer**
- **a)** Burkitt lymphoma (the endemic type, linked to Epstein–Barr virus).
- **b) Investigations:**
  - histology: medium-sized, uniform cells with round nuclei, several nucleoli and basophilic vacuolated cytoplasm; a very high mitotic rate and apoptosis; scattered pale macrophages with ingested debris give the "starry sky" appearance
  - Ki-67 proliferation index close to 100%
  - immunophenotype: B-cell markers CD19, CD20, CD10 and BCL6 positive, surface IgM positive, TdT negative, BCL2 negative
  - molecular: t(8;14) with MYC–IgH rearrangement in about 80% (or t(2;8) or t(8;22)); confirm by FISH or PCR
  - EBV by in-situ hybridisation (EBER)
  - staging and baseline: LDH, uric acid, HIV test, imaging, bone marrow and CSF examination

### Question 4
A 50-year-old man with known liver cirrhosis and splenomegaly is found to have a platelet count of 60×10⁹/L on routine bloods.
a) Interpret this platelet count in the context of his clinical condition. (2 marks)
b) State two causes of thrombocytopenia in this patient. (2 marks)
c) Outline the laboratory tests you would perform to reach a definitive diagnosis, stating the expected findings. (6 marks)

**Model answer**
- **a)** Moderate thrombocytopenia, most consistent with hypersplenism from portal hypertension. It raises the risk of bleeding, especially with oesophageal varices.
- **b) Causes:** sequestration of platelets in the enlarged spleen (hypersplenism); reduced thrombopoietin production by the diseased liver. Others: alcohol toxicity to the marrow, folate deficiency, DIC.
- **c) Tests:**
  - repeat count in citrate and a blood film: to exclude platelet clumping; a true low count with normal cell shape
  - full blood count: other cell lines may also be low (pancytopenia in hypersplenism)
  - PT/INR and aPTT: prolonged because of reduced liver synthesis
  - liver function tests and albumin: confirm the severity of the liver disease
  - abdominal ultrasound with Doppler: splenomegaly, portal vein dilatation
  - bone marrow examination if unclear: normal or increased megakaryocytes in hypersplenism

### Question 5
A 6-year-old boy presents with recurrent haemarthroses and easy bruising following minor trauma. Coagulation screen shows prolonged APTT with a normal PT and normal platelet count.
a) Interpret these coagulation results. (2 marks)
b) What is the most likely diagnosis? (1 mark)
c) List four additional confirmatory laboratory tests and the expected finding for each. (7 marks)

**Model answer**
- **a)** A prolonged APTT with a normal PT and platelets points to a defect in the intrinsic pathway (factor VIII, IX or XI). The extrinsic pathway and platelet number are normal.
- **b)** Haemophilia A (factor VIII deficiency, X-linked recessive). Haemophilia B is the alternative.
- **c) Tests:**
  - factor VIII assay: low (reduced activity defines the severity)
  - factor IX assay: normal (excludes haemophilia B)
  - mixing study: the prolonged APTT corrects with normal plasma (an inhibitor would not)
  - von Willebrand factor antigen and ristocetin cofactor: normal (excludes von Willebrand disease)
  - bleeding time or platelet function: normal
  - genetic testing for the factor VIII gene (intron 22 inversion), and a Bethesda assay to check for inhibitors

### Question 6
A 28-year-old woman who is HIV-positive and on zidovudine-based antiretroviral therapy develops progressive fatigue. Full blood count reveals MCV of 110 fL with a haemoglobin of 8.2 g/dL.
a) Explain the mechanism by which zidovudine causes this type of anaemia. (3 marks)
b) List three confirmatory laboratory tests with expected results. (3 marks)
c) State three other causes of macrocytic anaemia. (4 marks)

**Model answer**
- **a)** Zidovudine is a thymidine analogue. In marrow progenitors it blocks DNA synthesis (chain termination and mitochondrial toxicity), which causes ineffective erythropoiesis, red cell enlargement and bone marrow suppression.
- **b) Tests:**
  - full blood count: low haemoglobin with raised MCV
  - blood film: macrocytes (often non-megaloblastic) and a low reticulocyte count
  - serum vitamin B12 and folate: normal, which separates it from deficiency anaemias; bone marrow shows erythroid suppression
- **c) Other causes:** vitamin B12 deficiency, folate deficiency, alcohol excess, hypothyroidism, liver disease, myelodysplastic syndromes, drugs such as methotrexate and hydroxyurea, reticulocytosis after bleeding or haemolysis.

### Question 7
Describe five laboratory features of haemolytic anaemia, explaining the pathophysiological basis and diagnostic significance of each finding.
*(10 marks)*

**Model answer**
- **Raised reticulocytes (with polychromasia):** the marrow responds to red cell loss with more young cells. Shows an intact marrow.
- **Raised unconjugated (indirect) bilirubin:** haem from destroyed red cells is converted to bilirubin faster than the liver can conjugate it. Causes jaundice and raised urinary urobilinogen.
- **Raised LDH and low haptoglobin:** LDH is released from broken red cells; haptoglobin binds free haemoglobin and is used up. Suggests haemolysis.
- **Abnormal film:** spherocytes (membrane loss, autoimmune or hereditary spherocytosis), fragments and schistocytes (microangiopathic haemolysis), sickle cells, bite cells. Shows the cause.
- **Positive direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test:** antibody or complement on the red cell. Separates autoimmune from non-immune causes.
- Also: haemoglobinaemia, haemoglobinuria and haemosiderinuria in intravascular haemolysis.

### Question 8
Outline the immunophenotypic markers used to differentiate acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL) from acute myeloid leukaemia (AML), explaining the significance of each marker.
*(10 marks)*

**Model answer**
- **ALL, B-lineage:** TdT, CD19, CD10 (CALLA), CD22, CD79a, PAX5 (CD20 in some).
- **ALL, T-lineage:** TdT, CD1a, CD2, CD3, CD5 and CD7.
- **AML:** myeloperoxidase (MPO), CD13, CD33, CD117 and CD34. Monocytic cells also express CD14 and CD64; erythroid cells glycophorin A; megakaryocytic cells CD41 and CD61. Acute promyelocytic leukaemia is HLA-DR and CD34 negative.
- **Significance:** MPO in 3% or more of blasts assigns the leukaemia to the myeloid lineage. TdT, CD19 and CD10 assign it to the lymphoid lineage. The lineage decides treatment, e.g. rituximab for CD20-positive ALL and ATRA for acute promyelocytic leukaemia. The phenotype gives prognosis and is used to follow minimal residual disease. Mixed-lineage leukaemia is diagnosed when both sets appear.

### Question 9
A 68-year-old man presents with persistent bone pain, fatigue, and recurrent infections. Serum electrophoresis reveals a monoclonal spike in the gamma region.
a) What is the most likely diagnosis? (1 mark)
b) Describe the full diagnostic workup for this condition, including biochemical, haematological, radiological, and bone marrow findings. (9 marks)

**Model answer**
- **a)** Multiple myeloma.
- **b) Workup:**
  - biochemical: serum protein electrophoresis and immunofixation (monoclonal IgG or IgA band); serum free light chains with an abnormal kappa to lambda ratio; urine for Bence Jones protein; high calcium; raised creatinine and urea (renal damage); low albumin; raised beta-2 microglobulin and LDH (prognosis)
  - haematological: normocytic normochromic anaemia, rouleaux on the blood film, very high ESR, sometimes circulating plasma cells, thrombocytopenia
  - radiological: skeletal survey with punched-out lytic lesions ("pepper-pot" skull), osteopenia and fractures; MRI or PET-CT where needed
  - bone marrow: 10% or more clonal plasma cells (CD138 and CD38 positive, light-chain restricted)
  - cytogenetics and FISH: del(17p), t(4;14), t(11;14) guide prognosis
- Diagnosis is made by clonal plasma cells plus myeloma-defining events (high calcium, renal failure, anaemia or bone lesions).

### Question 1
Discuss the components of blood and blood products under the following headings:
a) Types of blood products and their preparation
b) Indications for transfusion of each product
c) Complications of blood transfusion, including transfusion reactions and their management
*(18 Marks)*

**Model answer**
- **a) Products and preparation:**
  - red cell concentrate: whole blood is centrifuged and the plasma removed; stored at 2 to 6 °C for up to 35 to 42 days; usually leucodepleted
  - platelet concentrate: pooled or apheresis; stored at 22 °C with agitation for up to 5 days
  - fresh frozen plasma: separated and frozen within 6 to 8 hours; contains all clotting factors
  - cryoprecipitate: the precipitate on slowly thawing plasma; rich in fibrinogen, factor VIII, von Willebrand factor and factor XIII
  - others: albumin, clotting factor concentrates, immunoglobulins, whole blood in massive bleeding
- **b) Indications:**
  - red cells: symptomatic anaemia, acute blood loss, chronic anaemia such as thalassaemia
  - platelets: thrombocytopenia with bleeding, or prophylaxis when the count is very low
  - fresh frozen plasma: clotting factor deficiency, liver disease, warfarin reversal, DIC, massive transfusion
  - cryoprecipitate: low fibrinogen, haemophilia A and von Willebrand disease when concentrate is not available
- **c) Complications and management:**
  - acute haemolytic reaction (ABO mismatch): fever, loin pain, hypotension, haemoglobinuria, DIC. Stop the transfusion, check the unit and patient identity, give IV fluids, monitor urine output, send blood to the laboratory, and inform the blood bank
  - febrile non-haemolytic reaction: slow or stop, give paracetamol
  - allergic reaction: antihistamine; anaphylaxis (e.g. IgA deficiency) needs adrenaline
  - TRALI (donor anti-HLA or granulocyte antibodies): non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema, supportive care and oxygen
  - TACO (volume overload): sit the patient up, oxygen, diuretic
  - delayed haemolytic reaction: monitor and treat anaemia
  - infections: HIV, hepatitis B and C, syphilis, malaria, CMV, and bacterial contamination of platelets
  - metabolic: hyperkalaemia, citrate toxicity with low calcium, hypothermia, iron overload with repeated transfusion
  - graft-versus-host disease (prevent with irradiated products)

### Question 2
Describe the full blood count components and explain the diagnostic significance of an abnormality in each parameter, giving a clinical example for each.
*(18 Marks)*

**Model answer**
- **Haemoglobin:** low in anaemia (iron deficiency); high in polycythaemia vera or dehydration.
- **Red cell count and haematocrit:** follow haemoglobin; raised in polycythaemia.
- **MCV:** low in iron deficiency and thalassaemia; high in vitamin B12 or folate deficiency and alcohol excess.
- **MCH and MCHC:** low in hypochromic anaemias; MCHC is raised in hereditary spherocytosis.
- **RDW:** raised when red cell sizes vary, e.g. mixed iron and B12 deficiency.
- **White cell count:** raised in infection and leukaemia; low in marrow failure, HIV and after chemotherapy.
- **Differential count:**
  - neutrophilia in bacterial infection; neutropenia after chemotherapy or in severe sepsis
  - lymphocytosis in viral infection and chronic lymphocytic leukaemia
  - eosinophilia in parasitic infection and allergy
  - basophilia in chronic myeloid leukaemia
  - monocytosis in tuberculosis and chronic infection
- **Platelets:** low in immune thrombocytopenia, DIC and hypersplenism; raised in iron deficiency, infection and essential thrombocythaemia.
- **Reticulocytes:** high in haemolysis and after bleeding; low in marrow failure and nutritional deficiency.
- The blood film adds shape and maturity: spherocytes, sickle cells, blasts and parasites.

### Question 3
Discuss Chronic Myeloid Leukaemia (CML) under the following headings:
a) Pathogenesis, including the role of the Philadelphia chromosome and BCR-ABL fusion gene
b) Clinical features and laboratory findings at presentation
c) Diagnosis and monitoring of treatment response
*(18 Marks)*

**Model answer**
- **a) Pathogenesis:** a clonal disease of the haematopoietic stem cell. A reciprocal translocation t(9;22)(q34;q11) produces the Philadelphia chromosome and fuses the BCR gene on chromosome 22 with the ABL1 gene from chromosome 9. The BCR-ABL1 protein (usually p210) is a constitutively active tyrosine kinase. It switches on the RAS/MAPK, PI3K/AKT and JAK/STAT pathways, so cells proliferate, resist apoptosis and adhere poorly to the marrow stroma.
- **b) Clinical:** slow onset; fatigue, weight loss, night sweats, left upper abdominal discomfort or fullness from splenomegaly, bleeding or bruising, and sometimes gout. Rarely, leucostasis symptoms.
- **Laboratory:** very high white count with all stages of myeloid maturation (a left shift), basophilia and eosinophilia, thrombocytosis, mild anaemia, low LAP score, raised uric acid, LDH and B12. Marrow: hypercellular with myeloid hyperplasia.
- **Phases:** chronic (under 10% blasts), accelerated (10 to 19% blasts), blast crisis (20% or more blasts).
- **c) Diagnosis:** demonstrating the Philadelphia chromosome by cytogenetics or FISH, and the BCR-ABL1 transcript by RT-PCR.
- **Monitoring:** full blood count; quantitative RT-PCR for BCR-ABL1 transcripts on the international scale at 3, 6 and 12 months; targets are 10% or less at 3 months, under 1% at 6 months and 0.1% or less (major molecular response) by 12 months. Cytogenetic response is also assessed.
- **Treatment:** tyrosine kinase inhibitors, imatinib first line, then dasatinib, nilotinib or ponatinib (for the T315I mutation). Allogeneic stem-cell transplant for resistant disease or blast crisis.
