## SummaryThis document provides a comprehensive overview of male reproductive system pathologies, covering congenital malformations, inflammatory conditions, and neoplasms of the penis, testis, epididymis, and prostate. It details conditions such as hypospadias, squamous cell carcinoma of the penis, cryptorchidism, testicular torsion, germ cell tumors, prostatitis, benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), and prostate carcinoma. Key risk factors, pathogenesis, clinical features, and management strategies are discussed for each pathology.## Key Points- **Hypospadias** is the most common penile malformation, characterized by a ventral urethral opening, and is associated with increased UTI risk.- **Squamous cell carcinoma** is the predominant penile neoplasm, with risk factors including poor hygiene, smoking, and HPV infection.
- **Cryptorchidism** significantly increases the risk of both sterility and testicular cancer, even in the contralateral descended testis.
- **Testicular torsion** is a urologic emergency caused by twisting of the spermatic cord, requiring prompt surgical intervention to prevent venous infarction.
- **Germ cell tumors** account for 95% of postpubertal testicular neoplasms, often presenting as painless masses, and are frequently associated with the molecular marker isochromosome i(12p).
- **Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH)** is an androgen-dependent proliferation of prostatic tissue in the transitional zone, causing obstructive and irritative urinary symptoms in many older men.
- **Prostate carcinoma** is the most common cancer in men, typically arising in the outer peripheral zone, and is graded by the Gleason system.
- **Tumor markers** like hCG, AFP, and LDH are crucial for the diagnosis and monitoring of testicular germ cell tumors.

## Detailed Notes### Congenital MalformationsMost penile malformations involve abnormal positioning of the distal urethral orifice.**Hypospadias**
- Urethral opening on the **ventral (underside)** of the penis
- Most common penile malformation — 1 in 300 live male births
- Constricted orifice → urinary obstruction + increased UTI risk
- Associated with inguinal hernia and cryptorchidism
-
-

**Epispadias**
- Urethral opening on the **dorsal (top)** of the penis
- Less common than hypospadias
- Severe cases associated with bladder exstrophy
-
-

### Inflammatory Lesions**Balanitis** — inflammation of the glans penis**Balanoposthitis** — inflammation of the glans AND prepuce (foreskin)
- Organisms: *Candida albicans*, anaerobic bacteria, *Gardnerella*, pyogenic bacteria
- Most common in uncircumcised males with poor hygiene
- **Smegma** (dead skin, sweat, debris) acts as the local irritant
-
-

**Phimosis**
- Foreskin cannot retract over the glans
- Can be congenital; most cases acquired from scarring after balanoposthitis
- Perpetuates smegma accumulation, recurrent infection, and increased SCC risk
-
-

### NeoplasmsOver 95% of penile neoplasms arise from squamous epithelium. More common in developing countries. Most cases occur in uncircumcised males over 40 years.**Risk Factors for Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC)**
- Uncircumcised males over 40
- Poor hygiene — smegma exposure
- Smoking
- HPV infection, especially types **16** and **18**
-
-

**Bowen Disease — SCC In Situ**
- Solitary plaque on the shaft of the penis in older uncircumcised males
- Histology: malignant cells throughout the epidermis — **no stromal invasion**
- Progresses to invasive SCC in ~10% of cases
-
-

**Invasive Squamous Cell Carcinoma**
- Gray, crusted, papular lesion — most commonly on the glans or prepuce
- Infiltration produces an indurated, ulcerated lesion with irregular margins
- Histology: typical keratinizing SCC
- Prognosis is stage-dependent
-
-

**Verrucous Carcinoma**
- Variant of SCC with papillary architecture and virtually no cytologic atypia
- Rounded, pushing deep margins
- Locally invasive — does **not** metastasize
-
-

### CryptorchidismFailure of complete testicular descent from the abdomen into the scrotum. Present in ~1% of 1-year-old males. Both bilateral and unilateral forms associated with tubular atrophy and sterility.- Microscopic tubular atrophy evident by age 5–6 years; hyalinization by puberty
- Foci of **intratubular germ cell neoplasia** develop within atrophic tubules — precursors to germ cell tumours
- Carries **3–5 fold increased risk** of testicular cancer; history of cryptorchidism found in ~10% of all testicular cancer cases
- Contralateral descended testis also carries increased cancer risk
- **Treatment:** **Orchiopexy** — reduces risk of both sterility and malignancy
-
-

Note: Similar atrophic changes occur with chronic ischemia, trauma, irradiation, chemotherapy, and elevated estrogen states (e.g. cirrhosis) — but these do **not** produce intratubular germ cell neoplasia.
### Inflammatory Lesions**Nonspecific Epididymitis and Orchitis**- Spreads from UTI via vas deferens or spermatic cord lymphatics
- Testis: swollen and tender
- Histology: predominantly neutrophilic inflammatory infiltrate
-
-

**Mumps Orchitis**
- Complicates mumps in ~20% of infected adult males; rare in children
- Testis: oedematous and congested
- Histology: lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate
- Severe cases → necrosis, tubular atrophy, fibrosis, and sterility
-
-

**Tuberculous Epididymo-orchitis**
- Most common cause of granulomatous orchitis
- Usually begins as epididymitis → secondary testicular involvement
- Histology: granulomatous inflammation with **caseous necrosis** — identical to TB elsewhere
-
-

### Vascular Disturbances — Testicular TorsionTwisting of the spermatic cord obstructs **venous drainage** while arteries remain patent → venous infarction. A **urologic emergency** — must be relieved within 6 hours to preserve viability.| | Neonatal Torsion | Adult Torsion |
|---|---|---|
| Timing | In utero or shortly after birth | Adolescence |
| Anatomic defect | None identified | **Bell-clapper abnormality** — increased testicular mobility |
| Onset | — | Sudden pain; may awaken patient from sleep |
**Management:** Surgical exploration + manual untwisting. Contralateral orchiopexy performed routinely to prevent torsion on the other side.
### Testicular Neoplasms**Epidemiology**- ~6 per 100,000 males; more common in whites
- Brothers of affected males have **8–10 fold** increased risk
- Cryptorchidism → 3–5 fold increased risk; present in ~10% of all testicular cancer cases
- Intersex syndromes (androgen insensitivity, gonadal dysgenesis) also increase risk
- Key molecular marker: **isochromosome i(12p)** — found in virtually ALL germ cell tumours regardless of histologic type
-
-

**Classification**
- 95% of postpubertal testicular tumours arise from germ cells — all malignant
- Sex cord-stromal tumours (Sertoli, Leydig cell) — uncommon and usually benign
-
-

### Germ Cell Tumour Summary| Tumour | Peak Age | Morphology | Tumour Markers ||---|---|---|---|
| Seminoma | 40–50 | Sheets of uniform polygonal cells, clear cytoplasm, lymphocytes in stroma | **hCG** elevated in 10% |
| Embryonal Carcinoma | 20–30 | Poorly differentiated pleomorphic cells in cords, sheets, or papillary formation | Negative (pure form) |
| Yolk Sac Tumour | <3 years | Poorly differentiated endothelium-like cuboidal/columnar cells | **AFP** elevated in 90% |
| Choriocarcinoma | 20–30 | Cytotrophoblast + syncytiotrophoblast, no villus formation | **hCG** elevated in 100% |
| Teratoma | All ages | Tissues from all 3 germ layers, variable differentiation | Negative (pure form) |
| Mixed Tumour | 15–30 | Variable depending on mixture | **hCG** + **AFP** elevated in 90% |
### Clinical Features of Testicular Tumours- Present as a **painless, non-translucent testicular mass** — non-translucence distinguishes it from hydrocele- Biopsy risks tumour spillage → standard management is **radical orchiectomy**
- **Seminomas** — remain confined to testis for long periods; metastasize to iliac and para-aortic lymph nodes; haematogenous spread occurs late
- **Nonseminomatous tumours** — metastasize earlier via lymphatic and haematogenous routes; commonest sites are liver and lungs
-
-

**Tumour Markers — Clinical Importance**
- Useful in both **diagnosis** and **monitoring treatment response**
- **hCG** — always elevated in choriocarcinoma; also elevated when syncytiotrophoblastic cells present in other tumours
- **AFP** — elevated indicates yolk sac tumour component
- **LDH** — correlates with overall tumour burden
-
-

### ProstatitisFour recognized categories:| Category | Frequency | Key Features |
|---|---|---|
| Acute bacterial prostatitis | 2–5% | Fever, chills, dysuria; tender boggy prostate on rectal exam; may cause sepsis |
| Chronic bacterial prostatitis | 2–5% | Recurrent UTIs, low back pain, dysuria, perineal and suprapubic discomfort |
| Chronic nonbacterial prostatitis / Chronic pelvic pain syndrome | 90–95% | Symptoms without identifiable uropathogen; no proven therapy |
| Asymptomatic inflammatory prostatitis | Unknown | Incidental leukocytes in prostatic secretions; no symptoms |
**Granulomatous Prostatitis**
- **BCG instillation** (bladder cancer treatment) → histologically indistinguishable from TB
- Fungal granulomatous prostatitis — seen only in immunocompromised hosts
- Nonspecific granulomatous prostatitis — reaction to secretions from ruptured prostatic ducts/acini
- Biopsy generally unhelpful in bacterial prostatitis; useful mainly in granulomatous cases
-
-

**Treatment:** Acute and chronic bacterial prostatitis → antibiotics. Chronic pelvic pain syndrome → no proven therapy.
### Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH / Nodular Hyperplasia)**Epidemiology**- Extremely common — present in significant numbers by age 40; prevalence reaches **90% by the eighth decade**
- Does not occur in males castrated before puberty or in those with androgen insensitivity
-
-

**Pathogenesis**
- Androgen-dependent proliferation of both stromal and glandular elements
- **DHT (dihydrotestosterone)** is the key mediator
-
-

- Synthesized from testosterone by **5α-reductase type 2** in the prostate
- DHT is **10x more potent** than testosterone in binding androgen receptors
- AR activation → drives growth and survival of prostatic epithelium and stroma
- Smooth muscle contraction via **α1-adrenergic receptors** worsens obstructive symptoms
-
-

**Morphology**
- Occurs in the **inner transitional zone** of the prostate
- Gland typically weighs 60–100 g; well-circumscribed nodules on cut surface
- Nodules may be solid or cystic (dilated glandular elements)
- Urethra compressed to a narrow slit; may project into bladder as ball-valve obstruction
- Microscopy: tall columnar epithelial cells + peripheral basal cell layer; **corpora amylacea** (inspissated proteinaceous secretions) in glandular lumina
-
-

**Clinical Features**
- Only ~10% of men with pathologic BPH develop clinical symptoms
-
-

| Type | Symptoms |
|---|---|
| Obstructive | Hesitancy, weak stream, intermittent flow, incomplete emptying |
| Irritative | Urgency, frequency, nocturia |
- Complications: recurrent UTIs (residual urine), complete urinary retention, hydronephrosis
-
-

**Treatment**
| Agent | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Finasteride** | **5α-reductase inhibitor** → reduces DHT |
| **Tamsulosin (Flomax)** | **α1-adrenergic blocker** → relaxes smooth muscle |
| Surgical | Reserved for severe, refractory cases |
### Carcinoma of the Prostate**Epidemiology**- **Most common cancer in men**
- Typically affects men aged 65–75 years
- More aggressive clinically in Black men than in white men; occult lesions occur equally across races
- Wide spectrum: indolent clinically insignificant lesions to aggressive, rapidly fatal disease
- Many men **die with prostate cancer rather than from it**
-
-

**Pathogenesis**
- Most common mutations: **TMPRSS2-ETS fusion genes** and activation of **PI3K/AKT signaling pathway**
- Arises most commonly in the **outer peripheral zone** — may be palpable on rectal examination
-
-

**Morphology**
- Adenocarcinoma with variable differentiation
- Neoplastic glands lined by a **single layer of cells** — loss of basal cell layer distinguishes carcinoma from BPH
- Graded by the **Gleason system** — correlates with pathologic stage and prognosis
-
-

**Clinical Features**
- Most localized cancers are clinically silent — detected by **PSA screening**
- Advanced disease: **osteoblastic bone metastases**
- **PSA** is useful but imperfect — significant false-positive and false-negative rates
- Post-treatment PSA monitoring is highly valuable for detecting recurrence
-
-

## Practice Questions- **Question:** A newborn male presents with the urethral opening on the underside of his penis. What is this condition, and is it common? → **Answer:** This is **Hypospadias**, the most common penile malformation, affecting 1 in 300 live male births.- **Question:** An uncircumcised male presents with inflammation of the glans and prepuce. What is this condition, and what is a common contributing factor? → **Answer:** This is **Balanoposthitis**, commonly caused by poor hygiene and accumulation of smegma.
- **Question:** A 50-year-old male is diagnosed with a solitary plaque on the shaft of his penis. Histology reveals malignant cells throughout the epidermis without stromal invasion. What is this diagnosis, and what is its prognosis? → **Answer:** This is **Bowen Disease (SCC in situ)**, which progresses to invasive SCC in approximately 10% of cases.
- **Question:** A 1-year-old male is found to have an undescended testis. What is this condition called, and what are the long-term risks if untreated? → **Answer:** This is **Cryptorchidism**, carrying a 3-5 fold increased risk of testicular cancer and potential sterility due to tubular atrophy.
- **Question:** An adolescent male presents with sudden, severe scrotal pain. Physical examination suggests testicular torsion. What is the immediate concern, and how is it managed? → **Answer:** Testicular torsion is a **urologic emergency** requiring surgical exploration and manual untwisting within 6 hours to preserve testicular viability.
- **Question:** A 25-year-old male is diagnosed with a testicular germ cell tumor. What molecular marker is found in virtually all germ cell tumors, regardless of histologic type? → **Answer:** The key molecular marker is **isochromosome i(12p)**.
- **Question:** A male patient experiences recurrent UTIs, low back pain, and perineal discomfort. Cultures are negative. What is the most likely diagnosis for his prostatitis, and what is the typical treatment approach? → **Answer:** This is most likely **Chronic nonbacterial prostatitis / Chronic pelvic pain syndrome**, for which there is no proven therapy.
- **Question:** An elderly man presents with obstructive and irritative urinary symptoms. Biopsy reveals benign prostatic hyperplasia. In which zone of the prostate does BPH typically occur, and what is the key hormonal mediator? → **Answer:** BPH occurs in the **inner transitional zone** of the prostate, and **dihydrotestosterone (DHT)** is the key mediator.
