# Diabetes in Pregnancy

**Year 4 · Obstetrics & Gynaecology · Medical Conditions in Pregnancy**

> **In one line:** pregnancy is a diabetogenic state. Placental hormones make the mother insulin-resistant, so a woman who cannot raise her insulin output enough becomes hyperglycaemic. The glucose crosses the placenta, the fetus makes extra insulin, and that one mechanism explains macrosomia, shoulder dystocia, neonatal hypoglycaemia and much more.

## What you must know

- Two groups: **pre-gestational diabetes** (known before pregnancy, or overt diabetes found in pregnancy) and **gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM)**.
- GDM is about **90%** of diabetes in pregnancy and affects 3 to 5% of pregnancies; it is commoner in African women than in white women.
- About **50% of women with GDM develop type 2 diabetes** within 5 to 20 years.
- Screen at **24 to 28 weeks** with a 75 g OGTT (earlier if there are risk factors).
- Poor control at conception causes **congenital malformations**; poor control later causes **macrosomia and neonatal hypoglycaemia**.
- Aim for tight control: diet, exercise, then metformin or insulin. Deliver at about **38 weeks** if on insulin or if there are complications.

## 1. Definitions and classification

Diabetes mellitus is a chronic metabolic disorder from absolute or relative insulin deficiency, or from reduced sensitivity of peripheral tissue to insulin.

Hyperglycaemia found in pregnancy is classified as one of two things:

| | Diabetes mellitus in pregnancy (DIP) | Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) |
|---|---|---|
| Who | Known diabetic who becomes pregnant, **or** hyperglycaemia first found in pregnancy that already meets the WHO criteria for diabetes in a non-pregnant person | Hyperglycaemia first found in pregnancy that does **not** meet the criteria for diabetes |
| Timing | Any time, including the first trimester | Usually the second or third trimester, most often after 24 weeks |
| Persists after delivery? | Yes | Often resolves, but the definition applies whether or not it does |

**Overt diabetes** is diagnosed by any one of: fasting plasma glucose of 7.0 mmol/L (126 mg/dL) or more, a 2-hour post-glucose (75 g) value of 11.1 mmol/L (200 mg/dL) or more, or HbA1c of 6.5% or more.

The **White classification** grades pre-gestational diabetes by age at onset, duration and end-organ damage. In the lecture notes the end-organ classes are: **R** retinopathy, **F** nephropathy, **H** coronary artery disease.

## 2. Epidemiology

- Diabetes complicates 1 to 14% of pregnancies; **90%** of these are GDM.
- The rate is rising because of sedentary lifestyles, dietary change, and more childhood and adolescent obesity.
- GDM recurs in more than 50% of later pregnancies.

## 3. Pathophysiology of GDM

1. Placental hormones (human placental lactogen, oestrogen, progesterone, cortisol) oppose insulin. The placenta also degrades insulin. The result is **insulin resistance**, which increases as pregnancy advances.
2. A healthy pancreas answers by making about **three times** the normal amount of insulin.
3. If it cannot, blood glucose rises: GDM.
4. After delivery the placenta is gone and glucose usually returns to normal.

> **Link to the baby (Pedersen hypothesis):** maternal glucose crosses the placenta freely; insulin does not. The fetal pancreas responds with hyperinsulinaemia. Insulin is a growth hormone for the fetus, so it lays down fat around the shoulders and trunk (macrosomia). After birth the glucose supply is cut but the high insulin level remains, so the baby becomes **hypoglycaemic**.

## 4. Risk factors for GDM

- Age over 35 years
- Obesity (BMI over 30 kg/m², or weight over 90 kg)
- Previous GDM, or a previous large baby (over 4 kg)
- Previous unexplained stillbirth or perinatal loss; poor obstetric history (miscarriages, anomalies)
- Family history of diabetes in a first-degree relative
- Polycystic ovarian syndrome
- Persistent glycosuria; known impaired glucose tolerance or impaired fasting glucose
- Black race
- Current pregnancy with polyhydramnios, multiple gestation; grand multiparity

## 5. Screening and diagnosis

- A woman with **more than one risk factor** should have a random or fasting blood glucose at her **first visit**.
- Screening is done at **24 to 28 weeks** (or at first contact if she comes late).
- The test is a **75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT)**: fasting sample, 75 g glucose by mouth after an overnight fast of 8 hours, then plasma glucose at 1 hour and 2 hours.
- Another approach uses a 50 g glucose challenge at any time of day; if abnormal, it is followed by the 75 g OGTT.

> **Remember:** a positive OGTT at any one of the three time points makes the diagnosis in most protocols (for example IADPG/WHO thresholds: fasting 5.1, 1-hour 10.0, 2-hour 8.5 mmol/L). Check the threshold your own hospital protocol uses.

## 6. Effects of pregnancy on diabetes

- **Control gets harder.** Insulin antagonism from placental lactogen, oestrogen, progesterone and cortisol, plus insulin degradation by the placenta, raise insulin requirements as pregnancy advances.
- **Ketoacidosis** is easier to precipitate: hyperemesis, infection, fasting in labour, and corticosteroids given for preterm labour are the usual triggers.
- Lipolysis is rapid after a short fast.
- **Insulin needs fall sharply after delivery** (the puerperium), so insulin doses must be cut or stopped immediately to avoid hypoglycaemia.
- Retinopathy, nephropathy, coronary disease and neuropathy can worsen during pregnancy.

## 7. Effects of diabetes on pregnancy

### Fetal and neonatal

- **Congenital malformations (6 to 10%, against about 3% in the general population):** this is *diabetic embryopathy*. It relates to the HbA1c at conception and early pregnancy, not to GDM that starts after organogenesis.
  - Cardiac defects (VSD, transposition of the great vessels, hypoplastic left heart)
  - CNS defects (anencephaly, spina bifida, holoprosencephaly)
  - **Caudal regression syndrome** (very characteristic)
  - Risk is about 3% when initial HbA1c is under 8%, rising to over 20% at 10% or more.
- Miscarriage and preterm birth
- **Macrosomia (40 to 50%)** and shoulder dystocia; brachial plexus injury, fractures
- Stillbirth; perinatal death rises 2 to 3 times
- **Neonatal problems:** respiratory distress syndrome, hypoglycaemia, hypocalcaemia, hypomagnesaemia, polycythaemia, jaundice
- Long term: childhood obesity, glucose intolerance, metabolic syndrome. Infants are twice as likely to be overweight and four times as likely to have metabolic syndrome.

> **Note:** congenital anomalies are *not* increased in true GDM, because glucose control was normal during organogenesis (the first 8 weeks).

### Maternal

- **Antenatal:** recurrent miscarriage, preterm labour (about 26%, from infection and polyhydramnios), urinary tract infection and vulvovaginal candidiasis, **pre-eclampsia (about 25%)**, **polyhydramnios (25 to 50%)**, worsening retinopathy and nephropathy
- **Labour:** prolonged labour with a big baby, **shoulder dystocia** (shoulders are disproportionately large compared with the head), perineal tears, postpartum haemorrhage, operative delivery
- **Puerperium:** puerperal sepsis, lactation failure

### Complication rates (diabetic vs non-diabetic pregnancies)

| Complication | Non-diabetic | Diabetic |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-eclampsia | about 8% | about 12% or higher |
| Stillbirth | baseline | roughly doubled |
| Macrosomia | about 10% | 40 to 50% |
| Congenital anomalies | 2 to 3% | 6 to 10% (pre-gestational) |

## 8. Management

### Before pregnancy (pre-gestational diabetes)

- Planned pregnancy and **contraception until control is good**, ideally for at least 3 months
- Target **HbA1c 6 to 6.5%**; fasting under 5.3 mmol/L; 1-hour post-meal under 7.8; 2-hour post-meal under 6.7
- Start **folic acid** (higher dose, 5 mg, is usually advised)
- Stop teratogenic drugs: **ACE inhibitors, ARBs, statins**; stop smoking; keep BP under 130/80
- Screen for nephropathy, retinopathy, thyroid disease, neuropathy, UTI and heart disease
- Weight optimisation. Risk of a diabetic child is 3 to 4% if one parent is diabetic and up to 40% if both are.

### During pregnancy

- **Team care** (obstetrician, physician, dietician), frequent visits: monthly in the first trimester, every 2 to 4 weeks in the second, every 2 weeks in the third
- Home glucose monitoring; adjust insulin to results
- **Diet and exercise first:** complex carbohydrates, restricted calories and refined carbohydrates; aerobic exercise
- **Targets:** fasting under 5.0; pre-meal under 6.0; 1 hour after meals under 7.8; 2 hours after meals under 6.7 mmol/L
- If not at target within **2 weeks**: add **metformin** or **insulin**. Insulin is needed in about 15 to 30% of GDM patients (up to 25% in some texts).
- Routine antenatal screening plus urinalysis, urea and electrolytes, FBC and ultrasound in the second and third trimesters
- **Detect malformations:** early HbA1c, ultrasound at 13 to 14 weeks, a detailed anatomy scan at 18 to 22 weeks, and fetal echocardiography
- Growth scans in the third trimester to assess macrosomia and liquor volume

### Delivery

| Situation | Plan |
|---|---|
| Diet-controlled GDM, good control, no complications | Await spontaneous labour; usually not beyond 40 weeks |
| On insulin, or complications (pre-eclampsia, macrosomia, polyhydramnios) | Induction or elective caesarean at about **38 weeks** |
| Estimated fetal weight over 4.5 kg | Consider elective caesarean to avoid shoulder dystocia |
| Preterm delivery needed | Steroids for lung maturity are given, but expect **hyperglycaemia** and increase insulin |

During labour keep blood glucose at **4 to 7 mmol/L** (a sliding-scale insulin and dextrose infusion in a woman on insulin). Be ready for shoulder dystocia and neonatal care.

### After delivery

- Stop or reduce insulin at once
- If glucose is above 7.8 mmol/L (random) or 6.1 mmol/L (fasting) after delivery, treat as pre-existing diabetes
- Otherwise repeat the **75 g OGTT at 6 weeks** to exclude persisting diabetes or impaired glucose tolerance
- If normal: check fasting glucose **once a year** for life
- Advise exercise, healthy diet, normal BMI, contraception from 6 weeks. Breastfeeding is encouraged.
- Counsel about the risk of GDM next time (over 50%) and of type 2 diabetes.

## Key points to revise

1. GDM = glucose intolerance first recognised in pregnancy; 90% of diabetes in pregnancy.
2. Screen with a 75 g OGTT at 24 to 28 weeks.
3. **Malformations** come from poor control at conception; **macrosomia and neonatal hypoglycaemia** come from poor control later (maternal glucose, fetal insulin).
4. Pre-eclampsia, polyhydramnios, shoulder dystocia and PPH are the key obstetric complications.
5. Diet first, then metformin or insulin; deliver at about 38 weeks if on insulin or complicated.
6. Insulin requirement falls sharply after delivery. OGTT at 6 weeks, then annual glucose.
