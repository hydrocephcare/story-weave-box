# Haemostasis and Inherited Bleeding Disorders

**Year 4 · Paediatrics · Haematology (coagulation)**

> **In one line:** a missing procoagulant protein causes bleeding, a missing anticoagulant protein causes thrombosis. The commonest inherited bleeding disorders are haemophilia A and B (X-linked factor VIII and IX deficiency, with joint bleeds and a long aPTT) and von Willebrand disease (mucosal bleeding), with factor XI deficiency (haemophilia C) milder. Treatment is replacement of the missing factor, early and to a target level.

## What you must know

- **PT** tests the extrinsic/common pathway (factor VII, X, V, II, fibrinogen); **aPTT** tests the intrinsic/common pathway (XII, XI, IX, VIII, X, V, II, fibrinogen). Normal PT is 10 to 13 seconds.
- **Haemophilia A** (factor VIII) and **B** (factor IX) are X-linked recessive, present in about 1 in 5,000 males (85% type A). Isolated **prolonged aPTT**, normal PT, platelets, bleeding time and thrombin time.
- **Hallmark of haemophilic bleeding is haemarthrosis**; severe disease is under 1% factor activity, moderate 1 to 5%, mild over 5%.
- Treat bleeds with factor concentrate to **35 to 50%** (mild or moderate bleed) or **100%** (life-threatening bleed). Replace factor **before** imaging a head injury.
- Complications: chronic arthropathy, **inhibitors** (25 to 35%), transfusion-transmitted infection.
- **von Willebrand disease (VWD)** is the commonest inherited bleeding disorder; mucosal bleeding (epistaxis, menorrhagia, post-extraction). Type 1 is 60 to 80%.
- **Desmopressin** works in mild haemophilia A and type 1 VWD, not in haemophilia B or severe haemophilia A; relatively contraindicated in type 2B VWD.
- Avoid aspirin and NSAIDs in all bleeding disorders.

## 1. Pathophysiology of haemostasis

- **Congenital deficiency of a single procoagulant** gives a bleeding disorder; **deficiency of an anticoagulant** (clotting factor inhibitor) predisposes to thrombosis.
- **Sepsis** and its secondary effects (shock, acidosis) activate coagulation and fibrinolysis. In **DIC**, platelets, procoagulant factors and anticoagulant proteins are consumed, leaving a system prone to both bleeding and clotting.
- **Newborns and patients with severe liver disease** have synthetic deficiency of both procoagulant and anticoagulant proteins, so mild or moderate triggers can cause haemorrhage or thrombosis.

### 1.1 The coagulation pathways

| Pathway | Steps |
|---|---|
| **Extrinsic** (tissue-factor mediated) | Tissue factor (thromboplastin) with calcium gives a burst of factor VIIa. The **tissue factor-VIIa complex activates factor X** |
| **Intrinsic** (surface/contact activation) | Factor XII is activated (helped by **prekallikrein** and **high-molecular-weight kininogen**) on a surface. XIIa activates XI to XIa, which activates IX to IXa. On the platelet phospholipid surface, **IXa + VIIIa + calcium form the "tenase" complex** that activates X |
| **Common** | Factor Xa on the platelet phospholipid surface complexes with factor V and calcium (**prothrombinase**) to convert prothrombin (II) to **thrombin (IIa)**. Thrombin converts **fibrinogen to fibrin**, the end point |

- In vivo, the VIIa-tissue factor complex also activates factor IX to initiate clotting.

### 1.2 Screening tests

| Test | What it measures | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **PT** | Clotting initiated by tissue factor with calcium (factor VII, X, V, II, fibrinogen) | Normal 10 to 13 s. **Not prolonged** by deficiency of VIII, IX, XI or XII. Standardised as the **INR** (used to monitor vitamin K antagonists such as warfarin) |
| **aPTT (PTT)** | Initiation at factor XII through to clot, using a contact activator (silica, kaolin, ellagic acid) with calcium and phospholipid | Does **not** measure factor VII, factor XIII or anticoagulants. Normal range varies between laboratories |

- An isolated single-factor deficiency prolongs PT, aPTT or both depending on its place in the cascade. This approach suits hereditary deficiencies. In **acquired** disorders (liver disease, DIC), several factors are usually low, so judge the relative prolongation of PT and aPTT.
- **Contact factor deficiencies** (XII, HMW kininogen, prekallikrein) markedly prolong aPTT but cause **no bleeding**.

> **Exam tip:** isolated long aPTT with normal PT = intrinsic pathway factor (VIII, IX, XI, XII) or VWD with low factor VIII, or an inhibitor.

## 2. Haemophilia A and B

### 2.1 Definition and pathophysiology

- Deficiency of **factor VIII (haemophilia A)** or **factor IX (haemophilia B)**: the most common and serious congenital coagulation factor deficiencies. Clinical findings are virtually identical. (Factor XI deficiency is haemophilia C.)
- VIII and IX form the **tenase complex** (with phospholipid and calcium) that activates factor X. PT is normal because it tests activation of X by VII.
- After injury the platelet plug forms normally, but **thrombin generation is inadequate**, so a tightly cross-linked fibrin clot is not formed. The patient forms a **soft, friable clot** that is delayed and not robust.
- In closed spaces (a joint) bleeding stops by **tamponade**; in open wounds profuse blood loss can occur, and rebleeding follows physiological clot lysis or minor trauma.

### 2.2 Genetics and classification

- Prevalence about **1 in 5,000 males**; 85% factor VIII, 10 to 15% factor IX. No racial predilection.
- Genes for VIII and IX lie near the end of the long arm of the **X chromosome**: **X-linked recessive**. About **30% of haemophilia A is a new (spontaneous) mutation**, so no family history does not exclude it.
- Most patients make reduced protein; 5 to 10% of type A and 40 to 50% of type B make a dysfunctional protein. **45 to 50% of severe haemophilia A have an intron 22 inversion** of the factor VIII gene with no protein produced.
- **Female carriers:** through lyonisation (X inactivation), some have low enough factor levels for mild bleeding. Measure factor levels in all known or possible carriers before surgery or when bleeding. The VIII:VWF ratio can help but gives false results; best to identify the family's mutation.
- 1 IU of a factor is the amount in 1 mL of normal plasma (so normal plasma has 100 IU/dL = 100%).

| Severity | Factor activity | Bleeding pattern |
|---|---|---|
| **Severe** | under 1% | Often spontaneous |
| **Moderate** | 1 to 5% | Usually after mild trauma |
| **Mild** | over 5% | Needs significant trauma; may not be diagnosed for years; usually no spontaneous bleeds; prolonged bleeding after dental work, surgery or moderate trauma |

### 2.3 Clinical features

- Neither VIII nor IX crosses the placenta, so bleeding can occur in the fetus or from birth.
- Newborn: intracranial haemorrhage in only 2%; **30% of male infants bleed after circumcision**. Without family history it may go unrecognised in the newborn.
- Easy bruising, intramuscular haematomas and **haemarthroses** start when the child begins to cruise. Torn frenulum or minor mouth lacerations can bleed for hours or days. Even in severe disease, only about 90% show increased bleeding by 1 year.
- **Haemarthrosis is the hallmark.** Minor trauma or spontaneous. First joint bleeds are usually in the **ankle**; in older children and adolescents, **knees and elbows**. Young children are recognised only after major swelling; older children report a **warm, tingling sensation** as the first sign.
- Repeated bleeds into one joint produce a **target joint**, where bleeding then becomes spontaneous.
- **Muscle haemorrhage:** pain and swelling. **Iliopsoas bleed** can lose large volumes (near hypovolaemic shock) with only vague groin pain; the hip is held **flexed and internally rotated**, with inability to extend the hip. Confirm with ultrasound or CT.
- **Life-threatening bleeding:** into the CNS or upper airway, or exsanguination (external trauma, GI or iliopsoas bleeds).

### 2.4 Laboratory findings and diagnosis

- **Prolonged aPTT** (in severe disease usually 2 to 3 times the upper limit). **Platelet count, bleeding time, PT and thrombin time are normal.**
- Confirm with **specific factor VIII and IX assays**.
- In 25 to 35% of those receiving factor infusions, an **inhibitor** (antibody against the active site) develops. Measure its titre by the **Bethesda assay**.

### 2.5 Differential diagnosis (infant with severe bleeding)

Severe thrombocytopenia; severe platelet function disorders (**Bernard-Soulier syndrome, Glanzmann thrombasthenia**); **type 3 VWD**; **vitamin K deficiency**. Also consider non-accidental injury in a child with bruising.

### 2.6 Management

**Replacement therapy**

- "Early, appropriate therapy is the hallmark of excellent hemophilia care."
- Mild to moderate bleeding: raise factor VIII or IX to **35 to 50%**.
- Major or life-threatening bleeding: aim for **100%**. In suspected significant **head trauma, give factor before imaging**.
- With recombinant products available, **prophylaxis** is the standard of care for most children with severe haemophilia, to prevent spontaneous bleeding and early joint damage.

**Prophylaxis**

- Considered optimal for severe haemophilia. Usually given every **2 to 3 days** to keep a trough level of **1 to 2%**. Long-acting factor IX products allow weekly or fortnightly dosing.
- If primary prophylaxis was not given, start **secondary prophylaxis** when a target joint develops.

**Other agents**

- **Desmopressin (DDAVP):** releases stored endogenous factor VIII in **mild** haemophilia A. Ineffective in moderate or severe haemophilia A (inadequate stores) and in haemophilia B. A concentrated intranasal form is used (not the enuresis or pituitary-replacement dose): **150 µg (1 spray) if under 50 kg, 300 µg (2 sprays) if over 50 kg**. Most centres do a trial dose to measure the factor VIII response.
- **Antifibrinolytics** (aminocaproic acid, tranexamic acid) for mucosal bleeding.
- **Emicizumab:** bispecific humanised monoclonal antibody bridging activated factor IX and X, mimicking factor VIII; once-weekly subcutaneous prophylaxis, works with or without inhibitors.
- **Gene therapy** (factor IX) in early trials with encouraging results.

**Supportive care**

- Anticipatory guidance: car seats, seatbelts, bicycle helmets; avoid high-risk behaviour. Older boys should avoid violent contact sports (hard in practice; boys with severe disease bleed without known trauma). Early psychosocial support helps the family balance overprotection and permissiveness.
- **Avoid aspirin and NSAIDs.**
- **Hepatitis B vaccination.** If plasma-derived products were used, screen periodically for hepatitis B, C, HIV and liver function.
- Care through a **comprehensive haemophilia centre** (physicians, nurses, orthopaedic surgeons, physiotherapists, psychosocial workers). Education is crucial, since children on prophylaxis may be less skilled at recognising bleeds.

> **Remember:** in Kenya, factor concentrates are limited. First-line options in a bleed also include cryoprecipitate (factor VIII, VWF, fibrinogen) or fresh frozen plasma (factor IX, XI) when concentrates are unavailable; give RICE and avoid IM injections.

### 2.7 Chronic complications

**Chronic arthropathy** is the major long-term disability.

- Repeated bleeds into a target joint; the young joint distends easily until tamponade or treatment.
- After a joint bleed, white cells release proteolytic enzymes and **haem iron induces macrophage proliferation**, causing synovitis. The synovium thickens and develops frond-like projections that are pinched and bleed again.
- Cartilage erodes, even exposing bone, and the joint may fuse. In advanced arthropathy, bleeds into a thickened synovium cause severe pain.
- When a target joint is developing, give short or long-term prophylaxis.

**Inhibitors**

- Antibodies against factor VIII or IX that block clotting. **Failure of a bleed to respond to appropriate replacement is usually the first sign.**
- Highly purified or recombinant factor IX seems to raise the inhibitor frequency, and some anti-factor IX inhibitors cause **anaphylaxis**.
- Many patients lose the inhibitor with continued infusions. Others go through **immune tolerance induction** (high doses of the factor to saturate the antibody and induce tolerance). Factor IX tolerance programmes have caused **nephrotic syndrome** in some.
- Rituximab, corticosteroids and other immunosuppressants are alternatives when tolerance fails.
- If tolerance fails, treat bleeds with **recombinant factor VIIa** or **activated prothrombin complex concentrates (FEIBA)**, which bypass the inhibitor but increase the risk of thrombosis. Emicizumab is another option. Low-titre inhibitors may respond to high-dose factor VIII.
- Refer to a centre with a comprehensive programme.

**Infection:** transfusion-transmitted hepatitis B and C and HIV (reduced with recombinant products).

## 3. Factor XI deficiency (haemophilia C)

- **Autosomal** deficiency, mild to moderate bleeding. Frequent in Ashkenazi Jews (1 to 3 per 1,000 homozygous in Israel) but found in many ethnic groups. Bleeding is milder than in VIII or IX deficiency and **does not correlate with the factor XI level**; some with severe deficiency have little bleeding at major surgery.
- Factor XI augments thrombin generation and activates **thrombin-activatable fibrinolysis inhibitor (TAFI)**, so surgical bleeding is worse at sites of high fibrinolysis such as the **mouth**.
- aPTT in homozygotes is often **longer** than in severe haemophilia A or B. Confirm with a factor XI assay.
- **Treatment:** no approved concentrate, so use **fresh frozen plasma (FFP)** pre-operatively unless previous surgery was uneventful. Plasma 1 IU/kg raises the level by 2%; **10 to 15 mL/kg gives 20 to 30%**, enough for moderate bleeding. Half-life is 48 hours or more, so levels are easy to maintain. Minor surgery: local pressure. Dental extractions: **tranexamic or aminocaproic acid**, plasma only if bleeding.
- Chronic joint bleeding is rare; concern is mainly at major surgery, unless there is a second defect (e.g. VWD).

## 4. von Willebrand disease (VWD)

### 4.1 Overview and pathophysiology

- **Most common inherited bleeding disorder**; prevalence 1 in 100 to 1 in 10,000 depending on diagnostic criteria. Presents typically with **mucosal bleeding**.
- Caused by defect or deficiency of **von Willebrand factor (VWF)**, a large multimeric glycoprotein stored in endothelial cells (Weibel-Palade bodies) and platelets.
- **Functions of VWF:** (1) tethers platelets to injured subendothelium by binding collagen and platelet **glycoprotein Ib (GPIb)**, a shear-dependent step needing **high-molecular-weight (HMW) multimers**; (2) **carrier of factor VIII**, protecting it from degradation (so low VWF means low factor VIII).

### 4.2 Clinical features

- Mucosal bleeding like a platelet disorder: **epistaxis, easy bruising, menorrhagia**; surgical bleeding (dental extractions, adenotonsillectomy). Symptoms do not always correlate with VWF levels.
- **Type 3** can have joint bleeds and CNS haemorrhage, resembling mild haemophilia.
- Most have a family history. Men and women are equally affected, but women are diagnosed more often because of menorrhagia.
- Minor bruising and nosebleeds are common in normal children. Significant unexplained bruising in infants and toddlers is more often **non-accidental injury** than a bleeding disorder.

### 4.3 Classification

| Type | Defect | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | Mild to moderate **quantitative** reduction | 60 to 80% of cases. Mucosal bleeding, bruising, surgical bleeding. **Type 1C**: increased VWF clearance; desmopressin gives only a brief rise |
| **2A** | Qualitative: defective multimerisation, **lack of HMW multimers**, reduced platelet-binding activity | Commonest type 2 (about 10% of VWD); more severe than type 1. Desmopressin may help minor bleeds; major bleeds need VWF concentrate |
| **2B** | **Gain of function**: increased platelet binding, so increased clearance of VWF and platelets, loss of HMW multimers | Thrombocytopenia (may appear only with stress, surgery, pregnancy). Increased response to low-dose **ristocetin** aggregation. **Desmopressin relatively contraindicated** |
| **2M** | Decreased activity with **normal multimers** (defect binding GPIb or collagen) | Minor bleeds may respond to desmopressin; usually needs VWF concentrate |
| **2N** | Defective binding of **factor VIII** | Low FVIII; may be **misdiagnosed as mild haemophilia**; suspect when FVIII is low with no family history of FVIII deficiency |
| **3** | **Severe quantitative**: VWF completely absent | About 1 per million; symptoms like mild haemophilia; very low FVIII (under 10 IU/dL); needs VWF concentrate; consider prophylaxis |
| Platelet-type pseudo-VWD | Platelet GPIb mutation causes spontaneous VWF binding | Looks like 2B; needs special tests; treated with platelet transfusion |

- NHLBI guideline: VWD diagnosed when **VWF:Ag is below 30 IU/dL** (most likely a genetic defect). VWF:Ag 30 to 50 IU/dL is "low VWF".
- VWF levels vary: **lower in blood group O**; **raised by stress, exercise and pregnancy**; lowered by hypothyroidism and valproic acid. A single normal level does not exclude VWD, so repeat testing may be needed.

### 4.4 Laboratory diagnosis

- **No reliable screening test.** aPTT may be prolonged if FVIII is low but is often normal, especially in type 1. Platelet function analysis and bleeding time are unreliable.
- Use a **panel**: **VWF antigen (VWF:Ag)** (total protein), **VWF activity** (ristocetin cofactor, VWF:RCo), **factor VIII activity**, VWF:GPIbM (not always available), collagen binding, and **multimer analysis** (HMW multimers). Low-dose ristocetin aggregation for 2B.

### 4.5 Treatment

- Depends on type and reason for treatment.
- **Type 1:** **desmopressin** (releases stored VWF). Exceptions: the rare non-responder, and type 1C (rapid clearance).
- **Types 2 and 3:** **VWF-containing concentrates** (like haemophilia treatment). Monitor VWF and FVIII for surgery and major trauma.
- **Adjuncts:** antifibrinolytics (tranexamic acid) for oral surgery, hormonal therapy for menorrhagia, local measures for epistaxis (cautery, packing), and **iron** for iron deficiency anaemia.
- Avoid aspirin and NSAIDs.

> **Exam tip:** haemophilia gives deep bleeds (joints, muscles) with long aPTT; VWD and platelet disorders give mucosal and skin bleeds. In VWD, both VWF and factor VIII are reduced, so the aPTT may be long.

## 5. Comparison

| Feature | Haemophilia A/B | VWD | Factor XI deficiency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inheritance | X-linked recessive | Autosomal (mostly dominant) | Autosomal |
| Bleeding site | Joints, muscles | Mucosal | Post-surgical, mouth |
| aPTT | Prolonged | Normal or prolonged | Often markedly prolonged |
| PT | Normal | Normal | Normal |
| Platelets | Normal | Normal (low in 2B) | Normal |
| Treatment | Factor VIII/IX, emicizumab | Desmopressin or VWF concentrate | FFP, tranexamic acid |

## Key points to revise

1. Procoagulant deficiency causes bleeding; anticoagulant deficiency causes thrombosis; sepsis and liver disease unbalance both.
2. PT = extrinsic/common (normal in VIII, IX, XI, XII deficiency); aPTT = intrinsic/common; contact factor deficiency prolongs aPTT without bleeding.
3. Haemophilia A and B: X-linked, 1 in 5,000 males, 30% new mutation, haemarthrosis, iliopsoas bleed, long aPTT with normal PT and platelets.
4. Severity: under 1% severe, 1 to 5% moderate, over 5% mild.
5. Target levels: 35 to 50% for minor or moderate bleeds, 100% for life-threatening; factor before imaging in head injury; prophylaxis for severe disease.
6. Desmopressin only for mild haemophilia A and type 1 VWD; emicizumab and bypassing agents for inhibitors.
7. Complications: arthropathy, inhibitors (25 to 35%), transfusion-transmitted infection.
8. VWD: commonest inherited bleeding disorder; type 1 most common; type 2B desmopressin contraindicated; types 2 and 3 need VWF concentrate.
9. Factor XI deficiency: mild, bleeding does not match factor level; FFP and tranexamic acid.
