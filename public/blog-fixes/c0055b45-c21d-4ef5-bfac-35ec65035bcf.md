# Acute Rheumatic Fever

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Cardiology (Rheumatic disease)**

> **In one line:** acute rheumatic fever (ARF) is a delayed autoimmune reaction, 2 to 4 weeks after **group A beta-haemolytic streptococcal pharyngitis**, in which antibodies against streptococcal antigens cross-react with heart, joint, skin and brain tissue (**molecular mimicry**). It causes migratory polyarthritis, carditis, chorea, nodules and rash, and its lasting harm is **rheumatic heart disease** (mainly mitral valve scarring).

## What you must know

- ARF follows **group A streptococcal (GAS) pharyngitis** (not skin infection in the classical model) after a latent period of about **3 to 4 weeks**.
- Mechanism: **antigenic mimicry**. Anti-M protein antibodies cross-react with myocardial sarcolemma, valve glycoprotein, neuronal tissue (caudate and subthalamic nuclei) and joint cartilage.
- Major features: **carditis, migratory polyarthritis, Sydenham's chorea, erythema marginatum, subcutaneous nodules**.
- Diagnosis uses the **modified Jones criteria**: 2 major, or 1 major + 2 minor, **plus evidence of preceding GAS infection**.
- Only the **heart** suffers permanent damage. Joints, skin and brain recover completely.
- **Aschoff body** is the pathognomonic lesion; the **mitral valve** is most often affected, then aortic.
- Treat: eradicate streptococci (penicillin), salicylates for arthritis and mild carditis, **prednisolone** for severe carditis, then **long-term secondary prophylaxis with benzathine penicillin**.

## 1. Definition

- Rheumatic fever (RhF) is an **inflammatory disease** that occurs as a **delayed sequel** to pharyngeal infection by **Lancefield group A beta-haemolytic streptococcus**, after a latent period of about **3 to 4 weeks**.
- The pharyngitis is often purulent.
- **M serotypes 3, 5, 18, 19 and 24** are frequently associated with ARF. Not all strains in these serotypes are rheumatogenic.
- The rheumatic process is an inflammatory reaction involving multiple organs: **joints, skin, brain and heart**.
- Usual acute manifestations: **migratory polyarthritis, fever and carditis**. Others: **Sydenham's chorea, subcutaneous nodules, erythema marginatum**.
- Joint, skin and brain involvement is normally **self-limiting**. Cardiac involvement can cause severe, life-threatening acute inflammation, or chronic disease with permanent scarring and dysfunction.

## 2. Epidemiology

- Untreated GAS pharyngitis precipitates ARF in **2 to 3%** of individuals with no previous attack (they have genetic predisposition).
- In people who have already had ARF the recurrence risk after another GAS infection is about **50%**.
- About **one third** of ARF cases follow mild or nearly asymptomatic pharyngitis. Some patients with rheumatic heart disease (RHD) give no history of pharyngitis.
- **Age:** first attacks occur between **6 and 15 years** in developed countries; in developing countries they occur earlier, from about **4 years**.
- Increased incidence in young adults at risk of streptococcal pharyngitis, such as **military recruits** and parents of school-age children.
- **No sex predilection** (chorea is commoner in girls, and mitral stenosis in women).
- Ethnic differences: higher in the Maori population of New Zealand; in the USA commoner in African and Hispanic Americans than in Caucasians.
- Incidence in the developed world was 0.5 to 3.1 per 100,000 at the turn of the century. In developing countries **mortality from RHD is about 10 times higher**.
- Contributing factors: **overcrowding** in homes and schools, poor availability of health care, and poor health-seeking behaviour.
- Sub-Saharan Africa, including Kenya, still carries a large RHD burden.

### Host susceptibility

- Genetic susceptibility is thought to be mediated by a **single recessive gene**.
- A specific **B-cell alloantigen** (identified by a monoclonal antibody) is found in **99%** of patients with RF compared with **14%** of controls.
- High incidence of **class II HLA** antigens in patients with RF.

## 3. Pathogenesis

- Tissue injury is an **immune reaction**, not direct bacterial invasion.
- **Antigenic mimicry:** antibodies made against streptococci cross-react with host tissue.

| Streptococcal antigen | Cross-reacts with |
|---|---|
| **M protein** | Myocardial sarcolemma |
| Protoplast membrane antigen | Sarcolemmal membrane of myocardium |
| Streptococcal glycoprotein | **Valvular glycoprotein** |
| Streptococcal protoplast | Neuronal membrane of **subthalamic and caudate nuclei** (chorea) |
| Hyaluronate of the capsule | **Articular cartilage** |

- Kaplan and colleagues showed that group A streptococci share antigenic determinants with myocardium.
- T-cell (mainly CD4) response also contributes to valve damage.

## 4. Pathology

- The inflammation involves **connective (collagen) tissue**. Most tissues can be involved, but clinically important disease is in joints, heart and brain.

### Skin

- The rash reflects **small-vessel vasculitis** with endothelial proliferation.

### Subcutaneous nodules

- Rare in acute RF; seen more in **chronic valvular disease such as mitral stenosis**.
- Central **fibrinoid necrosis** surrounded by epithelioid and mononuclear cells, similar to an Aschoff body.

### Heart (pancarditis)

- In acute RF the process involves **endocardium and myocardium**; the **pericardium** may be involved in severe myocarditis. Involvement of all three layers is **pancarditis**.
- Histology is not specific, and the degree of change does **not** correlate with clinical severity. Early on, with prominent cardiac dilatation, histology may be minimal though function is severely impaired, even fatally.
- Sequence of change:
  1. **Oedema** of tissues, followed by a cellular infiltrate of lymphocytes (mainly **CD4**) and plasma cells
  2. **Fibrinoid** (eosinophilic granular material from degenerating collagen, fibrin, globulin and other substances)
  3. Fibrinoid necrosis may also be seen in joint tissue
  4. **Aschoff body** formation: a perivascular infiltrate of large cells with polymorphous nuclei and basophilic cytoplasm arranged in a rosette around a fibrinoid centre. It is **pathognomonic of rheumatic carditis** and is commonly found in the interventricular septum, left ventricular wall and left atrial appendage.
- Aschoff bodies are characteristic of **chronic** rheumatic disease rather than the acute stage.

> **Check:** "Aschoff cells" with abundant cytoplasm and "caterpillar" (Anitschkow) nuclei are the typical histological cells. Aschoff bodies appear weeks after the acute attack, which is why they are found in chronic disease.

### Valves (endocarditis)

- Affects valvular tissue and mitral endocardium. **Mitral valve** involvement is commonest; **aortic** is second and much less frequent; **pulmonary** is rarely involved. Tricuspid is occasionally affected.
- Healing leads to fibrosis, leaflet thickening, commissural fusion and shortened chordae, producing stenosis and/or regurgitation.

## 5. Clinical features

### Carditis

- Manifested by **heart murmurs**. Severe cases show **pericarditis and heart failure**.
- Death from heart failure can occur in the acute phase, and permanent valve damage may follow.
- Range: from **fulminating fatal** disease to **low-grade subclinical** inflammation.
- Mild carditis is often missed until years later, when the patient presents with RHD and there is no history of RF.
- Signs: **tachycardia (out of proportion to fever), cardiomegaly, hepatomegaly, changing heart murmurs**.
- Typical murmurs: apical pansystolic (mitral regurgitation), **Carey Coombs** mid-diastolic murmur, and early diastolic murmur of aortic regurgitation. A **pericardial rub** indicates pericarditis.

### Arthritis

- Classic attack: **acute migratory polyarthritis with fever**.
- **Large joints** of upper and lower limbs (knees, ankles, elbows, wrists) are affected in a migratory manner; sometimes several joints are inflamed together.
- Usually a **high ESR**.
- Very painful but heals completely, and responds dramatically to aspirin.

### Subcutaneous nodules

- Small, **pea-sized, painless** swellings over bony prominences; often unnoticed.
- Sites: extensor tendons of hands and feet, elbows, margins of patellae, scalp, scapulae, vertebrae.
- Strongly associated with severe carditis.

### Erythema marginatum

- Erythematous rash with **clear centres** and round or serpiginous (snake-like) margins.
- On the **trunk and proximal limbs, never the face**.
- **Transient and migratory**, non-pruritic, not indurated.

### Sydenham's chorea (St Vitus' dance)

- CNS disorder: sudden, aimless, irregular movements, often with muscle weakness and emotional instability.
- Often a **late manifestation**, appearing long after other features have subsided.
- Onset gradual; symptoms **disappear during sleep**.
- Signs: milk-maid grip, darting tongue, handwriting deterioration.

## 6. Diagnosis: modified Jones criteria (1992)

Evidence of **preceding group A streptococcal infection** is needed: positive throat culture or rapid antigen test, or raised/rising **ASO** or anti-DNase B titre, or recent scarlet fever.

**Two major, or one major plus two minor** manifestations indicate a high probability of ARF, if supported by evidence of preceding GAS infection.

| Major criteria | Minor criteria |
|---|---|
| **Carditis** | Fever |
| **Polyarthritis** | Arthralgia (not if arthritis is the major criterion) |
| **Chorea** | Raised ESR or CRP |
| **Erythema marginatum** | Prolonged **PR interval** (not if carditis is the major criterion) |
| **Subcutaneous nodules** | History of previous ARF or RHD |

> **Exam tip:** chorea and indolent carditis may be diagnosed without proof of recent streptococcal infection. Arthralgia cannot be used as a minor criterion if arthritis is already counted as major, and a prolonged PR interval cannot be used if carditis is counted as major.

### Supporting investigations

- Throat swab culture, **ASO titre** (peaks at 3 to 6 weeks), anti-DNase B
- ESR and CRP raised; FBC (leucocytosis, normochromic anaemia)
- **ECG** (prolonged PR, other conduction block); chest X-ray (cardiomegaly); **echocardiography** with Doppler (valve regurgitation, effusion, subclinical carditis)
- Blood cultures to exclude infective endocarditis if fever persists

## 7. Differential diagnosis

- Septic arthritis, juvenile idiopathic arthritis, reactive arthritis, sickle cell crisis, SLE, infective endocarditis, viral myocarditis, Lyme disease; for chorea: Wilson's disease, SLE, thyrotoxicosis, drugs.

## 8. Management

### Principles

- Admit; **bed rest** during active carditis with slow, graded resumption of activity.
- **Eradicate the streptococcus** (see primary prevention doses below).

### Carditis

| Severity | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Mild to moderate** | **Salicylates 90 to 100 mg/kg/day**, keeping serum levels at 25 to 30 mg/dL; treat for **4 to 6 weeks**; when improved, withdraw gradually over 2 weeks |
| **Severe (with heart failure)** | **Prednisone 2 mg/kg/day** in the acute period, withdrawn gradually over **2 to 3 weeks**; start a therapeutic dose of salicylates **1 week before** stopping steroids (prevents rebound) |
| Heart failure | **Digitalis**: digitalising dose 0.02 to 0.03 mg/kg (total digitalising dose, TDD), then maintenance of **one quarter TDD** in two divided doses daily. Add **furosemide (Lasix)** and **spironolactone (Aldactone)** if response is inadequate |

> **Check:** in modern practice an ACE inhibitor is also used in heart failure, and digoxin is used cautiously in active carditis because the inflamed myocardium is sensitive to it.

### Arthritis

- **Aspirin 50 to 70 mg/kg/day** for **2 months**, then withdraw gradually over 2 to 3 weeks.
- A fall in ESR parallels clinical response.

### Sydenham's chorea

- Mild: bed rest in a quiet room and avoidance of stress.
- Severe: anticonvulsants or neuroleptics:
  - **Phenobarbitone** 3 to 8 mg/kg/day
  - **Haloperidol** 0.5 mg/kg/day in 8-hourly doses (may increase up to 2 mg 8-hourly)
  - **Sodium valproate** 15 to 20 mg/kg/day

> **Check:** haloperidol is normally dosed at about 0.01 to 0.03 mg/kg/day in children; the slide figure of 0.5 mg/kg/day is very high and likely means 0.5 mg per dose. Confirm against a paediatric formulary.

## 9. Prevention

### Primary prevention (eradicate streptococci in pharyngitis)

| Drug | Dose |
|---|---|
| **Benzathine penicillin** | 600,000 to 1.2 million units IM, single dose |
| **Penicillin V** | 250 mg three times daily for **10 days** |
| **Erythromycin** | 20 to 40 mg/kg/day for 10 days (for penicillin allergy) |
| Cefpodoxime | 10 mg/kg/24 hours once daily for 5 days |
| Azithromycin | 12 mg/kg/day once daily (usually 5 days) |

### Secondary prevention (prophylaxis against recurrence)

- **Benzathine penicillin G every 3 to 4 weeks**: **600,000 IU if under 27 kg**, **1.2 million units if over 27 kg**.
- Alternatives: penicillin V, sulfadiazine, erythromycin.
- Duration (standard guidance): at least 5 years after the last attack or until age 21 (whichever is longer) without carditis; 10 years or until age 21-40 with carditis but no residual valve disease; lifelong for severe valve disease or after valve surgery.
- **Health education** of parents, caretakers and child on adherence is essential.

## 10. Complications and prognosis

- Acute heart failure and death in fulminant carditis.
- **Chronic rheumatic heart disease:** mitral stenosis and regurgitation, aortic valve disease, atrial fibrillation, thromboembolism, infective endocarditis, pulmonary hypertension.
- Recurrences worsen valve damage, so adherence to prophylaxis determines outcome.

## Key points to revise

1. ARF = autoimmune sequel of **group A strep pharyngitis** after 3 to 4 weeks; antigenic mimicry (M protein with myocardium).
2. Only 2 to 3% of untreated pharyngitis causes ARF; recurrence risk about 50%.
3. Major: carditis, polyarthritis, chorea, erythema marginatum, nodules. **2 major or 1 major + 2 minor + evidence of GAS**.
4. Aschoff body is pathognomonic; mitral valve most affected, then aortic.
5. Arthritis is migratory, large-joint, and responds to aspirin; chorea appears late and disappears in sleep.
6. Mild carditis: aspirin 90 to 100 mg/kg/day; severe: prednisone 2 mg/kg/day plus heart failure treatment.
7. Primary prevention: benzathine penicillin or 10 days of penicillin V; secondary prevention: benzathine penicillin every 3 to 4 weeks.
8. Crowding and poor access to care explain high RHD mortality in developing countries.
