## GLOMERULAR DISEASES

## Section 1: Classification & Histologic Patterns

### Classification of Glomerular Diseases

Glomerular diseases are broadly divided into three groups:

**1. Primary Glomerulopathies** — kidney is the principal organ involved
- Acute proliferative glomerulonephritis (postinfectious and other)
- Rapidly progressive (crescentic) glomerulonephritis
- Membranous nephropathy
- Minimal change disease
- Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS)
- Membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis (MPGN)
- Dense deposit disease
- IgA nephropathy

**2. Systemic Diseases with Glomerular Involvement**
- Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)
- Diabetes mellitus
- Amyloidosis
- Goodpasture syndrome
- Microscopic polyangiitis / Granulomatosis with polyangiitis
- Henoch-Schönlein purpura

**3. Hereditary Disorders**
- Alport syndrome
- Thin basement membrane nephropathy
- Fabry disease

### Histologic Distribution — Key Terms

When a biopsy is reported, changes are described by their distribution:

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Diffuse | All glomeruli in the kidney are involved |
| Global | The entire individual glomerulus is involved |
| Focal | Only a fraction of glomeruli are involved |
| Segmental | Only part of an individual glomerulus is affected |
| Mesangial | Changes predominantly in the mesangium |
| Capillary loop | Changes predominantly in the capillary loops |

**Example:** In focal segmental glomerulosclerosis — only some glomeruli are affected (focal), and within those, only part of the tuft is sclerosed (segmental).

### Four Basic Pathologic Responses to Glomerular Injury

These are the fundamental tissue reactions seen across all glomerular diseases.

**1. Hypercellularity**

An increase in cell number within the glomerular tuft. Occurs via:
- Proliferation of mesangial or endothelial cells
- Leukocyte infiltration — neutrophils, monocytes, lymphocytes
- Endocapillary proliferation — combined leukocyte infiltration + swelling and proliferation of mesangial and/or endothelial cells
- Crescent formation — the most severe form

**Crescents — important:**
- Composed of proliferating parietal glomerular epithelial cells + infiltrating leukocytes
- Form in the urinary (Bowman's) space
- Triggered by immune/inflammatory injury to capillary walls
- Plasma proteins leak → tissue factor exposure → fibrin deposition → thrombin activation → crescent formation
- Multiple proinflammatory cytokines recruit leukocytes into crescents
- Hallmark of rapidly progressive GN (RPGN)

**2. Basement Membrane Thickening**

Seen as thickened capillary walls on light microscopy — best visualized with PAS stain. Three mechanisms on electron microscopy:
- Deposition of electron-dense material (immune complexes, fibrin, amyloid, cryoglobulins) on the endothelial or epithelial side, or within the GBM itself
- Increased synthesis of basement membrane proteins — as in diabetic glomerulosclerosis
- Duplication or additional layers of basement membrane matrix in subendothelial locations — as in MPGN

**3. Hyalinosis**
- Accumulation of homogeneous, eosinophilic, extracellular material (hyalin) seen on light microscopy
- Hyalin = plasma proteins that have leaked from the circulation into glomerular structures
- When extensive, obliterates capillary lumens
- Consequence of endothelial or capillary wall injury
- Represents the end result of various forms of glomerular damage

**4. Sclerosis**
- Deposition of extracellular collagenous matrix
- May be confined to mesangial areas (e.g., diabetic glomerulosclerosis), involve capillary loops, or both
- Can obliterate capillary lumens in affected glomeruli
- Represents irreversible damage — the final common pathway of glomerular injury

### Clinical Syndromes — Overview

| Syndrome | Key Manifestations |
|---|---|
| Nephritic syndrome | Haematuria, red cell casts, subnephrotic proteinuria, oliguria, oedema, hypertension, azotemia |
| Rapidly progressive GN | Acute nephritis + rapid loss of renal function over days to weeks |
| Nephrotic syndrome | Proteinuria >3.5 g/day, hypoalbuminemia, oedema, hyperlipidaemia, lipiduria |
| Chronic kidney disease | Azotemia progressing to uraemia over months to years |
| Isolated urinary abnormalities | Asymptomatic haematuria and/or subnephrotic proteinuria |

### Progression of Glomerular Disease

- Podocyte injury is a common final pathway across many glomerular diseases
- Injury triggers a cycle: glomerular loss → compensatory hypertrophy of remaining nephrons → further injury → glomerulosclerosis → ESRD
- Progressive glomerular injury is almost always accompanied by tubulointerstitial fibrosis

## Section 2: Pathogenesis of Glomerular Injury

### Overview

Immune mechanisms underlie most primary glomerulopathies and many secondary glomerular disorders. The three main pathways are:

1. Antibody-mediated injury
2. Cell-mediated immune injury
3. Activation of the alternative complement pathway

Cell-mediated immune reactions usually occur in concert with antibody-mediated events, not in isolation.

### A. In Situ Immune Complex Formation

Antibodies react directly with antigens already present in or deposited within the glomerulus.

**1. Fixed Intrinsic Tissue Antigens** — normally part of the glomerulus
- NC1 domain of type IV collagen — target in anti-GBM nephritis (Goodpasture syndrome)
- PLA2R (phospholipase A2 receptor) on podocytes — main target in primary membranous nephropathy (~75% of cases)
- THSD7A (thrombospondin type 1 domain containing 7A) — less common target in membranous nephropathy
- Mesangial antigens

**2. Planted Antigens** — foreign antigens lodge in the glomerulus first, then antibodies form against them
- Cationic molecules binding to anionic components of the GBM
- DNA, nucleosomes, nuclear proteins (seen in SLE)
- Bacterial products
- Large aggregated proteins e.g. aggregated immunoglobulins
- Exogenous antigens: infectious agents, drugs

Immunofluorescence pattern: **Granular** — reflecting the localized, discrete nature of antigen-antibody interactions

### B. Circulating Immune Complex Deposition

- Antigen-antibody complexes form in the circulation and are then trapped in the glomerulus
- Antibodies have no immunologic specificity for glomerular constituents
- Localization depends on physicochemical properties of the complexes and haemodynamic factors peculiar to the glomerulus

**Antigens involved:**

Endogenous:
- DNA, nuclear antigens (SLE)
- Tumour antigens
- IgA (IgA nephropathy)

Exogenous (infectious):
- Streptococcal proteins
- Hepatitis B surface antigen
- Hepatitis C virus antigens
- Treponema pallidum, Plasmodium falciparum
- Various other viruses

**Important:** A single agent can cause more than one pattern. Hepatitis B and C can cause either membranous GN (subepithelial in situ deposition) or MPGN (subendothelial/circulating complex deposition) depending on where the antigen-antibody interaction predominantly occurs.

### Anti-GBM Antibody Disease — Special Case

- Antibodies target the NC1 domain of type IV collagen, uniformly distributed along the entire GBM
- Immunofluorescence pattern: **Linear** — distinguishes it from all immune complex diseases
- Accounts for less than 5% of human glomerulonephritis
- Causes severe necrotizing and crescentic glomerular damage
- Clinical presentation: RPGN
- When combined with pulmonary haemorrhage: **Goodpasture syndrome**

### C. Cell-Mediated Immunity

- T lymphocytes and macrophages cause glomerular injury independent of antibodies
- Usually occurs alongside antibody-mediated events
- Relevant in diseases where immune complex deposition alone cannot fully explain the injury — e.g., minimal change disease, some forms of FSGS

### D. Activation of the Alternative Complement Pathway

- Occurs in dense deposit disease and C3 glomerulopathies
- Characterized by:
  - Decreased serum C3
  - Normal C1 and C4 (classical pathway not activated)
  - Decreased Factor B and properdin
  - Glomerular deposits of C3 and properdin but not IgG
- Often driven by **C3 nephritic factor (C3NeF)** — an autoantibody that stabilizes the alternative pathway C3 convertase, causing uncontrolled C3 consumption *(high-yield, frequently examined)*

### Mediators of Glomerular Injury

**Cells:**

| Cell | Role |
|---|---|
| Neutrophils | Release proteases and oxidants — acute injury |
| Monocytes/Macrophages | Release cytokines, growth factors, proteases |
| T lymphocytes | Drive cell-mediated injury |
| NK cells | Cytotoxic injury |
| Platelets | Release eicosanoids and growth factors |
| Mesangial cells | Proliferate and release mediators when activated |

**Molecules:**
- Oxidants, cytokines, chemokines
- Proteases, growth factors
- Eicosanoids, nitric oxide
- Complement fragments: C5a (chemotactic) and C5b-9 (membrane attack complex — direct cell lysis)

### Immunofluorescence Patterns — High Yield Summary

| Pattern | Meaning | Example Disease |
|---|---|---|
| Linear IgG along GBM | Anti-GBM antibodies | Goodpasture syndrome |
| Granular subepithelial | In situ immune complexes | Membranous nephropathy |
| Granular subendothelial/mesangial | Circulating immune complex deposition | MPGN type I, lupus nephritis |
| C3 only, no IgG | Alternative complement pathway activation | Dense deposit disease |
| Negative | Podocyte injury, no immune deposits | Minimal change disease |

## Section 3: Nephritic Syndrome — Overview, Acute Postinfectious GN & Crescentic GN

### Nephritic Syndrome — Overview

Nephritic syndrome results from **inflammation within the glomeruli**, causing injury to capillary walls.

**Classic features:**
- Haematuria — red blood cells and red cell casts in urine (hallmark)
- Proteinuria — usually subnephrotic range (less than 3.5 g/day)
- Azotemia — elevated blood urea and creatinine
- Hypertension
- Oliguria and oedema

**Pathophysiology:**
- Inflammatory injury damages capillary walls → blood enters urine (haematuria)
- Capillary injury + haemodynamic changes → reduced GFR → oliguria and azotemia
- Fluid retention + renin release from ischaemic kidneys → hypertension

**Diseases presenting with nephritic syndrome:**
- Postinfectious GN
- Crescentic (rapidly progressive) GN
- Proliferative lupus nephritis
- IgA nephropathy
- Henoch-Schönlein purpura

**Common histologic finding:** Proliferation of cells within glomeruli, often with leukocytic infiltrate — proliferative glomerulonephritis

### Acute Postinfectious Glomerulonephritis

**Definition:** Glomerulonephritis occurring after infection, caused by deposition of immune complexes — most commonly following Group A beta-haemolytic Streptococcus infection.

**Epidemiology:**
- Typically affects children and young adults
- Follows pharyngitis (nephritogenic strains: types 12, 4, 1) or impetigo (type 49)
- Can follow other organisms: Staphylococcus, pneumococcus, some viruses, Plasmodium falciparum
- Latent period: 1–3 weeks after pharyngitis; 3–6 weeks after skin infection

**Pathogenesis:**
- Deposition of immune complexes (streptococcal antigens + antibodies) mainly in the subepithelial spaces
- Complement activation → neutrophil recruitment → glomerular injury
- Planted antigen mechanism also plays a role

**Morphology:**

Light microscopy:
- Diffuse endocapillary proliferation — all glomeruli affected
- Prominent neutrophil and monocyte infiltration
- Glomeruli appear enlarged and hypercellular

Immunofluorescence:
- Granular deposits of IgG and C3 in GBM and mesangium
- Granular IgA in some cases
- "Starry sky" pattern

Electron microscopy:
- Subepithelial electron-dense deposits — **"humps"** (pathognomonic)
- Subendothelial deposits may also be seen in early disease

**Clinical features:**
- Sudden onset haematuria (cola/tea-coloured urine), oliguria, oedema, hypertension
- Elevated ASO titre (anti-streptolysin O) and anti-DNase B
- Low serum C3 (complement consumed)
- Normal C1 and C4 (alternative pathway activation predominantly)

**Prognosis:**
- Children: excellent — >95% recover fully
- Adults: worse prognosis — some progress to chronic renal insufficiency
- Microscopic haematuria may persist for months after recovery

### Crescentic (Rapidly Progressive) Glomerulonephritis

**Definition:** A clinical syndrome characterized by features of nephritic syndrome + rapid, progressive loss of renal function — often leading to renal failure within weeks to months if untreated.

**Pathology:** Defined by the presence of crescents in more than 50–60% of glomeruli on biopsy. Also called crescentic GN.

**Crescent formation:**
- Triggered by severe injury causing breaks in the GBM
- Parietal epithelial cells of Bowman's capsule proliferate
- Leukocytes infiltrate the urinary space
- Fibrin deposition occurs in the urinary space
- Crescents compress and eventually destroy the glomerular tuft
- Over time, cellular crescents become fibrocellular then fibrous (irreversible)

**Classification — Three Types Based on Pathogenesis:**

| Type | Mechanism | Immunofluorescence | Key Association |
|---|---|---|---|
| Type I | Anti-GBM antibodies | Linear IgG + C3 | Goodpasture syndrome |
| Type II | Immune complex deposition | Granular IgG/IgM + C3 | SLE, postinfectious GN, IgA nephropathy |
| Type III | Pauci-immune (ANCA-mediated) | Negative or minimal | Microscopic polyangiitis, Granulomatosis with polyangiitis |

**ANCA (Anti-Neutrophil Cytoplasmic Antibodies):**
- Most patients with Type III RPGN have circulating ANCAs
- p-ANCA (anti-MPO) — associated with microscopic polyangiitis
- c-ANCA (anti-PR3) — associated with granulomatosis with polyangiitis
- ANCAs activate neutrophils → endothelial injury → necrotizing vasculitis → crescents

**Morphology:**

Light microscopy:
- Extracapillary proliferation with crescents
- Glomerular necrosis and GBM breaks
- Fibrin within crescents

Immunofluorescence: varies by type (see table above)

Electron microscopy:
- Type I: no deposits
- Type II: immune complexes at various locations
- Type III: no deposits

**Clinical features:**
- Haematuria with red cell casts
- Proteinuria (variable)
- Rapidly declining GFR — creatinine rises over days to weeks
- Oliguria progressing to anuria
- Hypertension, oedema

**Prognosis:**
- Poor if untreated — most progress to ESRD
- Early aggressive treatment (high-dose steroids + cyclophosphamide ± plasmapheresis) can preserve renal function
- Plasmapheresis particularly useful in Type I (to remove circulating anti-GBM antibodies) and in ANCA-associated disease with pulmonary haemorrhage

## Section 4: Nephrotic Syndrome — Overview, Membranous Nephropathy & Minimal Change Disease

### Nephrotic Syndrome — Overview

Nephrotic syndrome results from increased glomerular capillary permeability to plasma proteins.

**Diagnostic criteria (all four features):**
- Massive proteinuria — more than 3.5 g/day in adults (less in children)
- Hypoalbuminemia — serum albumin less than 3 g/dL
- Generalised oedema
- Hyperlipidaemia and lipiduria

**Pathophysiology of each feature:**
- Proteinuria → loss of albumin → hypoalbuminemia
- Hypoalbuminemia → reduced plasma oncotic pressure → oedema
- Hypoalbuminemia → stimulates hepatic lipoprotein synthesis → hyperlipidaemia
- Excess lipoproteins filtered → lipiduria (oval fat bodies and fatty casts in urine)

**Additional complications not in your notes but frequently examined:**
- Hypercoagulable state — loss of antithrombin III, protein C and S in urine → risk of renal vein thrombosis, DVT, pulmonary embolism (especially in membranous nephropathy)
- Increased infection risk — loss of immunoglobulins in urine + oedema
- Vitamin D deficiency — loss of vitamin D-binding protein

**Causes by age group:**

| Age Group | Most Common Cause |
|---|---|
| Children (2–6 years) | Minimal change disease (75%) |
| Adults | Membranous nephropathy (30%), FSGS (35%) |

### Membranous Nephropathy

**Definition:** Diffuse thickening of the glomerular capillary wall due to accumulation of immune complex deposits containing immunoglobulin along the **subepithelial** side of the GBM.

**Epidemiology:**
- Most common cause of nephrotic syndrome in adults (non-diabetic)
- Peak incidence: 30–50 years
- Male predominance

**Classification:**
- Primary (idiopathic): ~75% of cases — autoimmune
- Secondary: ~25% — associated with identifiable causes

**Causes of Secondary Membranous Nephropathy:**
- Drugs: penicillamine, captopril, gold, NSAIDs
- Malignancy: lung, colon, melanoma — membranous nephropathy in older adults should always prompt malignancy workup
- SLE: 10–15% of lupus GN
- Infections: chronic hepatitis B, hepatitis C, syphilis, schistosomiasis, malaria
- Other autoimmune: thyroiditis

**Pathogenesis:**
- Autoimmune response directed against **PLA2R (phospholipase A2 receptor)** on podocytes — majority of primary cases
- Less commonly against **THSD7A**
- Antibodies form granular subepithelial immune deposits → complement activation (especially C5b-9 membrane attack complex) → podocyte injury → loss of foot processes → proteinuria
- Little or no inflammatory cell infiltrate — distinguishes it from nephritic diseases
- Disease is often **resistant to steroid therapy**

**Morphology:**

Light microscopy:
- Early disease (Stage 1): glomeruli may appear entirely normal
- Later: diffuse thickening of glomerular capillary walls
- Subepithelial spike formation on PAS or Jones silver stain (GBM material growing between deposits)
- No significant hypercellularity

Immunofluorescence:

- Finely granular IgG (predominantly IgG4) and C3
- Uniform subepithelial distribution in all glomeruli

Electron microscopy — 4 stages:
1. Scattered electron-dense deposits on epithelial side of GBM
2. Subepithelial deposits with GBM spikes between deposits
3. Deposits surrounded by GBM material (intramembranous)
4. Electron-lucent areas — resorption of prior immune complexes

**Clinical Features:**
- Nephrotic syndrome (main presentation)
- Less frequently: microscopic haematuria, hypertension
- Thrombotic and thromboembolic events — particularly renal vein thrombosis (important complication)
- PLA2R antibody titre correlates with disease activity

**Prognosis — Rule of Thirds:**
- One-third: spontaneous complete remission
- One-third: remain stable with persistent proteinuria, little progression
- One-third: progressive decline to ESRD

**Adverse prognostic factors:**
- High PLA2R antibody titre
- Nephrotic-range proteinuria persisting beyond 6 months
- Male sex, advanced age
- Renal insufficiency at presentation

**Recurrence after transplantation:** 10–30%

### Minimal Change Disease

**Definition:** A relatively benign disorder characterized by diffuse effacement (fusion) of foot processes of podocytes detectable only on electron microscopy, with glomeruli appearing **completely normal on light microscopy**.

**Epidemiology:**
- Most frequent cause of nephrotic syndrome in children
- Peak incidence: 2–6 years of age
- Less common in adults but still occurs
- Often follows a respiratory infection or routine immunization

**Aetiology:**
- Usually idiopathic, especially in children
- Secondary causes: viruses, drugs (NSAIDs — often with concurrent acute interstitial nephritis), allergic reactions, immunologic diseases, neoplasia
- Associated with **Hodgkin lymphoma** in adults — important association
- Rarely familial: gene mutations in NPHS2 (podocin), dysferlin

**Pathogenesis:**
- Not fully understood
- Thought to involve T-cell dysfunction → release of a circulating permeability factor → loss of glomerular polyanion (negative charge on GBM) → loss of charge-selective barrier → albumin leaks through
- No immune complex deposition
- The primary defect is podocyte injury causing foot process effacement

**Morphology:**

Light microscopy:
- Glomeruli appear entirely normal
- Lipid droplets in tubular cells (lipoid nephrosis — old name for this disease)

Immunofluorescence:
- Negative — no immune deposits

Electron microscopy:
- Diffuse effacement (fusion) of podocyte foot processes
- No electron-dense deposits
- This is the diagnostic finding

**Clinical Features:**
- Massive proteinuria — highly selective (predominantly albumin)
- No haematuria, no hypertension (distinguishes it from nephritic diseases)
- Renal function remains normal despite heavy proteinuria
- Dramatic response to corticosteroid therapy — characteristic feature

**Response to steroids:**
- More than 90% of children respond rapidly
- Adults are slower to respond but long-term prognosis also excellent
- Steroid-dependent or steroid-resistant cases occur
- Disease often resolves when children reach puberty

**Prognosis:** Excellent overall, even with relapses

### Comparison — Membranous Nephropathy vs Minimal Change Disease

| Feature | Membranous Nephropathy | Minimal Change Disease |
|---|---|---|
| Age | Adults | Children (2–6 yrs) |
| Light microscopy | GBM thickening, spikes | Normal |
| Immunofluorescence | Granular IgG4, C3 subepithelial | Negative |
| Electron microscopy | Subepithelial deposits | Foot process effacement only |
| Steroid response | Often resistant | Excellent (>90% children) |
| Proteinuria selectivity | Non-selective | Highly selective (albumin) |
| Thrombosis risk | High | Low |
| Prognosis | Rule of thirds | Excellent |
