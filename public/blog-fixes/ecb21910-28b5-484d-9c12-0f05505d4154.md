## An Overview
### Introduction
- The term "ameba" originates from the Greek word *amibe*, meaning "change."
- Amebae are structurally simple protozoans without a fixed shape.
- **Taxonomic Classification**:**Phylum**: Sarcomastigophora
- **Subphylum**: Sarcodina
- **Superclass**: Rhizopoda
- **Order**: Amebida

### Structure
- The cytoplasm is enclosed by a membrane and differentiated into: **Ectoplasm**: The outer layer.
- **Endoplasm**: The inner layer.

- **Pseudopodia**: Formed by thrusting out ectoplasm followed by endoplasm.
- Function in locomotion and food capture (phagocytosis).

### Reproduction
- Primarily occurs by: **Fission**: Splitting into two.
- **Budding**: A smaller outgrowth develops and detaches.

- **Cysts**: Formed under unfavorable conditions.
- Serve as the infective stage for vertebrate hosts (e.g., *Entamoeba histolytica*).

### Classification
Amebae are categorized into:

- **Intestinal Amebae**: Found in the alimentary canal.
- Includes species like:*Entamoeba histolytica* (pathogenic)
- *Entamoeba dispar*
- *Entamoeba coli*
- *Entamoeba polecki*
- *Entamoeba hartmanni*
- *Entamoeba gingivalis*
- *Endolimax nana*
- *Iodamoeba butschlii*

- **Note**: Only *Entamoeba histolytica* is pathogenic.

- **Free-Living Amebae**: Opportunistic pathogens that can cause infections.
- Includes species like:*Naegleria fowleri*
- *Acanthamoeba spp.*
- *Balamuthia mandrillaris*

## Entamoeba histolytica
### History and Discovery
- Discovered in 1875 by Losch in St. Petersburg, Russia, in dysenteric feces.
- Key developments:1890: William Osler reported dysentery leading to liver abscess.
- 1891: Councilman and Lafleur introduced the terms "amebic dysentery" and "amebic liver abscess" and studied its pathogenesis.

### Distribution
- Worldwide prevalence, more common in tropical regions.
- Found in poor sanitation areas, across all climates:From Alaska (61°N) to the Straits of Magellan (52°S).

- Infection statistics:~10% of the global population infected.
- ~50% prevalence in developing countries.
- ~1% prevalence in affluent countries like the USA.

- **Clinical Impact**: 80–99% of infections are asymptomatic.
- Invasive amebiasis:Affects 50 million people annually.
- Causes 50,000 deaths yearly, especially in tropical regions (Asia, Africa, Latin America).

- Third leading parasitic cause of death after malaria and schistosomiasis.

### Epidemiology in India
India can be divided into three zones based on prevalence:

- **High Prevalence (>30%)**: Chandigarh, Tamil Nadu, Maharashtra.

- **Moderate Prevalence (10–30%)**: Punjab, Rajasthan, Uttar Pradesh, Delhi, Bihar, Assam, West Bengal, Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Kerala.

- **Low Prevalence (<10%)**: Haryana, Gujarat, Himachal Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Odisha, Sikkim, Puducherry.

## Morphology of *Entamoeba histolytica*
*Entamoeba histolytica* occurs in three distinct forms:

- **Trophozoite**
- **Precystic stage**
- **Cystic stage**

### 1. Trophozoite Stage
- **Definition**: The trophozoite is the vegetative, actively growing stage of the parasite and the only form found in tissues.
- **Characteristics**:**Shape and Size**: Irregular shape; size ranges from **12–60 µm**, with an average of **20 µm**.
- Actively motile in freshly passed dysenteric stools, while smaller in convalescent and carrier individuals.
- When present as a commensal in the intestinal lumen, the trophozoite is smaller (~15–20 µm) and is called the *minuta* form.

- **Cytoplasm**:**Ectoplasm**: Clear, transparent, and refractile.
- **Endoplasm**: Granular with a "ground glass" appearance. Contains:Nucleus
- Food vacuoles
- Ingested erythrocytes (diagnostic feature)
- Occasionally leukocytes and tissue debris.

- **Pseudopodia**: Finger-like projections formed by sudden jerky movements of ectoplasm followed by streaming of endoplasm.
- Enable ameboid motility (crawling or gliding movement). Movement requires attachment to a surface or particle.
- Inhibited at low temperatures.

- **Nucleus**: Spherical, **4–6 µm** in size.
- Contains: **Central karyosome** surrounded by a clear halo.
- Fine radiating fibrils (Linin network), giving a cartwheel appearance.

- Nuclear membrane is lined with evenly distributed chromatin granules.
- Best visualized in stained preparations (e.g., iron hematoxylin).

- **Additional Features**: Trophozoites divide by **binary fission** every 8 hours.
- Trophozoites from acute dysenteric stools contain phagocytosed erythrocytes, a diagnostic feature not seen in other intestinal amebae.
- Survival:Up to **5 hours at 37°C**.
- Killed by drying, heat, and chemical sterilization.
- Non-infective stage: Rapidly destroyed in the stomach if ingested.

### 2. Precystic Stage
- **Definition**: Transitional stage as the trophozoite prepares for encystment in the intestinal lumen.
- **Characteristics**: The trophozoite extrudes its food vacuoles and becomes **round or oval** (~10–20 µm in size).
- Contains:A **large glycogen vacuole**.
- **Two chromatoid bodies**: Cigar-shaped, refractile rods with rounded ends.

- Encystment occurs only in the intestinal lumen and **not in tissues or outside the host body**.

### 3. Cystic Stage
- **Definition**: Infective, non-motile, and resistant form of the parasite.
- **Characteristics**:**Shape and Size**: Spherical; size ranges from **10–20 µm**.

- **Structures**:**Early Cyst**: Single nucleus.
- Contains:A **mass of glycogen**.
- **1–4 chromatoid bodies** (stainable with hematoxylin).

- Chromatoid bodies disappear as the cyst matures.
- Glycogen mass is prominent in the early stage but also disappears in mature cysts.

- **Mature Cyst**: Quadrinucleate (contains four nuclei).
- Highly refractile cyst wall provides resistance to gastric juice and environmental conditions.

- **Staining**:**Iron Hematoxylin**: Nuclear chromatin and chromatoid bodies stain deep blue/black.
- Glycogen mass remains unstained.

- **Iodine Stain**: Glycogen mass stains golden brown.
- Nuclear chromatin and karyosome stain bright yellow.
- Chromatoid bodies appear as clear spaces (unstained).

### Diagnostic Importance of Stages
- **Trophozoite**: Found in dysenteric stools.
- Phagocytosed erythrocytes are diagnostic for *E. histolytica*.
- Non-infective stage due to destruction by gastric juices.

- **Cyst**: Found in formed stools.
- Infective stage due to its resistance to stomach acid and environmental conditions.
- Essential for transmission of the infection.

### Life Cycle of *Entamoeba histolytica*
The life cycle of *Entamoeba histolytica* is simple, involving a single host—humans—and includes both infective and non-infective stages. The following is a detailed account of its life cycle:

### 1. Infective Form
- The infective form is the **mature quadrinucleate cyst**, which is passed in the feces of infected individuals, including convalescents and asymptomatic carriers.
- **Survival**: Cysts remain viable for up to **10 days** under moist conditions.
- The cyst wall is resistant to environmental conditions, including gastric juice.

### 2. Mode of Transmission
- Infection occurs by ingestion of cysts via: **Contaminated food and water.**
- **Poor hygiene** (e.g., unwashed hands, contaminated surfaces).

- The cyst wall is resistant to gastric juices, allowing cysts to pass through the stomach undamaged.

### **3. Excystation** (Occurs in the Small Intestine)
- When cysts reach the **cecum** or **lower ileum**, the **alkaline medium** and digestive enzymes (e.g., trypsin) weaken the cyst wall.
- The cyst wall is ruptured, and the **quadrinucleate ameba** is released, termed the **metacyst**.

### 4. Formation of Metacystic Trophozoites
- Inside the metacyst:Each nucleus undergoes division to form **eight nuclei**.
- Each nucleus is surrounded by cytoplasm, forming **eight small amebulae** or **metacystic trophozoites**.

- These trophozoites are carried to the **large intestine**, as they do not colonize the small intestine.

### 5. Colonization of the Large Intestine
- **Optimal Habitat**: The metacystic trophozoites lodge in the **submucosal tissue of the cecum and colon**, particularly in glandular crypts.

- **Growth and Multiplication**: The trophozoites grow and multiply by **binary fission**.

### 6. Differentiation into Cystic Forms
- Some trophozoites differentiate into: **Precystic forms**: Begin to round off and lose food vacuoles.
- **Mature cysts**: Develop a cyst wall and are passed in the feces.

### Summary of the Life Cycle
- The life cycle is **completed in one host**, as the cysts are excreted and ingested by the same or a new host, perpetuating the cycle.

### Pathogenicity and Carriage
- **Asymptomatic Carriers**: In most cases, *E. histolytica* remains a **commensal** in the large intestine without causing illness.
- Asymptomatic individuals pass infective cysts, contributing to the spread of infection in the community.

- **Pathogenic Potential**: Under certain conditions, *E. histolytica* becomes invasive, causing: **Intestinal disease** (e.g., amebic dysentery).
- **Extraintestinal disease** (e.g., liver abscess).

### Flowchart of the Life Cycle
- **Cysts ingested** from contaminated food and water.
- **Excystation** in the small intestine: Quadrinucleate metacyst is released.
- **Division into eight metacystic trophozoites**.
- Trophozoites are transported to the large intestine:Lodge in **cecum and colon**, where they grow and multiply.
- Some transform into cysts, which are passed in feces.

- **Cysts contaminate food and water**, continuing the cycle.

### Key Points of the Life Cycle
- **Direct host-to-host transmission**: The entire cycle occurs in humans, making sanitation a critical control measure.
- **Environmental resilience**: The cyst stage ensures survival and transmission in unfavorable conditions.
- **Asymptomatic carriers**: A major source of infection, especially in communities with poor hygiene.

### Pathogenesis and Clinical Features of *Entamoeba histolytica*

### Pathogenesis
*Entamoeba histolytica* can cause both **intestinal** and **extraintestinal amebiasis**. Its pathogenicity depends on the virulence of the strain, host factors, and the local environment in the intestine.

#### Key Steps in Pathogenesis
- **Adherence**: Mediated by amebic lectins such as Gal/GalNAc lectin (260 kDa protein).
- The trophozoites adhere to glycogen receptors of colonic epithelial cells.

- **Invasion and Cytolysis**: Trophozoites invade the intestinal mucosa, particularly the crypts of Lieberkühn, using: **Cysteine proteases**: Degrade extracellular matrix (ECM) and inactivate complement factor C3.
- **Amebapores**: Ionophore proteins that lyse host cell membranes.

- Trophozoites cause **tissue necrosis**, aided by host inflammatory response, lysosomal enzymes, and pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-8 and TNF-α).

- **Ulcer Formation**: Initial mucosal penetration produces **pinhead-sized ulcers**.
- Deeper invasion forms **flask-shaped ulcers** with a narrow neck and broad base.
- Ulcers are confined to the colon, predominantly in the **cecum** and **sigmoidorectal region**.

- **Complications**: Necrosis and abscess formation in the submucosa.
- In severe cases, penetration into deeper layers can cause:Perforation and peritonitis.
- Erosion of blood vessels leading to hemorrhage.

### Clinical Features
#### 1. Intestinal Amebiasis
- **Incubation Period**: Variable, ranging from **4 days to 4 months**.
- **Spectrum**: Ranges from asymptomatic carriage to fulminant colitis.

#### Typical Features
- **Amebic Dysentery**:**Onset**: Gradual.
- **Symptoms**:**Abdominal pain**: Localized tenderness (usually in the lower abdomen).
- **Stool**: Large, offensive, brownish-black with blood-streaked mucus.
- **Microscopy**: Clumped, reddish-brown RBCs.
- Presence of trophozoites containing ingested RBCs.
- **Charcot-Leyden crystals** (a sign of eosinophilic response).

- **Fever**: Generally absent.
- **Toxicity**: Mild or absent.

- **Chronic Infection**: Recurrent diarrhea, abdominal discomfort, and gradual weight loss.
- Chronic cecal involvement can mimic appendicitis.

#### Complications
- **Ameboma**: Pseudotumoral growth in the intestinal wall.
- Common in the **cecum** and **rectosigmoid junction**.
- May mimic a malignant tumor.

- **Fulminant Colitis**: Extensive necrosis, perforation, and toxic megacolon.
- Associated with fever, toxicity, and generalized peritonitis.

#### 2. Extraintestinal Amebiasis
- Occurs when trophozoites disseminate via blood.
- Common sites: **Liver**: Amebic liver abscess (common in the right lobe).
- **Lungs**: Pulmonary amebiasis (secondary to liver abscess rupture).
- **Cerebral, splenic, or cutaneous** involvement (rare).

### Differentiation Between Amebic and Bacillary Dysentery

### Key Notes
- **Asymptomatic Carriers**: Account for 90% of infections.
- Serve as reservoirs for transmission.

- **Virulence Factors**: Cysteine proteases, lectins, amebapores, and interaction with host factors (e.g., stress, malnutrition, and immunodeficiency).

- **Common Misdiagnosis**: Ameboma may be confused with malignancies.
- Chronic intestinal amebiasis can mimic appendicitis or other inflammatory conditions.

### Comprehensive Notes on Extraintestinal Amebiasis
#### Overview
Extraintestinal amebiasis occurs when *Entamoeba histolytica* spreads beyond the intestinal lumen to other organs, primarily through hematogenous dissemination. While intestinal invasion by trophozoites is necessary for extraintestinal spread, only a small percentage of individuals with intestinal amebiasis develop extraintestinal complications.

### Pathogenesis and Sites Affected
- **Spread of Infection**: Trophozoites invade colonic mucosa and reach the liver via the portal circulation.
- Further dissemination can occur through hematogenous routes, direct extension, or lymphatic spread.

- **Major Sites of Infection**:**Liver** (most common site)
- **Lungs** (often secondary to liver involvement)
- **Skin** (perianal and abscess-related)
- **Peritoneum** (due to rupture of abscess)
- **Brain**, **Kidneys**, **Spleen**, and **Adrenals** (rare hematogenous spread)
- **Genitourinary System** (direct extension)

### Hepatic Amebiasis
- **Amebic Hepatitis**: Occurs due to repeated trophozoite invasion or toxic substances reaching the liver.
- Liver enlargement and tenderness occur without significant fever or impairment of liver function.

- **Amebic Liver Abscess**: Found in 5-10% of cases with intestinal amebiasis.
- Characteristics:Most often solitary and located in the **right lobe of the liver**.
- Contains "anchovy sauce" pus—necrotic liver tissue that is bacteriologically sterile and free of ameba.
- Surrounded by almost normal liver tissue with invading trophozoites at the periphery.

- Clinical Presentation: **Painful hepatomegaly** is the hallmark feature.
- Fever, anorexia, weight loss, nausea, and fatigue are common.
- **Jaundice** occurs in severe cases with multiple abscesses or biliary compression.
- Leukocytosis (>10,000/µL) and elevated serum transaminases may be observed.

- **Complications**: Rupture into adjacent structures: **Diaphragm** → Lung or pleural cavity.
- **Pericardium**, **peritoneum**, **stomach**, **intestine**, or **inferior vena cava**.
- Skin via abdominal wall, forming discharging sinuses.

### Pulmonary Amebiasis
- **Pathogenesis**: Occurs due to extension of liver abscess through the diaphragm or rarely via direct hematogenous spread.

- **Common Features**: Affects the **lower part of the right lung**.
- Formation of hepatobronchial fistulas may result in expectoration of chocolate-brown sputum.
- Other manifestations include pleuritic chest pain, dyspnea, and nonproductive cough.

- **Complications**: Amebic empyema (accumulation of pus in the pleural cavity).

### Metastatic Amebiasis
- **Hematogenous Spread**: Leads to abscesses in distant organs like the brain, spleen, kidneys, and adrenal glands.
- **Cerebral Amebiasis**: Severe tissue destruction, often fatal.

- **Renal and Adrenal Lesions**: Rare, associated with systemic spread.

### Cutaneous Amebiasis
- **Direct Extension**: Seen around **anus**, **colostomy sites**, or through **discharging sinuses** from liver abscesses.

- **Clinical Features**: Presents as extensive gangrenous skin destruction.
- Often mistaken for condyloma or epithelioma.

### Genitourinary Amebiasis
- **Penile Amebiasis**: Acquired through anal intercourse.
- Lesions are ulcerative and destructive, resembling carcinoma.

- **Female Genital Lesions**: Affects the vulva, vagina, or cervix via extension from the perineum.

### Laboratory Diagnosis
- **Diagnosis of Amebic Liver Abscess**:**Microscopy**: Examination of aspirate from the abscess shows necrotic debris and few or no amebae.

- **Histopathology**: Amebae seen at the periphery of the lesion.

- **Serology**:**Indirect Hemagglutination Assay (IHA)**, **ELISA**, and **latex agglutination tests** are highly sensitive.

- **Imaging**:**Ultrasound**: Detects hypoechoic lesions.
- **CT and MRI**: Useful for detailed lesion assessment.
- **X-ray**: Shows elevated diaphragm or pleural effusion in complicated cases.

- **Diagnosis of Intestinal Amebiasis**:**Stool Examination**:**Macroscopic**: Foul-smelling, semi-liquid stool with blood and mucus.
- **Microscopy**:**Fresh wet mount** for motile trophozoites containing RBCs.
- **Iodine-stained preparation** for cysts.
- Trichrome stain highlights nuclear and cytoplasmic details.

- **Charcot-Leyden crystals** may be observed.

- **Stool Culture**: Specialized media like **Boeck and Drbohlav’s** or **Robinson’s media**.

- **Molecular Methods**: DNA probes and PCR to detect *E. histolytica* specifically.

### Summary of Clinical Features
- **Hepatic**: Painful hepatomegaly, fever, anorexia, jaundice in severe cases.

- **Pulmonary**: Chest pain, dyspnea, chocolate-colored sputum.

- **Cutaneous**: Gangrenous lesions around discharging sinuses.

- **Genitourinary**: Ulcerative lesions resembling carcinoma.

### Management
- **Pharmacologic Therapy**:**Metronidazole** is the drug of choice for both intestinal and extraintestinal amebiasis.
- **Tinidazole** and **chloroquine** are alternatives in resistant cases.

- **Surgical Management**: Indicated for large abscesses threatening to rupture or causing compressive symptoms.
- Drainage procedures include percutaneous aspiration.

### Complications
- **Hepatic Abscess Rupture**: Into peritoneum, pleura, or pericardium.

- **Pulmonary Complications**: Empyema, bronchopleural fistula.

- **Cerebral Amebiasis**: Rapidly fatal if untreated.

- **Skin and Genitourinary Lesions**: Can lead to extensive tissue destruction.

### Extraintestinal Amebiasis

#### Introduction
Extraintestinal amebiasis is a condition where *Entamoeba histolytica* extends beyond the intestinal tract, leading to systemic complications. These are summarized in **Flow Chart 2** and **Figure 4**.

#### Sites of Involvement in Extraintestinal Amebiasis
The spread occurs through **portal circulation**, resulting in lesions in multiple organs:

- **Liver**: Most common site.
- **Lungs**: Extension through the diaphragm.
- **Peritoneum**: Secondary to rupture of liver abscess.
- **Skin, perianal regions, and genitals**: Through direct extension or sinus drainage.
- **Other distant organs**: Brain, kidney, spleen, and adrenal glands via hematogenous or lymphatic spread.

#### Hepatic Amebiasis
#### A. **Acute Hepatic Involvement (Amebic Hepatitis)**
- Caused by repeated invasion of amebae or release of toxins from the colon.
- Symptoms:Enlarged, tender liver.
- Absence of fever in most cases.
- Liver damage attributed to lysosomal enzymes and cytokines, not directly to *E. histolytica*.

#### B. **Amebic Liver Abscess**
- Occurs in 5–10% of intestinal amebiasis cases.
- Features: **Content**: Central necrotic tissue resembling "anchovy sauce pus" (sterile, without amebae).
- **Location**: Right lobe of the liver (more common).
- **Symptoms**: Painful hepatomegaly.
- Fever, anorexia, nausea, weight loss, fatigue.
- Leukocytosis (>10,000/μL) and elevated transaminases in most cases.
- Jaundice in severe cases with biliary tract involvement.

- **Complications**: Rupture into the peritoneum, pleural cavity, pericardium, or externally.
- Rupture into the diaphragm may cause hepatobronchial fistula.

#### Pulmonary Amebiasis
- Often secondary to liver abscess rupture through the diaphragm.
- Symptoms:Severe pleuritic chest pain.
- Dyspnea and nonproductive cough.
- Chocolate-brown sputum (from hepatobronchial fistula).

- Less commonly, direct hematogenous spread from the colon bypassing the liver.

#### Metastatic Amebiasis
- Rare systemic involvement of organs: **Kidney, brain, spleen, adrenal glands**.
- Brain involvement leads to fatal destruction of tissue.

#### Cutaneous and Genitourinary Amebiasis
- **Cutaneous amebiasis**: Localized near anus, colostomy sites, or discharging sinuses.
- Lesions: Gangrenous, resembling carcinoma.

- **Genitourinary amebiasis**:**Penile lesions**: Acquired through anal intercourse.
- **Female lesions**: On vulva, vagina, or cervix via perineal spread.

#### Laboratory Diagnosis
#### A. **Intestinal Amebiasis**
- **Stool Examination**:**Macroscopic**: Semiliquid, brown-black stool with blood and mucus.
- **Microscopic**: Trophozoites with ingested RBCs (diagnostic for *E. histolytica*).
- Cysts with chromatoid bars, central karyosomes.

- **Stained preparations**: Iodine-staining shows nuclear and cytoplasmic details.
- Trichrome stains enhance visibility of intracellular structures.

- **Mucosal Scraping**: Obtained by sigmoidoscopy.
- Stained with iron hematoxylin or immunofluorescent stains.

- **Stool Culture**: Polyxenic cultures: Media include Boeck and Drbohlav, Robinson's, Craig’s.
- Axenic culture (Diamond’s medium): For pathogenicity studies and drug sensitivity.

#### B. **Extraintestinal Amebiasis**
- **Microscopy**: Pus aspirates from liver abscess show trophozoites in <20% of cases.
- Trophozoites found at the periphery of abscess; cysts are absent.

- **Liver Biopsy**: Demonstrates trophozoites in tissue.

- **Serodiagnosis**: Detects antibodies in late stages of intestinal and extraintestinal amebiasis.
- Tests include IHA, ELISA, latex agglutination, and counter-current immunoelectrophoresis.
- **Antigen detection**: Detects lipophosphoglycan (LPG), serine-rich protein (SREHP).
- Stool antigen detection by ELISA is sensitive and specific.

- **Molecular Diagnosis**: DNA probes and real-time PCR (RT-PCR) detect *E. histolytica* in stool and pus.

- **Radiological Tests**:**X-ray**: Elevated right diaphragm.
- **Ultrasound, CT, MRI**: Detect space-occupying lesions in the liver.

- **Radioisotope Scan**: Locates liver lesions.

#### Key Diagnostic Features
- **Amebic Liver Abscess**: Positive imaging (USG/CT) for liver lesions.
- Positive serology for *E. histolytica* antibodies.

- **Intestinal Amebiasis**: Identification of trophozoites with ingested RBCs in stool or mucosal scrapings.
- Positive stool culture or ELISA for coproantigen.

#### Conclusion
Diagnosis of extraintestinal amebiasis relies on a combination of imaging, serological, and molecular tests, along with clinical correlation. Early detection and treatment are crucial to prevent severe complications like abscess rupture or systemic spread.
