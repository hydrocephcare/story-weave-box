# Complications of Diabetes Mellitus

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Endocrinology**

> **In one line:** chronic hyperglycaemia damages small and large vessels through non-enzymatic glycosylation and the polyol (sorbitol) pathway, producing neuropathy, retinopathy, nephropathy, accelerated atherosclerosis, the diabetic foot and infections; acute complications are hypoglycaemia, diabetic ketoacidosis and hyperosmolar non-ketotic coma.

## What you must know

- Type 1 diabetes shortens life expectancy by **11 to 13 years**.
- Two key mechanisms: **non-enzymatic glycosylation** (advanced glycation end products, HbA1c) and the **polyol pathway** (sorbitol and fructose accumulation).
- Order of frequency (morbidity): **neuropathy, retinopathy, nephropathy, vasculopathy**; the order of mortality is the reverse.
- **Retinopathy** is the commonest cause of blindness in adults aged 30 to 65; **nephropathy** is the commonest cause of ESRD; the **diabetic foot** is the commonest cause of non-traumatic amputation; **coronary artery disease** is the commonest cause of death.
- **Microalbuminuria** is the screening test and the stage at which treatment can still prevent nephropathy; start **ACE inhibitors or ARBs** early.
- Macular oedema is **not** seen with an ophthalmoscope or retinal photograph alone.
- DKA: fluids first, insulin switches off ketogenesis. HONK: elderly type 2 patient, fluid loss about 8 L.
- Infection impairs control: insulin doses may need to rise by up to **25%**.

## 1. General pathophysiology

| Mechanism | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Non-enzymatic glycosylation** | Glucose binds proteins (as in HbA1c); advanced glycated end products accumulate, causing inflammation and injury |
| **Polyol pathway** | Glucose is converted via **aldose reductase** to **sorbitol and fructose**, altering vascular permeability and cell structure (as in the lens, nerve and retina) |

## 2. Acute complications

| Complication | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Hypoglycaemia** | Palpitations, confusion, loss of consciousness |
| **Hyperglycaemia, type 1** | **DKA** and hypovolaemia |
| **Hyperglycaemia, type 2** | **Hyperosmolar non-ketotic coma (HONK)** |
| Fluctuating vision | Osmotic changes in the lens with poor control |

### Lecturer's points

**Hypoglycaemia**

- Patients usually die from the **consequences** (crash while driving, drowning while swimming) rather than the low glucose itself. Prolonged hypoglycaemia causes brain damage.
- Cases cited: an 18-year-old died asphyxiated after a poorly managed attack at school; another drowsy patient was mistaken for being drunk, went untreated and developed DKA.
- Management: keep sugar at hand; educate the family **not to feed** an unconscious patient (aspiration) and to use **glucagon** instead.

**DKA**

- Fluid loss can be **4 to 8 L** (of about 12 L total body fluid in the slides' words). The **most important step is IV fluids** to restore volume; **insulin mainly shuts down ketogenesis**. Coma is from severe hypovolaemia and acidosis.

**HONK**

- Elderly type 2 patients; higher mortality; fluid loss up to **8 L**; precipitated by heart failure or diuretics. Severe hyperglycaemia and hyperosmolarity. Demyelination and brain oedema can follow mismanagement.
- MCQ rule given: **normal saline for DKA, half-normal for HONK.**

> **Check:** the slide says "severe hyponatraemia" in HONK; the problem is hypernatraemia and hyperosmolality (measured sodium may look normal or low because of the high glucose). Textbook guidance differs on fluids: Kumar advises starting **0.9% saline** and avoiding 0.45% because rapid dilution risks cerebral oedema; Davidson switches to 0.45% only if osmolality is rising despite 0.9% saline. The lecturer emphasised 0.45% saline.

## 3. Chronic complications

### 3.1 Vasculopathy

- **Microangiopathy:** thickened capillary **basement membrane** with increased vascular permeability throughout the body. It underlies retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy and accelerated atherosclerosis.
- **Macrovascular disease:** coronary artery disease, stroke and peripheral arterial disease. Three-vessel coronary disease is common, but **autonomic denervation of the heart means angina is often absent** (silent MI).
- **Coronary artery disease is the commonest cause of death** in diabetics.
- Glycation of antibodies reduces immunity, so infections are more frequent.

### 3.2 Neuropathy (most common complication, about 40%)

**Pathophysiology:** neurons are not insulin dependent, but the **Schwann cells** are. With insulin deficiency or resistance they use the **polyol pathway**, making **sorbitol**, which is viscous and draws water into the cell. The Schwann cell swells, compresses the axon and may rupture. Early pain is a good sign (the axon is still salvageable with good control); once sensation is lost the damage is irreversible.

| Type | Features |
|---|---|
| **Symmetrical sensory polyneuropathy** ("glove and stocking") | Starts distally. **Loss of vibration sense first**, numbness, paraesthesia, pain worse in the lower limbs; weakness and wasting (especially interossei) |
| **Diabetic amyotrophy** | Painful, usually asymmetrical wasting of **quadriceps and shoulder** muscles; resolves with time and good control |
| **Mononeuropathies** | Severe, rapid onset, then recovery. Cranial nerve palsies, especially **III and VI**; compression palsies are common |
| **Autonomic neuropathy** | See table below |

| System | Autonomic features |
|---|---|
| Cardiovascular | Postural hypotension, resting tachycardia, fixed heart rate |
| Gastrointestinal | Oesophageal atony (dysphagia), delayed gastric emptying (nausea, vomiting, early fullness), colonic atony (constipation), nocturnal diarrhoea, faecal incontinence |
| Genitourinary | Bladder atony with incontinence and recurrent UTIs, erectile dysfunction |
| Pupillary | Small pupils with slow light reflexes |

> **Exam tip:** patients with neuropathy may burn themselves without noticing (e.g. feet against a heater) or have a foreign body in a shoe unnoticed; always inspect the feet.

### 3.3 Nephropathy (about 20%)

**Pathophysiology**

1. Hyperglycaemia raises GFR and causes glomerular hypertrophy.
2. Afferent arteriolar vasodilation raises filtration further.
3. High intraglomerular pressure damages capillaries and hypertrophies mesangial cells.
4. Persistent poor control leads to **glomerulosclerosis** and progressive leakage of protein into the urine.

**Course:** hyperglycaemia → raised GFR → **microalbuminuria** → proteinuria → falling GFR → **ESRD**.

- **Microalbuminuria** is the screening test; it is the "rate-limiting step", and nephropathy follows if it is not managed.
- Once frank proteinuria is present, glucose control changes the course little.
- Start **ACE inhibitors or ARBs** (they reduce intraglomerular pressure and slow progression).
- Retinopathy and nephropathy nearly always coexist, so screen for one when the other is found.

### 3.4 Eye disease (retinopathy about 30%)

**Cataract**

- Protein denaturation in the lens makes it opaque. Can be acute in poor control with ketosis.
- **Glucose fluctuations** change lens osmotic state and cause fluctuating blurred vision (reading well in the morning but not in the evening, or the reverse); resolves with good control.

**Diabetic retinopathy**

- Damage to the retina; the **commonest cause of blindness in adults aged 30 to 65**. Retinal cells have a high energy demand.

| Stage and lesion | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Microaneurysms** | Damage to small-vessel walls in the peripheral retina |
| Haemorrhages and **hard exudates** | Rupture and leakage of protein and lipid; exudates cleared by macrophages |
| **Cotton-wool spots** | Micro-infarcts from blocked vessels (axoplasmic debris) |
| **Neovascularisation** (proliferative) | Ischaemia releases growth factors; fragile new vessels on the retinal surface bleed, causing **vitreous haemorrhage** and sudden loss of vision (can be triggered by sneezing or a knock to the head) |
| Fibrous bands | Collagen and fibrous tissue along new vessels contract and pull the retina, causing re-bleeding and **retinal detachment** |
| **Macular oedema** | Leaking fluid is poorly cleared at the macula; thickening and distortion cause loss of central vision; capillary occlusion also harms central vision. **Not visible** by ophthalmoscope or retinal photography |

- Sequence: hyperglycaemia → neovascularisation → rupture → bleed → clot → organisation → fibrous bands → retinal detachment.
- **Laser therapy** (photocoagulation) prevents bleeding; early proliferative change can regress with glucose control.

### 3.5 The diabetic foot

- 10 to 15% of diabetics develop foot ulcers; foot problems account for **50% of diabetes-related admissions**. It is the **commonest cause of non-traumatic amputation**.
- Main factors: **neuropathy, ischaemia, infection**, which together lead to tissue necrosis.

| Threat | Management |
|---|---|
| Infection | Early antibiotics; drain pus; excise infected bone (resistant osteomyelitis) |
| Ischaemia | Bypass surgery or angioplasty for occlusion |
| Abnormal pressure | Rest the leg; special shoes (continue after healing) |
| Wound environment | Proper dressing to prevent contamination and absorb exudate |
| Irreversible arterial insufficiency | **Amputation** |

- Prevention: regular review by the physician and a **podiatrist**.

### 3.6 Infections

High glucose impairs **neutrophil superoxide generation**, chemotaxis and phagocytosis. Infection also destabilises control and is a common precipitant of ketoacidosis; insulin doses may need to rise by up to 25%.

| Site | Infections |
|---|---|
| Skin | Staphylococcal abscesses, boils, carbuncles; mucocutaneous candidiasis |
| GIT | Periodontal disease; rectal and ischiorectal abscesses |
| Urinary tract | UTI (mostly women), pyelonephritis, perinephric abscess |
| Lungs | Staphylococcal and pneumococcal pneumonia; gram-negative pneumonia; **TB** |
| Bone | Spontaneous staphylococcal spinal osteomyelitis |

### 3.7 Joints and skin

- Joint contractures can complicate childhood diabetes.
- Thick, waxy skin on the backs of the fingers (collagen glycosylation).
- Type 1 patients may develop osteopenia of the extremities, rarely clinically significant.

## 4. Frequency and mortality of complications

| Complication | Approximate frequency |
|---|---|
| Neuropathy | 40% |
| Retinopathy | 30% |
| Nephropathy | 20% |
| Vasculopathy | 10% |

Going **up** the list, morbidity increases; going **down**, mortality increases.

> **Remember:** type 1 diabetics have more severe hyperglycaemia, but complications are seen more in type 2 simply because there are more of them and they are asymptomatic early.

## Key points to revise

1. Two mechanisms: glycosylation (HbA1c, AGEs) and polyol (sorbitol) pathway.
2. Microangiopathy (basement membrane thickening) is the basis of retinopathy, nephropathy and neuropathy.
3. Neuropathy: glove and stocking, vibration sense lost first; autonomic involvement causes postural hypotension, gastroparesis, bladder atony and erectile dysfunction.
4. Nephropathy: raised GFR, microalbuminuria, proteinuria, ESRD; ACE inhibitor or ARB early.
5. Retinopathy: microaneurysms, exudates, cotton-wool spots, neovascularisation, vitreous haemorrhage, detachment; macular oedema is invisible on ophthalmoscopy; laser treatment.
6. Diabetic foot: neuropathy plus ischaemia plus infection; commonest non-traumatic amputation.
7. Coronary disease is the commonest cause of death; MI may be silent.
8. DKA needs fluids first; infection raises insulin requirement.
