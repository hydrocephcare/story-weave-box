## 2. BILIRUBIN METABOLISM

### Pathway - Aging RBCs → Heme → (Heme oxygenase) → Biliverdin → (Biliverdin reductase) → Bilirubin - Bilirubin bound to albumin → liver → conjugated with glucuronic acid (UDP-glucuronosyltransferase) - Bilirubin glucuronides → excreted in bile → gut → urobilinogen → stercobilin (stool) - Some urobilinogen reabsorbed → enterohepatic circulation → excreted in urine

### Jaundice - Bilirubin > **2 mg/dl** → jaundice (yellowish skin + sclera = icterus) - **Kernicterus** = unconjugated bilirubin accumulation in brain → toxic ⭐

### Causes of Jaundice 1. Excessive production (hemolysis) 2. Reduced hepatocellular uptake 3. Impaired conjugation (Gilbert's, Crigler-Najjar) 4. Decreased hepatocellular excretion 5. Impaired bile flow (cholestasis)

### Types of Bilirubin ⭐ | Feature | Unconjugated | Conjugated | |---|---|---| | Water solubility | Insoluble | Soluble | | Albumin binding | Tight | Loose | | Urine | Absent | Present (tea-colored urine) | | Toxicity | **Toxic** (kernicterus) | Nontoxic | | Lab | Total minus direct | Direct bilirubin | | Cause | Hemolysis, impaired conjugation | Cholestasis, hepatocellular disease |

## 5. CHOLESTASIS

### Definition - Systemic retention of bilirubin and other solutes eliminated in bile (bile salts, cholesterol) - Results from hepatocellular dysfunction OR intra/extrahepatic biliary obstruction

### Clinical Features - Jaundice - **Pruritis** (bile salts deposited in skin) ⭐ - Skin xanthomas (cholesterol deposits) - Malabsorption (fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, K) - Pale/clay-colored stools - Dark urine

### Lab - ↑ Bilirubin (conjugated) - ↑↑ Alkaline phosphatase ⭐ - ↑ Lipids - ↑ GGT

### Biopsy Findings - Bile pigment accumulation within hepatic parenchyma - Hepatocyte swelling + foamy degeneration - Bile duct distension + proliferation - Bile lakes (rupture of bile ducts) - Portal tract fibrosis - Cholangitis + cholangiolitis

### Types - **Intrahepatic**: hepatocellular dysfunction - **Extrahepatic**: mechanical obstruction of bile ducts

## 6. HEPATIC FAILURE

### Definition - Loss of **80-90%** of hepatic functional capacity

### Causes 1. **Chronic liver disease** (cirrhosis) — progressive 2. **Massive hepatic necrosis** (fulminant failure): - Viral hepatitis - Drugs: acetaminophen, halothane, rifampicin, MOI antidepressants, CCl4 - Amanita phalloides mushroom toxins 3. **Hepatic dysfunction without necrosis**: - Reye's syndrome - Tetracycline toxicity - Acute fatty liver of pregnancy

### Clinical Consequences Hepatic dysfunction signs: - Jaundice + cholestasis - Hypoalbuminemia → edema, ascites - Hyperammonemia → encephalopathy - Hypoglycemia - Fetor hepaticus (musty odor) - Palmar erythema - Spider angiomas - Hypogonadism + gynecomastia (↓ estrogen metabolism) - Weight loss + muscle wasting - Coagulopathy (↓ clotting factors)

Portal hypertension from cirrhosis: - Ascites - Splenomegaly - Hemorrhoids - Caput medusae (abdominal skin) - Esophageal varices

Life-threatening complications: - Hepatic failure → multiple organ failure - Coagulopathy - Hepatic encephalopathy - Hepatorenal syndrome - Esophageal varices → rupture → massive GI bleed - Hepatocellular carcinoma

### Prognosis - **80% mortality** ⭐

## 7. HEPATIC ENCEPHALOPATHY

- Metabolic disorder of CNS + neuromuscular system - Associated with severe hepatocellular dysfunction + portosystemic shunting - **Pathogenesis**: ammonia → neuronal dysfunction + generalized brain edema ⭐ - **Asterixis** = flapping tremor = hallmark sign ⭐

### Clinical Spectrum - Subtle behavioral changes - Confusion → stupor → deep coma → death - Other: rigidity, hyperreflexia, EEG changes, seizures - Minor morphologic changes in brain

## 8. HEPATORENAL SYNDROME

- Renal failure in severe liver disease WITHOUT intrinsic kidney disease - Excluded: concomitant liver + kidney damage, ATN secondary to circulatory collapse - Pathogenesis: vasoconstriction → decreased renal blood flow - **Urine**: hyperosmolar, protein-negative, low Na⁺ ⭐ - Lab: ↑ blood urea + creatinine - Px: may hasten death or persist for months

## 9. CIRRHOSIS

### Definition - **Irreversible** end-stage chronic liver disease → parenchymal injury + fibrosis

### 3 Histologic Features ⭐ 1. Bridging fibrous septa 2. Disruption of **entire** liver architecture 3. Parenchymal nodules

### Types | Type | Nodule size | Associated with | |---|---|---| | Micronodular | Uniform, <3mm | **Alcohol** ⭐ | | Macronodular | Variable, >3mm | **Viral hepatitis** ⭐ | | Mixed | Both | |

### Etiologic Classification ⭐ | Cause | % | |---|---| | Alcoholic liver disease | **60-70%** | | Viral hepatitis | 10% | | Biliary diseases | 5-10% | | Primary hemochromatosis | 5% | | Wilson's disease | Rare | | α1-antitrypsin deficiency | Rare | | Cryptogenic | **10-15%** |

Others: drugs (α-methyldopa, acetaminophen), Ca, syphilis, galactosemia, tyrosinosis, cardiac cirrhosis

### Pathogenesis - Progressive fibrosis: **collagen types I & III** deposited in all portions of lobule - Key cell: **Ito cell (hepatic stellate cell)** ⭐ - Stimulated by: - Chronic inflammation + cytokines (TNF, IL-1) - Cytokine production by endogenous liver cells - Disruption of extracellular matrix - Direct stimulation of Ito cells by toxins - TGF-β = fibrogenesis ⭐ - Kupffer cells activate stellate cells → release PDGF, TNF, ET-1, TGF-β - → Severe disruption of blood flow + impaired diffusion of solutes

### Clinical Features - May be asymptomatic early - Nonspecific: malaise, anorexia, weight loss, weakness - Jaundice, ascites, peripheral edema - ↑ transaminases, bilirubin, alk. phosphatase - ↓ albumin, clotting factors, Hb - Advanced: frank debilitation → hepatic failure

### Prognosis — Death from: ⭐ 1. Progressive hepatic failure 2. Portal hypertension complications 3. Hepatocellular carcinoma

## 10. PORTAL HYPERTENSION

### Definition - Increased resistance to portal blood flow (normal portal pressure = 5-10 mmHg)

### Causes ⭐ | Level | Cause | |---|---| | Pre-hepatic | Portal vein thrombosis, massive splenomegaly | | Hepatic | **Cirrhosis** (most common) | | Post-hepatic | Severe Rt heart failure, constrictive pericarditis, hepatic vein outflow obstruction |

### Mechanisms in Cirrhosis - Increased resistance to portal blood flow in sinusoids - Compression of central veins by perivenular fibrosis + expanding parenchymal nodules - Arterio-portal anastomoses in fibrous bands

### 4 Major Consequences ⭐ 1. **Ascites** 2. **Portosystemic venous shunts** 3. **Congestive splenomegaly** 4. **Hepatic encephalopathy**

### Portosystemic Shunt Sites ⭐ | Site | Result | |---|---| | Cardioesophageal junction | **Esophageal varices** (most dangerous — rupture = massive bleed) ⭐ | | Rectum | **Hemorrhoids** | | Falciform ligament | **Caput medusae** (periumbilical veins) ⭐ | | Retroperitoneum | Retroperitoneal varices |

### Ascites - Excess serous fluid in abdominal cavity - Fluid: albumin, solutes, few mesothelial cells, mononuclear leukocytes - **Neutrophils = secondary infection (SBP)** ⭐ - Hydrothorax can occur

Pathogenesis: 1. Sinusoidal hypertension → ↑ hydrostatic pressure + hypoalbuminemia 2. Percolation of hepatic lymph into peritoneal cavity 3. Renal Na⁺ + water retention (secondary hyperaldosteronism)

### Splenomegaly - Congestive splenomegaly, may be massive - **Hypersplenism** → excessive removal of blood elements: - Anemia - Leukopenia - **Thrombocytopenia** ⭐ (most clinically significant)

## 11. VIRAL HEPATITIS

### Hepatotropic Viruses ⭐ | Feature | HAV | HBV | HCV | HDV | HEV | |---|---|---|---|---|---| | Type | ssRNA | **dsDNA** | ssRNA | ssRNA (defective) | ssRNA | | Incubation | 2-4 wks | 4-26 wks | 2-26 wks | Same as HBV | 2-8 wks | | Transmission | **Fecal-oral** | Parenteral, sexual, vertical | **Parenteral** (IV drug) | Parenteral (needs HBV) | **Fecal-oral** | | Chronicity | **Never** | 5-10% adults | **85%** ⭐ | Yes | **Never** | | Carrier | No | Yes | Yes | Yes | No | | HCC | No | **Yes** | Yes | No | No | | Fulminant | Rare | Rare | Very rare | **Yes (superinfection)** ⭐ | **Yes (pregnant)** ⭐ | | Vaccine | Yes | Yes | No | HBV vaccine protects | No |

### Other Viral Hepatitis - EBV, CMV, HSV, Arbovirus (Yellow fever), Rubella, Adenovirus, Enterovirus

### HBV Serology ⭐ (MUST KNOW) | Marker | Significance | |---|---| | HBsAg | Active infection (acute or chronic) | | Anti-HBs | Recovery + immunity; also post-vaccination | | HBeAg | Active replication, HIGH infectivity | | Anti-HBe | Low infectivity | | HBcAg | NOT in serum (only liver) | | Anti-HBc IgM | **Acute infection** ⭐ | | Anti-HBc IgG | Past infection | | **Window period** | HBsAg gone, Anti-HBs not yet → only Anti-HBc IgM positive ⭐ |

### HBV → HCC Mechanism ⭐ - HBV genome has NO oncogenic sequences - No selective integration site - Repeated cell death + regeneration → genomic instability + mutations - **HBx protein** = transcriptional activator → activates proto-oncogenes ⭐ - HBV proteins bind + **inactivate p53** ⭐

### Clinical Syndromes of Viral Hepatitis

1. Carrier State - Reservoir of infection - "Healthy" carrier — essentially normal liver biopsy - Asymptomatic chronic liver disease - HBV: 90-95% if infected at birth; 1-10% in adulthood - HBV histology: **ground glass hepatocytes, sanded nuclei** ⭐ - HCV: 0.2-1% of blood donors; chronic hepatitis usually present histologically
2. Asymptomatic Infection - Serologic hepatitis only - Elevated liver enzymes + positive viral antibodies
3. Acute Hepatitis - Stage 1: Incubation period — peak infectivity - Stage 2: Pre-icteric — malaise, fatigue, nausea, fever, headache, myalgia, vomiting, diarrhea - Stage 3: Icteric — jaundice (conjugated hyperbilirubinemia), dark urine, light stools, pruritis - Usual in adult HAV, half of HBV, NOT in children HAV and HCV ⭐ - Stage 4: Convalescence — few weeks to months

Histology of Acute Hepatitis: - Ballooning degeneration - Cholestasis (bile plugs) - Steatosis (HCV) ⭐ - Hepatocyte necrosis: - Dropped out cells surrounded by macrophages = **Councilman bodies** ⭐ - Apoptotic cells - Kupffer cell hypertrophy + hyperplasia - Portal tracts infiltration → spill over = interface hepatitis

Prognosis of Acute Hepatitis: - HBV: 1% fulminant; 5-10% chronic hepatitis - HCV: 85% chronic hepatitis; 15% resolution ⭐

**4. Chronic Hepatitis** (>6 months) - Symptomatic, biochemical or serologic evidence of continuing/relapsing hepatic disease - **Histology:** - Portal inflammation (mildest form) - Piecemeal necrosis (periportal) — more severe - Bridging inflammation + necrosis - Bile duct epithelial proliferation - **Lymphoid aggregates** (HCV specific) ⭐ - Fibrosis (portal, periportal, bridging) - → Cirrhosis - Lab: ↑ PT, bilirubin, alk. phosphatase; ↓ globulins - Px: highly variable

5. Fulminant Hepatitis - Hepatic insufficiency → encephalopathy within **2-3 weeks** (subfulminant = up to 3 months) - Causes: - **50-65% viral** (HBV > HCV >>> HDV-HBV, HAV, HEV, HSV) ⭐ - **25-30% drugs**: acetaminophen, INH, MOI antidepressants, halothane, methyldopa, Amanita phalloides ⭐ - Rarely: hepatic vein obstruction, Wilson's, malignancy, autoimmune hepatitis, hyperthermia, microvesicular steatosis syndromes - **Histology:** Extensive liver destruction, collapse of normal architecture, residual hepatocytes + inflammatory cells, confluent necrosis - **Clinical:** Jaundice, fetor hepaticus, stigmata of chronic liver disease - **Life-threatening complications:** Coagulopathy with bleeding, CV instability, RF, ARDS, electrolyte + acid-base disturbances, sepsis - **Mortality: 90%** (25% with liver transplantation) ⭐

### Autoimmune Hepatitis ⭐ - Chronic hepatitis, histologically indistinguishable from chronic viral hepatitis - **Female predominance (70%)** ⭐ - No serologic viral markers - ↑ serum IgG > 2.5 g/dl - High titers autoantibodies (80%): **ANA, anti-smooth muscle** ⭐ - Increased HLA-B8 or HLA-DRw - 60% have other autoimmune diseases (RA, Sjogren's) - Overall risk of cirrhosis: **5%** - Rx: **good response to immunosuppressive therapy** ⭐

## 12. ALCOHOLIC LIVER DISEASE

### Overview - Alcohol = 5th leading cause of death in USA - Effects on: liver, GIT (ulcers, gastritis), CNS (peripheral neuropathy, Wernicke-Korsakoff), CVS (cardiomyopathy, hypertension), pancreas (pancreatitis), pregnancy (fetal alcohol syndrome) - **3 forms** (can coexist/overlap): 1. Hepatic steatosis 2. Alcoholic hepatitis 3. Alcoholic cirrhosis

### Pathogenesis of Alcohol-Induced Liver Damage

Steatosis mechanism: 1. Generation of excess **NADH** by alcohol dehydrogenase + acetaldehyde dehydrogenase → ↑ lipid biosynthesis 2. Impaired assembly + secretion of lipoproteins 3. Increased peripheral catabolism of fat

Hepatocellular damage mechanism: 1. Induction of cytochrome P-450 → free radicals 2. Generation of reactive oxygen species 3. Direct effect on microtubular + mitochondrial function + membrane fluidity 4. **Acetaldehyde** → lipid peroxidation + acetaldehyde-protein adducts → disrupts cytoskeletal + membrane function ⭐ 5. Antigenic alteration of hepatocytes → immunologic attack

**Alcohol-induced fibrosis:** Multifactorial, poorly understood

Other mechanisms: - Malnutrition + vitamin deficiencies - Impaired digestive function (chronic gastric + intestinal mucosal damage + pancreatitis)

### A. Hepatic Steatosis - Lipid droplets accumulate in hepatocytes - 2 histologic types: **Microvesicular + Macrovesicular** ⭐ - Initially **centrilobular** → later panlobular - Grossly: large (4-6 kg), soft, **yellow greasy liver** ⭐ - **Completely reversible with abstinence** ⭐ - Clinically: asymptomatic; mild ↑ bilirubin + alk. phosphatase

### B. Alcoholic Hepatitis - Hepatocyte swelling (**ballooning**) due to fat + water accumulation + cell necrosis - **Mallory bodies** = characteristic eosinophilic cytoplasmic inclusions (cytokeratin intermediate filaments) ⭐ - Neutrophilic reaction - Fibrosis - Clinically: minimal to severe manifestations; ↑ bilirubin, alk. phosphatase, WBCs

### C. Alcoholic Cirrhosis - Initially: yellow-tan, fatty, enlarged liver - Eventually: brown, shrunken, non-fatty - **Micronodular** pattern ⭐ - Fibrous septa become thicker + extend through sinusoids - Regenerative nodules with entrapped hepatocytes

### Clinical Features of Alcoholic Liver Disease - **Steatosis:** Asymptomatic, mild ↑ bilirubin + alk. phosphatase - **Hepatitis:** Minimal to severe; ↑ bilirubin, alk. phosphatase, WBCs - **Cirrhosis:** Similar to other forms of cirrhosis

### Prognosis ⭐ - 5-yr survival approaches **90% in abstainers** (free of jaundice, ascites, hematemesis) - Drops to **50-60%** in those who continue drinking - Immediate causes of death: 1. Hepatic failure 2. Massive GI hemorrhage 3. Intercurrent infection 4. Hepatorenal syndrome 5. Hepatocellular carcinoma

## 13. NAFLD (Non-Alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease)

- Most common metabolic liver disease ⭐ - Most common cause of incidental elevation of serum transaminases - Fatty liver in individuals who **do NOT drink alcohol** - May present as steatosis OR **NASH** (nonalcoholic steatohepatitis) — similar to ASH histologically ⭐ - Significant contributor to cryptogenic cirrhosis

### Associated Conditions ⭐ - **Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus** - **Obesity** - **Dyslipidemia** (↑ TG, ↓ HDL, ↑ LDL) - **Insulin resistance** (consistent association) ⭐

### Pathogenesis - Insulin resistance → TG accumulation in hepatocytes - Fat-laden hepatocytes → sensitive to lipid peroxidation → liver damage + inflammation - Steatosis patients: **asymptomatic** - NASH patients: may have fatigue, malaise, RUQ discomfort, or symptoms of chronic liver disease

### Prognosis - Progression from steatosis → NASH → cirrhosis is **LOW frequency** ⭐

## 14. METABOLIC LIVER DISEASES

### A. Hemochromatosis ⭐ (MUST KNOW) - Most common inherited liver disease - **Autosomal recessive** - **HFE gene mutation** (chromosome 6) ⭐ - Excessive iron absorption → iron deposits in multiple organs

Organs affected: - Liver → **cirrhosis** - Pancreas → **diabetes** ("bronze diabetes") ⭐ - Heart → cardiomyopathy - Skin → bronze pigmentation - Joints → arthropathy - Pituitary/gonads → hypogonadism

**Classic Triad: Cirrhosis + Diabetes + Bronze skin** ⭐

Lab: - ↑ Serum iron - ↑ Ferritin - ↑ Transferrin saturation (>50%) ⭐ - ↓ TIBC - Stain: **Prussian blue** (hemosiderin) ⭐

**Complications:** Cirrhosis, HCC (200x risk ⭐), diabetes, cardiomyopathy, hypogonadism

**Rx:** **Phlebotomy** (weekly) ⭐

**Secondary hemochromatosis:** Multiple transfusions, hemolytic anemias

### B. Wilson's Disease ⭐ - **Autosomal recessive** - Defect in **copper-transporting ATPase** (chromosome 13) ⭐ - Copper accumulates in: **liver, brain, eye, kidney**

Clinical: - **Kayser-Fleischer rings** = copper deposits in cornea ⭐ - Liver: hepatitis → cirrhosis - Brain: basal ganglia degeneration → neuropsychiatric symptoms - Hemolytic anemia - Renal tubular dysfunction (Fanconi syndrome)

Lab: - ↓ Serum ceruloplasmin ⭐ - ↑ Urine copper - ↑ Liver copper (biopsy)

**Dx:** Liver biopsy + copper quantification

**Rx:** **D-penicillamine** (copper chelation) ⭐

### C. Alpha-1 Antitrypsin Deficiency ⭐ - **Autosomal recessive** - AAT = protease inhibitor produced by liver - Deficiency → two effects: 1. **Emphysema** (lungs unprotected from neutrophil elastase) ⭐ 2. **Cirrhosis** (liver accumulates abnormal misfolded AAT) - Histology: **PAS-positive, diastase-resistant globules** in hepatocytes ⭐ - Major cause of neonatal cholestasis (15%) ⭐ - Rx: Liver transplantation

### D. Reye's Syndrome ⭐ - Children given **aspirin** during viral illness (influenza/varicella) - Mitochondrial dysfunction - **Microvesicular steatosis** of liver - Hypoglycemia, hyperammonemia - **Encephalopathy WITHOUT jaundice** ⭐ - High mortality - **Aspirin now contraindicated in children** ⭐

## 15. INFLAMMATORY LIVER DISEASE (INFECTIONS)

### Pathways of Liver Infection 1. Arterial supply (bacteremia) 2. Portal vein (pylephlebitis) 3. Ascending biliary infection 4. Direct liver invasion from nearby organ 5. Penetrating injury

### Organisms - **Bacterial:** Staphylococcal bacteremia, salmonellosis, miliary TB - **Viral:** EBV, CMV, HSV, Arbovirus (Yellow fever), Rubella, Adenovirus, Enterovirus - **Protozoal:** Malaria, amebiasis, echinococcus ⭐

### Liver Abscess ⭐ - **Pyogenic:** E. coli, Klebsiella (from portal pyemia/pylephlebitis) - **Amoebic:** Entamoeba histolytica → **"anchovy paste" pus** ⭐ - Multiple liver abscesses → pylephlebitis ⭐

## 16. TUMORS OF THE LIVER

### Classification Benign: - Non-neoplastic: Cysts (simple, congenital, choledochal, polycystic), focal nodular hyperplasia - Neoplastic: **Hemangioma** (most common), adenoma

Malignant: - Primary: HCC, cholangiocarcinoma - Secondary: metastatic (most common hepatic malignancy overall) ⭐

### Most Common Benign: Cavernous Hemangioma ⭐ - Most common benign liver tumor - Usually incidental finding - **Do NOT biopsy** (risk of massive hemorrhage) ⭐

### Focal Nodular Hyperplasia (FNH) - **Central stellate scar** ⭐ - NOT associated with OCPs - No malignant potential - No rupture risk

### Liver Cell Adenoma ⭐ - Mostly in **young women on OCPs** ⭐ - Well-demarcated yellow-tan, bile-stained nodule - May reach up to 30 cm - Histology: sheets + cords of cells resembling normal hepatocytes, minimal pleomorphism, **no portal tracts** ⭐ - Clinical significance: 1. Misdiagnosed as HCC 2. May **rupture → serious intraabdominal hemorrhage** ⭐ - **May regress on stopping OCPs** ⭐

### Adenoma vs FNH ⭐ | Feature | Adenoma | FNH | |---|---|---| | OCP association | **Yes** | No | | Rupture risk | **Yes** | No | | Malignant potential | Low | **None** | | Central scar | No | **Yes** ⭐ | | Portal tracts | Absent | Present |

### Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC) ⭐ - Malignant tumor from hepatocytes - Wide geographic variation - **M:F = 3:1 to 8:1**; adulthood - **HCC frequently associated with cirrhosis** - **Chronic HBV carrier = 200x increased risk** ⭐

Risk Factors: - Viruses: HBV, HCV - Chemicals: alcohol, food hepatocarcinogens (**Aspergillus flavus aflatoxin B1**) ⭐ - Chronic liver disease (cirrhosis)

HBV Mechanism: - HBV genome has NO oncogenic sequences - Repeated cell death + regeneration → genomic instability - **HBx protein** = transcriptional activator → activates proto-oncogenes ⭐ - HBV proteins bind + **inactivate p53** ⭐

Pathology: - Unifocal, multifocal, or diffusely infiltrative - Cirrhosis of surrounding parenchyma frequently present - **Strong propensity for vascular invasion** ⭐ (portal vein, hepatic vein) - Histology: well-differentiated to anaplastic - Well-diff: resembles hepatocytes, may show intracytoplasmic bile globules - **Fibrolamellar variant:** better prognosis, young patients, no cirrhosis ⭐

Clinical Features: - Asymptomatic hepatomegaly - In cirrhosis: rapid ↑ liver size, sudden worsening ascites, **bloody ascites**, fever + pain - Lab: **AFP > 1000 ng/ml highly suggestive** ⭐ - AFP = nonspecific tumor marker

Prognosis: - Grim: death within **6 months - 1 year** from: 1. Severe cachexia 2. GI bleeding 3. Liver failure 4. Tumor rupture - **Rx: complete surgical resection** ⭐

### Cholangiocarcinoma ⭐ - Malignant epithelial neoplasm of bile duct origin - Less common than HCC - **NOT associated with HBV/HCV** ⭐ - AFP normal

Etiology: - Majority: no known risk factor - Radiologic contrast material: **Thorotrast** ⭐ - **Opisthorchis sinensis** infestation of biliary tract ⭐ - **Primary sclerosing cholangitis** ⭐

### HCC vs Cholangiocarcinoma ⭐ | Feature | HCC | Cholangiocarcinoma | |---|---|---| | Origin | Hepatocytes | Bile duct epithelium | | HBV/HCV | **Yes** | **No** | | AFP | ↑↑ | Normal | | Risk factors | Cirrhosis, HBV, aflatoxin | PSC, Opisthorchis, Thorotrast |

### Other Primary Malignant Tumors - **Hepatoblastoma:** Aggressive tumor of **children** ⭐ - **Angiosarcoma:** Association with **vinyl chloride, arsenic + Thorotrast** ⭐ - Primary lymphomas, sarcomas

### Metastatic Tumors ⭐ - **Most common hepatic malignancy** overall - Sources: **colon > lung > breast** ⭐ - May be single or multiple - Asymptomatic or symptomatic

## 17. CIRCULATORY DISORDERS OF THE LIVER

### Classification 1. Impaired blood flow **into** the liver - Hepatic artery inflow - Portal vein obstruction 2. Impaired blood flow **through** the liver - Passive congestion + centrilobular necrosis - Peliosis hepatis 3. Hepatic venous **outflow** obstruction - Budd-Chiari syndrome - Veno-occlusive disease

### A. Impaired Blood Inflow

Hepatic Artery Obstruction: - Causes: Thrombosis, embolism, vascular disease (polyarteritis nodosa), compression by neoplasia, sepsis - **Liver infarcts are RARE** due to double blood supply ⭐ - Interruption of main hepatic artery does NOT always produce ischemic necrosis (accessory vessels + portal venous system compensate) - When infarcts occur: small + localized; complete organ loss rare

Portal Vein Obstruction:

*Extrahepatic causes:* - Peritoneal sepsis + subsequent pylephlebitis - Lymphatic metastasis with enlarged hilar LN - Pancreatitis with splenic vein thrombosis - Hypercoagulability; post-surgical thromboses - **Banti's syndrome:** subclinical portal vein thrombosis due to neonatal omphalitis OR umbilical vein catheterization → fibrotic partially recanalized blood vessel ⭐

*Intrahepatic causes:* - Cirrhosis - Vascular invasion by cancer in liver - Hypercoagulable states

Infarct of Zahn ⭐: - Sharply demarcated area of red-blue discoloration - Due to hepatocellular atrophy secondary to marked sinusoidal congestion - **NOT a true infarct** ⭐ - Caused by obstruction of portal vein or its radicles

Clinical Manifestations of Portal Vein Obstruction: - Symptoms are variable - Portal hypertension (ascites, esophageal varices, splenomegaly) - Intestinal congestion + pain - Bowel infarction if visceral blood flow impaired - **Multiple liver abscesses** (in pylephlebitis) ⭐

### B. Impaired Blood Flow Through the Liver

Causes: 1. **Cirrhosis:** portal blood flow obstruction due to compression of sinusoids + central veins → increased resistance at sinusoidal level 2. **Physical occlusion of hepatic sinusoid:** Sickle cell anemia 3. **Systemic circulatory compromise:** DIC - Eclampsia of pregnancy: associated with DIC → may result in extensive **subcapsular hepatic hematoma**

Passive Congestion + Centrilobular Necrosis: - Manifestations of systemic circulatory collapse - **Right-sided heart failure** → passive congestion → **centrilobular congestion** ⭐ - **Left-sided heart failure/shock** → hepatic hypoperfusion + hypoxia → **centrilobular necrosis** ⭐ - **Combined Rt + Lt heart failure** → **centrilobular hemorrhagic necrosis = "Nutmeg liver"** ⭐ - **Cardiac sclerosis ("cardiac cirrhosis"):** centrilobular fibrosis → uncommon complication of sustained severe CHF ⭐

Peliosis Hepatis ⭐: - **Rare condition:** sinusoidal dilation → blood-filled cavities within hepatic parenchyma - **Associated with:** - Anabolic steroids, OCPs, corticosteroids, danazol ⭐ - Marasmus, bacterial endocarditis, lymphoproliferative disease - **AIDS: Bacillary angiomatosis** caused by **Rochalimaea (Bartonella) henselae** ⭐ - Unknown pathogenesis - **Usually asymptomatic** ⭐ - Complications of advanced peliosis: - Intraabdominal hemorrhage - Hepatic failure

### C. Hepatic Venous Outflow Obstruction

Budd-Chiari Syndrome (Hepatic Vein Thrombosis) ⭐:

*May be:* - Acute: **usually fatal** ⭐ - Subacute - Chronic

*Associated with:* ⭐ - Polycythemia vera - Pregnancy - Post-partum state - Oral contraceptive use - **Paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH)** ⭐ - Intraabdominal cancers, particularly **HCC** ⭐ - **30% of cases are idiopathic** ⭐

*Clinical Manifestations:* - Hepatomegaly - Ascites - Abdominal pain + distension - Jaundice - Death

*Pathology:* - Thrombosis in major hepatic veins or **inferior vena cava** ⭐ - Swollen **red-purple liver** with tense capsule ⭐ - Severe hepatic centrilobular congestion + necrosis - Centrilobular fibrosis in chronic cases

*Prognosis:* - Acute form: **high mortality** ⭐ - Rx: **surgical** ⭐

Veno-Occlusive Disease:

*Associated with:* - Drinking **Pyrrolizidine alkaloid-containing bush tea** ⭐ - **Bone marrow transplantation** followed by graft-versus-host disease: - Up to **25% of allogeneic BMT patients** ⭐ - **30% mortality rate** ⭐ - Chemotherapy + radiotherapy induced toxic endothelial injury

*Pathology:* - Small hepatic vein radicles obliterated by **subendothelial swelling + collagen deposition** - **Clinically indistinguishable from Budd-Chiari syndrome** ⭐ - Centrilobular congestion + necrosis +/- sclerosis

### Budd-Chiari vs Veno-Occlusive ⭐ | Feature | Budd-Chiari | Veno-occlusive | |---|---|---| | Vessels | Major hepatic veins/IVC | Small hepatic vein radicles | | Pathology | Thrombosis | Subendothelial swelling + collagen | | Causes | Hypercoagulable, OCPs, PNH, HCC | Bush tea, BMT, chemo/radiotherapy | | Clinical | **Indistinguishable from each other** ⭐ | |

## 18. INTRAHEPATIC BILIARY TRACT DISEASE

### Classification Due to general liver disease: - Drug toxicity - Viral hepatitis - Liver transplantation

Due to biliary tract disease: - Biliary cirrhosis (Primary + Secondary) - Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis

### Primary Biliary Cirrhosis (PBC) ⭐

- **Autoimmune** disease - Lymphocyte-mediated destruction of **intrahepatic** bile duct epithelium - Chronic progressive + often fatal cholestatic liver disease - Characterized by destruction of intrahepatic bile ducts + portal inflammation + scarring - Primary feature: **nonsuppurative granulomas** ⭐

Demographics: - **Middle-aged women** (onset 20-80 yrs) ⭐ - Insidious onset

Clinical Course: - Pruritis (early) → later jaundice appears - Cirrhosis + hepatic failure develops over **2 or more decades** ⭐ - Signs of portal hypertension + hepatic encephalopathy

Lab: - ↑ Alk. phosphatase + cholesterol - Bilirubin = **late development** indicating hepatic decompensation ⭐

Autoantibody: - **Anti-mitochondrial Ab in >90% of patients** ⭐ - Target: mitochondrial pyruvate dehydrogenase ⭐

Associated Extra-hepatic Conditions: - Sjogren's syndrome - Scleroderma - Thyroiditis - Rheumatoid arthritis - Celiac disease

Histology (Pre-cirrhosis) — Florid Duct Lesion: - Granulomas with lymphocytes, macrophages, plasma cells + eosinophils ⭐ - Bile duct proliferation - Inflammation + necrosis of periportal hepatic parenchyma

**Rx:** Liver transplantation ⭐

### Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis (PSC) ⭐

- Inflammation + obliterative fibrosis + segmental dilation of **both intrahepatic + extrahepatic** bile ducts ⭐ - Irregular strictures + dilations → **characteristic "beading" picture** on barium contrast radiography ⭐

Demographics: - **3rd-5th decades** - **M:F = 2:1** ⭐

KEY ASSOCIATION: - **70% of patients have associated Ulcerative Colitis** ⭐ - About 4% of UC patients have PSC

Clinical: - Progressive fatigue, pruritis + jaundice - Signs + symptoms of chronic liver disease

Lab: - ↑ Alkaline phosphatase ⭐

**Autoantibodies:** < 10% of patients (unlike PBC) ⭐

Histology: - **Fibrosing cholangitis** of bile ducts ⭐ - Concentric **"onion skin" fibrosis** ⭐ - Progressive duct atrophy + luminal obliteration

Prognosis: - Protracted course over many years - Increased risk of **cholangiocarcinoma** ⭐

**Rx:** Liver transplantation ⭐

### PBC vs PSC ⭐ (EXAM FAVORITE) | Feature | PBC | PSC | |---|---|---| | Gender | **Women** (middle-aged) | **Men** (2:1) | | Ducts | Intrahepatic only | **Both** intra + extrahepatic | | Autoantibody | **Anti-mitochondrial >90%** | <10% | | Association | Sjogren's, scleroderma, RA | **Ulcerative colitis 70%** | | Histology | Florid duct lesion + **granulomas** | **Onion skin fibrosis** | | Imaging | Normal | Beading pattern | | Cancer risk | Low | **Cholangiocarcinoma** ⭐ | | Rx | Liver transplant | Liver transplant |

### Secondary Biliary Cirrhosis

- Prolonged obstruction of **extrahepatic** biliary tree → profound liver damage

Causes: 1. Extrahepatic cholelithiasis 2. Biliary atresia 3. Tumors of biliary tree + pancreas head 4. Strictures secondary to previous surgery

Pathology: - Initially: reversible features of cholestasis - → Periportal fibrosis, scarring + nodule formation - Subtotal obstruction → secondary **ascending cholangitis** (coliforms + enterococci) ⭐

## 19. EXTRAHEPATIC BILE DUCT DISEASES

### Cholelithiasis (Gallstones) ⭐

- Common disease: affects **10-20% of adults** - **>80% are asymptomatic** ⭐ - Two main types:

#### Stone Types ⭐ | Feature | Cholesterol Stones | Pigment Stones — Black | Pigment Stones — Brown | |---|---|---|---| | Location | **Exclusively in GB** ⭐ | Anywhere in biliary tree | Infected intra/extrahepatic ducts | | Composition | Cholesterol | Calcium bilirubin salts | Calcium bilirubinate, mucin, cholesterol | | Appearance | Ovoid, firm, faceted | Small, numerous, friable | Single/few, soft, greasy | | Radiopacity | **Mostly radiolucent** ⭐ | **50-75% radiopaque** ⭐ | **Radiolucent** | | Associated with | Western, females, obesity | Sterile GB bile, hemolysis | Infected ducts, Crohn's |

#### Pathogenesis (Cholesterol Stones) 1. **Bile supersaturation** with cholesterol ⭐ 2. **Nucleation** (promoted by calcium salts, mucus glycoproteins) 3. **GB stasis** with cholesterol crystals remaining long enough to aggregate

#### Risk Factors — Cholesterol Stones (5 F's) ⭐ - **Fat** (obesity) - **Female** - **Fertile** (pregnancy) - **Forty** (age: elderly > young adults) - **Fair** (Western world, Native Americans) - PLUS: OCPs, rapid weight loss, hyperlipidemia, GB stasis, inborn disorders of bile acid metabolism, treatment with **clofibrate** ⭐

#### Risk Factors — Pigment Stones ⭐ - Demography: **Asians, rural areas** - Chronic hemolytic syndromes - Biliary infection - GI disorders: ileal disease (Crohn's), ileal resection or bypass - Cystic fibrosis with pancreatic insufficiency

#### Clinical Features - 70-80% asymptomatic ⭐ - Biliary pain (constant or colicky from obstructed GB/biliary tree) - Associated with inflammation of GB

#### Complications ⭐ - Empyema - Perforation - Fistulae - Inflammation of biliary tree - Obstructive cholestasis - Pancreatitis - **Gallstone ileus** (intestinal obstruction) ⭐

### Cholecystitis ⭐

- Inflammation of the gallbladder - **Almost always associated with gallstones** - One of most common indications for abdominal surgery

Classification: - Acute calculous - Acute acalculous - Chronic - Acute + chronic

#### Acute Calculous Cholecystitis (90%) - Caused by obstruction of GB neck or cystic duct by stones - Mechanism: - Chemical irritation + inflammation of GB wall - Mucosal phospholipases hydrolyze biliary lecithin → **lysolecithin** ⭐ - Disruption of protective glycoprotein mucous layer - Prostaglandin release within GB wall - BF compromise due to GB distension + ↑ pressure

Pathology: - Enlarged (2-3x), tense GB with discolorations (subserosal hemorrhages) - Serosal fibrinous/suppurative exudate - Stones obstructing GB neck or cystic duct in **90%** ⭐ - GB lumen: turbid bile + fibrin + hemorrhage + pus - **Empyema of GB** = full of pus ⭐ - Thickened edematous hyperemic wall - **Gangrenous cholecystitis** = black necrotic GB ⭐ - Histology: edema, WBC infiltration, congestion + necrosis

#### Acute Acalculous Cholecystitis (5-12%) ⭐ - Seen in: 1. Post-operative states 2. Severe trauma 3. Severe burns 4. Sepsis 5. Postpartum - Factors: - Dehydration - GB stasis + sludging - Vascular compromise - Bacterial contamination

#### Chronic Cholecystitis - +/- history of acute cholecystitis - Gallstones almost always present (but stones + obstruction do NOT play major role in initiation of inflammation) ⭐ - **Supersaturation of bile** predisposes to chronic inflammation + stone formation ⭐ - Bacteria isolated in **1/3 of cases** - Variable morphologic appearance: minimal changes, contraction, enlargement, mucosal ulceration, wall thickening - Histology: **lymphocytic infiltration + fibrosis** ⭐

#### Clinical Features (All Types) - Acute + chronic calculous: similar + variable symptoms - Minimal nonspecific symptoms → biliary colic → severe **RUQ pain** ⭐ - Fever, nausea, **leukocytosis** + prostration - **Conjugated hyperbilirubinemia** - Acute acalculous: symptoms obscured by general condition - **Dx: Ultrasonography** ⭐ - Complications: cholangitis, sepsis, GB perforation, abscess, rupture, cholecyst-enteric fistula, intestinal ileus

### Choledocholithiasis ⭐ - Stones present within the biliary tree - May be: 1. Derived from gallbladder 2. Primary ductal + intrahepatic stone formation - Stones do NOT necessarily obstruct major bile ducts - **Asymptomatic in 10%** ⭐ - Symptoms due to: 1. Biliary obstruction 2. Pancreatitis 3. Cholangitis 4. Hepatic abscess 5. Chronic liver disease with secondary biliary cirrhosis 6. Acute acalculous cholecystitis

### Ascending Cholangitis ⭐ - Acute inflammation of the wall of bile ducts - Caused by **any lesion obstructing bile flow:** 1. Choledocholithiasis (most common) 2. Tumors 3. Indwelling catheters or stents 4. Acute pancreatitis 5. Benign strictures 6. Microorganisms: fungi, viruses, protozoa, bacteria

Clinical: - Fever, chills, abdominal pain, jaundice - **Charcot's Triad:** Fever + RUQ pain + Jaundice ⭐ - **Reynolds Pentad:** Charcot's + Shock + Confusion = Suppurative cholangitis ⭐ - Suppurative cholangitis → **risk of liver abscess** ⭐ - Complication: **Sepsis** ⭐

### Extrahepatic Biliary Atresia ⭐

- **Major cause (1/3) of neonatal cholestasis** ⭐ - Incidence: **1:10,000 live births** - Complete bile flow obstruction due to destruction or absence of all/part of extrahepatic bile ducts - **NOT a true atresia** — acquired inflammatory disorder of unknown cause ⭐ - Slight **female preponderance** - Normal birth weight infants - **Lab: conjugated hyperbilirubinemia** ⭐ - **Biopsy essential for diagnosis** ⭐

Pathology: 1. Inflammation + fibrosing stricture of hepatic or common bile ducts + progressive destruction of intrahepatic biliary tree 2. Periductal inflammation of intrahepatic ducts 3. Liver: marked bile duct proliferation, portal tract edema + fibrosis, parenchymal cholestasis + **giant multinucleated hepatocytes** ⭐ 4. Periportal fibrosis + cirrhosis within **3-6 months** ⭐

**Prognosis:** Death within **2 years** of birth ⭐

**Rx:** Liver transplantation ⭐

### Gallbladder Carcinoma ⭐

- **Commonest extrahepatic biliary tract cancer** ⭐ - Slightly more common in **women**; peak **7th decade** - Due to recurrent trauma + inflammation: usually associated with stones; pyogenic or parasitic disease - **Pathology:** Infiltrating or fungating growth pattern - **Most are adenocarcinomas** (5% squamous) ⭐ - At Dx: most have **invaded liver** +/- portahepatic LN ⭐ - Seeding to peritoneum, GIT, lungs - Insidious symptoms (similar to cholelithiasis) - If obstruction develops early → early Dx + Rx (better outcome) - **Px: dismal — 5-year survival = 1%** ⭐

### Carcinoma of Extrahepatic Bile Ducts ⭐

- At time of diagnosis: tumors tend to be **small in size** - Most are **firm nodules** within bile duct wall - Some are diffusely infiltrative - A few are papillary polypoid lesions - **Adenocarcinomas** (squamous features uncommon) - Clinical: jaundice, light stools, nausea, vomiting, hepatomegaly + palpable GB - ↑ Alk. phosphatase + transaminases + PT - **Dx: endoscopy** ⭐ - **Px: despite small size, most are NOT surgically resectable** at Dx ⭐

## 20. NEONATAL CHOLESTASIS

- **Prolonged conjugated hyperbilirubinemia** in the newborn ⭐ - **Major causes: EHBA + neonatal hepatitis** ⭐ - Clinical: jaundice, dark urine, light stools, hepatomegaly

### Causes - Bile duct obstruction: **Extrahepatic biliary atresia (EHBA)** - Neonatal infections: CMV, sepsis, UTI, syphilis - Toxic: drugs, parenteral nutrition - Metabolic diseases: tyrosinemia, Niemann-Pick, galactosemia, **AAT deficiency**, cystic fibrosis - Miscellaneous: shock, hypoperfusion, **Alagille's syndrome** (paucity of bile ducts) - Idiopathic neonatal hepatitis

### Frequency ⭐ - Idiopathic neonatal hepatitis: **50-60%** ⭐ - Extrahepatic biliary atresia: **20%** ⭐ - AAT deficiency: **15%** ⭐

### Key Points - Distinction between disorders is essential (management differs) ⭐ - **Liver biopsy is important in diagnosis** ⭐
