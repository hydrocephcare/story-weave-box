# Pneumonia

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Respiratory Medicine**

> **In one line:** pneumonia is an acute lower respiratory infection with new radiological shadowing, usually bacterial (about half pneumococcal), acquired in the community or in hospital. Diagnose from cough, fever, breathlessness and consolidation on X-ray, grade severity, start antibiotics within 4 hours, and look for complications such as empyema, abscess and respiratory failure.

## What you must know

- **Definition:** acute respiratory illness with **recently developed radiological pulmonary shadowing** (segmental, lobar or multilobar).
- Classified as **community-acquired (CAP)**, **hospital-acquired (HAP)** and pneumonia in the **immunocompromised host**.
- About **50% of pneumonia is pneumococcal** (*Streptococcus pneumoniae*): rust-coloured sputum, lobar consolidation, high white cell count.
- **Atypical** organisms (*Mycoplasma*, *Legionella*) cause extrapulmonary features: haemolysis, erythema multiforme, hepatitis, hyponatraemia.
- **Sputum culture and Gram stain** in all; **blood cultures** in moderate to severe CAP, ideally before antibiotics. **Offer HIV testing.**
- First antibiotic dose **within 4 hours**; uncomplicated CAP: **amoxicillin 500 mg 8-hourly**.
- Persistent symptoms or poor response: consider **TB**, complications, or an alternative diagnosis. **Repeat the chest X-ray at about 6 weeks** to exclude underlying malignancy.
- HAP: onset at least **2 days after admission**; mortality about 30%.

## 1. Definition and classification

- Acute respiratory illness with new radiological shadowing, **segmental, lobar or multilobar**.
- Clinical picture: **cough, purulent sputum, breathlessness and fever**.
- Usually bacterial; can be viral or fungal.

### By setting

- **Community-acquired (CAP)**
- **Hospital-acquired (HAP)**: at least 2 days after admission
- **Pneumonia in immunocompromised hosts** (HIV, transplant, chemotherapy, steroids)

### By anatomy (CAP)

| Type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Lobar** | Localised; the whole of one or more lobes affected |
| **Bronchopneumonia** | Infection centred on bronchi and bronchioles (patchy) |
| **Diffuse** | Lobules of the lung mainly affected |

## 2. Community-acquired pneumonia

### Epidemiology and risk factors

- Occurs at all ages but is commoner at the **extremes of age**.
- Most common organism: ***Streptococcus pneumoniae***.

| Group | Risk factors |
|---|---|
| **Age** | Over 65 years |
| **Comorbidity** | **HIV infection**, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, malnutrition, recent viral respiratory infection |
| **Other respiratory conditions** | Cystic fibrosis, bronchiectasis, COPD, obstructing lesion (endoluminal cancer, inhaled foreign body) |
| **Lifestyle** | Smoking, excess alcohol, intravenous drug use |
| **Iatrogenic** | Immunosuppression, prolonged corticosteroids |

### Pathophysiology

- Spread by **respiratory droplets** (and aspiration of oropharyngeal flora).
- Presentation and organisms vary with **age, effectiveness of host immune response and innate defences** (mucociliary clearance, cough, alveolar macrophages).
- Alveoli fill with pus and debris, impairing gas exchange (hypoxaemia).
- Non-infective pneumonitis: **chemical insult** (aspiration of vomitus), **radiotherapy**, **allergic mechanisms**.

### Causative organisms (general knowledge, as the slide was an image)

| Group | Organisms |
|---|---|
| Typical bacteria | *S. pneumoniae* (commonest), *H. influenzae*, *Staph. aureus*, *Klebsiella* |
| Atypical | *Mycoplasma pneumoniae*, *Legionella pneumophila*, *Chlamydophila* |
| Viral | Influenza, RSV, adenovirus, SARS-CoV-2 |
| Opportunistic | *Pneumocystis jirovecii*, TB (in HIV) |

## 3. Clinical features

- **Cough:** dry or productive; haemoptysis can occur. In **pneumococcal** pneumonia sputum is classically **rust-coloured**.
- **Breathlessness:** alveoli filled with pus impair gas exchange. **Coarse crackles** and **bronchial breath sounds** over consolidation.
- **Fever:** may reach **39.5 to 40°C**. **Swinging fevers** suggest **empyema**.
- **Pleuritic chest pain** from pleural inflammation; a **pleural rub** may be heard early.
- In consolidation: dull percussion, increased vocal resonance, bronchial breathing.

### Extrapulmonary features (not universal)

| Organism | Features |
|---|---|
| **Legionella and Mycoplasma** | Myalgia, arthralgia, malaise |
| **Mycoplasma** | Myocarditis, pericarditis, **erythema multiforme**, erythema nodosum, **haemolysis (about 50% of cases)** |
| **Legionella** | Headache, abdominal pain, diarrhoea and vomiting, **hepatitis** |
| **Pneumococcus** | Labial **herpes simplex** reactivation |
| Any | Thrombocytopenia is relatively common; **Stevens-Johnson syndrome** is a rare, potentially life-threatening complication |

### Elderly

- May present with **confusion** or non-specific symptoms such as **recurrent falls**, with little fever or cough. Consider CAP in every sick elderly patient.
- If symptoms last **several weeks** or fail to respond to standard antibiotics, **always remember tuberculosis**.

## 4. Investigations

### Exclude mimics

Pulmonary infarction, pulmonary or pleural TB, **pulmonary oedema (can be unilateral)**, pulmonary eosinophilia, malignancy (**bronchoalveolar cell carcinoma**), and cryptogenic organising pneumonia (COP / BOOP).

Also assess severity and identify complications.

### Chest X-ray

- Shows consolidation with **air bronchograms**; effusions and collapse (retained secretions) can be seen.
- **Radiological change lags behind clinical signs.** A normal film at presentation should be **repeated after 2 to 3 days** if CAP is suspected.
- Repeat at **6 weeks** to exclude malignancy as the underlying cause.

| Organism | X-ray pattern |
|---|---|
| ***S. pneumoniae*** | Lobar consolidation with air bronchograms, effusions, collapse |
| ***Mycoplasma*** | Usually one lobe, but can be bilateral and extensive |
| ***Legionella*** | Lobar then multilobar shadowing, occasional small effusion, cavitation rare |

- Right middle lobe pneumonia: opacification beneath the horizontal fissure and loss of the right heart border (silhouette sign) on PA view; confined to the anterior middle lobe on lateral view.

### Blood tests

CBC, urea and electrolytes, biochemistry and CRP.

| Organism | Findings |
|---|---|
| ***S. pneumoniae*** | WCC over 15 x 10⁹/L (90% neutrophils); ESR over 100 mm/hour; CRP over 100 mg/L |
| ***Mycoplasma*** | WCC usually normal; if anaemic, exclude haemolysis (**direct Coombs' test and cold agglutinins**) |
| ***Legionella*** | **Lymphopenia** without marked leucocytosis; **hyponatraemia**, hypoalbuminaemia, high liver transaminases |

### Microbiology and other tests

- **Sputum culture and Gram stain** in all patients: *S. pneumoniae* = Gram-positive diplococci; *S. aureus* = Gram-positive cocci in clusters ("bunch of grapes"); Gram-negative rods.
- **Blood cultures** in moderate to severe CAP, before antibiotics if possible.
- **Pulse oximetry**, and **arterial blood gases** if saturation is below 94%.
- **HIV test**: pneumonia is a common first presentation of undiagnosed HIV.
- Urinary antigens for pneumococcus and *Legionella* where available.

> **Remember:** severity is scored with **CURB-65** (Confusion, Urea over 7 mmol/L, Respiratory rate 30 or more, BP low (systolic under 90 or diastolic 60 or less), age 65 or more). 0 to 1 manage at home, 2 consider admission, 3 or more severe.

## 5. Management of CAP

General measures: **oxygen, IV fluids, antibiotics, thromboprophylaxis, physiotherapy, nutrition, analgesia**.

### Oxygen

- Indicated in tachypnoea, hypoxaemia, hypotension or acidosis; aim for **PaO2 of at least 8 kPa (60 mmHg)** (SaO2 above 92%).
- Use **high concentrations (35% or more)**, humidified, in all patients without COPD-related hypercapnia.
- Consider **assisted ventilation early** if hypoxaemia persists despite adequate oxygen.

### Fluids

- IV fluids in severe illness, the elderly and those who vomit; otherwise encourage oral intake. **Inotropes** for circulatory shock.

### Antibiotics

- **First dose within 4 hours of presentation**; do not delay for investigations.
- Switch from **parenteral to oral** once temperature has settled for **24 hours**.
- If no response: seek microbiological advice and reconsider the diagnosis.
- Adjust to culture and sensitivity results.

| Situation | Regimen |
|---|---|
| **Uncomplicated CAP** | **Amoxicillin 500 mg 8-hourly orally** |
| Penicillin allergy | **Clarithromycin 500 mg 12-hourly** or **erythromycin 500 mg 6-hourly** orally |
| *Staphylococcus* cultured or suspected | **Flucloxacillin 1 to 2 g 6-hourly IV** plus **clarithromycin 500 mg 12-hourly IV** |
| *Mycoplasma* / *Legionella* suspected | Clarithromycin 500 mg 12-hourly (oral or IV) or erythromycin 500 mg 6-hourly, plus **rifampicin 600 mg 12-hourly IV** in severe cases |
| **Severe CAP** | Clarithromycin 500 mg 12-hourly IV or erythromycin 500 mg 6-hourly IV **plus** one of: co-amoxiclav 1.2 g 8-hourly IV; ceftriaxone 1 to 2 g daily IV; cefuroxime 1.5 g 8-hourly IV; or amoxicillin 1 g 6-hourly IV with flucloxacillin 2 g 6-hourly IV |

### Other supportive care

- **Thromboprophylaxis:** subcutaneous **low molecular weight heparin** if admitted over 12 hours; TED stockings.
- **Physiotherapy:** helps expectoration, especially if cough is suppressed by pleural pain or mucus plugging causes collapse.
- **Analgesia:** relieves pleural pain so the patient can breathe and cough effectively. Paracetamol, co-codamol or NSAIDs; opiates only with extreme caution in poor respiratory function.
- **Nutritional supplementation.**

## 6. Complications

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| **General** | Respiratory failure, sepsis, multi-system failure |
| **Local** | Pleural effusion, **empyema**, **lung abscess**, organising pneumonia |

Delayed recovery means either a complication, a wrong diagnosis, or pneumonia secondary to proximal bronchial obstruction or recurrent aspiration.

## 7. Prognosis, discharge and follow-up

- Most patients respond promptly. **Fever may persist several days; the X-ray can take weeks or months to clear**, especially in the elderly.
- **Mortality:** under 1% for adults managed at home; typically **5 to 10%** in hospital; as high as **50%** in severe illness (ICU).
- Discharge depends on home circumstances and likelihood of complications. **No repeat X-ray before discharge** if recovery is satisfactory.
- **Clinical review at about 6 weeks**, with a chest X-ray if symptoms or signs persist or malignancy is suspected.

## 8. Prevention

- Smoking cessation.
- **Influenza and pneumococcal vaccination** in selected patients.
- ***Legionella* requires notification** to the health authority.
- In developing countries: tackle **malnutrition and indoor air pollution**; immunise against **measles, pertussis and *Haemophilus influenzae* type b** (and pneumococcal conjugate vaccine).

## 9. Hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP)

- New pneumonia occurring **at least 2 days after admission**.
- **Second most common hospital-acquired infection** and the leading cause of HAI-associated death.

### At risk

- Older people; ICU patients, especially **ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP)**.
- **Healthcare-associated pneumonia (HCAP)**: at least 2 days in hospital in the last 90 days, haemodialysis, recent IV antibiotics, or residence in a nursing home or long-term facility.

### Aetiology

- **Early HAP (4 to 5 days):** organisms similar to CAP.
- **Late-onset HAP:** **Gram-negative bacteria** (*Escherichia*, *Pseudomonas*, *Klebsiella*), ***Staph. aureus* including MRSA**, and **anaerobes**.

### Predisposing factors

| Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|
| Reduced host defences | Corticosteroids, diabetes, malignancy; reduced cough (post-operative); disordered mucociliary clearance (anaesthetics); bulbar or vocal cord palsy |
| Aspiration | Immobility, low conscious level, vomiting, dysphagia, achalasia, severe reflux, nasogastric tube |
| Bacteria introduced into the lower tract | Endotracheal tube or tracheostomy, infected ventilators / nebulisers / bronchoscopes, dental or sinus infection |
| Bacteraemia | Abdominal sepsis, IV cannula infection, infected emboli |

### Diagnosis

- No universally agreed criteria. Suspect in a hospitalised or ventilated patient with **purulent sputum or tracheal secretions, new radiological infiltrates, unexplained increase in oxygen requirement, temperature above 38.3°C, leucocytosis or leucopenia**.
- Biomarkers are non-specific. Investigations are as for CAP, with microbiological confirmation wherever possible.
- In ventilated patients: **bronchoscopic protected brush specimens or BAL**; endotracheal aspirates are easy but less reliable.

### Management

- Base empirical antibiotics on **local pathogens and resistance patterns, length of stay, recent antibiotics and comorbidity**.
- Ensure **Gram-negative cover**: a third-generation cephalosporin (e.g. cefotaxime) with an aminoglycoside (e.g. gentamicin); or meropenem; or aztreonam (monocyclic beta-lactam) with flucloxacillin.
- **MRSA:** IV **vancomycin**; oral doxycycline, rifampicin or linezolid when appropriate.
- Give antibiotics IV at least initially; physiotherapy for immobile or elderly; adequate oxygen, fluids and monitoring.

### Prevention of HAP

- Mortality about **30%** despite treatment, so prevention matters.
- Hand washing and equipment hygiene; minimise aspiration (head-up positioning).
- **Limit stress-ulcer prophylaxis with PPIs.**
- **Chlorhexidine 2%** oral antiseptic to decontaminate the upper airway.
- Some ICUs use selective decontamination of the digestive tract when ventilation is expected to exceed 48 hours.

## Key points to revise

1. Pneumonia = acute LRTI with new shadowing; classify as CAP, HAP or immunocompromised.
2. About 50% pneumococcal: rust-coloured sputum, lobar consolidation, WCC over 15.
3. *Mycoplasma*: haemolysis, erythema multiforme, cold agglutinins; *Legionella*: hyponatraemia, hepatitis, lymphopenia.
4. Sputum Gram stain and culture for all; blood cultures if moderate to severe; test for HIV.
5. Antibiotics within 4 hours; amoxicillin for uncomplicated CAP, macrolide if allergic, combination for severe disease.
6. Swinging fever suggests empyema; slow response should prompt a search for TB, complications or obstruction.
7. Repeat the CXR at 6 weeks in smokers and the older patient to exclude cancer.
8. HAP is at least 2 days after admission; late HAP is Gram-negative or MRSA; mortality about 30%.
