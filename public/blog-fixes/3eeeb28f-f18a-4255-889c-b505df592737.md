## Breast Carcinoma — Understanding It From Zero

## First, Understand the Breast
The breast is made of two main structures — **ducts** and **lobules.**
- **Lobules** are the small glands that produce milk
- **Ducts** are the tubes that carry milk from the lobules to the nipple
- These two meet at a junction called the **terminal duct lobular unit** — and this is where almost all breast cancers begin
Think of it like a tree. The lobules are the leaves, the ducts are the branches, and the nipple is the trunk. Cancer starts at the point where a leaf meets a branch.

## What is Breast Cancer?
Normal breast cells grow, do their job, and die in an orderly way. Breast cancer happens when cells in the ducts or lobules start growing out of control — they stop following the rules, multiply uncontrollably, and eventually invade surrounding tissue or spread to other organs.

## How Does Cancer Start? — The Receptors
Here is the most important concept to understand before anything else.
Breast cells have **receptors** — think of receptors as locks on the surface of a cell. Certain substances act as keys that fit into these locks and tell the cell what to do.
Three receptors matter most in breast cancer:
**1. Estrogen Receptor (ER)**
- Estrogen is the female hormone
- If a cancer cell has the ER lock, estrogen can stimulate it to grow
- We call this cancer **ER positive**
- ER positive cancers can be treated by blocking estrogen — this is why tamoxifen works
**2. Progesterone Receptor (PR)**
- Similar idea — progesterone stimulates growth if the receptor is present
- Almost always goes together with ER
**3. HER2 (Human Epidermal growth factor Receptor 2)**
- HER2 is a protein that tells cells to grow and divide
- Normally, cells have a small amount of HER2
- In some cancers, the HER2 gene is amplified — meaning too many copies are made — so the cell has way too much HER2 on its surface
- This makes the cancer grow very fast
- We call this **HER2 positive**
- The good news: we have a drug called trastuzumab (Herceptin) that specifically targets HER2
Now, based on whether these three receptors are present or absent, breast cancers fall into three groups:

## The Three Types of Breast Cancer
**Type 1 — Luminal (ER positive, HER2 negative)**
The word "luminal" just means the cancer cells look and behave like the normal cells that line the inside of the ducts and lobules — the lumen. These are the most well-behaved cancer cells.
- ER is present → estrogen feeds this cancer
- HER2 is absent → not driven by HER2
- Grows slowly
- Responds well to hormone-blocking drugs like tamoxifen
- Spreads mostly to bone
- Best prognosis of all three types
- Most common — about 50–65% of all breast cancers
**Type 2 — HER2 Positive**
- Too much HER2 protein on the cancer cell surface
- This makes the cancer divide very fast
- More aggressive than luminal
- Can be ER positive or ER negative
- Spreads to bone, organs, and brain
- Treated with trastuzumab which blocks HER2
- About 20% of breast cancers
**Type 3 — Triple Negative (TNBC)**
Triple negative simply means the cancer has none of the three receptors — no ER, no PR, no HER2. It is triple negative because all three tests come back negative.
This matters a lot because:
- ER negative → tamoxifen won't work
- HER2 negative → trastuzumab won't work
- No targeted therapy exists — the only option is chemotherapy
- It is the most aggressive type
- Grows fast, spreads fast, relapses early
- Most common in young women, women of African descent, and women with BRCA1 gene mutations

## How Each Type Develops — The Three Pathways
Think of each type having its own road from normal cell to cancer.
**Road 1 — Luminal Cancer**
Normal breast cell → acquires small mutations (especially in a gene called PIK3CA) → cells start looking slightly abnormal but are still inside the duct → this stage is called **flat epithelial atypia** → cells become more abnormal → **atypical ductal hyperplasia** (ADH) — abnormal cells are now multiplying more than normal but still contained → **DCIS** (cancer cells fully inside the duct, not yet broken out) → finally breaks through the duct wall → **invasive luminal cancer**
**Road 2 — TNBC**
Normal breast cell → BRCA1 gene is mutated (inherited or acquired) → TP53 gene also mutates → cells develop a p53 signature (early warning sign, hard to see under microscope) → **DCIS** → breaks out → **invasive TNBC**
**Road 3 — HER2 Cancer**
Normal breast cell → HER2 gene gets amplified (too many copies made) → cells grow fast → **DCIS** → breaks out → **HER2 positive invasive cancer**
Note: No clear precursor lesion has been identified for HER2 cancer before the DCIS stage.

## Before It Breaks Out — Carcinoma In Situ
"In situ" is Latin for "in its original place." This means the cancer cells are still trapped inside the duct or lobule. They cannot spread to other organs because they have no access to blood vessels or lymphatics yet.
Think of it like a fire inside a sealed room. It is dangerous but contained.
**DCIS — Ductal Carcinoma In Situ**
- Cancer cells inside the ducts, not yet broken through the wall
- Found almost always on mammogram — the cells die in the centre and calcify, and these calcifications show up on imaging
- Without mammogram screening, less than 5% of cancers are caught at this stage
- With screening, 15–30% are caught here — this is why screening matters
- If left untreated, it becomes invasive at about 1% per year
- Mastectomy at this stage cures more than 95% of women
It grows in different patterns inside the duct:
- **Comedo** — central part dies and becomes necrotic; high grade; calcifies heavily
- **Cribriform** — cells form round cookie-cutter like spaces inside the duct
- **Micropapillary** — cells form small finger-like projections but without a central blood vessel
- **Papillary** — true finger-like projections with a central blood vessel (fibrovascular core)
**LCIS — Lobular Carcinoma In Situ**
- Same idea but happening inside the lobules
- The cells lose E-cadherin — a protein that makes cells stick together. Without it, cells become loose and rounded
- Because they are loose and rounded, they do not form calcifications and do not cause any reaction in surrounding tissue
- This means LCIS is completely invisible on mammogram — always found by accident when biopsy is done for something else
- Bilateral in 20–40% of cases — meaning both breasts are often involved
- Always ER positive, HER2 negative
- In some cases it is a true precursor to invasive lobular carcinoma
**Paget Disease of the Nipple**
- Looks exactly like eczema on the nipple — red, itchy, crusty
- Easily mistaken and often treated as eczema for months before the real diagnosis is made
- What is actually happening: cancer cells from DCIS deep inside the breast travel up through the ducts all the way to the nipple skin
- The cancer cells disrupt the normal skin barrier, causing fluid to leak out and form the crust
- If a lump is felt alongside it — almost certainly invasive cancer underneath
- If no lump — usually DCIS only
- Usually ER negative and HER2 positive
- Prognosis depends on what is underneath, not on the Paget disease itself

## When It Breaks Out — Invasive Carcinoma
Once cancer breaks through the duct or lobule wall, it is invasive. Now it can access blood vessels and lymphatics and potentially spread anywhere in the body.
**No Special Type — Most Common**
- Hard, irregular lump
- Causes a reaction in surrounding tissue called desmoplasia — the stroma becomes dense and fibrous
- This is why it feels hard and makes a grating sound when cut during surgery
- Large tumors pull on the skin causing dimpling, or pull on the nipple causing it to retract inward
- Graded 1 to 3 based on how abnormal the cells look under the microscope
**Lobular Carcinoma**
- Happens when CDH1 gene (which makes E-cadherin) is lost
- Without E-cadherin, cells cannot stick together
- They infiltrate in a single file line — called the **Indian file pattern**
- Because there is no desmoplasia, the breast tissue feels normal on examination and looks normal on imaging — making it very easy to miss
- Spreads to unusual places: lining of the stomach and abdomen (peritoneum), lining of the brain (leptomeninges), ovaries, and GI tract — not the typical bone and lung pattern
**Medullary Pattern**
- Looks terrible under the microscope — high grade, poorly differentiated
- But paradoxically behaves better than expected
- Reason: it is surrounded by large numbers of lymphocytes (immune cells) that are actively attacking the tumor
- More than 50% are associated with BRCA1 mutations
- The immune response is what gives it a better prognosis

## Grading — How Bad Does It Look?
Nottingham grading scores three things — how well the cells form tubules, how abnormal the nuclei look, and how fast they are dividing (mitotic rate). Score gives Grade 1, 2, or 3.
- **Grade 1** — cells still look somewhat normal, growing slowly, forming tubules, good prognosis
- **Grade 2** — in between
- **Grade 3** — cells look very abnormal, dividing fast, no tubule formation, worst prognosis

## Genetics — The Key Genes
**BRCA1**
- Normally a tumor suppressor gene — it repairs DNA damage
- When mutated, DNA damage accumulates → cancer
- Leads mostly to TNBC
- Also increases risk of ovarian, fallopian tube, and pancreatic cancer
**BRCA2**
- Also a DNA repair gene
- When mutated, leads mostly to ER positive luminal cancer
- Most important genetic cause of breast cancer in men
- About 6% of men who carry BRCA2 mutations develop breast cancer
**CDH1**
- Makes E-cadherin — the cell glue protein
- When lost → cells become dyscohesive → lobular carcinoma
- Also causes hereditary diffuse gastric cancer (signet ring type)
**TP53**
- Makes p53 — the guardian of the genome
- When mutated → Li-Fraumeni syndrome
- Breast cancers tend to be ER and HER2 positive
- Also causes sarcomas, leukemias, brain tumors

## What Determines Survival — Prognostic Factors
**Lymph node status — number one factor**
- Axillary lymph nodes (under the armpit) are the first place breast cancer spreads
- The more nodes involved, the worse the prognosis
- Clinical examination is unreliable — biopsy is needed
**Tumor size**
- Smaller than 1 cm with no node involvement → more than 90% survive 10 years
- Larger than 2 cm → survival drops to 77%
**Distant metastasis**
- Once cancer spreads to distant organs, cure becomes very unlikely
- Long remissions are possible in ER positive disease with hormone therapy
**Lymphovascular invasion**
- Cancer cells found inside blood or lymph vessels
- Present in about half of invasive cancers
- Strongly associated with lymph node spread
**Inflammatory carcinoma**
- Cancer cells block the lymphatic channels inside the skin of the breast
- The breast becomes red, warm, swollen, and resembles an infection — but it is cancer
- Only 3–10% survive 3 years — worst prognosis of all breast cancer presentations
- More common in African descent women and younger women

## Male Breast Cancer
Men have very little breast tissue — just small ducts near the nipple. Cancer in men almost always presents as a lump directly under or near the nipple.
- Only 1% of all breast cancers
- More than 90% are luminal type (ER positive)
- BRCA2 is the most important genetic risk factor
- Because the breast tissue is so small, even small tumors can quickly reach the skin or chest wall
- Lymph nodes are involved in about 50% at diagnosis
- Treated the same way as women; same prognosis when matched for stage

## Quick Exam Reminders
- BRCA1 → TNBC; BRCA2 → ER positive luminal
- LCIS → invisible on mammogram; bilateral; always ER positive; found by accident
- DCIS → found on mammogram; mastectomy cures >95%
- Paget disease → looks like eczema; always has cancer underneath; check for a lump
- Lobular carcinoma → Indian file; spreads to stomach lining and brain lining; hard to detect
- Inflammatory carcinoma → looks like infection; worst prognosis; 3 yr survival 3–10%
- Axillary lymph node status → number one prognostic factor
- Medullary pattern → looks aggressive; behaves better; full of lymphocytes
- TNBC → no targeted therapy; chemo only; most aggressive; early relapse
