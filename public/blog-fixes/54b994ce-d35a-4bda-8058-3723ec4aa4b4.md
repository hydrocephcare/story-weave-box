### 1.0 Introduction
The Circulatory system is a continuous circuit comprising of the cardiovascular system (heart and blood vessels) and lymphatic system (lymph vessels, lymph nodes and lymphoid-organs -spleen, thymus and tonsils). Blood serves numerous functions including the transport of respiratory gases, nutritive molecules, metabolic wastes and hormones. Blood is transported through the body in a system of vessels leading from and returning to the heart.

Functions of the Circulatory System

1) Transportation
• Respiratory - red blood cells (erythrocytes) transport oxygen to tissue cells and carbon dioxide is transported by blood to the lungs foe elimination
• Nutritive - blood carries absorbed products of digestion through the liver to body cells
• Excretory - metabolic wastes, excessive water and ions are filtered through the capillaries of the kidney and excreted in urine

2) Regulation
• Blood carries hormones and other regulatory molecules from the site of origin to target tissues

3) Protection
   • Protects against injury and foreign microbes or toxins
   • Clotting mechanism prevents blood loss when vessels are damaged
   • Leucocytes (white blood cells) provide immunity

THE CARDIOVASCULAR SYSTEM

Is made up of the heart and blood vessels

The Heart

The heart is a four chambered; double pump that keeps blood flowing through the circulation and a normal heart weighs 250 – 350 g in an adult. Its pumping action creates the pressure needed to push blood in the vessels to the lungs and body cells.
The function of the heart is to pump sufficient oxygenated blood containing nutrients, metabolites and hormones to meet the moment to moment metabolic needs and preserve a constant milieu. The flow of blood through the circulatory system is made possible by the pumping action of the ventricles and atria

The Heart Pump

The heart has 2 main pumps that operate as connected in series i.e. the left pump (for the peripheral organs) and the right pump (for the lungs). The right and left pumps are separated by a muscular wall (septum) which prevents mixing of blood from the two sides of the heart. Each pump has 2 chambers (the ventricle and the atria). In detail the heart has 4 pumps of which 2 are primer pumps (the atria) and 2
power pumps (the ventricles). The atria receive blood from the venous system then pump it into the ventricles which pump blood into the arterial system.

The Heart Valves

Normal functioning of the heart depends on the mechanical efficiency of the 4 valves namely aortic valve, mitral valve, tricuspid valve and pulmonary valve. The atrioventricular valves (mitral and tricuspid valves) consist of 4 kinds of structures namely an annulus ring, cusps, papillary muscles and chordae tendinae. Normal functioning depends on the mechanical efficiency of the cusps, chordae tendinae, papillary muscles, pliability and size of the fibrous ring or annulus and adequacy of left ventricular contraction. The semi-lunar valves (aortic and pulmonary valves) each consist of 2 kinds of structures namely an annulus and semi-lunar cusps

The Conducting System

The contraction and relaxation of the heart’s musculature is highly coordinated during the cardiac cycle. The coordination is controlled by a system of highly specialized myocardial cells (the conducting system). The conducting system provide a nodular cluster of cells which initiate the conduction signal and supplies cellular tracts to propagate this signal through the atria and ventricular musculature. The 3 parts of the conducting system found in the walls of the right atrium are: - sino-atrial node (SAN),

THE LAWS

1. Starling’s Law
   • Increase dilatation leads to increased filling, contraction and stroke volume.
• During exertion, there is a greater venous return to the heart with subsequent increase in diastolic filling and the stretching of the muscle fibre. The response is a more vigorous contraction

2. Frank-Starling Law
• Within physiological limits, the heart pumps all the blood that comes to it without allowing excessive damming of blood in the veins
• The greater the heart is filled during diastole, the greater will the amount of blood pumped into the aorta

3. Laplaces Law
• The circumferential force tending to stretch the muscle fibres in the vessel wall is proportional to the to diameter of the vessel multiplied by the pressure inside the vessel
   • F=DXP
• Or the wall tension required to counteract a given pressure in a spherical cavity is proportional to the radius of the cavity

BLOOD VESSELS

•   Form a tubular network throughout the body that permits blood flow to flow from the heart to all the living cells of the body and back to the heart
•   The thick muscle layer of the arteries allows them to transmit blood ejected from the heart under high pressure and the elastic recoil of the larger arteries further contributes to blood flow
•   The thinner muscle layer of veins allows them to distend when an increased amount of blood enters them and their one-way valves ensure that blood flows back to the heart
•   Capillaries are composed of a single layer of endothelium which allows water and other molecules to move across the capillary walls permitting exchanges between

the blood and tissue fluid
•   The walls of arteries and veins have 3 coats(“tunics”) – tunica externa, media and intima
•   The arterial system branches extensively to deliver blood to over 40 billion capillaries in the body
•   Capillaries provide a total surface area of 1000 sq. Miles for exchanges between blood and tissue fluid
•   Pre-capillary sphincter muscles determine the amount of blood passing through a particular capillary bed
•   Blood flow through an organ is determined by the action of pre-capillary sphincters and the degree of resistance to blood flow due to constriction or dilatation of small arteries and arterioles in the organ
•   The walls of capillaries are composed of only one cell layer – a simple squamous epithelium or endothelium
•   Absence of smooth muscle and connective tissue layers allows rapid rate of transport of materials between the blood and tissues

Types of Capillaries

•   Different organs have different capillaries
•   Classified in terms of their endothelial lining
•   There are 3 types – continuous, discontinuous and fenestrated
•   Continuous capillaries found in muscles, lungs, adipose tissue and central nervous system (CNS) have their adjacent endothelial cells are closely joined together. In the CNS they lack intercellular channels a fact that contributes to the blood-brain barrier. In other organs they have narrow intercellular channels that allow passage of small molecules
•   Discontinuous capillaries found in bone marrow, liver and spleen appear as little cavities(sinusoids) and have the distance between endothelial cells is so great
•   Fenestrated capillaries found in endocrine glands, kidneys and intestines have wide intercellular pores covered by a layer serving as a diaphragm

### 5.0 PALPATION

•   Is an important examination method

### 5.1 Approach

•   Lay the whole hand flat on the chest wall to get a general impression of cardiac activity.
•   Now localize the apex beat and assess the character
•   Ask patient to roll onto the left side if necessary
•   Identify any other pulsations particularly in the parasternal area

### 5.2 Procedure

•   Should be carried out with the patient first in recumbent position and then the erect posture systematically.
•   The fingers are placed systematically over the apex area and over the base of the heart.
•   The palm of the hand should be placed on all areas of the preacordium in order to detect any abnormal pulsations or vibrations.
•   The cardiac impulse is usually circumscribed at the apex beat.
•   The palm is too large for accurate palpation; hence information obtained by it should be supplemented by use of fingers.
•   Palpation is a more reliable method of estimating the size of the heart.
•   In palpation, examine for:- the apex beat, heaves and thrills

### 6.0 APEX BEAT

•   Is the lower most, outer most point of maximum cardiac impulse (pulsation/activity) or the further point outward and downward at which the cardiac impulse can be palpated.

MURMURS

Introduction

•   Are musical sounds made by the turbulent blood flow in the heart.
•   Murmurs can be innocent (insignificant) or organic (significant).
•   Are produced by normal volume of blood passing through an abnormal valve or increased volume of blood passing through a normal valve (flow murmurs).
•   Originate at the valves or great vessels.
•   Tend to be propagated in the same direction of flow of blood.
•   When examining murmurs consider the following features.
       1. Site
       2. Timing
       3. Character
       4. Intensity/Pitch
       5. Radiation
       6. Relationship to respiration, posture and exercise
