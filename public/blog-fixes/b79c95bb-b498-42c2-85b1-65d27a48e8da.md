# Anaemias of Inadequate Red Cell Production

**Year 4 · Paediatrics · Haematology**

> **In one line:** these are anaemias in which the marrow fails to make enough red cells (low reticulocytes), either because red cell precursors are missing (Diamond-Blackfan anaemia, transient erythroblastopenia, parvovirus B19), because the drive or raw materials are lacking (chronic disease, renal disease, iron, folate, vitamin B12), or because DNA synthesis is impaired (megaloblastic anaemia). The MCV and the reticulocyte count point to the cause.

## What you must know

- **Diamond-Blackfan anaemia (DBA):** congenital pure red cell aplasia, macrocytic, low reticulocytes, **raised erythrocyte adenosine deaminase (eADA)**, 50% have anomalies (thumb), ribosomal protein gene mutations; treat with **prednisolone 2 mg/kg/day**, transfusion, or stem cell transplant.
- **Transient erythroblastopenia of childhood (TEC):** acquired, 6 months to 3 years, normocytic, **normal eADA and HbF**, recovers in 1 to 2 months, steroids useless.
- **Parvovirus B19** attacks erythroid progenitors via the **P antigen**; causes **aplastic crisis** in haemolytic anaemias (sickle cell disease, spherocytosis).
- **Anaemia of chronic disease:** normocytic, **low iron, low transferrin saturation, normal or low TIBC**, caused by **hepcidin**; treat the underlying disease.
- **Renal anaemia:** low **erythropoietin**; treat with ESAs plus iron.
- **Megaloblastic anaemia:** folate or B12 deficiency; macrocytosis, **hypersegmented neutrophils**; B12 deficiency also causes neurological damage and **folic acid alone can worsen it**.
- **Iron deficiency** is the commonest nutritional disorder worldwide; treat with **3 to 6 mg/kg/day elemental iron**, continue **2 to 3 months after Hb normalises**.

## 1. Approach

| MCV | Think of |
|---|---|
| **Microcytic** | Iron deficiency, thalassaemia, chronic disease (sometimes), lead |
| **Normocytic, low reticulocytes** | TEC, parvovirus, chronic disease, renal disease, marrow infiltration or failure |
| **Macrocytic** | Folate or B12 deficiency, DBA, drugs, hypothyroidism |

- A **low absolute reticulocyte count** in the face of anaemia means production failure.

## 2. Congenital hypoplastic anaemia (Diamond-Blackfan anaemia)

### 2.1 Background

- Rare congenital **bone marrow failure syndrome**, usually symptomatic in early infancy. More than 90% are recognised in the first year (92% in the first year per the slides).
- Incidence about **7 per million live births**. Profound anaemia usually by **2 to 6 months**; 25% are anaemic at birth; hydrops fetalis is rare.
- Mostly mutations in **ribosomal protein (RP) genes** (up to 70%), so it is a **ribosomopathy**, mostly **autosomal dominant**.

### 2.2 Clinical features

- Pallor and symptoms of anaemia from early infancy.
- **40 to 50%** have congenital anomalies; more than one anomaly in 25%.
  - **Craniofacial (50%):** snub nose, high-arched palate.
  - **Skeletal (30%), mostly upper limb/hand:** thumb abnormalities, flat thenar eminence, **triphalangeal thumb** (unilateral or bilateral); absent radial pulse possible.
  - **Genitourinary (38%), cardiac (30%)**, eye and musculoskeletal anomalies.
  - **Short stature** is common.

### 2.3 Investigations

- Anaemia usually **normochromic and macrocytic**; **no hypersegmented neutrophils** or other megaloblastic features on the smear.
- **Reticulocytopenia** despite severe anaemia.
- **Marrow:** normally cellular but with greatly reduced or absent red cell precursors; other lines normal.
- **Raised erythrocyte adenosine deaminase (eADA)** in most patients, which separates DBA from TEC.
- Platelets often raised (thrombocytosis), rarely low; occasional neutropenia.
- **Serum iron raised.**

### 2.4 Management

- **Corticosteroids** are the mainstay; about **80%** respond initially. Because steroids impair linear growth, physical and neurocognitive development, use **chronic transfusion** and delay steroids until after **1 year of age**.
- **Prednisone/prednisolone 2 mg/kg/day.** Marrow red cell precursors rise at **1 to 3 weeks**, then reticulocytosis; Hb normalises in **4 to 6 weeks**.
- Once Hb is rising, taper gradually to the lowest effective single daily dose, then double it and give on alternate days, tapering further while keeping **Hb at 9 g/dL or more**.
- **Chronic transfusion** in about **35%**: never steroid-responsive (30%), steroid-refractory (15%), or cannot be weaned to a low dose (50%). Give every **3 to 5 weeks** to keep **Hb above 8 g/dL** (some younger children need above 9 g/dL for growth and activity). Screen for iron overload and start **chelation**.
- **Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation** can cure.
- **Spontaneous remission** occurs (steroid/transfusion independent): about **25% by age 25**, most in the first decade.

### 2.5 Prognosis

- DBA is a **cancer predisposition syndrome**: myelodysplastic syndrome, AML, colon carcinoma, osteogenic sarcoma, female genital cancers.
- Iron overload causes endocrine problems (diabetes, hypogonadism).
- Overall survival about **75% at 40 years**: about **87% on steroids**, about **57% if transfusion-dependent**. Of deaths, 67% were treatment-related and 22% DBA-related (malignancy, severe aplastic anaemia).

## 3. Acquired pure red cell aplasia: transient erythroblastopenia of childhood (TEC)

- **Commonest acquired red cell aplasia** in children, more common than DBA.
- Previously healthy children aged **6 months to 3 years** (mostly over 12 months); only 10% are over 3 years.
- Incidence about **4.3 per 100,000** children (probably underestimated as it resolves spontaneously).
- Suppression of erythropoiesis linked to **IgG, IgM and cell-mediated** mechanisms; familial cases occur. Often follows a **viral illness** (no single virus implicated).
- **Findings:** reticulocytopenia, moderate to severe **normocytic** anaemia; **MCV normal** for age; **HbF normal** (before recovery); neutropenia in up to 20%; platelets normal or high; **eADA normal**.
- **Course:** virtually all recover in **1 to 2 months**. Anaemia develops slowly so symptoms occur only when severe. **Transfuse** only for severe anaemia with no sign of recovery. **Steroids are of no value.**

> **Exam tip:** TEC versus DBA: TEC is older (after 1 year), normocytic, normal HbF and eADA, recovers spontaneously. DBA is under 1 year, macrocytic, raised eADA, other anomalies. A child with presumed TEC needing **more than one transfusion** should be reassessed.

| Feature | DBA | TEC |
|---|---|---|
| Age | Under 1 year | 6 months to 3 years |
| MCV | Macrocytic | Normal |
| eADA | Raised | Normal |
| HbF | Raised | Normal until recovery |
| Anomalies | 40 to 50% | None |
| Course | Chronic | Resolves in 1 to 2 months |

## 4. Red cell aplasia with parvovirus B19

- Parvovirus B19 causes **erythema infectiosum (fifth disease)**. It is a small, non-enveloped, single-stranded DNA virus that is cytotoxic to erythroid progenitors by binding the **red cell P antigen**.
- Best documented viral cause of red cell aplasia in **chronic haemolysis, immunocompromise and in utero**.
- In healthy children (red cell life span 100 to 120 days) the pause lasts under 2 weeks and anaemia is absent or unnoticed.
- In shortened red cell survival (**hereditary spherocytosis, immune haemolysis, sickle cell disease**) a brief stop in production causes severe anaemia: **aplastic crisis**. It usually occurs **once** per child.
- **Diagnosis:** parvovirus **IgM and IgG** and **PCR**. In immunodeficient patients serology is impaired, so use **PCR of blood or marrow**.
- **Treatment:** recovery is usually spontaneous; transfuse if symptomatic. **Persistent infection** (congenital immunodeficiency, lymphoproliferative disease, immunosuppression, **HIV/AIDS**) is treated with **high-dose IV immunoglobulin** (short-term effect).
- **In utero:** destruction of erythroid precursors causes fetal loss in the 1st and 2nd trimesters, or **hydrops fetalis** with anaemia.

> **Remember:** in sickle cell disease, an aplastic crisis shows a sudden fall in Hb with a **very low reticulocyte count**, unlike a haemolytic or sequestration crisis where reticulocytes are high.

## 5. Anaemia of chronic disease (anaemia of inflammation)

- Occurs with ongoing immune activation: **infections (TB, HIV), malignancy, chronic kidney disease, autoimmune disease, graft-versus-host disease**.
- Typically **mild to moderate**, normocytic and normochromic, hypoproliferative anaemia with **low serum iron and low transferrin saturation**.
- **Mechanisms:** shortened red cell life span, impaired erythropoiesis, and iron trapping in reticuloendothelial cells.
  - Cytokines such as **IL-1** increase macrophage ingestion and destruction of red cells.
  - Cytokines inhibit **erythropoietin** production and suppress marrow.
  - **Hepcidin** (made mainly by hepatocytes, also monocytes and macrophages) is raised by inflammation. It binds and degrades the iron exporter **ferroportin**, so iron is trapped in macrophages and gut absorption falls, causing functional iron deficiency and iron-restricted erythropoiesis.
- **Laboratory:** Hb usually **6 to 9 g/dL**; sometimes modest microcytosis and hypochromia (especially with coexisting iron deficiency); reticulocytes normal or low; leukocytosis common; serum iron low **without the rise in transferrin (TIBC) seen in iron deficiency**; ferritin normal or high (acute phase reactant).
- **Treatment:** treat the underlying disorder. Transfusion is temporary and rarely indicated. **ESAs (recombinant EPO)** raise Hb and well-being; iron is usually needed alongside. ACD does not respond to iron alone unless there is true iron deficiency.

## 6. Anaemia of renal disease

- Common in **chronic kidney disease (CKD)**; most children with **ESRD** are anaemic. Hb falls as **GFR drops below about 43 mL/min/1.73 m2**.
- **Main cause:** **reduced erythropoietin** from diseased kidneys. Others: absolute or functional iron deficiency (blood sampling, surgery, dialysis losses, disturbed iron metabolism), inflammation (raised proinflammatory cytokines in dialysis patients), and vitamin deficiency.
- **Laboratory:** hypoproliferative, **normocytic, normochromic** (unless iron/vitamin deficiency); **EPO level and reticulocytes low**; white cells and platelets normal; ferritin low if iron deficient. Anaemia is defined by age-specific Hb.
- **Treatment:**
  - **Oral iron** for all anaemic CKD children; **IV iron** for maintenance haemodialysis or oral failure.
  - **ESAs are the mainstay**; they greatly reduce transfusions, iron overload and **alloimmunisation**. **Darbepoetin** is equally effective with a longer half-life (less frequent dosing). Give iron with ESAs because erythropoiesis needs more iron.
  - Rarely, **anti-EPO antibody-mediated pure red cell aplasia**: stop the ESA and consider immunomodulation.

## 7. Megaloblastic anaemias

- Caused by **impaired DNA synthesis**: red cells are large at every stage, with **nuclear-cytoplasmic asynchrony**. Myeloid and platelet precursors are affected too (giant metamyelocytes and bands in marrow; often leucopenia and thrombocytopenia).
- Smear: large, often **oval (macro-ovalocytes)** red cells, raised MCV, **hypersegmented neutrophils (more than 5 lobes)**.
- Most childhood cases are due to **folate or vitamin B12 deficiency**; rarely inborn errors of metabolism.

### 7.1 Folate deficiency

**Physiology**
- Folates must be reduced to **tetrahydrofolate** by **dihydrofolate reductase** and are essential for DNA replication and cell proliferation.
- Humans cannot synthesise folate: sources are **green vegetables, fruit, liver, kidney**. It is **heat-labile and water-soluble**, so boiling destroys it.
- Dietary polyglutamates are hydrolysed and absorbed in the **proximal small intestine**; carried in blood as methyltetrahydrofolate.
- Body stores are small: on a folate-free diet, megaloblastic anaemia occurs after **2 to 3 months**.

**Causes**
- **Inadequate intake / increased demand:** pregnancy, rapid growth (premature or ill infants), chronic haemolysis. **Goat's milk** is folate deficient and powdered milk may be poor unless supplemented; breast milk, formula and pasteurised cow's milk are adequate. Peak incidence of megaloblastic anaemia from folate deficiency is **4 to 7 months** (earlier than iron deficiency; both may coexist). **Malnutrition** is the commonest cause in older children; haemoglobinopathies and infections increase risk.
- **Malabsorption:** chronic diarrhoea (also disrupts enterohepatic circulation), coeliac disease, chronic infective enteritis, enteroenteric fistulas; **anticonvulsants (phenytoin, primidone, phenobarbital)** impair absorption (frank anaemia is rare and responds to folate even if the drug continues).
- Disorders of folate metabolism or transport (congenital).
- Give **folate supplements from the start of pregnancy** to prevent neural tube defects and meet fetal needs.

**Clinical features:** features of anaemia plus **irritability, chronic diarrhoea, poor weight gain**; bleeding if thrombocytopenic. Congenital folate malabsorption: hypogammaglobulinaemia, severe infections, failure to thrive, neurological abnormalities, cognitive delay.

**Laboratory**
- Macrocytic anaemia (**MCV above 100 fL**), variable cell shape and size, low reticulocytes, megaloblastic nucleated red cells, large hypersegmented neutrophils; neutropenia and thrombocytopenia in severe, long-standing disease.
- Marrow hypercellular with erythroid hyperplasia and megaloblastic change.
- **Serum folate:** normal 5 to 20 ng/mL, deficiency **below 3 ng/mL**. **Red cell folate** is a better marker of chronic deficiency (normal 150 to 600 ng/mL of packed cells).
- Serum iron and B12 normal or high. **LDH markedly raised** (ineffective erythropoiesis).

**Treatment**
- **Folic acid 0.5 to 1.0 mg/day** orally or parenterally for **3 to 4 weeks** until a definite haematological response; then maintenance with a multivitamin (0.2 mg folate).
- If the diagnosis is uncertain, give **0.1 mg/day for 1 week** as a test; a response is expected within **72 hours**.
- Doses above 0.1 mg can correct the anaemia of B12 deficiency but may **worsen neurological damage**.
- Transfuse only if severe anaemia or very ill.

### 7.2 Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) deficiency

**Physiology**
- Water-soluble, central cobalt atom with a corrin ring. Active forms **methylcobalamin** and **adenosylcobalamin** are cofactors for: (1) **methylation of homocysteine to methionine**; (2) **methylmalonyl-CoA to succinyl-CoA** (methylmalonyl-CoA mutase).
- Made only by microorganisms; humans depend on **animal products** (meat, eggs, fish, milk).
- Stores last **3 to 5 years** in older children and adults; infants of B12-deficient mothers present at **6 to 18 months**.
- **Absorption:** released from food by gastric peptic digestion, binds **haptocorrin** (salivary), pancreatic proteases free it in the duodenum, it binds **intrinsic factor** (from gastric parietal cells), and the **Cbl-IF complex is taken up in the distal ileum** by receptor-mediated endocytosis; IF is degraded in lysosomes and Cbl released (carried in blood by transcobalamin).

**Causes**
| Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Inadequate intake** | Infants of B12-deficient mothers (breast milk low; mother with pernicious anaemia, *H. pylori*, coeliac, Crohn, pancreatic insufficiency, gastric bypass, PPIs); strict unsupplemented vegan/vegetarian diet. Infants are born with low stores, so onset is gradual, usually first year. Longer deficiency means more permanent disability |
| **Impaired absorption** | Gastric surgery, acid-suppressing drugs (low IF); pancreatic insufficiency; necrotising enterocolitis, IBD, coeliac, terminal ileal resection; **bacterial overgrowth** (diverticula, duplications; responds to antibiotics); **fish tapeworm *Diphyllobothrium latum*** |
| **Hereditary IF deficiency (HIFD)** | Autosomal recessive IF gene mutations |
| **Imerslund-Grasbeck syndrome** | Autosomal recessive selective ileal B12 malabsorption; presents in first 6 years with megaloblastic anaemia, neurological defects (hypotonia, developmental delay, brain atrophy, movement disorder, dementia) and **proteinuria**; fatal if untreated; early IM or intranasal B12 reverses changes |
| **Juvenile pernicious anaemia** | Autoimmune gastritis (anti-IF and anti-H+/K+ ATPase antibodies), adolescence; may have candidiasis, hypoparathyroidism, other endocrine deficiency; gastric atrophy and achlorhydria; lifelong IM or intranasal B12 |
| **Transcobalamin deficiency** | Autosomal recessive; presents in first weeks; **total serum B12 often normal**; severe megaloblastic anaemia with normal folate; diagnosed by plasma TC level; needs high serum B12 (high-dose oral, IM or intranasal) |

**Clinical features**
- Non-specific: weakness, lethargy, feeding difficulty, failure to thrive, irritability; pallor, **glossitis**, vomiting, diarrhoea, jaundice (icterus).
- **Neurological:** paraesthesia, sensory deficits, hypotonia, seizures, developmental delay or **regression**, neuropsychiatric change, MRI changes of brain/spine. Neurological problems **may occur without anaemia**.

**Laboratory**
- Same blood picture as folate deficiency: macrocytic, marked macro-ovalocytosis, hypersegmented neutrophils; in advanced cases neutropenia and thrombocytopenia mimic aplastic anaemia or leukaemia.
- **Low serum B12; raised methylmalonic acid (MMA) and homocysteine**; iron and folate normal or high; LDH markedly raised.
- Folate deficiency has raised homocysteine but **normal MMA**.

**Diagnosis**
- Careful history (diet, disease, surgery, drugs), examination (irritability, pallor, neurology).
- Total or transcobalamin-bound B12; assays have **frequent false positives and negatives**, so do not discount B12 deficiency when there are symptoms, macrocytic anaemia, abnormal smear and normal folate. MMA and total homocysteine are very useful in untreated patients; urinary MMA is also sensitive.
- If confirmed without dietary or maternal cause, **investigate malabsorption**. The **Schilling test** is no longer available. Use **anti-IF and anti-parietal cell antibodies** for pernicious anaemia; specialised testing for rarer disorders.

**Treatment**
- Dose and route depend on the cause; evidence in children is limited. **Cyanocobalamin nasal spray** is an alternative to injections.
- Physiological need is **1 to 3 micrograms/day**. A minidose can serve as a **therapeutic test** when diagnosis is doubtful, or when anaemia is so severe that higher initial doses might cause severe metabolic disturbance.
- Adjust to clinical status and laboratory results.

> **Exam tip:** never treat suspected B12 deficiency with folic acid alone: the anaemia improves but the **neurological damage progresses**.

## 8. Iron deficiency anaemia

### 8.1 Epidemiology and iron balance

- The **commonest nutritional disorder worldwide**; **30 to 50%** of the global population has iron deficiency anaemia, mostly in developing countries.
- Newborn: about **0.5 g** iron; adult: about **5 g**. So about **0.8 mg/day** must be absorbed during the first 15 years, with about **1 mg/day** needed to stay in positive balance. As under 10% of dietary iron is absorbed, a dietary intake of **8 to 10 mg/day** is required.
- Breast and cow's milk contain about 1 mg/L iron. **Breastfed infants absorb iron 2 to 3 times better** than cow's-milk-fed infants, but still need iron-fortified foods by 6 months.
- Haem iron is **10 times more bioavailable** than non-haem iron.

### 8.2 Aetiology

- Neonatal iron is mostly in haemoglobin; as the high Hb falls over the first 2 to 3 months, iron is recycled. Stores last **6 to 9 months** in term infants but run out sooner in **premature, low-birthweight and perinatal blood loss** infants.
- **Cord clamping:** delayed (1 to 3 min) improves iron status; early (under 30 s) increases risk.
- Dietary iron deficiency alone typically causes anaemia at **9 to 24 months**, uncommon later. Worldwide, undernutrition is the usual cause.
- **Blood loss must be considered in every case:** menstruation, recurrent epistaxis, intravascular haemolysis with haemoglobinuria (e.g. malaria), **GI lesions** (peptic ulcer, **Meckel diverticulum**, polyp, haemangioma, IBD), **whole cow's milk protein enteropathy** in infants (earlier and more severe anaemia than diet alone predicts; prevent by breastfeeding or delaying and limiting cow's milk in the first year), chronic diarrhoea.
- **Parasites:** **hookworm, *Trichuris trichiura*, *Plasmodium*** (very relevant in Kenya).
- **Impaired absorption** (proximal duodenum, needs gastric acid): gastric bypass, ***H. pylori***, coeliac disease, giardiasis.
- Adolescents: about 2% of girls (growth spurt, menses); over 30% of teenagers who are or have been pregnant.

### 8.3 Clinical features

- Most children are asymptomatic, found on screening at **9 to 12 months**.
- **Pallor** (palms, palmar creases, nail beds, conjunctivae) is visible only when **Hb is 7 to 8 g/dL or lower**.
- Older children: cold intolerance, fatigue, exercise dyspnoea, poor concentration.
- Hb 6 to 10 g/dL: compensation (raised **2,3-DPG**, right shift of the oxygen dissociation curve) means few symptoms except mild irritability.
- Hb **below 5 g/dL**: irritability, anorexia, lethargy, systolic flow murmurs; further fall causes tachycardia and **high-output cardiac failure**.
- **Non-haematological effects:** impaired neurocognitive function in infancy, with possibly irreversible later cognitive deficits; **pica** (may cause **lead poisoning**), **pagophagia** (ice craving).

### 8.4 Investigations

- FBC and peripheral smear (microcytic hypochromic, pencil cells, anisocytosis); **serum iron, TIBC and ferritin** (low iron, high TIBC, low ferritin).
- Tests for blood loss and other causes: urine for haemosiderin/haemoglobin, **pulmonary haemosiderosis**, stool microscopy for ova and occult blood.
- **Haemoglobin electrophoresis** with HbA2 and HbF; **reticulocyte haemoglobin content**.

### 8.5 Differential diagnosis of microcytic anaemia

- **Alpha- and beta-thalassaemia** and other haemoglobinopathies (HbE, HbC): the commonest alternatives.
- **Anaemia of inflammation**: usually normocytic, sometimes microcytic.
- **Lead poisoning**: more often iron deficiency causes pica and then secondary lead intoxication.

### 8.6 Prevention

- Encourage **breastfeeding with iron supplement from 4 months**.
- Non-breastfed infants: **iron-fortified formula (12 mg iron/L)** for the first year; afterwards limit cow's milk to **under 500 mL/day**.
- **Screen with Hb or haematocrit at 9 to 12 months** (earlier if high-risk at 4 months); CBC for children with risk factors.

### 8.7 Treatment

- **Oral ferrous salts** (most often ferrous sulfate): cheap and effective.
- Dose by **elemental iron: 3 to 6 mg/kg/day** in 1 or 2 doses (higher in severe cases); **maximum 150 to 200 mg elemental iron/day**. Ferrous sulfate is **20% elemental iron**. Give **between meals with vitamin C-containing juice**.
- **Inhibitors:** calcium, fibre, **tea** (strong inhibitor); overcome with vitamin C. Intolerance is uncommon in young children; GI upset in older children may improve with lower doses.
- **Parenteral iron** (low-molecular-weight iron dextran, iron sucrose, ferric carboxymaltose, ferric gluconate) only if malabsorption or poor compliance.
- Iron may increase virulence of **malaria** and some gram-negative bacteria in developing countries; iron overdose is linked to *Yersinia* infection.
- **Diet:** limit cow's milk, increase dietary iron (haem sources). Adolescent menorrhagia: iron plus hormonal control of menses.
- **Monitoring:** reticulocytosis in **48 to 96 hours** (earlier confirmation in severe cases); Hb rises **0.1 to 0.4 g/dL/day**; in mild cases repeat FBC at **1 month** (Hb up by at least 1 to 2 g/dL, often normal). Continue iron for **2 to 3 months after normalisation** to rebuild stores.
- **Poor response:** reconsider the diagnosis (thalassaemia, ongoing loss, non-adherence, malabsorption, chronic disease).
- **Transfusion** rarely: only when heart failure is imminent or severe anaemia with major ongoing blood loss; give slowly to avoid precipitating heart failure unless actively bleeding.

> **Remember:** in practice in Kenya, treat malaria, deworm (albendazole/mebendazole), and give iron; MoH guidelines also recommend routine iron-folate supplementation in pregnancy.

## Key points to revise

1. DBA: macrocytic, low reticulocytes, raised eADA, thumb and craniofacial anomalies, ribosomal protein genes; prednisolone 2 mg/kg/day, transfusion, or HSCT; cancer risk.
2. TEC: age 6 months to 3 years, normocytic, normal eADA and HbF, resolves in 1 to 2 months, no steroids.
3. Parvovirus B19 enters via the P antigen; aplastic crisis in haemolytic anaemias; IVIG for persistent infection; PCR for immunodeficient patients.
4. Anaemia of chronic disease: hepcidin traps iron; low iron and low TIBC, normocytic; treat the cause; ESA plus iron.
5. Renal anaemia: low EPO; ESAs (epoetin or darbepoetin) plus iron are the mainstay.
6. Megaloblastic anaemia: macro-ovalocytes, hypersegmented neutrophils, high LDH; folate 0.5 to 1 mg/day; B12 as IM or intranasal, and never folate alone.
7. B12 absorption needs gastric IF and a healthy terminal ileum; B12 deficiency has high MMA and homocysteine; folate deficiency has normal MMA.
8. Iron deficiency: 3 to 6 mg/kg/day elemental iron, continue 2 to 3 months after normalisation; always look for blood loss and hookworm.
