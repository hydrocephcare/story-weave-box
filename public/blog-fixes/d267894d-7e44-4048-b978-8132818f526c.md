## CVS History Taking & Physical Examination
## 1. CARDIAC SYMPTOMS
**Chest pain**
- Acute, severe → myocardial ischaemia, pericarditis, aortic dissection, PE (most common causes)
- Chronic, recurrent → angina, oesophageal reflux, musculoskeletal pain
- **Myocardial ischaemia** — imbalance between myocardial O2 supply and demand → angina
- **Pericarditis** — central, sharp, worse on deep inspiration/cough/postural change, worse lying flat, better sitting forward
- **Aortic dissection** — severe tearing pain, front or back of chest, abrupt onset (unlike crescendo ischaemic pain)
- **Pulmonary embolism** — peripheral PE: sudden sharp pleuritic pain + breathlessness + haemoptysis; major/central PE: breathlessness + chest pain that mimics ischaemic pain + syncope
**Dyspnoea** — abnormal awareness of breathing at rest or unexpectedly low exertion; major symptom of left heart failure
**Causes of heart failure** (by mechanism)
| Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|
| Restricted filling | Mitral stenosis, constrictive pericarditis, restrictive cardiomyopathy, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy |
| Pressure loading | Hypertension, aortic stenosis, coarctation of the aorta |
| Volume loading | Mitral regurgitation, aortic regurgitation |
| Contractile impairment | Coronary artery disease, dilated cardiomyopathy, myocarditis |
| Arrhythmia | Severe bradycardia, severe tachycardia |
**Orthopnoea** — lying flat → steep rise in left atrial/pulmonary capillary pressure → pulmonary congestion + severe dyspnoea; relieved by pillows or sleeping upright in a chair
**Paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea (PND)** — sudden breathlessness during sleep, waking the patient gasping; caused by frank pulmonary oedema; relieved by standing (gravitational pooling lowers left atrial/pulmonary capillary pressure); patient often seeks air at an open window
**Fatigue and palpitations**
- Fatigue — typically exertional in heart disease
- Palpitations — common with exertion/emotion; may indicate abnormal rhythm
- Rapid **irregular** palpitations → atrial fibrillation
- Rapid **regular**, abrupt-onset palpitations → atrial, junctional, or ventricular tachyarrhythmias
**Dizziness and syncope**
- Due to transient hypotension → abrupt cerebral hypoperfusion
- Usually brief lightheadedness or no warning at all before syncope
- Recovery is rapid (unlike stroke, epilepsy, overdose)
Causes:
- **Postural hypotension** — syncope on standing; inadequate baroreceptor-mediated vasoconstriction; common in elderly
- **Vasovagal syncope** — autonomic overactivity from emotional/painful stimuli, or cough ("cough syncope")/micturition ("micturition syncope")
- **Carotid sinus hypersensitivity** — exaggerated response to carotid sinus pressure → bradycardia, vasodilation, hypotension, possible loss of consciousness
- **Valvular obstruction** — fixed aortic stenosis prevents normal rise in cardiac output on exertion → abrupt BP/cerebral perfusion drop → syncope; vasodilator therapy can cause similar syncope; left atrial myxoma/thrombus can intermittently obstruct the mitral valve
- **Stokes-Adams attacks** — periodic fainting from intermittent complete heart block/high-grade arrhythmia → loss of spontaneous circulation and cerebral blood flow

## 2. PHYSICAL EXAMINATION
### Full examination routine (standard sequence)
1. Wash hands; introduce yourself; recline patient at 45°
2. Observe general appearance — comfortable, breathless, pale?
3. Inspect hands — clubbing, splinter haemorrhages, nicotine staining
4. Examine radial pulse(s) — symmetry, rate, rhythm, character (collapsing?)
5. Measure blood pressure
6. Assess height and waveform of JVP
7. Examine carotid pulse — character (slow-rising?) and volume (Corrigan's sign?)
8. Inspect face, eyes, mucous membranes — xanthelasma, corneal arcus, anaemia, cyanosis
9. Inspect chest — scars, pulsations
10. Assess position and character of apex beat
11. Palpate praecordium — heaves and thrills
12. Auscultate the heart
13. Auscultate the lungs
14. Examine ankles and sacrum for oedema
15. Examine peripheral pulses
### Inspection findings
- Pectus excavatum — may compress heart, displace apex
- Median sternotomy scar — prior CABG and/or valve surgery
- Prominent venous collaterals on chest wall — SVC obstruction
- Pallor — anaemia may worsen angina and heart failure
**Cyanosis**
- Peripheral — vasoconstriction slows blood flow, ↑ O2 extraction in skin/lips
- Central — reduced arterial O2 saturation from cardiac/pulmonary disease; affects skin AND oral mucous membranes
**Clubbing** — enlargement of fingertips + downward sloping of nails; check with **Schamroth sign** (loss of diamond-shaped window when opposing nails placed together)
**Other general signs**
- Cold extremities → reduced cardiac output in heart failure
- Pyrexia → consider infective endocarditis
- Pitting oedema → cardinal feature of CCF; press over bony prominence (tibia, lateral malleoli, sacrum) for effective compression
### Arterial pulse
**Rate and rhythm** — palpate right radial pulse; rate = beats counted over 15 sec × 4
- Normal sinus rhythm is regular; young patients may show sinus arrhythmia (phasic variation with respiration)
- Irregular rhythm → usually AF; also frequent ectopics or self-limiting paroxysmal arrhythmias
**Character** — volume + waveform; best assessed at the **right carotid** (closest to heart, least distortion)
- Small volume → heart failure
- Large volume → aortic regurgitation
- Waveform is of greater diagnostic importance than volume alone
### Blood pressure measurement
**Mechanics** — cuff occludes brachial artery blood flow; as cuff deflates, systolic = point where cuff pressure < systolic pressure (pulse audible); diastolic = point where cuff pressure < diastolic pressure
**Preparation**
- Patient resting quietly ≥5 min in a chair
- Empty bladder; no eating, caffeine, smoking, or exercise for ≥30 min prior
- No talking during the procedure
- Correct cuff size: bladder width ≈ 40% of arm circumference; bladder length ≈ 80–100% of arm circumference
  - Too large a cuff → falsely low readings
  - Too small a cuff → falsely high readings
- Cuff lower edge 2–3 cm above elbow crease; bladder centred over brachial artery
- Seated, back supported, arm supported at heart level, legs uncrossed
**Auscultatory technique**
- Inflate rapidly to 30 mmHg above the point the brachial/radial pulse disappears
- Deflate at ~2 mmHg per heartbeat
- **Systolic** = appearance of Korotkoff sounds; **Diastolic** = disappearance of Korotkoff sounds (if sounds persist to zero, use point of muffling)
- Record to nearest 2 mmHg; avoid terminal digit preference (rounding to a zero)
- Take readings in both arms at initial visit; use the higher-reading arm for follow-up; take ≥2 readings per visit and average
**BP classification**
| Category | Systolic (mmHg) | Diastolic (mmHg) |
|---|---|---|
| Low | <90 | <60 |
| Normal | <120 | <80 |
| Pre-hypertension | 120–139 | 80–89 |
| Stage 1 hypertension | 140–159 | 90–99 |
| Stage 2 hypertension | ≥160 | ≥100 |
### Jugular Venous Pulse (JVP)
**Distinguishing JVP from carotid pulse — "POLICE"**
| Feature | JVP |
|---|---|
| Palpation | Non-palpable |
| Occlusion | Readily occludable |
| Location | Between heads of sternocleidomastoid; lateral to carotid |
| Inspiration | Drops with inspiration |
| Contour | Biphasic waveform |
| Erection/position | Drops when sitting upright |
**Measurement**
- Sternal angle (angle of Louis) ≈ 5 cm from centre of right atrium — constant reference point regardless of position
- Recline patient 30–90°, neck muscles relaxed; shine light tangentially across skin over the internal jugular vein to see the oscillating top of the venous column
- Draw one horizontal line at the top of the pulsation, one at the sternal angle; add the vertical distance between them to 5 cm (e.g., 4 + 5 = 9 cm)
- Convert to mmHg: 1.3 cm of water = 1 mmHg
- Normal JVP does not exceed 7 mmHg (e.g., 9 cm / 1.3 ≈ 6.9 mmHg)
**Causes of elevated JVP**
- Congestive heart failure
- Cor pulmonale
- Pulmonary embolism
- Right ventricular infarction
- Tricuspid valve disease
- Cardiac tamponade
- Constrictive pericarditis
- Hypertrophic/restrictive cardiomyopathy
- Superior vena cava obstruction
- Iatrogenic fluid overload (esp. surgical/renal patients)
### Palpation of the chest wall
- **Apex beat** — lowest, most lateral point where cardiac impulse is palpated; normal location = 5th intercostal space, mid-clavicular line
- Inferior/lateral displacement → cardiac enlargement
- Left parasternal systolic thrust (heave) → right ventricular enlargement
- Also palpate for thrills
### Auscultation of the heart
**4 auscultation areas** correspond loosely to the 4 valves:
- Apex → mitral
- Lower left sternal edge → tricuspid
- Upper left sternal edge → pulmonary
- Upper right sternal edge → aortic
**Heart sounds**
- **S1** — mitral + tricuspid valve closure, onset of systole
- **S2** — aortic + pulmonary valve closure, following ventricular ejection
**Full auscultation routine**
1. Auscultate apex with diaphragm
2. Reposition patient on left side; listen with diaphragm (mitral regurgitation) then bell (mitral stenosis)
3. Return patient to 45° reclining
4. Diaphragm at lower left sternal edge — tricuspid regurgitation, tricuspid stenosis, VSD
5. Diaphragm at upper left sternal edge — pulmonary stenosis, pulmonary regurgitation, PDA
6. Diaphragm at upper right sternal edge — aortic stenosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy
7. Sit patient forward; diaphragm at lower left sternal edge in held expiration — aortic regurgitation ("breathe in… breathe out… stop")
8. Auscultate carotid arteries — radiation of aortic stenosis murmur, carotid bruits

## 3. INVESTIGATIONS
### ECG
**Basic electrophysiology**
- Resting cardiac cells are polarized (inside negative relative to outside), maintained by membrane ion pumps (K+, Na+, Cl-, Ca2+)
- **Depolarization** — influx of Na+/Ca2+ makes cell interior less negative → contraction
- **Repolarization** — restoration of resting polarity
- Depolarization spreads cell-to-cell across the heart as a wave, detectable by surface electrodes
**3 cell types**
1. **Pacemaker cells** — spontaneous, repetitive depolarization; rate set by intrinsic properties + neurohormonal input. **SA node** = dominant pacemaker, located high in right atrium, fires 60–100 bpm
2. **Electrical conducting cells** — rapid, efficient current transmission (e.g., Bachmann's bundle → rapid left atrial activation)
3. **Myocardial cells** — contractile machinery; depolarization triggers intracellular Ca2+ release → actin-myosin interaction → contraction
**Ectopic pacemakers** — every cardiac cell can act as a pacemaker; normally suppressed unless SA node fails or abnormal stimulation occurs (sympathetic drive, disease)
**ECG paper**
- Small square = 1×1 mm = 0.04 sec (horizontal) / 0.1 mV (vertical)
- Large square = 5×5 mm = 0.2 sec / 0.5 mV
**Waves and intervals**
- **P wave** — atrial depolarization; right atrium first (first part of P wave), then left (second part); amplitude ≤0.25 mV (2.5 mm); most positive lead II, most negative aVR
- **PR interval** — start of atrial depolarization → start of ventricular depolarization; includes AV nodal delay; normal **0.12–0.2 sec**
- **PR segment** — end of atrial depolarization → start of ventricular depolarization; usually flat, along baseline
- AV node delay allows atrial contraction to finish before ventricles begin
- **QRS complex** — ventricular depolarization; amplitude >> P wave (ventricles have far more muscle mass)
  - Q wave = first deflection, downward
  - R wave = first upward deflection
  - R' = second upward deflection
  - S wave = first downward deflection after an upward one
  - QS wave = entirely one downward deflection
  - Septal Q waves — from left-to-right septal depolarization; visible in leads I, aVL, V5, V6
  - Earliest QRS = septal depolarization (septal fascicle of left bundle); bulk of QRS = left ventricular activation (LV mass ~3x RV)
  - Normal QRS duration: **0.06–0.1 sec**
- **ST segment** — end ventricular depolarization → start ventricular repolarization; normally flat or gently upsloping
- **T wave** — ventricular repolarization; amplitude ⅓–⅔ of R wave
  - Normal T inversion — leads with dominantly negative QRS (aVR, V1, sometimes III)
  - Pathological T inversion — ventricular hypertrophy, myocardial ischaemia, MI
  - Peaked T wave — earliest ECG sign of STEMI; also seen in hyperkalaemia
  - Atrial repolarization wave is hidden within the QRS (coincides with ventricular depolarization)
- **QT interval** — start ventricular depolarization → end ventricular repolarization; inversely proportional to heart rate (faster HR → shorter QT); ≈40% of the cardiac cycle
- **Segment** = straight line between two waves; **interval** = one or more waves + connecting line
**Electrode polarity concepts**
- Wave of depolarization moving TOWARD positive electrode → positive deflection
- Wave moving AWAY → negative deflection
- Wave moving perpendicular → biphasic deflection
- Repolarization wave: positive electrode still records positive deflection when repolarization approaches (opposite mechanism to depolarization but same deflection direction because polarity of the moving front differs)
**12-lead ECG**
- 6 limb leads (frontal plane) + 6 precordial leads (horizontal plane)
- Electrode placement: 2 on arms, 2 on legs (limb leads); 6 across chest (precordial)
**Limb leads (frontal plane) — angle of orientation**
| Lead | Positive/negative | Angle |
|---|---|---|
| I | LA+ / RA− | 0° |
| II | Legs+ / RA− | 60° |
| III | Legs+ / LA− | 120° |
| aVL | LA+ / others− | −30° |
| aVR | RA+ / others− | −150° |
| aVF | Legs+ / others− | +90° |
**Precordial (chest) leads**
- V1 — 4th ICS, right sternal border
- V2 — 4th ICS, left sternal border
- V3 — between V2 and V4
- V4 — 5th ICS, mid-clavicular line
- V5 — between V4 and V6
- V6 — 5th ICS, mid-axillary line
- View the heart in the horizontal plane; record anterior/posterior forces
**Vectors** — each lead records the instantaneous average of all current directions (like the average direction of many kicks)
**P wave axis**
- Atrial vector points right-to-left, slightly inferior
- Positive P wave: leads I, aVL (left lateral), II, aVF (inferior)
- Lead III — perpendicular to atrial current → often biphasic P wave
- aVR — current moving away → purely negative P wave
- Horizontal plane: V5/V6 positive (like I/aVL); V1 biphasic (like III, perpendicular); V2–V4 variable
- Normal P wave axis: **0°–70°**
**QRS axis and R wave progression**
- LV dominates QRS (3x RV mass) → average vector swings leftward, normally **0° to +90°**
- Frontal plane: large R waves in left lateral/inferior leads; aVR shows deep S wave
- Horizontal plane: V1 deep S wave (current moving away); V5/V6 tall R waves
- V3/V4 = transition zone — often biphasic (R ≈ S amplitude)
- **R wave progression** = progressively increasing R wave amplitude V1 → V5 (V1 smallest, V5 largest; V6 slightly smaller than V5)
**Clinical ECG applications**
- History of MI → pathological Q waves, T-wave inversion
- **Exercise ECG** — treadmill with increasing workload; in CAD, O2 demand outstrips supply → regional ischaemia → downsloping ST depression, reversing on recovery
**Acute coronary syndrome spectrum**
| Syndrome | Chest pain | ECG | Troponin |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stable angina | Yes | Normal | Normal |
| Unstable angina | Yes | ST depression/T inversion | Normal |
| NSTEMI | Yes | ST depression/T inversion | Elevated |
| STEMI | Yes | ST elevation | Elevated |
**Arrhythmia detection**
- Sustained arrhythmias → 12-lead ECG recording
- Paroxysmal arrhythmias → in-hospital monitoring, ambulatory (Holter) monitoring, patient-activated recorders
### Chest X-ray (CXR)
- Normal heart diameter ≤50% of thoracic width
- Cardiac enlargement = dilated chambers OR pericardial effusion
- **CTR (cardiothoracic ratio) = (a + b) / c**
**Left atrial dilatation** (LVF, mitral valve disease)
1. Flattening then bulging of left heart border below main pulmonary artery
2. Elevation of left main bronchus, widened carina
3. Double-density sign — medial border of left atrium seen behind right heart border
**Ventricular dilatation**
- PA CXR cannot reliably distinguish LV vs RV dilatation
- Lateral CXR: LV dilatation → encroaches retrocardiac space; RV dilatation → encroaches retrosternal space
**Vascular dilatation**
- Aortic aneurysm/dissection → widened upper mediastinum
- Pulmonary artery dilatation (pulmonary HTN, pulmonary stenosis) → prominence below aortic knuckle
**Increased left atrial pressure** (mitral stenosis, LVF)
- Early — prominent upper lobe veins
- Later — interstitial pulmonary oedema: prominent interlobular septa, esp. lung bases (**Kerley B lines**)
- Severe — alveolar pulmonary oedema: perihilar "bat's-wing" shadowing
- Kerley lines: A (long, from periphery to hilum), B (short, peripheral, at lung bases), C (fine reticular)
### Echocardiography
- Phased-array transducer uses piezoelectric crystals to send ultrasound pulses; reflected echoes converted to electrical signals
- Echo time delay → depth; echo amplitude → image brightness
- **M-mode** — single scan line over time; excellent temporal resolution for LV wall and valve motion
- **2-D echo** — multiple scan lines; real-time cardiac anatomy
**Clinical uses**
- Congenital heart disease
- Myocardial disease — dilatation, hypertrophy, contractile function
- Valvular disease — structural + dynamic abnormalities, chamber dilatation/hypertrophy
- Pericardial disease — most sensitive test for pericardial effusion
**Stress echocardiography** — LV imaging during increasing dobutamine infusion or exercise; assesses regional wall motion response; decreasing systolic wall motion/thickening → ischaemia, warrants further workup
### Biomarkers and other labs
- **Cardiac enzymes/troponin** — time course of serum markers important post-MI (rise/peak/fall pattern used for diagnosis and timing)
- **Pro-BNP and BNP** — released with increased ventricular wall stress (heart failure, LVH); cause vasodilatation and natriuresis
- **Creatinine** — renal dysfunction is both a cause and consequence of hypertension; in HF, renal function often deteriorates as kidney perfusion falls
- **Potassium** — hypokalaemia → risk of lethal arrhythmias; hyperkalaemia → bradyarrhythmias, heart block
- **Glucose and lipids** — atherosclerosis risk factors
- **Blood cultures** — for suspected infective endocarditis
**Other investigations** — cardiac catheterization, cardiovascular radionuclide imaging
