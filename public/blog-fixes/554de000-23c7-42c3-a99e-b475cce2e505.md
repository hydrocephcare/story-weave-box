## HIV / AIDS — Complete Study Notes
## 1. The Virus
HIV (Human Immunodeficiency Virus) is a retrovirus that progressively destroys the immune system, ultimately leading to Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS).
- **Family:** Retroviridae
- **Genus:** Lentivirus
- **Genome:** Single-stranded RNA (two identical copies — diploid)
- **Type:** Enveloped virus

## 2. Structure
**Envelope glycoproteins:**
- **gp120** — surface unit; binds CD4 receptor on target cells
- **gp41** — transmembrane unit; mediates fusion with host cell membrane
- Together they form the precursor **gp160**
**Core proteins:**
- **p24** — capsid protein; major diagnostic marker detected by ELISA
- **p17** — matrix protein
- **p7/p9** — nucleocapsid proteins
**Enzymes packaged inside the virion:**
- Reverse transcriptase
- Integrase
- Protease

## 3. Transmission
- Sexual contact — anal, vaginal (most common globally)
- Blood and blood products — transfusions, sharing needles
- Mother to child (vertical) — pregnancy, delivery, breastfeeding
- Needlestick injuries in healthcare workers
HIV is NOT transmitted by casual contact, mosquitoes, saliva, tears, or shared utensils.

## 4. Target Cells
HIV infects cells that express the **CD4 receptor:**
- CD4+ T-helper lymphocytes — primary target
- Macrophages
- Dendritic cells
- Microglial cells (CNS)
**Co-receptors required for entry:**
- **CCR5** — used by macrophage-tropic strains (early infection)
- **CXCR4** — used by T-cell tropic strains (late infection)

## 5. Pathogenesis — The Most Examined Topic
### Step 1: Attachment and Entry
- gp120 binds CD4 receptor
- Co-receptor (CCR5 or CXCR4) binding causes conformational change
- gp41 inserts into host membrane → fusion → viral core enters cytoplasm
### Step 2: Reverse Transcription
- Reverse transcriptase converts viral RNA → single-stranded DNA
- RNase H activity degrades the original RNA strand
- Second DNA strand synthesized → double-stranded DNA (dsDNA)
- High error rate — no proofreading — causes mutations and drug resistance
### Step 3: Integration
- dsDNA transported into nucleus
- Integrase cuts host DNA and inserts viral dsDNA → **provirus**
- Provirus remains permanently in host genome
- This is why HIV infection is lifelong and cannot be cured
### Step 4: Transcription and Translation
- Host RNA polymerase transcribes proviral DNA → viral mRNA and genomic RNA
- Viral proteins synthesized by host ribosomes
- Protease cleaves large polyprotein precursors into functional proteins
### Step 5: Assembly and Budding
- New viral particles assemble at the cell membrane
- Virus buds off, acquiring its envelope from the host cell membrane
- Mature, infectious virions released

## 6. The Three Key Enzymes — Highest Yield
### Reverse Transcriptase
- Converts RNA → DNA (unique to retroviruses)
- Has RNase H activity
- No proofreading → high mutation rate
- Drug targets: NRTIs (e.g., Zidovudine/AZT, Tenofovir) and NNRTIs (e.g., Nevirapine, Efavirenz)
### Integrase
- Integrates viral dsDNA into host chromosome as provirus
- Provirus is permanent — basis of lifelong infection
- Drug targets: Integrase strand transfer inhibitors — Raltegravir, Dolutegravir, Elvitegravir (drugs ending in -tegravir)
### Protease
- Cleaves Gag and Gag-Pol polyproteins into functional structural proteins and enzymes
- Required for viral maturation — without it, virions are non-infectious
- Drug targets: Protease inhibitors — Lopinavir, Ritonavir, Atazanavir (drugs ending in -navir)
**Why these enzymes make antiretrovirals selectively toxic:**
These enzymes are unique to HIV and have no equivalent in normal human cells. Drugs targeting them therefore kill or inhibit virus-infected cells specifically without harming uninfected human cells — this is the definition of selective toxicity.

## 7. Clinical Stages of HIV Infection
### Stage 1: Acute HIV Infection (Primary Infection)
- Occurs 2–4 weeks after exposure
- High viral load, rapid drop in CD4 count
- Symptoms: fever, sore throat, lymphadenopathy, rash, myalgia — resembles glandular fever
- Highly infectious at this stage
- Body mounts immune response → viral load falls, CD4 count partially recovers
### Stage 2: Chronic HIV Infection (Clinical Latency)
- Can last years (average 10 years without treatment)
- Patient may be asymptomatic or have mild symptoms
- Virus still replicating slowly
- CD4 count gradually declining
### Stage 3: AIDS
- Defined as CD4 count below 200 cells/mm³ OR development of AIDS-defining illness
- Severe immunodeficiency → opportunistic infections and malignancies

## 8. AIDS-Defining Conditions
**Opportunistic infections:**
- Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PCP) — most common
- Toxoplasmosis (brain)
- Cryptococcal meningitis
- CMV retinitis
- Candidiasis (oesophageal, tracheal)
- Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC)
- Tuberculosis (TB) — especially in Sub-Saharan Africa
**Malignancies:**
- Kaposi's sarcoma (caused by HHV-8)
- Non-Hodgkin's lymphoma
- Cervical cancer (caused by HPV)

## 9. Diagnosis
**Direct detection:**
- HIV RNA by PCR — detects viral load; used in early infection and monitoring
- p24 antigen — detectable before antibodies appear
**Antibody detection:**
- ELISA — screening test (sensitive)
- Western Blot — confirmatory test (specific)
**CD4 count:**
- Monitors immune status and guides when to start treatment
- Normal: 500–1500 cells/mm³
- Start ART: below 500 cells/mm³ (or any count in current guidelines)
- AIDS: below 200 cells/mm³
**Window period:**
- The gap between infection and detectable antibodies (2–12 weeks)
- Patient is infectious but tests negative during this period

## 10. Antiretroviral Therapy (ART)
Treatment uses combination therapy (at least 3 drugs from 2 different classes) — called HAART (Highly Active Antiretroviral Therapy).
**Drug classes and mechanisms:**
| Class | Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| NRTIs | Block reverse transcriptase (competitive inhibition) | Zidovudine (AZT), Tenofovir, Lamivudine |
| NNRTIs | Block reverse transcriptase (non-competitive) | Nevirapine, Efavirenz |
| Protease Inhibitors | Block viral protease | Lopinavir, Ritonavir, Atazanavir |
| Integrase Inhibitors | Block integrase | Raltegravir, Dolutegravir |
| Fusion Inhibitors | Block gp41-mediated fusion | Enfuvirtide (Fuzeon) |
| CCR5 Antagonists | Block CCR5 co-receptor | Maraviroc |
**Goals of ART:**
- Suppress viral load to undetectable levels
- Preserve and restore CD4 count
- Prevent opportunistic infections
- Reduce transmission

## 11. Prevention
- Consistent condom use
- ART for HIV-positive individuals (Treatment as Prevention — TasP)
- Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) — Tenofovir/Emtricitabine for high-risk individuals
- Post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) — within 72 hours of exposure
- Safe blood transfusion practices
- Needle exchange programmes
- Prevention of mother-to-child transmission (PMTCT) — ART during pregnancy

## Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV-1 and HSV-2) — Complete Study Notes
## 1. The Virus
Herpes Simplex Virus causes lifelong latent infections with periodic reactivation. It is one of the most clinically important herpesviruses.
- **Family:** Herpesviridae
- **Subfamily:** Alphaherpesvirinae
- **Genome:** Double-stranded DNA (dsDNA)
- **Enveloped virus**
- **Two serotypes:** HSV-1 and HSV-2

## 2. Structure
- Large, complex enveloped virus
- Icosahedral capsid surrounded by a **tegument** layer (unique to herpesviruses)
- Envelope studded with glycoproteins (gB, gC, gD, gH, gL) — used for attachment and fusion
- **gC** and **gB** — bind heparan sulphate on host cells
- **gD** — binds specific entry receptors (HVEM, nectin)
- **gH/gL** — mediate fusion

## 3. Differences Between HSV-1 and HSV-2
| Feature | HSV-1 | HSV-2 |
|---|---|---|
| Primary site | Orolabial (mouth, lips) | Genital area |
| Portal of entry | Oral mucosa, skin | Genital mucosa, skin |
| Latency site | Trigeminal ganglion | Sacral dorsal root ganglion |
| Transmission | Direct contact, saliva | Sexual contact |
| Reactivation triggers | Stress, fever, UV light, illness | Stress, menstruation, immunosuppression |
Note: HSV-1 can cause genital herpes (increasingly common), and HSV-2 can cause oral lesions — the distinction is not absolute.

## 4. Portal of Entry — High Yield
**HSV-1:**
- Oral and labial mucosa
- Conjunctiva
- Skin (abraded)
- Respiratory mucosa (rare)
**HSV-2:**
- Genital mucosa
- Perianal skin
- Mucous membranes
Both viruses enter through **mucosal surfaces and broken skin** — they cannot penetrate intact skin.

## 5. Pathogenesis
### Primary Infection
- Virus enters via mucosa or abraded skin
- Replicates locally at the entry site → causes vesicular lesions
- Travels **retrograde** along sensory nerve axons to the **sensory ganglion**
- Establishes **latency** in the ganglion — viral DNA maintained as circular episome
- Immune response limits primary infection but cannot clear latent virus
### Latency
- Virus becomes latent in sensory ganglia
- Only LAT (Latency-Associated Transcripts) expressed — virus is invisible to immune system
- No viral replication during latency
- Cell remains alive — virus does not destroy the neuron during latency
### Reactivation
- Triggered by: stress, fever, UV light, menstruation, immunosuppression, trauma
- Virus travels **anterograde** back down the axon to the skin/mucosa
- Replication resumes → new lesions (recurrent herpes)
- Reactivation may be symptomatic (lesions) or asymptomatic (silent shedding)
- Silent shedding is a major source of transmission

## 6. Diseases Caused — High Yield
**HSV-1:**
- Herpes labialis — cold sores (most common)
- Gingivostomatitis — primary oral infection in children
- Keratoconjunctivitis — eye infection; can cause corneal scarring and blindness
- Herpes encephalitis — most common cause of fatal sporadic encephalitis
- Herpetic whitlow — infection of the finger
**HSV-2:**
- Genital herpes — painful vesicular ulcers on genitalia
- Neonatal herpes — acquired during delivery through infected birth canal; can be fatal
- Aseptic meningitis
**Both HSV-1 and HSV-2:**
- Encephalitis
- Disseminated infection in immunocompromised patients
- Erythema multiforme (hypersensitivity reaction triggered by HSV)

## 7. Neonatal Herpes — Special Importance
- Neonate acquires HSV-2 (rarely HSV-1) during passage through infected birth canal
- Risk highest when mother has primary genital herpes at time of delivery
- Three forms:
  - Localized skin, eye, mouth (SEM disease)
  - CNS disease — encephalitis
  - Disseminated disease — involves multiple organs; highest mortality
- Management: Caesarean section if active genital lesions at term; IV Acyclovir for infected neonates

## 8. HSV Encephalitis — Special Importance
- Most common cause of fatal sporadic (non-epidemic) viral encephalitis worldwide
- Caused predominantly by HSV-1
- Affects the **temporal lobe** preferentially
- Clinical features: fever, headache, altered consciousness, seizures, focal neurological deficits
- Diagnosis: MRI (temporal lobe changes), PCR of CSF (gold standard)
- Treatment: IV Acyclovir — must be started early

## 9. Diagnosis
- **Tzanck smear** — multinucleated giant cells seen on smear from lesion base (not specific for HSV vs VZV)
- **PCR** — gold standard; detects HSV DNA in CSF, swabs, tissue
- **Viral culture** — from vesicle fluid; definitive but slow
- **Direct fluorescent antibody (DFA)** — rapid, from lesion swab
- **Serology** — IgM (acute), IgG (past infection/latency); type-specific serology distinguishes HSV-1 from HSV-2

## 10. Treatment — Why Acyclovir is Selectively Toxic (High Yield)
**Acyclovir** is the drug of choice for HSV infections.
**Mechanism of selective toxicity — step by step:**
1. Acyclovir enters all cells (infected and uninfected)
2. In HSV-infected cells only, viral thymidine kinase (TK) phosphorylates acyclovir → acyclovir monophosphate
3. Host cell kinases add two more phosphates → acyclovir triphosphate (active form)
4. Acyclovir triphosphate inhibits viral DNA polymerase competitively
5. It is also incorporated into viral DNA as a chain terminator — DNA synthesis stops
6. Human DNA polymerase has very low affinity for acyclovir triphosphate — human cells are spared
**Why it is selective:**
- Normal uninfected cells lack viral thymidine kinase — they cannot activate acyclovir
- Only virus-infected cells activate the drug
- Therefore acyclovir is toxic only to HSV-infected cells — this is the definition of selective toxicity
**Drugs in the acyclovir family:**
| Drug | Notes |
|---|---|
| Acyclovir | IV or oral; first-line for HSV and VZV |
| Valacyclovir | Oral prodrug of acyclovir; better bioavailability |
| Famciclovir | Oral prodrug of penciclovir; used for genital herpes and zoster |
| Ganciclovir | Used for CMV (not HSV first-line) |
**Resistance:**
- Occurs through mutation of viral thymidine kinase (most common) or viral DNA polymerase
- Resistant strains treated with Foscarnet or Cidofovir (do not require TK activation)

## 11. Prevention
- Avoid direct contact with active lesions
- Suppressive acyclovir therapy — reduces frequency of recurrences and asymptomatic shedding
- Condom use — reduces but does not eliminate transmission (virus shed from areas not covered)
- Caesarean section for mothers with active genital lesions at term
- No licensed vaccine currently available

## 12. Immune Evasion by HSV
HSV has developed multiple strategies to evade the immune system:
- **Latency** — virus hides in neurons where immune surveillance is limited
- **Downregulation of MHC class I** — infected cells become less visible to cytotoxic T cells
- **Blocking complement** — gC binds complement component C3b, blocking complement activation
- **Blocking antibody-dependent cytotoxicity** — gE/gI complex acts as Fc receptor, binding IgG and blocking immune recognition
