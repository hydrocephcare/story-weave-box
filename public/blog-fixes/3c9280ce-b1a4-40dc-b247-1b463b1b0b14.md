# Chronic Diarrhoea and Malabsorption

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Gastroenterology**

> **In one line:** chronic diarrhoea (4 weeks or more) is classified by mechanism (osmotic, secretory, inflammatory, fatty) using stool character, response to fasting and the faecal osmotic gap. Malabsorption (failure of mucosal transport) and maldigestion (failure of luminal breakdown) give fatty stool and specific nutrient deficiencies; the common causes are coeliac disease, Whipple disease, bacterial overgrowth, tropical sprue, pancreatic insufficiency and bile-acid diarrhoea.

## What you must know

- **Diarrhoea** = stool output over 200 to 300 mL/day. **Acute** under 2 weeks (usually infection), **subacute** 2 to 4 weeks, **chronic** 4 weeks or more.
- **Faecal osmotic gap (FOG) = 290 - 2 x (stool Na + stool K).** Over 50 mOsm/kg suggests osmotic diarrhoea and **over 100** is more specific. Under 50 suggests secretory, under 25 is definitive.
- **Osmotic diarrhoea stops with fasting; secretory diarrhoea does not.**
- Coeliac disease: **anti-tTG IgA** is the best screening test; biopsy shows **villous atrophy**; treat with a **gluten-free diet**. Think of it in a young patient with iron-deficiency anaemia. Complications: **small bowel T-cell lymphoma** and adenocarcinoma.
- Whipple disease: *Tropheryma whipplei*; **foamy macrophages, PAS-positive**; diarrhoea + arthralgia + CNS signs.
- Small bowel bacterial overgrowth (SBBO): **low B12 with high folate**; gold standard is jejunal aspirate culture.
- Tropical sprue: travel to the tropics, **low B12 and low folate**, treat with **tetracycline** for 3 to 6 months plus vitamins.

## 1. Definitions

- **Organic** diarrhoea must be separated from **functional** (frequent small stools, stool weight under 250 g). Exceptions: distal colon cancer and proctitis are organic yet give frequent small stools.
- First exclude **infection** in any patient with diarrhoea: stool cultures, then flexible sigmoidoscopy with biopsy if symptoms persist and no diagnosis is made.
- IBD and IBS cause chronic, not acute, diarrhoea.
- **Maldigestion:** inadequate breakdown of triglycerides in the lumen (secretory insufficiency, impaired motility, abnormal enzymes).
- **Malabsorption:** inadequate mucosal transport of digested products from lumen to portal vein or lymphatics (mucosal disease, lymphatic disease, vascular disease).

## 2. Pathophysiology and fluid handling

- About **9 to 10 L** of fluid enters the jejunum daily.
- The small intestine absorbs about **90%**. The colon absorbs about **90% of what remains** (about 900 mL/24 h), leaving **80 to 100 mL** in faeces.
- A healthy colon can absorb up to **3 to 4 L/day** to compensate for small bowel disease.
- Hence **small bowel disease gives large-volume diarrhoea, and large bowel disease gives small-volume diarrhoea.**

## 3. Classification by mechanism

| Feature | Osmotic | Secretory | Inflammatory | Fatty (steatorrhoea) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Basis | Poorly absorbed, osmotically active solute holds water in the lumen | Net secretion of electrolytes and water (or failure of absorption) | Mucosal destruction and exudate | Malabsorption or maldigestion of fat |
| Volume | Variable | Large (over 1 L/day) | Small, with blood, mucus, pus | Bulky, pale |
| Fasting | **Improves** | **No response** | Persists | Persists |
| FOG | Over 50 (over 100 specific) | Under 50 (under 25 definite) | Not discriminating | Raised |
| Clues | Pain, fever absent | Isotonic fluid loss | Fever, pain, positive faecal leucocytes, blood, raised ESR/CRP, leucocytosis | Greasy, malodorous, hard-to-flush stool, weight loss, vitamin deficiencies |

**Causes of osmotic diarrhoea**

- Loss of a nutrient transporter or enzyme: **lactase deficiency**, coeliac disease (lactose is osmotically active).
- Poorly absorbed agents (osmotic laxatives): sugars (fructose, mannitol, sorbitol, lactulose), **magnesium antacids**, phosphate and sulphate laxatives.

**Causes of secretory diarrhoea**

- Bacterial toxins (cholera, some *E. coli*); enteropathic viruses (rotavirus, Norwalk).
- Reduced mucosal surface area (surgery or disease); absent ion-transport mechanism (congenital chloridorrhoea).
- Bile salts and fatty acids in the colon; **circulating secretagogues** (Addison's disease, hyperthyroidism, carcinoid syndrome, phaeochromocytoma, gastrinoma, VIPoma, somatostatinoma).
- Microscopic colitis, diverticulitis; pancreatic insufficiency; abnormal motility (diabetic dysfunction, IBS, post-vagotomy).
- Malignancy (colon cancer, lymphoma, rectal villous adenoma), vasculitis, connective tissue disease (SLE), drugs, stimulant laxative abuse, toxins, idiopathic (Brainerd) diarrhoea.

> **Remember:** non-invasive organisms (cholera) give watery diarrhoea; invasive organisms (Shigella, Salmonella) give bloody diarrhoea. *E. coli* can do both.

**Causes of inflammatory diarrhoea** (infection, inflammation, ischaemia, malignancy)

- IBD: ulcerative colitis, Crohn's disease, diverticulitis, ulcerative jejunoileitis.
- Infection: ***C. difficile* pseudomembranous colitis** (antibiotic-induced), invasive bacteria (*E. coli*, Shigella, TB, yersiniosis), ulcerating viruses (CMV, HSV), invasive parasites (amoebiasis, strongyloides).
- Ischaemic colitis, radiation colitis, neoplasia (colon cancer, lymphoma).

**Causes of fatty diarrhoea**

- Features: bloating, flatulence, greasy malodorous stools, weight loss, anaemia, hypoalbuminaemia, osteopenia, coagulopathy (vitamin K), and fat-soluble vitamin (**A, D, E, K**) deficiency with peripheral neuropathy and easy bruising.
- Most fat is carried in lymphatics, so lymphatic obstruction (lymphoma, TB, heart failure) also causes fatty diarrhoea.
- **Malabsorption:** mucosal disease (coeliac, Whipple), mesenteric ischaemia, short bowel or post-resection, SBBO (bile salt deconjugation).
- **Maldigestion:** pancreatic exocrine insufficiency (chronic pancreatitis, cystic fibrosis), low luminal bile acids (for example advanced primary biliary cirrhosis).

## 4. Where nutrients are absorbed

| Site | Absorbed |
|---|---|
| Duodenum and jejunum | Carbohydrates and simple sugars, amino acids, iron, calcium, folate, fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K), magnesium, minerals and other vitamins |
| Ileum | **Vitamin B12**, fats, **bile salts** (terminal ileum), magnesium |
| Colon | Short-chain fatty acids, **vitamin K and biotin** (partly made by gut flora, which is why antibiotics can cause vitamin K deficiency) |

> **Remember:** iron, calcium and folate are absorbed proximally; B12 and bile salts in the ileum. So coeliac disease (proximal) causes iron and folate deficiency, while ileal disease causes B12 deficiency and bile-acid diarrhoea.

## 5. Common drugs and toxins that cause diarrhoea

Acid-reducing agents (H2 blockers, PPIs), magnesium antacids (osmotic), antiarrhythmics (digoxin, quinidine), antibiotics, antineoplastics, antiretrovirals, beta-blockers, colchicine, levothyroxine, SSRIs, furosemide, **metformin**, NSAIDs and aspirin, misoprostol, theophylline, amphetamines, **caffeine** (stimulates bowel movement), alcohol, opioid withdrawal.

## 6. Clinical approach

**History**

- Onset, aggravating (drugs) and relieving factors, travel, stool characteristics (watery, fatty, bloody), faecal incontinence (an anal sphincter problem: normal stool can still leak), abdominal pain (IBS, IBD), weight loss, laxative abuse, systemic disease (diabetes with metformin, SLE, systemic sclerosis).

**Examination**

- Carcinoid: a carcinoid tumour of the small bowel or bronchus secretes serotonin and histamine. While drained by the portal system, the liver metabolises serotonin to 5-HIAA and there are no symptoms. After **liver metastasis**, serotonin escapes metabolism and causes **carcinoid syndrome**: flushing, diarrhoea, and less often heart failure, vomiting and wheeze.

**Basic tests**

- **FBC:** anaemia (iron, folate, B12 deficiency, chronic disease, blood loss); leucocytosis (infection, inflammation).
- **Stool analysis** (output over 200 mL): electrolytes for osmotic gap (under 25 secretory, over 100 osmotic); **pH under 6** suggests carbohydrate malabsorption (colonic fermentation to CO2, H2 and short-chain fatty acids; lactase-deficient children get perianal rash).

**Specific tests**

- Culture (mainly useful for acute), ova and parasites, *Giardia* antigen, ***C. difficile* toxin**, coccidia, microsporidia, cryptosporidia (opportunistic infections, especially in HIV).
- Faecal leucocytes, or lactoferrin and calprotectin (neutrophil markers, important in IBD); faecal occult blood.
- **Fat content:** quantitative 48 or 72-hour collection (over **6 g/day** is abnormal); qualitative Sudan stain only confirms fat.
- **Laxative screen** (magnesium, etc.; repeat if positive; seen in patients with body-image disorders).
- Imaging: small bowel series, CT/MR enterography (malignancy).
- Endoscopy with small bowel biopsy and aspirate for quantitative culture; colonoscopy with random biopsies.

## 8. Coeliac disease

**Definition and pathophysiology**

- Small bowel malabsorption after ingestion of **gluten** (wheat) and related proteins in rye and barley. It is an **autoimmune** disease (unlike non-coeliac gluten intolerance, which has no antibodies or histological change).
- **Gliadin** is deamidated by tissue transglutaminase, presented to T cells, and drives antibody production and mucosal damage.
- Findings: loss of villi (flattening), elongated crypts opening on a flat surface, a leaky mucosa with damaged tight junctions, and increased plasma cells and lymphocytes in the lamina propria. The **duodenum** is affected first.
- Villous atrophy is characteristic but **not specific** (also in tropical sprue, Whipple's); a strict gluten-free diet gives prompt clinical and histological recovery, and reintroducing gluten causes relapse.

**Associations**

- **Type 1 diabetes (3 to 6%), autoimmune thyroid disease (6 to 8%), IgA deficiency (2 to 3%)**, Down syndrome, connective tissue disease. First-degree relatives should be screened.

**Presentations**

- Asymptomatic (found by screening).
- **3 D's:** diarrhoea, distension (bloating), dyspepsia.
- Nutritional: **iron and folate-deficiency anaemia**, osteoporosis. In a young patient with iron-deficiency anaemia, exclude coeliac disease; in an older patient exclude a GI tumour.
- Fat-soluble vitamin deficiency: A (night blindness, hyperkeratosis), D (hypocalcaemia, osteoporosis), K (bruising, high INR).
- **Dermatitis herpetiformis:** itchy papules and vesicles on extensor surfaces and above the elbows; biopsy shows **IgA deposits** (absent if IgA-deficient); responds to gluten-free diet.
- Raised ALT and AST (steatosis).
- **Malignancy:** small bowel **T-cell lymphoma** and **small bowel adenocarcinoma** (also oesophageal squamous cancer); may be the first presentation. Suspect when a treated patient develops anaemia, weight loss, night sweats and diarrhoea.

**Investigations**

- **Serology:** **anti-tissue transglutaminase (anti-tTG) IgA is the best marker** (sensitivity 95%, specificity 97.5%).

| Test | Sensitivity | Specificity |
|---|---|---|
| Endomysial antibody (IgA) | 85 to 98% | 97 to 100% |
| IgA anti-gliadin | 75 to 90% | 82 to 95% |
| IgG anti-gliadin | 69 to 85% | 73 to 90% |

- In **IgA-deficient** patients, use IgG-based tests (IgG endomysial, tTG or gliadin).
- Endoscopy: normal, or loss of mucosal folds (flattening), **scalloping**, ulcerations.
- **Biopsy:** villous atrophy, crypt hyperplasia, lymphocytic infiltration of the lamina propria. Not every villous atrophy is coeliac disease, and not every coeliac patient has villous atrophy.

> **Check:** patients must be eating gluten when tested, or serology and histology may be falsely negative.

**Management**

- Education; lifelong **gluten-free diet** (avoid wheat, barley, rye; the slides also advise avoiding oats).
- Correct deficiencies (iron, calcium, vitamin D, folate).
- Anticipate complications: ulcerative jejunitis, malnutrition, malignancy.

> **Exam tip:** young patient + iron-deficiency anaemia + villous atrophy, scalloped duodenal mucosa, or IgA deficiency with diarrhoea means coeliac disease.

## 9. Whipple disease

- Chronic **systemic infection by *Tropheryma whipplei*** affecting the GI tract, CNS, heart, joints and skin.
- Pathology: macrophage-predominant infiltration of the small bowel mucosa and **obstruction of mesenteric lymph nodes** (lymphangiectasia).

| System | Features |
|---|---|
| GI | Malabsorption: weight loss, watery or fatty diarrhoea, abdominal pain |
| CNS | Progressive dementia, **supranuclear ophthalmoplegia**, altered consciousness, oculo-masticatory and oculo-facial myorhythmia |
| CVS | **Culture-negative endocarditis** (mitral valve commonest), myocarditis, pericarditis |
| MSK | Oligo- or polyarthralgia (often precedes diarrhoea) |
| Skin | Hyperpigmentation (common) |

- **Endoscopy:** whitish to yellow plaque-like patches in 75% (lipid droplets and lymphangiectasia).
- **Histology:** **foamy macrophages** on H&E with **PAS-positive granular particles** (lysosomes filled with the organism).
- **Treatment:** 2 weeks of an antibiotic that crosses the blood-brain barrier: **ceftriaxone 2 g IV daily**, or **penicillin G 2 MU IV 4-hourly plus streptomycin**; then **co-trimoxazole (Septra) DS twice daily for 1 year**.

> **Exam tip:** fatty diarrhoea + joint pain + CNS signs, or foamy macrophages that are PAS-positive = Whipple disease. Any cause of lymphatic obstruction (TB, lymphoma) can also give steatorrhoea.

## 10. Small bowel bacterial overgrowth (SBBO)

**Normal bacterial counts (CFU/mL):** stomach 0 to 10^3, jejunum 0 to 10^4 (Gram-positive aerobes such as oral streptococci and lactobacilli), ileum 10^5 to 10^9, colon 10^10 to 10^12 (anaerobes: bacteroides, peptostreptococcus, bifidobacterium, clostridium; facultative aerobes: *E. coli*, enterococcus, staphylococcus).

| Normal protective barrier | Cause of SBBO when it fails |
|---|---|
| Gastric acid and pancreatic enzymes (bactericidal) | Chronic pancreatitis, low acid (gastric surgery, PPI) |
| Intact bowel structure | Surgery, diverticula, blind loops (stagnation) |
| Motility (migrating motor complex cleansing) | Dysmotility (diabetes, scleroderma) |
| Secretory IgA | Immunodeficiency |
| Competent ileocaecal valve | Scarring or surgical removal (reflux of colonic content) |
| Competition from other bacteria | Broad-spectrum antibiotics |

**Effects of overgrowth**

- **Bile acids deconjugated** (steatorrhoea); bilirubin deconjugated.
- Carbohydrate fermentation gives CO2, H2, D-lactic acid and short-chain fatty acids; protein metabolism gives amines and ammonia.

**Features:** bloating, diarrhoea, abdominal pain, weight loss, neuropathy; **B12 deficiency (bacteria consume it) with normal or high folate (bacteria make it)**; deficiency of vitamins A, D, E, K; iron deficiency; hypoalbuminaemia; fat and carbohydrate malabsorption.

> **Exam tip:** low B12 + high folate = SBBO. In tropical sprue both B12 and folate are low.

**Diagnosis**

- **Jejunal aspirate culture: gold standard.**
- C14-labelled bile acid (glycocholic) breath test; C14-xylose breath test (bacteria cleave the substrate and radiolabelled CO2 appears in breath).
- **Hydrogen breath test:** early or double peak of hydrogen.
- **Schilling test** (below).

**Management**

- Treat the underlying cause (stop acid suppressants, treat Crohn's, remove strictures).
- Replace fat-soluble vitamins and B12; low-carbohydrate diet to limit substrate.
- **7 to 10 days of antibiotics:** metronidazole, amoxicillin-clavulanate, tetracycline, doxycycline or amoxicillin (rotate antibiotics to avoid resistance).
- Bowel cleansing with polyethylene glycol; prokinetics to improve cleansing; low-dose octreotide (promotility effect; hypomotility at high doses); probiotics have minimal evidence.

### The Schilling test (vitamin B12 absorption)

Radiolabelled B12 is given orally and absorption is measured in urine. A parenteral B12 injection first saturates liver stores so absorbed isotope is excreted in urine. B12 absorption: R-factor binds B12 in saliva, intrinsic factor (IF) from the stomach, trypsin from the pancreas releases R-factor so IF can bind B12, and absorption occurs in the terminal ileum.

| Stage | Addition | Normalisation indicates |
|---|---|---|
| I | IM B12 (saturates tissue stores) | Normal result means the cause was reduced intake |
| II | + intrinsic factor | Pernicious anaemia (or gastric resection) |
| III | + oral antibiotics | Bacterial overgrowth |
| IV | + pancreatic enzymes | Pancreatic insufficiency |

If it never normalises: **ileal disease**.

> **Check:** the Schilling test is largely obsolete in practice (radiolabelled B12 is no longer widely available); it is still examined for the logic.

## 11. Tropical sprue

- **Villous atrophy and crypt hyperplasia** like coeliac disease, but due to an **infectious / environmental** cause. It is acquired, not present in newborns; affects long-term residents and **travellers** to the tropics (Asia, Africa, Middle East, Caribbean, Central and South America).
- Cause unknown: probably continuous bacterial contamination of the upper small bowel plus nutritional deficiency.
- Features: chronic diarrhoea often with steatorrhoea, anorexia, cramps, bloating; **both B12 and folate deficiency**.
- Diagnosis: history of long stay in the tropics; jejunal aspirate and biopsy if suspicion is high.
- **Treatment:** folate and B12 supplements, plus **tetracycline for 3 to 6 months**.

## 12. Bile-acid-induced diarrhoea

- Bile acids are made in the liver; **95% are reabsorbed in the terminal ileum**, 5% reach the colon. After ileal resection or disease, unabsorbed bile acids irritate the colon, causing secretion of water and electrolytes and **increased oxalate absorption (renal oxalate stones)**.
- **Limited resection:** watery (secretory, not osmotic) diarrhoea. **Extensive resection:** fatty diarrhoea (bile-salt pool depleted).
- **Treatment: cholestyramine** (binds bile acids).

## 13. Exocrine pancreatic insufficiency

- Mainly **chronic pancreatitis** (history of abdominal pain and heavy alcohol use) or cystic fibrosis. **Lipase deficiency** causes fat maldigestion and **steatorrhoea**.
- Improves with **pancreatic enzyme replacement** (lipase and protease).

## 14. Practice cases (summary of answers)

1. Acute watery diarrhoea with vomiting after a barbecue, normal observations: **gastroenteritis**.
2. Mild gastroenteritis, well patient: **oral rehydration advice and antiemetics, discharge** (antibiotics only if a bacterial pathogen is isolated; IV fluids if severe dehydration).
3. Two weeks of diarrhoea after food poisoning: **stool microscopy, culture and sensitivity** first.
4. Diarrhoea 5 days after amoxicillin: *C. difficile*; best test is **detection of toxin in stool** (culture is non-specific because of carriage; faecal leucocytes may be negative). Stop the offending antibiotic; give oral metronidazole or vancomycin; cholestyramine can bind the toxin. Sigmoidoscopy shows yellow pseudomembranes.

## Key points to revise

1. Chronic diarrhoea is 4 weeks or more; classify as osmotic, secretory, inflammatory or fatty, using fasting response and FOG (over 100 osmotic, under 25 secretory).
2. Small bowel disease causes large-volume diarrhoea; colonic disease causes small-volume diarrhoea.
3. Coeliac disease: anti-tTG IgA, duodenal villous atrophy, gluten-free diet; look for iron deficiency, IgA deficiency, dermatitis herpetiformis and lymphoma.
4. Whipple disease: foamy PAS-positive macrophages; diarrhoea, arthralgia, CNS signs; ceftriaxone then co-trimoxazole for a year.
5. SBBO: low B12, high folate; jejunal aspirate culture; treat the cause plus antibiotics.
6. Tropical sprue: tropical travel, low B12 and folate, tetracycline for 3 to 6 months.
7. Bile-acid diarrhoea follows ileal resection and responds to cholestyramine; pancreatic insufficiency gives steatorrhoea that responds to enzymes.
8. Iron, calcium and folate are absorbed proximally; B12 and bile salts in the ileum.
