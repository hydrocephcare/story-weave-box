# Gastrointestinal Bleeding

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Gastroenterology**

> **In one line:** gastrointestinal bleeding is classed as upper (above the ligament of Treitz) or lower. Upper GI bleeding is the commonest GI emergency (about 10% mortality), most often from peptic ulcer or varices. Resuscitate first, stratify risk, give a high-dose PPI (plus a vasoactive drug and antibiotics if variceal), and do endoscopy within 24 hours (12 hours for varices) to find and treat the source.

## What you must know

- **Upper GI bleed** = source above the **ligament of Treitz**; features are **haematemesis and melaena**. Mortality is about 10%.
- **Peptic ulcer disease is the commonest cause (36 to 50%)**; **variceal bleeding is second**.
- **Melaena** suggests an upper source about 90% of the time; **haematochezia** is usually lower GI, but 5 to 10% come from a massive upper bleed with shock.
- **Resuscitate before any diagnostic procedure**: airway, breathing, large-bore IV access, crystalloids, then blood. Aim for **permissive hypotension** and avoid over-resuscitation.
- **Restrictive transfusion**: red cells if Hb below 7 g/dL (below 10 in cardiovascular disease or symptomatic patients).
- **Raised urea with a normal creatinine** implies a large upper GI bleed.
- Risk scores: **Glasgow-Blatchford** (pre-endoscopy, best), **Rockall**, **AIMS65**.
- Endoscopy within **24 hours** (non-variceal) and **12 hours** (variceal). Test for ***H. pylori*** and treat it.

## 1. Definitions and site

| Type | Source | Includes |
|---|---|---|
| **Upper GI bleed** | Above the ligament of Treitz (suspensory muscle of the duodenum) | Oesophagus, stomach, duodenum |
| **Lower GI bleed** | Below the ligament of Treitz | Small and large bowel, rectum |

- **Acute upper GI bleeding:** the commonest GI emergency, about 10% mortality. Cardinal features are haematemesis and melaena. Unaltered blood per rectum (haematochezia) can occur, but only with massive bleeding, almost always with shock.
- **Acute lower GI bleeding:** massive bleeding is rare, with profuse red or maroon diarrhoea and shock, usually from **diverticular disease** or **ischaemic colitis**. Subacute or chronic small bleeds come from **haemorrhoids** (bright red blood during or after defecation, on the paper) and **anal fissures** (fresh blood with anal pain on defecation).
- **Chronic GI bleeding:** presents with **iron-deficiency anaemia**; can come from any lesion that bleeds acutely. The main concern is to **exclude cancer** (stomach or right colon) and coeliac disease. **Hookworm** is the commonest worldwide cause of chronic GI blood loss (important in Kenya).

## 2. Epidemiology

- Incidence of upper GI bleeding is **57 to 78 per 100 000** population.
- Most bleeds are non-variceal. Peptic ulcer disease is declining globally because of PPIs, ***H. pylori*** eradication, and more cautious NSAID use (often with gastroprotection).

## 3. Causes of upper GI bleeding

| Rank | Cause | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Peptic ulcer disease** (duodenal and gastric) | Commonest cause (36 to 50%) |
| 2 | **Variceal bleeding** | Second commonest; high flow venous bleeding from portal hypertension. Common in regions with high hepatitis B/C and schistosomiasis |
| 3 | Mucosal erosive disease | Oesophagitis, gastritis, duodenitis |
| 4 | **Mallory-Weiss tear** | Linear mucosal tear at the oesophagogastric junction from a sudden rise in intra-abdominal pressure (coughing, retching, "dry heaves" after alcohol, chemotherapy, pregnancy). Haematemesis or coffee-ground vomit |
| 5 | Malignancy | Gastric and oesophageal cancer |
| 6 | Arteriovenous malformation | "Tree-like" vessels; seen in cirrhosis, pregnancy (high oestrogen) and hereditary telangiectasia |
| 7 | **Gastric antral vascular ectasia (GAVE)** | "Watermelon stomach": red stripes in the antrum; elderly, renal dialysis, or no cause |
| 8 | **Dieulafoy lesion** | Rare; a submucosal artery opens and bleeds, then disappears; may be missed unless the patient is actively bleeding |

> **Remember:** if the Mallory-Weiss tear goes through the **full thickness** (muscle tear, not just the mucosa) it is **Boerhaave syndrome**: mediastinitis with high mortality.

### Risk factors for ulcer bleeding

- Age over 65; previous peptic ulcer or ulcer bleed; multiple or high-dose NSAIDs; NSAID-related dyspepsia.
- **Aspirin**, including cardioprotective low doses; **anticoagulants** or coagulopathy; antiplatelets (clopidogrel); **corticosteroids** (especially combined with NSAIDs, which gives a synergistic rise in bleeding); oral bisphosphonates (alendronate); SSRIs.
- ***H. pylori*** infection; smoking and alcohol; chronic debilitating disease (cardiovascular disease, rheumatoid arthritis, cirrhosis, renal disease).
- COX-1 versus COX-2 selection has little influence on GI haemorrhage in the slides' view. Anticoagulants and antiplatelets do not cause bleeding per se but make bleeding from any cause worse.

> **Check:** many texts say selective COX-2 inhibitors reduce ulcer risk compared with non-selective NSAIDs, though not to zero and not when combined with aspirin.

## 4. Clinical features

### 4.1 Types of bleeding

| Type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Haematemesis** | Vomiting fresh red blood; upper source; rapid, profuse (moderate to severe) bleed |
| **"Coffee-ground" vomit** | Upper source; slower, less severe bleed so blood is oxidised |
| **Melaena** | Black, tarry, **foul-smelling, soft** stool. Blood has been in the gut for several hours (action of enzymes and bacteria on haemoglobin). Upper GI source about 90% of the time, but any lesion proximal to the right colon can cause it |
| **Haematochezia** | Bright red blood per rectum. Usually lower GI (left colon, rectum): diverticulosis, AV malformations, haemorrhoids, colon cancer. Can be a massive upper bleed (5 to 10%); the patient is usually haemodynamically unstable |
| **Occult blood** | Guaiac-positive stool, usually presenting with **iron-deficiency anaemia**; the source can be anywhere; the most important cause is **colorectal cancer** |

> **Exam tip:** dark stool can also follow **bismuth, iron, spinach, charcoal and liquorice**. Iron stool is not loose and does not have the foul smell of melaena.

### 4.2 Volume depletion and shock

- Signs: hypotension, tachycardia, pallor, cold peripheries, altered mental state, reduced urine output. Severity depends on the amount of blood lost.
- Shock produces a **vicious cycle of acidosis, hypothermia and coagulopathy** (the lethal triad). Large volumes of cold IV fluid dilute clotting factors and platelets and cool the patient, so **fluids can worsen bleeding**; warm fluids where possible.

### 4.3 Anaemia

Fatigue, pallor, exertional dyspnoea. Chronic loss gives a low MCV (iron deficiency); acute loss is normocytic.

### 4.4 Summary of diagnostic clues

| Domain | Findings |
|---|---|
| Symptoms | Coffee-ground vomit, haematemesis, melaena, haematochezia, anaemic symptoms |
| History | Liver cirrhosis, NSAID use |
| Signs | Hypotension, tachycardia, pallor, confusion, melaena or blood per rectum, low urine output |
| Bloods | Anaemia, raised urea, high urea:creatinine ratio |
| Endoscopy | Ulcers, varices, Mallory-Weiss tear, erosive disease, neoplasm, vascular ectasia and malformations |

## 5. Management of acute upper GI bleeding

### 5.1 Initial resuscitation (pre-endoscopy)

- **Airway, breathing, circulation.** Large-bore IV access (at least one; two if possible), monitored setting, oxygen.
- **Crystalloids first** ("IV fluids, then blood"). The blood bank takes time; if blood is needed urgently, give **unmatched O-negative** blood.
- **Avoid over- and under-resuscitation.** Use **permissive hypotension**: aim for about 70 to 80% of normal rather than full normalisation to avoid dilution and re-bleeding.
- A wider cannula gives faster flow (16 gauge is largest of the usual peripheral sizes: about 1 L in 5 minutes). The intraosseous route is useful in children and trauma.
- Crystalloid (balanced) outcomes were better than saline in the SMART trial (critically ill); the SALT-ED trial in less severe patients showed no difference.

### 5.2 Initial blood tests

| Test | Why |
|---|---|
| **FBC** | Anaemia; Hb may be **normal in acute bleeds**. Low MCV = chronic loss. Thrombocytopenia suggests hypersplenism in liver disease |
| **Urea and electrolytes** | Urea rises in upper GI bleeding because blood protein is digested, absorbed and converted to urea in the liver. **Raised urea with normal creatinine = severe bleed**. May also show renal failure |
| **LFTs** | Chronic liver disease |
| **Coagulation (PT, PTT, INR, platelets)** | Liver disease, anticoagulation |
| **Group and cross-match** | At least **2 units** if significant bleed (Davidson); at least **4 units** if large bleed: BP under 100 mmHg, pulse over 100, cool extremities with slow capillary refill, Hb below 100 g/L (Kumar) |

### 5.3 Transfusion (restrictive strategy)

- Restrictive transfusion improves survival and reduces re-bleeding; liberal transfusion is linked to worse outcomes.
- Acceptable Hb: **above 7 to 8 g/dL** in young, healthy patients without active bleeding; **above 10 g/dL** in most elderly patients (especially with heart disease).
- **Packed red cells** if Hb below 7 g/dL, or below 10 g/dL in cardiovascular disease or symptomatic patients.
- **Platelets** only if count below 50 × 10⁹/L, or below 100 × 10⁹/L with suspected platelet dysfunction.
- **Fresh frozen plasma** if INR is raised.

### 5.4 Risk stratification

Early identification of low versus high risk minimises morbidity and mortality. High-risk patients: inform the surgeon (may need theatre). Where surgery would carry high risk, involve interventional radiology and gastroenterology and admit to ICU. Factors affecting rebleeding and death: age, comorbidity (heart failure, ischaemic heart disease, CKD, malignancy), shock, endoscopic diagnosis, stigmata of recent haemorrhage (adherent clot, spurting vessel) and signs of chronic liver disease.

**AIMS65** (one point each)

| Factor | Score |
|---|---|
| Albumin below 3 g/dL | 1 |
| INR above 1.5 | 1 |
| Altered mental status | 1 |
| Systolic BP 90 mmHg or less | 1 |
| Age over 65 | 1 |

**Glasgow-Blatchford score** (0 to 23; no endoscopic findings; the best score for deciding on admission and intervention)

| Item | Threshold | Score |
|---|---|---|
| Urea (mmol/L) | 6.5 to 7.9 | 2 |
| | 8 to 9.9 | 3 |
| | 10 to 24.9 | 4 |
| | 25 or more | 6 |
| Hb, men (g/L) | 120 to 130 | 1 |
| | 100 to 119 | 3 |
| | below 100 | 6 |
| Hb, women (g/L) | 100 to 120 | 1 |
| | below 100 | 6 |
| Systolic BP (mmHg) | 100 to 109 | 1 |
| | 90 to 99 | 2 |
| | below 90 | 3 |
| Heart rate | over 100 | 1 |
| Melaena | present | 1 |
| Syncope | present | 2 |
| Hepatic disease | present | 2 |
| Cardiac failure | present | 2 |

A score above 0 is considered higher risk and needs admission.

**Rockall score** (clinical plus endoscopic)

| Variable | Finding | Score |
|---|---|---|
| Age | under 60 / 60 to 79 / 80 or over | 0 / 1 / 2 |
| Shock | HR over 100 / systolic BP below 100 | 1 / 2 |
| Comorbidity | Ischaemic heart disease, heart failure, other major illness | 2 |
| | Renal or hepatic failure, metastatic cancer | 3 |
| Endoscopic diagnosis | No lesion or Mallory-Weiss tear | 0 |
| | Peptic ulcer, erosive disease, oesophagitis | 1 |
| | Upper GI cancer | 2 |
| Stigmata of recent haemorrhage | Clean-base ulcer, flat pigmented spot | 0 |
| | Blood in upper GI tract, active bleeding, visible vessel, clot | 2 |

> **Remember:** you do not need to memorise the scoring tables; know that Blatchford is pre-endoscopic, Rockall needs endoscopy, and AIMS65 is simple.

### 5.5 Pre-endoscopic therapy

- **IV erythromycin** 30 minutes before endoscopy (prokinetic to clear the stomach).
- **High-dose IV PPI** started. (The slides note it is not used in this way any more in some settings; follow local protocol.)
- Routine **nasogastric lavage** and **tranexamic acid** are **not** recommended.
- Anticoagulated patients: correct coagulopathy, but do not delay endoscopy for a high INR unless supratherapeutic. Stop aspirin, NSAIDs and warfarin and reverse the INR if necessary. An INR below about 2 to 2.5 is acceptable for intervention; for supratherapeutic INR (6 to 9) give vitamin K (warfarin), the heparin antidote (protamine), or FFP.

### 5.6 Endoscopic management

- **Timing:** early endoscopy is within **2 to 24 hours** of presentation: **non-variceal within 24 hours; variceal within 12 hours.**
- Delay or defer in **active acute coronary syndrome** and **suspected perforation** (surgery is better for perforation).
- Endoscopy allows visualisation of stigmata, risk assessment and treatment.

| Risk | Endoscopic finding |
|---|---|
| **High risk** | Spurting vessel, oozing blood, non-bleeding visible vessel, adherent clot |
| **Low risk** | Flat pigmented spot, clean base (PPI only, and look for *H. pylori*) |

**Haemostasis techniques** (often combined)

- **Epinephrine injection** (diluted with saline) around the vessel: vasoconstriction and tamponade.
- **Thermal therapy** (cautery).
- **Mechanical clips**, including the over-the-scope clip (OTSC).
- **Haemostatic powder spray** (fast and easy to use in diffuse bleeding).
- A **combination** (for example thermal plus clips, or powder plus clips) is common in practice.

### 5.7 Post-endoscopy drug therapy

- It takes about **72 hours** for most high-risk lesions to become low risk after endoscopic therapy. **60 to 76%** of re-bleeds after endoscopic haemostasis plus high-dose PPI occur in the **first 72 hours**.
- In-hospital mortality is **1.7 to 3.7%**; 30-day mortality is **6 to 11%**, and much of this is **non-bleeding** (pneumonia, UTI, stroke).
- Admit to a monitored setting for **at least 24 hours** if: older age, severe comorbidity, haemodynamic instability, active bleeding at endoscopy, or ulcer over **2 cm**.

**Initial treatment of ulcer bleeding by endoscopic feature**

| Feature | Active bleeding or visible vessel | Adherent clot | Flat pigmented spot | Clean base |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Endoscopic therapy | Yes | May consider | No | No |
| PPI | Intensive | Intensive | Once daily | Once daily |
| Diet | Clear liquids about 2 days | Clear liquids about 2 days | Regular | Regular |
| Hospital stay | 3 days | 3 days | 1 to 2 days | Discharge after endoscopy |

> **Remember:** **intensive PPI** = IV bolus **80 mg** then infusion **8 mg/hour for 72 hours**, or a bolus (e.g. 80 mg) followed by intermittent high-dose PPI (40 to 80 mg twice daily) for 3 days.

**Aetiology-specific plan**

| Ulcer type | Plan |
|---|---|
| ***H. pylori*** | Test all bleeding ulcer patients and eradicate if positive; confirm eradication (urea breath test or stool antigen). Repeat negative tests done in the acute setting. No need to continue PPI after eradication |
| **NSAID-induced** | Stop the NSAID; PPI no longer needed afterwards. If an NSAID is essential, use a COX-2 inhibitor plus PPI. If low-dose aspirin is needed for secondary prevention, restart quickly (stop for under 3 days) with a PPI |
| **Idiopathic** | PPI indefinitely |

### 5.8 Variceal bleeding

**Primary prevention (moderate or large varices that have not bled)**

| Drug | Dose and target |
|---|---|
| **Propranolol** | 20 mg orally twice daily, adjust every 2 to 3 days to a resting heart rate of **50 to 55 bpm**; maximum 320 mg per day |
| **Nadolol** | 40 mg once daily, adjust every 2 to 3 days to the same goal; maximum 160 mg per day |
| **Carvedilol** | 6.25 mg once daily, increased after 3 days to 12.5 mg; maximum 12.5 mg per day (except in hypertensive patients); keep systolic BP above 90 mmHg |
| **Endoscopic variceal ligation (EVL)** | Every 1 to 4 weeks until varices are obliterated; first repeat endoscopy 1 to 3 months after obliteration, then every 6 to 12 months |

Beta-blockers are continued indefinitely and need no repeat endoscopy follow-up; check adherence at every visit. Newly diagnosed cirrhosis with varices should start beta-blocker prophylaxis to lower portal pressure.

**Acute variceal haemorrhage**

| Drug | Dose | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **Somatostatin** | 250 mcg IV bolus (repeat in first hour if still bleeding), then infusion 250 to 500 mcg/h | Inhibits vasodilator hormones (glucagon-like), causing splanchnic vasoconstriction and less portal flow |
| **Octreotide** (somatostatin analogue) | 50 mcg IV bolus (repeat in first hour), then 50 mcg/h infusion | Same |
| **Terlipressin** (vasopressin analogue) | 2 mg IV every 4 hours for the first 48 hours until bleeding controlled, then 1 mg every 4 hours to prevent re-bleeding | Splanchnic vasoconstriction; active metabolite lysine-vasopressin released gradually, so fewer systemic side effects |

- Vasoactive therapy continues for **up to 5 days**; keep the patient admitted and on the infusion after endoscopy.
- Give **prophylactic antibiotics** (ciprofloxacin or norfloxacin; ceftriaxone is commonly used) to prevent infection such as spontaneous bacterial peritonitis.
- Endoscopic variceal ligation (banding) is first-line endoscopic therapy.

> **Exam tip:** ongoing bleeding after banding (as in case 4: melaena and falling Hb 12 hours after banding) means **persistent bleeding**: consult gastroenterology, interventional radiology and surgery and admit to ICU (rescue options include TIPS, balloon tamponade and surgery).

### 5.9 When to go to surgery

Surgery is for failure of endoscopic and radiological control, perforation, or recurrent bleeding in a high-risk patient, ideally decided jointly with gastroenterology and interventional radiology.

## 6. Summary approach

1. Initial assessment with detailed history and examination.
2. Haemodynamic status and resuscitation (tachycardia, hypotension).
3. Blood transfusion if Hb below 7 or uraemic.
4. Risk assessment and stratification.
5. Pre-endoscopic therapy: IV PPI (and octreotide if variceal) so the view is clearer.
6. Timing of endoscopy.
7. Endoscopic therapy: clipping, banding, cautery.
8. Post-endoscopy care, monitoring and *H. pylori* eradication.

| 65-year-old diabetic, 4 months of weight loss, fatigue and intermittent dark stool, occasional aspirin | Ask about pain, haematemesis, dysphagia, anaemic symptoms, liver disease, NSAIDs. Likely **gastric cancer**; next step **endoscopy** |

| Alcoholic liver disease, large haematemesis, varices banded, then melaena and hypotension 12 hours later | Re-bleed: call gastroenterology, interventional radiology, surgery; ICU |

## Key points to revise

1. Upper GI bleed: above the ligament of Treitz; peptic ulcer commonest, varices second.
2. Melaena is usually upper GI; haematochezia usually lower GI, but can be a massive upper bleed.
3. Raised urea with normal creatinine means a large upper GI bleed.
4. Resuscitate first (permissive hypotension), restrictive transfusion (Hb below 7, or below 10 in heart disease).
5. Glasgow-Blatchford before endoscopy; Rockall after; AIMS65 is quick.
6. Endoscopy within 24 hours (variceal within 12); high-risk stigmata get endoscopic haemostasis plus 72 hours of intensive IV PPI.
7. Variceal bleed: vasoactive drug (terlipressin, somatostatin, octreotide), antibiotics, band ligation; propranolol, nadolol or carvedilol plus banding to prevent bleeding.
8. Test and eradicate *H. pylori*; stop NSAIDs.
