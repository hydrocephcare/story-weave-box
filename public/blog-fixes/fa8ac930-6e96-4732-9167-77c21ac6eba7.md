## SECTION A: NORMAL CELL CYCLE & REGULATION (6 Questions)
**1. Explain the role of the Cyclin D-CDK4 complex in regulating the G1/S checkpoint.**

**Answer:**

- Cyclin D synthesized in mid-G1 phase
- Binds to CDK4 forming Cyclin D-CDK4 complex
- Complex phosphorylates retinoblastoma (RB) protein
- Hypophosphorylated RB normally binds E2F transcription factor (inactive state)
- Phosphorylated RB releases E2F
- Free E2F activates transcription of genes for S phase (Cyclin E, DNA polymerases)
- Acts as molecular "on switch" for cell cycle progression

**2. Describe the two main families of CDK inhibitors (CKIs) and their mechanisms.**

**Answer:** **Cip/Kip family:**

- Components: p21, p27, p57
- Bind and inactivate Cyclin-CDK complexes
- p21 controlled by p53 tumor suppressor
- Important for DNA damage response

**INK4/ARF family:**

- Encodes p16INK4a and p14ARF
- p16INK4a competes with Cyclin D for CDK4 binding
- Prevents RB phosphorylation → cell cycle arrest in late G1
- Frequently mutated/hypermethylated in cancers
- Act as tumor suppressors

**3. What happens at the G1/S and G2/M checkpoints?**

**Answer:** **G1/S checkpoint:**

- Checks for DNA damage before S phase
- S phase is "point of no return"
- If damage detected: repair mechanisms activated and cell cycle arrested
- If irreparable: apoptosis triggered
- Prevents replication of damaged DNA

**G2/M checkpoint:**

- Monitors completion of DNA replication
- Ensures cell can safely initiate mitosis
- Critical after ionizing radiation exposure
- Defects lead to chromosomal abnormalities
- Prevents mitosis with incomplete/damaged DNA

**4. Explain p53's role as "Guardian of the Genome."**

**Answer:**

- Central monitor of cellular stress
- Activated by: DNA damage, anoxia, inappropriate oncogene signaling
- Upon activation (by phosphorylation):Drives transcription of CDKN1A (p21) → blocks RB phosphorylation → G1/S arrest
- Allows time for DNA repair
- If repair fails: induces cellular senescence or apoptosis

- Mutated in ~70% of human tumors (biallelic loss)
- One defective allele in Li-Fraumeni Syndrome
- Can be inactivated by viral oncoproteins (HPV)

**5. Describe how Cyclin B-CDK1 initiates mitosis.**

**Answer:**

- Cyclin A-CDK2 complex forms at G2/M transition
- Regulates events at mitotic prophase
- Cyclin B-CDK1 complex subsequently formed
- Activated by protein phosphatase
- Causes nuclear membrane breakdown
- Initiates mitosis proper
- Essential for M phase entry

**6. What is the significance of E2F transcription factor in cell cycle progression?**

**Answer:**

- Bound and inactivated by hypophosphorylated RB in G1
- Released when RB is phosphorylated by Cyclin D-CDK4
- Activated E2F increases transcription of:Cyclin E (forms complex with CDK2)
- DNA polymerases
- Genes essential for S phase progression

- Drives DNA synthesis
- Also induces Cyclin A transcription for G2/M transition
- Master regulator of S phase entry

## SECTION B: ONCOGENES & SELF-SUFFICIENCY IN GROWTH (5 Questions)
**7. Differentiate between proto-oncogenes and oncogenes.**

**Answer:** **Proto-oncogenes:**

- Normal physiologic regulators of cell proliferation and differentiation
- Required for normal growth and development
- Tightly regulated
- Examples: RAS, MYC, growth factor receptors

**Oncogenes:**

- Mutated/altered versions of proto-oncogenes
- Promote autonomous cell growth even without normal mitogenic signals
- Oncoproteins lack important regulatory elements
- Constitutively active
- Contribute to cancer development

**8. Explain how cancer cells achieve growth factor self-sufficiency.**

**Answer:**

- Normal cells require external growth factors for proliferation
- Cancer cells achieve autonomy through: **Autocrine stimulation:** Synthesize growth factors they can respond to (have receptors for same)
- **Mutant receptors:** Constitutively dimerized and activated without ligand binding
- Deliver continuous mitogenic signals
- Independence from external growth signals

- Examples: PDGF in gliomas, TGF-α in sarcomas

**9. Describe the RAS oncogene and its role in cancer.**

**Answer:**

- RAS = signal-transducing protein
- Located under cell membrane
- Normal function: receives signals from growth factor receptors, transmits to nucleus
- In cancers:Point mutations prevent GTPase activity
- RAS locked in active GTP-bound state
- Continuous proliferative signaling
- No external growth factor needed

- Mutations in RAS family (HRAS, KRAS, NRAS) found in ~30% human cancers
- Especially common in pancreatic, colon, lung cancers

**10. How does the BCR-ABL fusion protein cause cancer?**

**Answer:**

- Results from t(9;22) Philadelphia chromosome translocation
- Occurs in Chronic Myeloid Leukemia (CML) and some ALL
- c-ABL gene (chromosome 9) fused to BCR gene (chromosome 22)
- BCR-ABL fusion protein produced
- Loss of regulatory region controlling tyrosine kinase activity
- Constitutively active tyrosine kinase
- Continuous proliferative signaling independent of growth factors
- Targeted by imatinib (Gleevec) - tyrosine kinase inhibitor

**11. Explain the role of MYC oncogene in human cancers.**

**Answer:**

- Most commonly involved oncogene in human cancers
- Nuclear transcription factor
- Normal role: regulates cell growth, proliferation, differentiation
- In cancer (when overexpressed/amplified):Potent transcriptional activator
- Multiple downstream effects on proliferation
- Drives cell cycle progression
- Inhibits differentiation
- Promotes angiogenesis

- Examples: Burkitt lymphoma (MYC translocation), breast cancer (amplification)

## SECTION C: TUMOR SUPPRESSORS & GROWTH INHIBITION (5 Questions)
**12. Explain Knudson's "Two-Hit Hypothesis" using retinoblastoma as example.**

**Answer:**

- Both copies of tumor suppressor gene must be lost for cancer
- RB gene at chromosome 13q14

**Familial retinoblastoma:**

- First hit: inherited germline mutation (one defective allele)
- Second hit: somatic mutation in retinal cell (loss of normal allele)
- Earlier onset, bilateral tumors, multiple lesions

**Sporadic retinoblastoma:**

- Both hits: somatic mutations in same retinoblast
- Later onset, unilateral, single lesion
- Both alleles lost randomly

**13. How does RB protein act as "Governor of the Cell Cycle"?**

**Answer:**

- Controls G1/S checkpoint
- Active form: hypophosphorylated RB
- Binds E2F transcription factor
- Prevents transcription of S phase genes (Cyclin E, DNA replication genes)
- Blocks cell cycle progression
- When phosphorylated by Cyclin D-CDK4:Releases E2F
- Cell cycle proceeds

- Loss of RB function: uncontrolled progression through G1/S
- Almost all cancers have disabled G1 checkpoint (RB mutation or genes affecting RB)

**14. Describe how TGF-β pathway inhibits cell proliferation.**

**Answer:**

- TGF-β is potent growth inhibitor in normal epithelial, endothelial, hematopoietic cells
- Binds to TGF-β receptor complex (types I and II)
- Receptor dimerization upon ligand binding
- Signaling cascade activated
- Results:Transcriptional activation of CDKIs (growth suppression)
- Repression of growth-promoting genes (MYC, CDK2, CDK4, Cyclins A and E)
- Cell cycle arrest

- Mutations in pathway components:100% of pancreatic cancers
- 83% of colon cancers

- Late-stage tumors: TGF-β paradoxically promotes EMT and metastasis

**15. Explain the role of APC gene in colon cancer.**

**Answer:**

- APC = Adenomatous Polyposis Coli tumor suppressor gene
- Normal function: regulates destruction of cytoplasmic β-catenin
- With APC loss/mutation:β-catenin not destroyed
- Accumulates in cytoplasm
- Translocates to nucleus
- Acts as growth-promoting transcription factor
- Activates proliferative genes

**Clinical significance:**

- Familial Adenomatous Polyposis (FAP): germline APC mutation + somatic loss
- Hundreds of colonic polyps at young age
- Inevitably progresses to colon cancer
- ~70% sporadic colon cancers: somatic loss of both APC alleles

**16. How do E-cadherin and NF2 maintain contact inhibition?**

**Answer:** **E-cadherin:**

- Acts as intercellular "glue"
- Maintains cell-cell adhesion
- Cytoplasmic portion binds β-catenin
- Transmits anti-growth signals by sequestering β-catenin
- Lost in almost all epithelial cancers

**NF2 gene (Neurofibromin-2/Merlin):**

- Tumor suppressor
- Facilitates E-cadherin-mediated contact inhibition
- Mediates growth arrest when cells contact each other

**Loss in cancer:**

- Contact inhibition abolished
- Cells pile on top of each other
- Unrestricted growth
- Loss of tissue organization

## SECTION D: EVASION OF APOPTOSIS & AUTOPHAGY (3 Questions)
**17. Describe the intrinsic (mitochondrial) pathway of apoptosis.**

**Answer:**

- Triggered by internal stress signals (DNA damage, hypoxia, growth factor withdrawal)
- Regulated by BCL-2 family proteins:

**Pro-apoptotic:** BAX, BAK

- Promote mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP)

**Anti-apoptotic:** BCL-2, BCL-XL

- Prevent MOMP

**BH3-only proteins:** BAD, BID, PUMA

- Sensors of stress
- Tilt balance toward pro-apoptotic proteins
- MOMP releases cytochrome c
- Activates caspase cascade
- Cell death

**18. How do cancer cells evade apoptosis?**

**Answer:**

- **Overexpression of anti-apoptotic proteins: **BCL-2 overexpression (follicular lymphomas - t(14;18))
- BCL-XL upregulation

- **Loss of pro-apoptotic proteins: **Mutations in BAX, BAK
- Reduced BH3-only protein expression

- **p53 inactivation: **Cannot trigger apoptosis in response to DNA damage
- ~70% of cancers

- **Loss of death receptor signaling: **Reduced Fas/FasL expression

- **Increased survival signals: **PI3K/AKT pathway activation
- Inhibits apoptotic machinery

**19. What is autophagy and its dual role in cancer?**

**Answer:** **Definition:**

- Self-eating process
- Cells consume their own components during stress
- Normally: cellular housekeeping and survival mechanism

**Dual role in cancer:**

**Tumor suppressive (early):**

- Removes damaged organelles and proteins
- Prevents accumulation of toxic materials
- Maintains genomic stability
- Mutations to avoid autophagy promote early carcinogenesis

**Tumor promoting (established cancers):**

- Cancer cells exploit autophagy for survival
- Provides nutrients during metabolic stress
- Supports rapid growth in nutrient-poor tumor environment
- Resistance to therapy

## SECTION E: LIMITLESS REPLICATIVE POTENTIAL & ANGIOGENESIS (4 Questions)
**20. Explain how telomeres and telomerase relate to cellular immortality.**

**Answer:** **Normal cells:**

- Telomeres shorten with each cell division
- After 50-70 divisions (Hayflick limit): critically short telomeres
- Activate cell cycle checkpoints → senescence
- Limit on replicative potential

**Cancer cells:**

- Disabled checkpoints: shortened telomeres trigger chromosomal instability
- Leads to mitotic crisis (massive cell death)
- Survivors reactivate telomerase enzyme
- Telomerase adds TTAGGG repeats to chromosome ends
- Maintains telomere length indefinitely
- Achieves cellular immortality
- ~90% of cancers have telomerase reactivation

**21. Describe the "angiogenic switch" in tumor development.**

**Answer:** **Early tumor growth:**

- Small tumors (≤1-2 mm) survive without new blood vessels
- Maximal diffusion distance for O₂ and nutrients
- Vascular quiescence state

**Angiogenic switch:**

- Balance shifts toward pro-angiogenic factors
- Increased production of angiogenic factors (VEGF, bFGF)
- Loss of angiogenesis inhibitors (TSP-1)

**Triggers:**

- Hypoxia (low oxygen)
- Oncogene activation
- Loss of p53 (normally induces TSP-1)

**Result:**

- Neoangiogenesis or vasculogenesis
- New blood vessel formation
- Tumor can grow beyond 1-2 mm
- Access to vasculature for metastasis

**22. Explain the role of HIF-1α and VHL in tumor angiogenesis.**

**Answer:** **Normal oxygen conditions:**

- HIF-1α continuously produced
- VHL protein binds HIF-1α
- HIF-1α ubiquitinated and destroyed
- Low VEGF production

**Hypoxic conditions (tumor):**

- Lack of oxygen prevents VHL recognition of HIF-1α
- HIF-1α not destroyed
- Translocates to nucleus
- Activates transcription of target genes (VEGF, glycolytic enzymes)
- Increased angiogenesis

**VHL as tumor suppressor:**

- Germline VHL mutations cause:Hereditary renal cell cancers
- Pheochromocytomas
- CNS hemangiomas (VHL syndrome)

**23. Why is tumor vasculature abnormal and what are the implications?**

**Answer:** **Characteristics of tumor vessels:**

- Leaky and dilated
- Haphazard, disorganized pattern
- Incomplete basement membranes
- Irregular blood flow
- Poor oxygen delivery despite increased vessels

**Dual effects on tumor:**

**Positive (for tumor):**

- Supplies nutrients and oxygen (even if inefficiently)
- Newly formed endothelial cells secrete growth factors (IGF, PDGF, GM-CSF)
- Stimulate adjacent tumor cell growth
- Provide route for metastasis

**Implications:**

- Required for tumors >1-2 mm
- Necessary for metastasis (access to circulation)
- Target for anti-angiogenic therapy (bevacizumab - anti-VEGF)
- Abnormal vessels contribute to hypoxic tumor microenvironment

## SECTION F: INVASION & METASTASIS (5 Questions)
**24. Outline the steps of the invasion-metastasis cascade.**

**Answer:** **Local invasion:**

- Loosening of tumor cell-to-cell contacts
- Degradation of basement membrane and ECM
- Attachment to ECM proteins
- Migration through degraded matrix

**Vascular dissemination:** 5. Intravasation into blood/lymph vessels 6. Survival in circulation (as single cells or emboli) 7. Arrest in capillary beds of distant organs

**Colonization:** 8. Extravasation from vessels 9. Formation of micrometastases 10. Growth into macroscopic tumors (colonization)

**Key points:**

- Sequential, multi-step process
- Can be interrupted at any stage
- Most cells fail - metastasis is inefficient

**25. Explain the four steps of ECM invasion by tumor cells.**

**Answer:** **Step 1: Loosening of cell-cell contacts**

- Loss of E-cadherin function (mutation, reduced expression)
- Loss of β-catenin sequestration
- Upregulation of SNAIL/TWIST (suppress E-cadherin)

**Step 2: Degradation of ECM**

- Secretion of proteases (MMPs, cathepsin D, urokinase)
- Induction of stromal cells to produce proteases
- MMP-9 cleaves type IV collagen (basement membrane)
- Release of ECM-sequestered growth factors (VEGF)

**Step 3: Attachment to ECM**

- Expression/modulation of integrin receptors
- Binding to laminin, collagen, fibronectin
- Matrix modification creates new binding sites
- Loss of normal polarity

**Step 4: Locomotion**

- Chemotaxis toward cleaved ECM products
- Response to autocrine motility factors
- Paracrine factors from stroma (HGF/SCF)
- Cytoskeletal rearrangement for migration

**26. Describe the role of Matrix Metalloproteinases (MMPs) in cancer.**

**Answer:** **Function:**

- Proteolytic enzymes that degrade ECM components
- Particularly MMP-2 and MMP-9 (gelatinases) - cleave type IV collagen

**Pro-metastatic activities:**

- Breakdown basement membrane and interstitial matrix
- Create paths for tumor cell migration
- Release ECM-sequestered growth factors (VEGF, bFGF)
- Generate chemotactic cleavage products
- Promote angiogenesis
- Facilitate intravasation and extravasation

**Clinical correlation:**

- Overexpressed in many cancers
- Benign tumors: low MMP activity
- Malignant counterparts: high MMP activity
- Reduced levels of tissue inhibitors of MMPs (TIMPs)
- Balance tilted toward degradation

**27. Explain organ tropism in metastasis.**

**Answer:** **"Seed and Soil" hypothesis:**

- Metastasis depends on both tumor cells (seed) and target organ (soil)

**Mechanisms of organ tropism:**

**1. Anatomical (first capillary bed):**

- Liver: GI cancers via portal circulation
- Lungs: most cancers via systemic venous circulation

**2. Adhesion molecules:**

- Tumor cells express adhesion molecules
- Match ligands on endothelium of target organs
- Selective extravasation

**3. Chemokine-receptor interactions:**

- Breast cancer expresses CXCR4 and CCR7 receptors
- Ligands (CXCL12, CCL21) highly expressed in bones, lungs, liver, lymph nodes
- Directed migration to these sites

**4. Receptive stroma:**

- Target organ must provide growth-permissive environment
- Some tissues nonpermissive (skeletal muscle - rarely metastatic site)

**28. What is the role of stromal cells in tumor invasion and metastasis?**

**Answer:** **Tumor-stromal cell interactions:**

- Not passive barrier but active, dynamic environment
- Reciprocal signaling between tumor and stroma

**Stromal cell types:**

- Fibroblasts (cancer-associated fibroblasts - CAFs)
- Immune cells (macrophages, lymphocytes)
- Endothelial cells

**Tumor-promoting activities:**

**CAFs produce:**

- Growth factors (HGF/SCF, EGF)
- ECM-remodeling enzymes (MMPs, LOX)
- Pro-angiogenic factors (VEGF)
- Chemokines attracting immune cells

**Immune cells:**

- Intended to destroy tumor
- Paradoxically produce growth factors
- Cause DNA damage (reactive oxygen species)
- Promote angiogenesis

**Outcome:**

- Successful tumors co-opt stromal environment
- Create tumor-promoting microenvironment

## SECTION G: EMERGING HALLMARKS (4 Questions)
**29. Explain the Warburg effect (aerobic glycolysis) in cancer cells.**

**Answer:** **Normal cells:**

- Use oxidative phosphorylation when oxygen available (36 ATP/glucose)
- Glycolysis only when hypoxic (2 ATP/glucose)

**Cancer cells (Warburg effect):**

- Shift to glycolysis even with adequate oxygen (aerobic glycolysis)
- Less efficient (2 ATP/glucose vs 36)
- Paradox: rapidly growing tumors use inefficient pathway

**Advantages for cancer:**

- Rapid production of biosynthetic precursors
- Glycolytic intermediates shunted to biosynthesis (lipids, nucleotides, amino acids)
- Supports rapid cell division
- Lactate production acidifies microenvironment (promotes invasion)

**Molecular basis:**

- Oncogenes (RAS, AKT, MYC) upregulate glucose transporters
- p53 loss promotes glycolysis
- HIF-1α activation (even in normoxia)
- Metabolic reprogramming "hard-wired" into tumor cells

**Clinical application:**

- PET scanning uses ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose
- Tumors are "glucose hungry" - PET positive

**30. Describe genomic instability as an enabler of malignancy.**

**Answer:** **Concept:**

- Genomic instability accelerates acquisition of cancer-enabling mutations
- "Mutator phenotype"

**DNA repair defects associated with cancer:**

**1. Mismatch repair defects:**

- HNPCC (Lynch syndrome)
- Microsatellite instability (MSI)
- Colon cancer predisposition

**2. Nucleotide excision repair defects:**

- Xeroderma pigmentosum
- Cannot repair UV-induced pyrimidine dimers
- Skin cancer risk

**3. Homologous recombination defects:**

- Bloom syndrome, Ataxia-telangiectasia, Fanconi anemia
- BRCA1/BRCA2 mutations (familial breast/ovarian cancer)
- Hypersensitivity to DNA-damaging agents

**4. Lymphoid-specific:**

- RAG1/RAG2, AID mutations
- V(D)J recombination errors
- Lymphoid neoplasms

**Result:**

- Increased mutation rate
- Accelerated tumor evolution
- Acquisition of multiple hallmarks

## SECTION H: TUMOR-PROMOTING INFLAMMATION & CARCINOGENESIS (3 Questions)
**31. Explain tumor-promoting inflammation in two clinical settings.**

**Answer:** **Setting 1: Chronic inflammation preceding cancer**

**Examples:**

- Barrett esophagus → esophageal adenocarcinoma
- Ulcerative colitis → colorectal cancer
- H. pylori gastritis → gastric cancer
- Hepatitis B/C → hepatocellular carcinoma
- Chronic pancreatitis → pancreatic cancer

**Mechanisms:**

- Chronic tissue injury
- Compensatory proliferation for repair
- Inflammatory cells produce: growth factors, cytokines, chemokines
- Persistent replication increases mutation risk
- Neutrophils generate reactive oxygen species → DNA damage
- Reduced apoptosis

**Setting 2: Inflammation in response to tumors**

**Tumor-infiltrating leukocytes:**

- Present in virtually every tumor
- Adaptive and innate immune cells

**Paradoxical effects:**

- Intended: anti-tumor immunity
- Actual: often tumor-promotingProduce growth factors
- Inflict DNA damage (ROS)
- Promote angiogenesis
- Remodel ECM

**Therapeutic implications:**

- COX-2 inhibitors for cancer prevention/treatment
- COX-2 converts arachidonic acid → prostaglandins
- COX-2 overexpressed in colon and other cancers

**32. Describe the multistep carcinogenesis model using colon cancer.**

**Answer:** **Principle:**

- Cancer develops through accumulation of multiple mutations
- Morphologically identifiable stages
- Stepwise acquisition of malignant phenotype

**Colon carcinogenesis sequence:**

**1. Normal epithelium** ↓ Loss of APC (both alleles)

**2. Hyperproliferative epithelium** ↓ Continued proliferation

**3. Early adenoma** ↓ RAS mutation (activation)

**4. Intermediate adenoma** ↓ Loss of chromosome 18q tumor suppressor

**5. Late adenoma** ↓ Loss of TP53 (both alleles)

**6. Carcinoma** ↓ Additional mutations

**7. Metastatic carcinoma**

**Key concepts:**

- APC loss: initiating event
- RAS: promotes growth
- 18q loss: further growth advantage
- p53 loss: genomic instability, evasion of apoptosis
- Temporal sequence may vary but combination required
- ~10-15 years from initiation to cancer

**33. What are the clinical and therapeutic implications of understanding cancer hallmarks?**

**Answer:** **Diagnostic implications:**

- PET scanning exploits Warburg effect
- Tumor markers based on oncogene/tumor suppressor alterations
- Genetic testing for hereditary syndromes (BRCA, Lynch, FAP)
- Microsatellite instability testing

**Prognostic implications:**

- p53 status predicts response to therapy
- HER2 expression guides treatment decisions
- Angiogenic profiles predict aggressive behavior

**Therapeutic targeting:**

**1. Self-sufficiency in growth:**

- Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (imatinib for BCR-ABL)
- Anti-EGFR antibodies (cetuximab)
- HER2 inhibitors (trastuzumab)

**2. Evasion of apoptosis:**

- BCL-2 inhibitors (venetoclax)
- p53 restoration strategies

**3. Angiogenesis:**

- Anti-VEGF antibodies (bevacizumab)
- Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (sunitinib, sorafenib)

**4. Genomic instability:**

- PARP inhibitors for BRCA-mutant cancers
- Checkpoint kinase inhibitors

**5. Immune evasion:**

- Checkpoint inhibitors (anti-PD-1, anti-CTLA-4)
- CAR-T cell therapy

**6. Metabolism:**

- Targeting glycolysis pathways (under development)

**Prevention:**

- COX-2 inhibitors for high-risk patients
- Anti-inflammatory strategies
