## Section B: Short Answer Questions

**1.** Explain what you understand by "signal transduction". (5 marks)

Answer:
- Signal transduction is the process by which a cell converts an extracellular signal (a ligand such as a hormone or neurotransmitter) into an intracellular response.
- The ligand binds a specific receptor (on the surface or inside the cell), which changes shape.
- The receptor activates intracellular messengers, for example G proteins and second messengers (cAMP, IP3, DAG, Ca²⁺) or kinase cascades.
- Each step amplifies the signal, so a few ligand molecules can cause a large response.
- The end result is a change in enzyme activity, ion channels, gene expression or cell behaviour, and the signal is switched off by degradation of messengers or receptor desensitisation.

**2.** Explain how G-protein acts as a molecular switch. (5 marks)

Answer:
- A G protein is a heterotrimer (alpha, beta, gamma) bound to the inner face of the membrane and linked to a receptor.
- Inactive state: the alpha subunit carries GDP.
- Ligand binding changes the receptor shape, which makes the alpha subunit swap GDP for GTP.
- Active state: the alpha-GTP subunit separates from the beta-gamma dimer and acts on a target such as adenylyl cyclase or phospholipase C.
- Off switch: the alpha subunit has GTPase activity, hydrolyses GTP to GDP and re-joins beta-gamma, which turns the signal off. This on/off cycling is why it is called a molecular switch.

**3.** Briefly describe the roles of sodium ions and potassium ions in the development and maintenance of the resting membrane potential.

Answer:
- Resting membrane potential (about −70 mV) is set mainly by the K⁺ gradient: K⁺ is high inside and leaks out through leak channels, leaving the inside negative.
- Na⁺ is high outside and enters slowly, because the membrane is only slightly permeable to it; this makes the inside slightly less negative.
- The Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pumps 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in using ATP, which maintains both gradients and is electrogenic.
- Because the membrane is about 50 to 100 times more permeable to K⁺ than Na⁺, the potential lies close to the K⁺ equilibrium potential (about −90 mV).

**4.** (a) Define non-propagated potentials. (b) What are the characteristics of non-propagated potentials?

Answer:
- (a) Non-propagated (graded) potentials are local changes in membrane potential that do not spread far and do not regenerate.
- (b) Characteristics:
  - their size depends on the strength of the stimulus (they are not all-or-none)
  - they can be depolarising or hyperpolarising
  - they decay with distance (decremental conduction)
  - they can add together (summation)
  - there is no refractory period
  - examples: receptor (generator) potentials, end-plate potentials, EPSPs and IPSPs.

**5.** Describe the process of myelination of a myelinated peripheral nerve.

Answer:
- Myelination is carried out by Schwann cells in the peripheral nervous system, with one Schwann cell per internode.
- A Schwann cell contacts the axon and its membrane surrounds the axon.
- The cell then rotates around the axon, wrapping many layers of its membrane in a spiral.
- Cytoplasm is squeezed out so the layers compact into the lipid-rich myelin sheath.
- Gaps between adjacent Schwann cells are the nodes of Ranvier, which allow saltatory conduction.

**7.** Describe the structure of a cell membrane.

Answer:
- The cell membrane is a fluid mosaic, a phospholipid bilayer about 7.5 to 10 nm thick.
- Phospholipids have hydrophilic heads facing the water and hydrophobic fatty acid tails facing inward.
- Cholesterol sits between the phospholipids and controls fluidity.
- Integral (transmembrane) proteins form channels, carriers, pumps and receptors; peripheral proteins attach to the surface, mostly the inner face.
- Carbohydrates are attached to the outer face as glycolipids and glycoproteins (glycocalyx) used in cell recognition.

**8.** Define and explain feed-forward control with an example.

Answer:
- Feed-forward control anticipates a change and acts before the controlled variable moves, so the disturbance is reduced or prevented.
- It works from a signal that predicts the change, rather than from the error afterwards.
- Example: the thought, sight or smell of food starts gastric and salivary secretion before food arrives (cephalic phase). Another example is the rise in ventilation and heart rate at the start of exercise before blood gases change.
- It speeds the response and works together with feedback control.

**9.** List and quantify the body fluid compartments.

Answer:
- Total body water is about 60% of body weight, about 42 L in a 70 kg man.
- Intracellular fluid: about 28 L (two thirds, 40% of body weight).
- Extracellular fluid: about 14 L (one third, 20% of body weight), made of
  - interstitial fluid about 10.5 L
  - plasma about 3.5 L
  - transcellular fluid (CSF, joint, pleural and similar fluids) about 1 L.

**10.** Contrast the functional properties of the smooth and rough endoplasmic reticulum.

Answer:
- Smooth ER has no ribosomes. Its functions are lipid and steroid hormone synthesis, detoxification of drugs, glycogen metabolism and calcium storage (sarcoplasmic reticulum in muscle).
- Rough ER is covered with ribosomes. It makes proteins for secretion, the membrane and lysosomes, and begins their folding and glycosylation.
- Rough ER is flattened sacs near the nucleus; smooth ER is a network of tubules.
- Cells that secrete protein (plasma cells, pancreatic cells) have much rough ER; steroid-making and liver cells have much smooth ER.

**11.** Describe the reasons why transfer RNA is often referred to as an adaptor molecule.

Answer:
- tRNA is the adaptor between the language of nucleic acids (codons) and that of proteins (amino acids).
- One end of the molecule carries a specific amino acid, attached by an aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase.
- The anticodon loop on the opposite side base-pairs with the matching mRNA codon on the ribosome.
- So each codon brings in the correct amino acid and the chain is built in the order the mRNA specifies.
- There is at least one tRNA for each amino acid, and wobble pairing lets some tRNAs read more than one codon.

**12.** Describe the functions and properties of the inner mitochondrial membrane.

Answer:
- The inner mitochondrial membrane is highly folded into cristae, which increases its surface area.
- It contains the electron transport chain complexes I to IV and ATP synthase (complex V) for oxidative phosphorylation.
- It is impermeable to most ions and molecules, which allows a proton gradient to build across it; transport proteins carry in pyruvate, ADP and phosphate.
- It is rich in cardiolipin and has a high protein-to-lipid ratio (about 75% protein).
- Its main function is making most of the cell's ATP.

## Section C: Essays (answer only ONE)

**1.** Discuss cellular communication under the following sub-topics:
(a) Discuss the properties of membrane receptors. (15 marks)
(b) Discuss the various types of membrane receptors. (10 marks)
(c) Explain what a second messenger is and how it is formed. (5 marks)

Answer:
**(a) Properties of membrane receptors (15 marks)**
- Receptors are proteins (or glycoproteins) that bind specific signalling molecules (ligands).
- Specificity: each receptor binds only ligands that fit its binding site.
- High affinity: they respond to very low concentrations of ligand.
- Saturability: there is a finite number of receptors, so the response reaches a maximum.
- Reversible, non-covalent binding with the ligand.
- Binding changes receptor shape and starts a response; ligand binding is coupled to an effector (channel, enzyme, G protein).
- Amplification: one ligand can trigger many intracellular molecules.
- Regulation: the number and sensitivity change; down-regulation (fewer receptors with high ligand levels), up-regulation (more with low levels) and desensitisation.
- Agonists activate and antagonists block them; they are the targets of many drugs.
- Located in the cell membrane for water-soluble ligands, and inside the cell for lipid-soluble ones.

**(b) Types of membrane receptors (10 marks)**
- Ion channel-linked (ligand-gated) receptors: ligand opens a channel, for example the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor; fast responses.
- G protein-coupled receptors: seven-transmembrane receptors acting through G proteins and second messengers, for example adrenergic and muscarinic receptors.
- Enzyme-linked receptors: receptor has or associates with enzyme activity, for example the insulin receptor (tyrosine kinase) and receptor guanylyl cyclase (ANP).
- Cytokine receptors linked to JAK-STAT pathways.
- (Intracellular receptors for steroid and thyroid hormones are not membrane receptors.)

**(c) Second messengers (5 marks)**
- A second messenger is a small intracellular molecule that relays and amplifies the signal after the first messenger (ligand) binds its receptor.
- cAMP: receptor activates Gs, which activates adenylyl cyclase to turn ATP into cAMP.
- IP3 and DAG: receptor activates Gq, which activates phospholipase C to split PIP2 into IP3 (releases Ca²⁺ from the ER) and DAG (activates protein kinase C).
- cGMP: formed from GTP by guanylyl cyclase.
- Ca²⁺ is another messenger, released from stores or entering through channels.

**2.** Discuss the functions of the sodium-potassium ATPase pump under the following headings:
(a) The pump mechanism (5 marks)
(b) Effect on resting membrane potential (5 marks)
(c) The control of cell volume (5 marks)
(d) Cerebellar Purkinje cell function (5 marks)
(e) Absorption of glucose in the gut (5 marks)
(f) The movement of calcium in skeletal muscles (5 marks)

Answer:
**(a) Pump mechanism (5 marks)**
- The Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase is an integral protein with alpha and beta subunits.
- It pumps 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in against their gradients, using the energy of one ATP.
- Cycle: three Na⁺ bind inside, the pump is phosphorylated by ATP and changes shape, releasing Na⁺ outside; two K⁺ bind outside, the pump is dephosphorylated and returns, releasing K⁺ inside.
- It is inhibited by digoxin and ouabain.

**(b) Effect on resting membrane potential (5 marks)**
- It is electrogenic: moving 3 positive charges out for 2 in adds about −3 to −4 mV to the potential.
- Its greater contribution is indirect, maintaining the Na⁺ and K⁺ gradients that produce the resting potential.

**(c) Control of cell volume (5 marks)**
- Cells contain impermeant negatively charged proteins, which pull in cations and water (Donnan effect).
- By pumping out 3 Na⁺ for 2 K⁺ the pump keeps intracellular osmolarity down and prevents swelling and bursting.
- If the pump fails, Na⁺ and water accumulate and the cell swells.

**(d) Cerebellar Purkinje cell function (5 marks)**
- Purkinje cells fire at a high, steady rate and carry a heavy Na⁺ load.
- The pump restores Na⁺ and K⁺ gradients after each action potential so they can keep firing.
- Pump failure or mutation causes cerebellar dysfunction (for example ataxia).

**(e) Absorption of glucose in the gut (5 marks)**
- Basolateral Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase keeps intracellular Na⁺ low.
- This steep Na⁺ gradient drives SGLT1, which carries glucose in with Na⁺ (secondary active transport).
- Glucose leaves across the basolateral side through GLUT2 by facilitated diffusion.

**(f) Movement of calcium in skeletal muscle (5 marks)**
- The Na⁺ gradient made by the pump drives the Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger, which removes Ca²⁺ from the cell.
- The sarcoplasmic reticulum Ca²⁺-ATPase (SERCA) separately pumps Ca²⁺ back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum for relaxation.

**4.** Write an essay under the following subheadings:
(a) Discuss how molecules move across cell membranes. (15 marks)
(b) Discuss the functions of membrane proteins. (15 marks)

Answer:
**(a) How molecules move across cell membranes (15 marks)**
- Passive transport (no energy)
  - Simple diffusion: lipid-soluble molecules and gases (O₂, CO₂) move down their concentration gradient; rate depends on gradient, area, thickness and lipid solubility (Fick's law).
  - Osmosis: water moves through the bilayer and aquaporins towards the higher solute concentration.
  - Facilitated diffusion: carrier proteins (for example GLUT) and ion channels (leak, voltage-gated, ligand-gated) move molecules down the gradient; shows saturation and specificity.
- Active transport (needs energy)
  - Primary active transport: pumps use ATP directly, for example Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, Ca²⁺-ATPase, H⁺/K⁺-ATPase.
  - Secondary active transport uses the Na⁺ gradient: co-transport (symport, for example SGLT1 for glucose) and counter-transport (antiport, for example Na⁺/Ca²⁺ and Na⁺/H⁺ exchangers).
- Vesicular transport: endocytosis (phagocytosis, pinocytosis, receptor-mediated), exocytosis (secretion, adding membrane proteins) and transcytosis.
- Factors: concentration or electrochemical gradient, size, charge, lipid solubility, number of carriers or channels and temperature.

**(b) Functions of membrane proteins (15 marks)**
- Transport: channels and carriers allow selective movement of ions and molecules; pumps move substances against gradients.
- Receptors: bind hormones, neurotransmitters and growth factors and start signalling.
- Enzymes: for example brush border enzymes and adenylyl cyclase.
- Cell adhesion and junctions: cadherins, integrins and CAMs attach cells to each other and to the matrix (desmosomes, gap junctions).
- Cell recognition (glycoproteins): self recognition, blood group antigens, immune responses.
- Anchoring cytoskeleton: link the membrane to the cytoskeleton and give shape and mechanical strength.
- Electrical signalling: voltage-gated channels produce action potentials.
- Signal transduction: G proteins and kinase-linked proteins relay signals inside the cell.

**5.** Write an essay on Homeostasis under the following headings (10 marks each):
a) The internal environment
b) The theoretical control model types

*Source: physiology/questions/end sem-muinde.pdf.*

Answer:
**(a) The internal environment**
- Claude Bernard's milieu intérieur: the extracellular fluid in which the cells live (plasma and interstitial fluid).
- Cells exchange nutrients, oxygen and wastes with it, and nearly all body systems work to keep it stable.
- Variables kept within narrow limits include temperature, pH (7.35 to 7.45), osmolarity (about 285 mOsm/L), glucose, oxygen and carbon dioxide tension, and ion concentrations (Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺).
- Homeostasis is the dynamic maintenance of this constancy; failure of it causes disease.

**(b) The theoretical control model types**
- Components: a controlled variable, sensor (receptor), control centre (integrator, with set point), effector, and the disturbance or forcing function that moves the variable.
- Negative feedback: the response opposes the change and returns the variable to its set point (for example blood pressure and temperature control). It is the most common and stabilising type.
- Positive feedback: the response amplifies the change and the variable moves further from the start until a limit stops it (for example labour, blood clotting, the action potential upstroke).
- Feed-forward control: anticipates a change and acts before the variable moves (for example salivation at the sight of food).
- Open-loop control: no feedback from the output.
- Adaptive control: the controller changes its own characteristics in response to demand.
