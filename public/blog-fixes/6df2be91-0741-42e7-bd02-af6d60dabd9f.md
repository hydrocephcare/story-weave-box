# Malaria

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Infectious Diseases and HIV**

> **In one line:** malaria is a protozoan infection of red cells spread by the female *Anopheles* mosquito. *P. falciparum* is the killer because infected cells stick to the walls of small vessels (sequestration), which starves the brain, kidney, lung and gut of blood. Diagnose with a blood smear, treat uncomplicated disease with an ACT, and treat severe disease as an emergency with IV artesunate.

## What you must know

- Five species infect humans: *P. falciparum*, *P. vivax*, *P. ovale*, *P. malariae*, and *P. knowlesi* (Southeast Asia, from monkeys).
- *P. falciparum* causes nearly all severe disease and deaths in Africa.
- *P. vivax* and *P. ovale* have **hypnozoites** in the liver, so they **relapse**; they need primaquine as well.
- Diagnosis: **thick film** to find parasites, **thin film** to name the species and count parasitaemia; rapid tests (RDTs) when microscopy is not available.
- Kenya first line for uncomplicated malaria: **artemether-lumefantrine (AL)**. Severe malaria: **IV or IM artesunate** (a minimum of 3 doses or 24 hours), then a full course of AL.
- Severe malaria: coma, hypoglycaemia, acidosis, severe anaemia, acute kidney injury, pulmonary oedema, bleeding.

## 1. Epidemiology

- Malaria has been eliminated from the USA, Canada, Europe and Russia, but it has risen in parts of the tropics because of **drug-resistant parasites, insecticide-resistant mosquitoes** and human travel and migration.
- **Kenya** has four epidemiological zones, set mainly by altitude, rainfall and temperature:
  1. **Endemic** (lake and coast)
  2. **Seasonal transmission** (arid and semi-arid)
  3. **Epidemic-prone** (western highlands)
  4. **Low risk** (central highlands, including Nairobi)
- *P. falciparum* predominates in Africa. *P. vivax* is commoner in Central America. *P. malariae* is found in most endemic areas but is uncommon. *P. ovale* is mostly West African and under 1% of isolates elsewhere.

### Endemicity (parasitaemia or palpable spleen rate in children aged 2 to 9)

| Class | Rate |
|---|---|
| Hypoendemic | under 10% |
| Mesoendemic | 11 to 50% |
| Hyperendemic | 51 to 75% |
| Holoendemic | over 75% |

- **Stable transmission:** constant, year-round infection; adults acquire partial immunity and are often asymptomatic.
- **Unstable transmission:** low or erratic; immunity is incomplete, so people of **all ages** get symptomatic and severe disease.
- Transmission is measured by the **entomological inoculation rate** (infective bites per person per year): under 1 in parts of Latin America and Asia, over 300 in tropical Africa.

### Genetic protection

The inherited blood disorders are common where falciparum malaria was common, because carriers are protected: **sickle cell trait (HbAS)**, HbC and HbE, thalassaemias, **G6PD deficiency** and hereditary ovalocytosis. People who are **Duffy-negative** (most West Africans) are resistant to *P. vivax*, because the parasite enters red cells through the Duffy antigen.

## 2. Life cycle and pathogenesis

1. An infected female *Anopheles* bites and injects **sporozoites**.
2. Sporozoites reach the liver within minutes and enter hepatocytes (the **exo-erythrocytic stage**; no symptoms).
3. They multiply to **schizonts**, which burst and release **merozoites** into the blood.
4. In *P. vivax* and *P. ovale*, some liver forms stay dormant as **hypnozoites** for 3 weeks to a year or more. They are the cause of **relapse**.
5. Merozoites invade red cells (for *P. falciparum* the protein PfRh5 is essential) and become **trophozoites**, then **schizonts**. By the end the parasite has consumed about two-thirds of the cell's haemoglobin. The red cell bursts, releasing more merozoites and causing the fever.
6. Some blood-stage parasites become **gametocytes**. When a mosquito bites, it takes these up and the sexual cycle completes in the mosquito.

> **Why falciparum is worse:** infected red cells display adhesion proteins and **cytoadhere** to vessel walls and clump with uninfected cells (rosetting). This blocks the microcirculation. It is why *falciparum* parasites seen in the blood film are only the young rings; the older forms are hidden in the tissues. It also explains the multi-organ failure.

Disease is caused by the direct effects of red-cell invasion and destruction, plus the host's inflammatory reaction (cytokines such as TNF).

## 3. Clinical features

### Uncomplicated malaria

- Fever (irregular at first; classic periodicity is uncommon), chills, rigors, sweating, headache, myalgia, malaise, nausea and vomiting
- Mild jaundice, pallor, enlarged spleen (takes several days to become palpable in a non-immune person), tender liver
- In children, convulsions, vomiting and diarrhoea are common

Uncomplicated falciparum malaria (patient swallows medicines and food) has a mortality below 0.1%. **Once there is vital-organ dysfunction, or more than 2% of red cells are infected** (more than 10¹² parasites in an adult), the risk of death rises steeply.

### Severe falciparum malaria (WHO criteria in brief)

Any one of the following with asexual parasitaemia:

- **Impaired consciousness / coma** (Glasgow Coma Score under 11, Blantyre score under 3 in children) or prostration
- **Multiple convulsions** (more than 2 in 24 hours)
- **Severe anaemia** (Hb below 5 g/dL in children, below 7 in adults)
- **Acidosis** (bicarbonate below 15 mmol/L, or base deficit), deep "acidotic" breathing
- **Hypoglycaemia** (glucose below 2.2 mmol/L)
- **Acute kidney injury** (creatinine over 265 µmol/L)
- **Pulmonary oedema / ARDS**
- **Shock**, abnormal bleeding or DIC
- **Jaundice** (bilirubin over 50 µmol/L) with other organ dysfunction
- **Hyperparasitaemia** (over 10% in low-transmission areas)

Poor prognostic signs include coma, convulsions, acidotic breathing, hypoglycaemia, shock, high lactate, high parasitaemia with many pigmented schizonts and neutrophils containing pigment, and renal failure. **Plasma bicarbonate or lactate are the best biochemical predictors.**

### Complications in detail

| Complication | Key facts |
|---|---|
| **Cerebral malaria** | Coma is ominous: death in about 20% of adults and 15% of children. Diffuse symmetric encephalopathy, **focal signs are unusual**. Convulsions, divergent eyes, corneal reflexes preserved, variable reflexes. **Retinal haemorrhages in about 15%**; retinal whitening 30 to 60%; papilloedema in 8% of children; cotton-wool spots under 5%. |
| **Hypoglycaemia** | Worst in children and pregnant women. Failed hepatic gluconeogenesis plus higher glucose use. **Quinine** stimulates insulin secretion and makes it worse. In severe disease the usual sweating and tachycardia are absent. |
| **Metabolic acidosis** | Lactic acid from anaerobic glycolysis in tissues with sequestered parasites, lactate made by the parasites, and poor liver and kidney clearance. Often coexists with hypoglycaemia. |
| **Pulmonary oedema (ARDS)** | Mortality over 80%. Made worse by too much IV fluid. |
| **Acute kidney injury** | Acute tubular necrosis from sequestration; **cortical necrosis does not occur**. Early dialysis or haemofiltration improves survival. |
| **Anaemia** | Increased splenic removal, destruction at schizogony, and ineffective erythropoiesis. Common after drug-resistant, repeated infection. |
| **Coagulopathy** | **Thrombocytopenia** is almost universal (a normal platelet count should make you doubt the diagnosis). DIC with significant bleeding in under 5%. |
| **Liver** | Haemolytic jaundice; severe jaundice from haemolysis, hepatocyte injury and cholestasis. Liver dysfunction adds to hypoglycaemia, lactic acidosis and slow drug clearance. |

### Malaria in pregnancy

- Early pregnancy: **abortion**. Later: **low birth weight** (about 170 g lower on average), stillbirth, premature labour, fetal distress.
- Stable transmission: mother is often asymptomatic, but the placenta is heavily infected.
- Unstable transmission: severe disease, high parasitaemia, anaemia, hypoglycaemia, pulmonary oedema.
- **HIV co-infection** causes more frequent, denser infections and congenital malaria.

### Other routes

Malaria can be transmitted by **blood transfusion, needle-stick, shared needles and organ transplant**. The incubation period is short because there is no liver stage.

### Chronic complications

- **Hyperreactive malarial splenomegaly (tropical splenomegaly):** massive splenomegaly and hepatomegaly, very high IgM and malaria antibodies, normochromic normocytic anaemia, sinusoidal lymphocytosis. Give lifelong antimalarial prophylaxis while living in an endemic area.
- **Quartan malarial nephropathy:** immune-complex glomerular injury from *P. malariae*, causing **nephrotic syndrome** (focal or segmental glomerulonephritis, splitting of the basement membrane, subendothelial deposits).
- **Burkitt lymphoma** and EBV infection: common in malarious regions of Africa.

## 4. Diagnosis

### Blood film (gold standard)

- Stain with **Giemsa at pH 7.2** (Field's, Wright's or Leishman's are alternatives).
- **Thick film:** concentrates parasites 40 to 100 times, so it is more **sensitive**. Parasites and white cells are counted; parasite count per µL = (parasites per 200 WBCs) × 40, assuming a WBC count of 8000/µL.
- **Thin film:** identifies the **species** and lets you express parasitaemia as the percentage of infected red cells.
- Count at least 200 WBCs; examine **100 to 200 oil-immersion fields** before calling a thick film negative.
- If the first film is negative and suspicion is high, **repeat every 12 to 24 hours for 2 days**.
- In high-transmission areas, partially immune people can carry up to 10 000 parasites/µL with no symptoms, so a positive film does not always mean the fever is malaria.

### Other tests

- **Rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs):** detect parasite antigens (HRP2, pLDH); simple and fast, but **do not quantify parasitaemia**.
- **PCR:** most sensitive and best for species, but only in reference centres.

### Laboratory findings

Normochromic normocytic anaemia; normal white cell count (slight monocytosis, lymphopenia and eosinopenia, then reactive lymphocytosis and eosinophilia after recovery); raised acute phase reactants; prolonged PT and PTT; **thrombocytopenia**; reduced antithrombin III; hypergammaglobulinaemia in semi-immune people. Always check **glucose, bicarbonate/lactate, urea and creatinine, Hb, and parasite count**.

## 5. Treatment (Kenya Ministry of Health guidelines)

### Uncomplicated falciparum malaria

- **First line:** artemether-lumefantrine (**AL**), twice a day for 3 days, with fatty food.
- **Second line:** dihydroartemisinin-piperaquine (**DHA-PPQ**).
- All ACTs must include at least 3 days of an artemisinin derivative.

### Special groups

| Group | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Pregnant, first trimester** | Quinine plus clindamycin for 7 days (an ACT if these are not available) |
| **Pregnant, 2nd and 3rd trimesters** | AL |
| **Infants under 5 kg** | ACT at the same mg/kg dose as for 5 kg children |
| **Hyperparasitaemia** | ACT plus close monitoring |

### Non-falciparum (*P. vivax*, *P. ovale*, *P. malariae*)

- Treat with the first-line ACT as for falciparum.
- **To prevent relapse (*P. vivax* and *P. ovale*):** add **primaquine 0.25 to 0.5 mg/kg/day for 14 days**. **Not** in pregnancy, breastfeeding, infants, or **G6PD deficiency** (risk of haemolysis).

### Severe malaria

It is a medical emergency. Admit to the highest level of care available.

1. **Pre-referral** (where treatment is not possible): one dose of **IM artesunate 2.4 mg/kg**, then refer. Alternatives are IM artemether 3.2 mg/kg or quinine 20 mg/kg.
2. **IV or IM artesunate 2.4 mg/kg** at 0, 12 and 24 hours (a minimum of 3 doses), then daily. Artesunate is the drug of choice for **everyone**, including pregnant women in all trimesters, and needs no dose change in liver or renal failure.
3. Once the patient has had at least 24 hours of parenteral therapy and can take tablets, **finish with a full 3-day course of AL**.
4. If artesunate is unavailable: artemether, or quinine / quinidine with an **initial loading dose**. Quinine and quinidine given rapidly cause **dangerous hypotension**, so use a rate-controlled infusion (or deep IM into the anterior thigh).
5. A rectal artesunate formulation exists for community pre-referral treatment in rural areas.

### Supportive care for complications

| Problem | Management |
|---|---|
| **Convulsions** | IV or rectal benzodiazepine; check glucose |
| **Hypoglycaemia** | 50% dextrose 0.5 g/kg slowly, then 10% dextrose infusion; check glucose every 4 to 6 hours in an unconscious patient and keep it **above 4 mmol/L** |
| **Severe anaemia** | Transfuse (preferably fresh blood) if PCV falls below 20%; give immediately to a child with severe anaemia and acidotic breathing |
| **Acute kidney injury / severe acidosis** | Early haemofiltration or haemodialysis |
| **Pulmonary oedema / ARDS** | Sit up 45°, oxygen, IV diuretics; **early positive-pressure ventilation** if no response; avoid fluid overload |
| **Bleeding / DIC** | Fresh blood and IV vitamin K |
| **Aspiration pneumonia** | Antibiotics, oxygen, chest physiotherapy |
| **Sepsis suspected / not improving** | Add broad-spectrum antibiotics (gram-negative sepsis can accompany severe malaria) |

## 6. Prevention

- **Insecticide-treated nets (ITNs)** and indoor residual spraying
- Avoid mosquito bites from **dusk to dawn**; repellents with 10 to 35% **DEET** (or 7% picaridin); long sleeves and trousers
- **RTS,S/AS01 vaccine** recommended by WHO for children in sub-Saharan Africa (approved September 2022 in the lecture notes); R21/Matrix-M is a second vaccine
- Global eradication is not feasible in the near future because *Anopheles* breeding sites are so widespread
- **Intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy (IPTp):** sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP) to all pregnant women at every scheduled antenatal visit from the start of the second trimester, **at least one month apart**

### Chemoprophylaxis (travellers)

- Atovaquone-proguanil (Malarone), doxycycline 100 mg daily, or mefloquine (neuropsychiatric side effects).
- **Mefloquine** 250 mg weekly is the only drug advised for pregnant travellers to resistant areas (safe in the 2nd and 3rd trimesters); proguanil is also safe.
- Start 2 days to 2 weeks before travel and continue **4 weeks** after leaving. Atovaquone-proguanil and primaquine act on the liver stage, so they can stop **1 week** after leaving.
- Children born to non-immune mothers in endemic areas need prophylaxis from birth.

## Key points to revise

1. Female *Anopheles* → sporozoites → liver → merozoites → red cells → gametocytes.
2. Hypnozoites (*P. vivax*, *P. ovale*) cause relapse → add **primaquine** (check G6PD).
3. **Thick film = detect; thin film = species and % parasitaemia.** Repeat negative films.
4. **Severe malaria = IV artesunate**, then AL. Always check glucose.
5. Cerebral malaria: coma without focal signs, retinal haemorrhages.
6. Thrombocytopenia is the rule; a normal platelet count makes malaria less likely.
7. First trimester: quinine plus clindamycin. IPTp with SP monthly from the second trimester.
