# Community-Acquired Pneumonia

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Respiratory Medicine and Infectious Diseases**

> **In one line:** community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) is infection of the lung parenchyma acquired outside hospital, in which alveoli fill with inflammatory exudate (consolidation). *Streptococcus pneumoniae* is the commonest cause; "typical" organisms give an abrupt illness with lobar consolidation, "atypical" organisms (no cell wall, not seen on Gram stain, not covered by beta-lactams) give an insidious illness with extrapulmonary features. Severity (CURB-65) decides where the patient is treated and empirical antibiotics are chosen to cover the likely organisms.

## What you must know

- Pneumonia is a clinical syndrome: acute **cough, purulent sputum and fever** with signs or X-ray changes of consolidation.
- ***S. pneumoniae*** (Gram-positive diplococcus) is the **commonest cause** of CAP; ***Mycoplasma pneumoniae*** is the commonest atypical cause.
- Typical CAP: sudden **rigors, fever, pleuritic pain, productive cough**, lobar consolidation. Atypical CAP: insidious, dry cough, extrapulmonary features, **pulse-temperature dissociation**, diffuse interstitial changes.
- **Chest X-ray** is the diagnostic standard and separates pneumonia from acute bronchitis; do not give antibiotics if the film is not suggestive.
- **CURB-65** (Confusion, Urea over 7, RR over 30, BP low, age 65 or over): score 0 to 1 outpatient, 2 inpatient, 3 or more severe (consider ICU).
- Treatment: outpatients get a **macrolide or doxycycline** (or amoxicillin); inpatients get a **fluoroquinolone** or **macrolide + beta-lactam**; duration usually 5 to 10 days.
- Pleural fluid in pneumonia always needs analysis; **empyema (pH under 7.2, low glucose, high LDH) needs drainage**.

## 1. Definition and epidemiology

- **Pneumonia:** inflammation of the lung parenchyma (alveoli) with abnormal alveolar filling by fluid, causing airspace disease (consolidation and exudation).
- Clinically an **acute illness** with cough, purulent sputum and fever, with signs or radiology compatible with consolidation.
- Pneumonia and influenza together are the **6th leading cause of death** worldwide (they are lumped together in statistics, so pneumonia alone is hard to quantify) and the single commonest cause of infection-related mortality. Age-adjusted death rate about 22 per 100,000 per year.
- **Mortality:** 1 to 5% outpatients, about 12% inpatients, about 40% in ICU. It rises with age and comorbidity. Both sexes and all races are equally affected.

## 2. Pathogenesis (routes of infection)

| Route | Mechanism | Typical organisms |
|---|---|---|
| **Inhalation (primary)** | Organisms bypass cilia, cough, nasal hairs and humidification, or are inhaled from the upper airway or respiratory equipment | *S. pneumoniae* (nasopharynx), TB, viruses, *Legionella* |
| **Aspiration** | Oropharyngeal or gastric contents enter the lower airway (reduced consciousness, seizures, alcohol, sleep, tracheo-oesophageal fistula). The stomach is a reservoir of Gram-negative rods and anaerobes; gastric acid itself causes an intense, sometimes fatal chemical injury | Anaerobes, Gram-negative rods |
| **Haematogenous** | Septic emboli from a distant source, for example tricuspid or right-sided endocarditis | *Staphylococcus aureus* |
| **Direct extension** | Penetrating trauma or from adjacent infection | |

Most CAP organisms are normal inhabitants of the pharynx; disease follows loss of normal defences.

## 3. Types of pneumonia

| | CAP | Hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP) |
|---|---|---|
| Timing | In the community or within **48 hours** of admission | New episode **more than 48 hours** after admission |
| Organisms | Often a single organism; cause not found in 50% or more despite testing; bacteria, viruses, fungi | Resistant, Gram-negative and MDR organisms |

- **Healthcare-associated pneumonia (HCAP):** pneumonia within 48 hours of admission in patients with healthcare exposure, so it is not true CAP. Risk factors: hospitalisation of more than 2 days in the past 90 days; nursing home or long-term-care residence; chronic dialysis within 30 days; home IV therapy or home wound care within 30 days; a family member with a multidrug-resistant pathogen.
- **Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP):** develops 48 hours or more after intubation.

### Typical versus atypical organisms

The classification rests on whether the organism has a cell wall.

- **Typical** organisms have a cell wall: seen on Gram stain, grow on routine media, killed by **beta-lactams** (penicillins, cephalosporins, monobactams, carbapenems), and cause prominent chest symptoms (cough, pleuritic pain). But not all are beta-lactam sensitive: *H. influenzae* can make **beta-lactamase**.
- **Atypical** organisms lack a cell wall: **not visible on Gram stain**, not cultured on standard blood agar, **resistant to beta-lactams**. They need antibiotics that enter cells and block protein synthesis (**macrolides, tetracyclines, quinolones**), and often cause extrapulmonary symptoms (otitis, laryngitis), making diagnosis difficult. They account for about **15%** of CAP.

| Typical | Atypical |
|---|---|
| *S. pneumoniae*, *H. influenzae*, *Klebsiella pneumoniae*, *S. aureus*, *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, *Acinetobacter*, anaerobes, *Francisella tularensis* (as listed in the slides) | *Mycoplasma pneumoniae*, *Chlamydophila pneumoniae*, *Legionella*, *Coxiella burnetii*, *Pneumocystis jirovecii*, *Chlamydia psittaci*, viral CAP |

> **Check:** *Francisella tularensis* is usually grouped with atypical organisms (the slides' own atypical table lists it); *Pneumocystis jirovecii* is a fungus; TB also behaves atypically.

## 4. Causative organisms in detail

### *Streptococcus pneumoniae* (pneumococcus)

- **Gram-positive diplococci**; the commonest cause of CAP.
- Symptoms: malaise, **shaking chills**, fever, **rusty sputum**, pleuritic pain, cough. X-ray: **lobar infiltrate**. About **25% are bacteraemic**.
- Risk factors: **splenectomy or asplenia** (the spleen clears encapsulated organisms, so give vaccines about 2 weeks before elective splenectomy), **sickle cell disease** and other haematological disease, **HIV**, asthma and COPD, smoking, alcohol, recent influenza.
- **Prevention:**

| Vaccine | Content | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Pneumococcal conjugate (PCV) | 13 serotypes | Infants and young children (in the Kenya EPI schedule) |
| Pneumococcal polysaccharide (PPSV) | 23 serotypes | Adults and children at special risk: chronic lung disease, heart failure, severe renal or liver disease, diabetes, immunosuppression (HIV, SLE, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, long-term steroids), asplenia |

- **Specific treatment** (guided by sensitivity): penicillin G if sensitive; if resistant, ceftriaxone; then azithromycin; then levofloxacin; high-level penicillin resistance (likely resistant to all of these) needs **vancomycin**.

### *Haemophilus influenzae*

- Non-motile Gram-negative rods (coccobacilli). Type b has a polyribosylribitol phosphate capsule that resists phagocytosis and complement, causing invasive disease.
- Usually a **secondary infection on top of a viral illness**. Risks: COPD, smoking, immunodeficiency, splenectomy. Frequent cause of COPD exacerbations.
- Prevention: **Hib conjugate vaccine** (part of Kenya's pentavalent vaccine).
- Treatment: amoxicillin-clavulanate; or ceftriaxone, fluoroquinolone, co-trimoxazole.

### *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*

- Rare in healthy people. Risks: immunocompromise (HIV, transplant, neutropenia, chronic oral steroids), alcohol, frequent antibiotics, recent admission, **structural lung disease (cystic fibrosis, bronchiectasis, severe COPD)**. Tobramycin nebulisers can be used as prophylaxis in CF.
- Gram stain and good-quality sputum culture usually excludes the need for empirical cover.
- Treatment: **ceftazidime is the drug of choice**. Alternatives: cefepime, piperacillin-tazobactam, amikacin, tobramycin, aztreonam, ciprofloxacin, carbapenems, polymyxin B.

### *Acinetobacter*

- CAP risks: alcohol, smoking, chronic lung disease, diabetes, residence in a tropical developing country.
- HAP and VAP risks: burns unit or ICU, mechanical ventilation, long stay, surgery, wounds, previous infection, faecal colonisation, broad-spectrum antibiotics, central lines and urinary catheters, parenteral nutrition.
- Treatment: polymyxin B (colistin) or tigecycline.

### *Klebsiella*, anaerobes, *S. aureus*

| Organism | Features |
|---|---|
| *Klebsiella* | Diabetes, alcoholism, aspiration; **haemoptysis and red currant-jelly sputum**; cavitation |
| Anaerobes | Alcoholism, aspiration, **poor dentition**; abscess, large effusion |
| *S. aureus* | Chronic haemodialysis, recent influenza, bronchiectasis, hospitalisation, nursing home; cavities, effusions |
| *Pseudomonas* and *Acinetobacter* | Hospitalisation, nursing home, alcoholism, bronchiectasis, COPD, HIV |

### Atypical organisms

| Organism | Risk factors | Associated features |
|---|---|---|
| ***Mycoplasma pneumoniae*** (commonest atypical) | Young, healthy | Bullous myringitis (otitis media), non-exudative pharyngitis, watery diarrhoea, erythema multiforme, **raised cold agglutinin titre** |
| *Chlamydophila pneumoniae* | Smokers, COPD | Laryngitis, hoarseness |
| ***Legionella*** | Hotel or cruise ship stays, air-conditioning, water tanks; smokers, COPD, young healthy males | Inhalation of water mist; **GI and CNS symptoms** (diarrhoea, abdominal pain, confusion), myalgia, severe pneumonia |
| *Coxiella burnetii* (Q fever) | Farm animals, parturient cats | Severe headache, hepatitis, pneumonia; chronic: endocarditis, fever of unknown origin, liver granulomas |
| *Pneumocystis jirovecii* (PCP) | HIV | Dry cough, hypoxia, interstitial infiltrates |
| *Chlamydia psittaci* | Birds (owners, pet-shop workers, vets) | Psittacosis |
| *Francisella tularensis* | Rabbits, squirrels, rodents (hunters, landscapers) | Tularaemia |
| TB | Alcohol, HIV | Haemoptysis, cavitation, weight loss |

**Treatment of specific atypicals:** Q fever: doxycycline (rifampicin or hydroxychloroquine in chronic disease); psittacosis: tetracycline or doxycycline, macrolide as alternative; tularaemia: streptomycin. Q fever is diagnosed by complement fixation and rising serology or nucleic acid testing.

### Viral pneumonia

- **RSV, influenza and parainfluenza** are the commonest causes in children. **Influenza** is the most important viral cause in adults, especially in winter, and is preventable by **annual vaccination**.
- Pathogenesis: aerosol inhalation, 1 to 4 days incubation, uncomplicated influenza (fever, myalgia, malaise, rhinitis), then pneumonia. Adults over 65 account for 63% of influenza hospitalisations and 85% of influenza deaths. **Bacterial pneumonia (*S. pneumoniae*, *S. aureus*) may follow viral pneumonia.**
- H1N1 caused the first pandemic of the century (2009 to 2010). H1N1 risk factors: pregnancy, obesity, cardiopulmonary disease, chronic renal and liver disease.
- Findings: chest X-ray ranges from subtle to ARDS. Diagnosis: nasopharyngeal swab for rapid antigen of influenza A and B; H1N1 PCR.
- Precautions: respiratory (droplet) isolation, mask and gloves.

| Antiviral | Dose | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Oseltamivir** (Tamiflu) | 75 mg orally twice daily (150 mg twice daily in severe disease) | Neuraminidase inhibitor; influenza A and B |
| Zanamivir (Relenza) | 10 mg (2 inhalations) twice daily | Neuraminidase inhibitor |
| Amantadine | 100 mg twice daily | Adamantane; influenza A only |
| Rimantadine | 100 mg once daily | Adamantane; influenza A only |

- **H1N1 is resistant to adamantanes.** Neuraminidase inhibitors are 70 to 90% effective for prophylaxis; give within **48 hours** of onset to reduce duration, severity and viral shedding.

### MERS-CoV

- A novel coronavirus first described in **September 2012** (Middle East Respiratory Syndrome); **camels** are the reservoir.
- Severe disease with mortality reaching about 40%; spread mostly in hospital outbreaks, so early recognition and immediate **airborne and contact isolation** are vital. It is clinically indistinguishable from other febrile respiratory illnesses; diagnosis is by PCR on nasopharyngeal aspirate.

## 5. Clinical features

**Typical CAP:** a sudden chill followed by fever, pleuritic pain and productive cough.

- Symptoms: acute onset fever and shaking chills; cough with thick purulent sputum; **pleuritic chest pain** (suggests pleural involvement or effusion); dyspnoea; in severe disease confusion, cachexia, wheeze.
- Signs: tachycardia and tachypnoea; **late inspiratory crackles, bronchial breathing, increased tactile and vocal fremitus**, dullness to percussion, **aegophony** (patient says "ee", it sounds like "ay"), pleural rub (with effusion).

**Atypical CAP:** insidious onset with headache, sore throat, fatigue and myalgia; **dry cough**; fever with uncommon chills. Signs: **pulse-temperature dissociation** (normal pulse despite high fever), wheeze, rhonchi and crackles. Extrapulmonary features (see table above) are clues.

## 6. Investigations

**Chest X-ray (PA and lateral)**

- The standard for diagnosis: the **only reasonable way to differentiate pneumonia from acute bronchitis**, and shows complications (effusion, multilobar disease). If the findings are not suggestive of pneumonia, do not give antibiotics.
- Acute bronchitis: normal film. Atypical pneumonia: **diffuse reticulonodular (interstitial) infiltrates**. Typical pneumonia: lobar or multilobar consolidation (multilobar means very serious).
- Changes should clear by **6 weeks**; persistent changes suggest a bronchial lesion (for example carcinoma).

| X-ray pattern | Likely organisms |
|---|---|
| Lobar | *S. pneumoniae*, *Klebsiella*, *H. influenzae*, Gram-negatives |
| Patchy | Atypicals, viruses, *Legionella* |
| Interstitial | Viruses, PCP, *Legionella* |
| Cavitary | Anaerobes, *Klebsiella*, TB, *S. aureus*, fungi |
| Large effusion | *Staphylococcus*, anaerobes, *Klebsiella* |

**Microbiology**

- **Sputum Gram stain and culture** for typical organisms; blood cultures; **pleural fluid analysis** (thoracocentesis) if aetiology is unclear.
- Atypical-specific tests:

| Organism | Test |
|---|---|
| *Mycoplasma pneumoniae* | Cold agglutinins, PCR, serology, special culture media |
| *Chlamydia pneumoniae* and *C. psittaci* | Rising serological titre |
| *Legionella* | **Urine antigen**, culture |
| *Coxiella burnetii* | Rising serological titre |
| *Pneumocystis jirovecii* | Bronchoalveolar lavage (silver stain) |

**Other tests:** FBC, urea and electrolytes, LFTs, arterial blood gases, CT chest, nasopharyngeal aspirate for influenza or MERS-CoV PCR, sputum AFB and TB culture, fungal culture, special stains (silver stain, India ink), bronchoscopy, serology.

## 7. Management

### Where to treat: severity assessment

**CURB-65** (very important): one point each for

- **C**onfusion
- **U**rea (blood urea nitrogen) over 7 mmol/L
- **R**espiratory rate over 30 per minute
- **B**lood pressure: systolic under 90 or diastolic 60 or less
- age **65** or over

| Score | Disposition | Mortality |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 1 | Outpatient | Low |
| 2 | Inpatient | About 9% (2 factors) |
| 3 or more | Severe; consider ICU | About 15% (3 factors); about 57% (5 factors) |

> **Check:** the slide states "score over 3 goes to ICU"; the usual rule is a score of 3 or more is severe and ICU review is needed particularly for 4 to 5.

- The **Pneumonia Severity Index (PSI)** uses 20 variables including underlying disease and sorts patients into 5 mortality classes; it is difficult and rarely used. No trials compare CURB-65 with PSI.

### Likely pathogens by setting

| Outpatient | Inpatient (non-ICU) | ICU |
|---|---|---|
| *S. pneumoniae*, *Mycoplasma*, *Chlamydophila*, *H. influenzae*, respiratory viruses | The same plus *Legionella* | *S. pneumoniae*, *S. aureus*, *Legionella*, Gram-negative bacilli, *H. influenzae* |

### Empirical antibiotics

| Setting | Regimen |
|---|---|
| Outpatient, healthy, no antibiotics in past 3 months | **Macrolide** (azithromycin or clarithromycin) **or doxycycline** |
| Outpatient with comorbidity or antibiotics in past 3 months | **Fluoroquinolone** (levofloxacin or moxifloxacin); or advanced macrolide + beta-lactam (amoxicillin, or amoxicillin-clavulanate) |
| Inpatient | **Fluoroquinolone** (levofloxacin or moxifloxacin); or **macrolide + beta-lactam** (azithromycin + ceftriaxone) |

- Continue **5 days** and stop when afebrile for 48 hours (outpatient regimen as per the slide).
- If pneumonia is suspected to be due to aspiration with chemical pneumonitis only (a choking alcoholic with a few crackles), antibiotics are generally not indicated, but observe for deterioration.

> **Kenya note:** national guidance uses oral amoxicillin for non-severe pneumonia and adds a macrolide or doxycycline when atypical cover is needed; severe pneumonia is treated with IV benzylpenicillin or ceftriaxone with oxygen. Check local policy.

### Pearls of inpatient therapy

- Give the **first dose in the emergency department** (no set time frame in the slides).
- Switch from IV to oral when haemodynamically stable and improving (WBC near normal, afebrile, eating).
- Discharge when clinically stable, off oxygen, with no active medical problems.
- Duration usually **7 to 10 days**, minimum 5 days.
- Before stopping: afebrile 48 to 72 hours, haemodynamically stable, RR under 24, O2 saturation over 90%, normal mental state.
- Treat longer if initial therapy did not cover the identified organism, or with complications (lung abscess, empyema).
- Supportive care: oxygen, fluids, analgesia for pleuritic pain, thromboprophylaxis.

## 8. Complications

- Bacteraemia; respiratory and circulatory failure.
- **Parapneumonic effusion, empyema and lung abscess.** Pleural fluid should always be analysed in pneumonia (thoracocentesis); empyema **always needs drainage** (chest tube, ultrasound-guided, or surgical).
- Empyema fluid: exudate (protein over 30 g/L), **pH under 7.2, raised LDH, low glucose (under 3.3 mmol/L)**. Light's criteria define an exudate if any: pleural/serum protein over 0.5, pleural/serum LDH over 0.6, or pleural LDH over two-thirds of the upper limit of normal serum LDH.
- Others: ARDS, septic shock, metastatic infection (meningitis, endocarditis), pleural thickening.

## 9. Worked cases (answers from the slides)

1. Smoker with 1 week of fever, myalgia, abdominal pain, diarrhoea, then dry cough and dyspnoea: ***Legionella***.
2. Nursing-home resident with right middle lobe consolidation: considered **healthcare-associated** with Gram-negative risk; admit for IV third-generation cephalosporin (the slide chooses IV cefepime).
3. Vomiting, intoxicated man with crackles at the right base: **aspiration pneumonitis**; antibiotics not indicated, observe and repeat the chest film.
4. Elderly woman with confusion, urea 30 mg/dL, infiltrate, creatinine clearance about 26 mL/min: **renal-dosed third-generation cephalosporin plus macrolide, admit**.
5. Reducing VAP in a ventilated patient: **daily sedation interruption**, head of bed raised, oral (not nasal) intubation, subglottic suction; prophylactic IV antibiotics are not recommended.
6. Typical atypical organism: ***Legionella pneumophila***.
7. Fit 55-year-old, CURB-65 score 0: **oral amoxicillin** (add a macrolide if atypical exposure).
8. Empyema pleural fluid: **pH under 7.2, raised LDH, low glucose**.

## Key points to revise

1. CAP is pneumonia acquired in the community or within 48 hours of admission; *S. pneumoniae* is the commonest cause.
2. Typical organisms (cell wall, beta-lactam sensitive) cause abrupt lobar pneumonia; atypicals (no cell wall) cause insidious interstitial pneumonia with extrapulmonary features and need macrolides, tetracyclines or quinolones.
3. Mycoplasma: young, cold agglutinins, bullous myringitis; Legionella: air-conditioning, GI and CNS features, urine antigen.
4. Chest X-ray confirms pneumonia and distinguishes it from bronchitis; patterns suggest the organism.
5. CURB-65 decides disposition: 0 to 1 home, 2 ward, 3 or more severe.
6. Empirical therapy: macrolide or doxycycline for mild disease; fluoroquinolone or macrolide + beta-lactam for admitted patients; usually 5 to 10 days.
7. Influenza: oseltamivir within 48 hours; H1N1 is resistant to adamantanes; vaccinate annually.
8. Empyema (pH under 7.2, low glucose, high LDH) needs drainage; give pneumococcal vaccine to asplenic and other high-risk patients.
