# Anaemia: Red Cell Indices, Classification and Common Types

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Haematology**

> **In one line:** anaemia is a fall in red cell mass (low Hb or haematocrit) with reduced oxygen-carrying capacity, and it is almost always a sign of an underlying disease. Approach it with **Hb, MCV and reticulocyte count plus a blood film**, which sorts it into **microcytic, normocytic (low or high retic) and macrocytic** groups, each with a short list of causes.

## What you must know

- **WHO definition:** Hb **below 12 g/dL in women** and **below 13 g/dL in men**. About 30% of the world is anaemic, and about 600 million have iron deficiency.
- Approach: **Hb, then MCV, then reticulocyte count, plus a peripheral blood smear.**
- **Microcytic:** iron deficiency (commonest worldwide), thalassaemia, anaemia of chronic disease (sometimes), sideroblastic anaemia.
- **Normocytic:** haemolysis, acute bleeding, anaemia of chronic disease, renal failure, marrow failure, early nutritional deficiency. The **reticulocyte count** separates appropriate from inappropriate marrow response.
- **Macrocytic:** **B12 and folate deficiency** (megaloblastic), plus alcohol, liver disease, hypothyroidism, MDS and drugs (non-megaloblastic).
- Ferritin is the **most specific test for iron deficiency**; in adults, always look for **GI blood loss** in men and post-menopausal women.
- **Transfuse only if Hb is below 7 g/dL** or the patient has cardiopulmonary disease or needs more oxygen-carrying capacity.
- **TTP** (schistocytes + low platelets + anaemia) is a **medical emergency**: urgent plasma exchange.

## 1. Red cell basics and terminology

- A normal red cell is a biconcave disc with **central pallor about one third of the cell**. The shape depends on **cytoskeletal proteins** (spectrin and others); a defect in any of them gives an abnormal shape.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Spherocytes** | Loss of cytoskeletal proteins, blebs on the surface, cells lose central pallor; destroyed by splenic macrophages (**extravascular haemolysis**) |
| **Elliptocytes** | Cylindrical (oval) red cells |
| **Spur cells and burr cells** | Both have spikes |
| **Schistocytes (helmet cells)** | Fragmented red cells with two sharp ends; most important cause is **microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA)** from **TTP**; also **DIC** |

### Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP)

- Fatal if untreated (**mortality above 90%**), but highly treatable.
- **Triad:** low platelets, anaemia, schistocytes.
- **Pentad:** the triad plus neurological signs (seizures, stroke, confusion, loss of consciousness), renal failure, and fever.
- Treatment: **urgent plasma exchange (PLEX)**; survival **above 85%** if treated.

> **Exam tip:** schistocytes + low platelets + anaemia = TTP until proven otherwise. Do not transfuse platelets unless life-threatening bleeding, as it may worsen thrombosis.

### Red cell indices

| Index | Meaning and calculation |
|---|---|
| **MCV** | Average red cell volume (size), from the distribution of individual cell volumes. Normal about 80 to 100 fL |
| **Haematocrit** | Percentage of packed red cells in the sample. Automated Hct = MCV × RBC count |
| **MCH** | Average Hb in one cell (picograms) = Hb (g/L) / RBC count (10¹²/L) |
| **MCHC** | Hb per dL of packed cells = Hb (g/dL) / Hct. Example: Hb 15.4 / Hct 0.441 = 34.9 g/dL. Look at it mainly when **spherocytosis** is suspected (raised) |
| **RDW** | Coefficient of variation of cell size (**anisocytosis**) |

**RDW points:**

- Microcytic: more often raised in **iron deficiency** than in thalassaemia or anaemia of chronic disease.
- Macrocytic: more often raised in **B12 or folate deficiency** than in liver disease or hypothyroidism. So RDW is mostly raised in **nutritional deficiencies**.
- MDS or transfusion to a patient with a low or high MCV produces a **dimorphic picture** (two populations) with a very wide RDW.
- If all cells are uniformly large or small, RDW is normal or even low.

## 2. Reading the white cell count

A normal total WBC does not exclude disease (one line may be up and another down). **Order the differential and use absolute counts, not percentages.**

| Finding | Causes |
|---|---|
| **Neutrophilia** | Acute: bacterial infection, steroids (demargination). Chronic: CML |
| **Lymphocytosis** | Acute: viral infection. Chronic: CLL |
| **Monocytosis** | Fungal infection, TB, malignancy |
| **Eosinophilia** | Allergy, parasites, autoimmune disease, eosinophilic leukaemia |
| **Basophilia** | Very rare; CML (not as an isolated finding) |

| Neutropenia | ANC (× 10⁹/L) | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Mild | 1.0 to 1.5 | Infection risk not increased; usually no work-up (about 25% of people) |
| Moderate | 0.5 to 1.0 | Infection risk not clearly increased; investigate |
| Severe | below 0.5 | **Infection risk increased**; must investigate |

## 3. Definition, compensation and clinical features

- Anaemia: reduction in **red cell mass** (measured by Hb or Hct) and oxygen-carrying capacity. Reference values vary with **sex, age, race, altitude and socioeconomic factors**; anaemia is defined as more than 2 standard deviations below the reference population mean.
- Red cells need a minimum Hb content before release. If Hb synthesis is slow, cells divide more times and are **small (low MCV)**. If cell division is impaired but Hb synthesis is normal, cells are **large (high MCV)**.

### Compensation (delays symptoms when anaemia is gradual)

- Increased cardiac output (rate and stroke volume), which may eventually cause **high-output heart failure**.
- Increased **oxygen extraction** from Hb.
- **Rightward shift** of the oxygen dissociation curve (raised **2,3-DPG**).
- Plasma volume expansion; tachycardia and hyperventilation.

> **Remember:** the faster Hb falls, the more symptomatic the patient, because there is no time to compensate.

### Clinical features

Anaemia is rarely a disease in itself; it reflects tissue hypoxia plus the underlying cause.

- **General:** fatigue, weakness, headache, confusion, reduced mental acuity, exertional dyspnoea, palpitations, nausea, vague abdominal discomfort, diarrhoea, pallor (best seen in the **conjunctiva**), hypotension and tachycardia.
- **Specific clues:**

| Sign | Suggests |
|---|---|
| Premature greying of hair | Pernicious anaemia |
| Hair loss, brittle hair, **koilonychia** (spooning of nails) | Iron deficiency |
| Glossitis, burning tongue, **angular stomatitis (cheilitis)**, **sideropenic dysphagia**, **pica/pagophagia** (eating ice or mud) | Iron deficiency |
| Chronic leg ulcers | Sickle cell or other haemolytic anaemia |
| Painful ulcerative mouth lesions | Aplastic anaemia or leukaemia |
| Jaundice | Haemolysis |
| Blood in stool | GI bleeding |

### Transfusion

Not recommended unless **Hb is below 7 g/dL** or the patient needs increased oxygen-carrying capacity (cardiopulmonary disease).

## 4. Approach to anaemia

Look at three CBC parameters in order, plus the film:

1. **Haemoglobin.**
2. **MCV.**
3. **Reticulocyte count.**
4. **Peripheral blood smear** (additional required test).

| Category | MCV | Reticulocytes | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Microcytic** | below 80 fL | variable | Iron deficiency, thalassaemia, ACD |
| **Normocytic, low retic** | 80 to 100 fL | low | Marrow failure, renal disease, ACD, endocrine; "inappropriately low marrow response" |
| **Normocytic, high retic** | 80 to 100 fL | high | Haemolysis, bleeding, treated deficiency; "appropriate marrow response" |
| **Macrocytic** | above 100 fL | variable | B12/folate deficiency, alcohol, liver, hypothyroidism, MDS |

### Reticulocyte count

- Normal retic count without anaemia is about 1% (or about 100 × 10⁹/L). In anaemia with a healthy marrow it rises (up to about 10% in severe cases).
- **High retic count = healthy marrow responding. Low = diseased marrow or nutritional deficiency.**
- Normal range: men 0.8 to 2.5%; women 0.8 to 4.1%.
- **Corrected retic** = (patient Hb / normal Hb) × retic %. **Reticulocytosis:** above 100,000/mm³.

## 5. Microcytic anaemias

### 5.1 Iron deficiency anaemia (IDA)

The **commonest cause of anaemia worldwide.**

**Causes:**

- **Blood loss:** the commonest cause in adults.
  - Women: heavy menses.
  - **Men and post-menopausal women: always investigate the GI tract** (occult bleeding, colon cancer, gastric malignancy, gastritis, peptic ulcer, aspirin/NSAIDs, inflammatory bowel disease, diverticulitis, polyps, angiodysplasia).
  - **Hookworm and schistosomiasis** are common causes in endemic countries such as Kenya.
- **Malabsorption:** iron is absorbed in the upper small intestine (coeliac disease). Gastric acid releases iron from food and keeps it as soluble ferrous iron; **achlorhydria** (elderly, **PPIs**) reduces availability.
- **Dietary deficiency or increased needs** (pregnancy, growth): a rarer cause in adults but common in children and pregnancy in Kenya.

**Clinical features:** glossitis, angular stomatitis, pica, koilonychia, pallor, fatigue, weakness, exertional dyspnoea, orthostatic light-headedness, tachycardia, hypotension if acute.

**Diagnosis:**

| Test | In IDA |
|---|---|
| Serum iron | Low |
| Transferrin / **TIBC** | **High** (made in proportion to need) |
| **Transferrin saturation** = (Fe / TIBC) × 100 | **Low**; below 20% indicates iron deficiency; above 50% may mean iron overload |
| **Ferritin** | **Low; the most specific test** |
| Bone marrow biopsy | Gold standard but rarely needed; indicated if iron deficiency is shown and no source of loss is found |

**Treatment:**

1. **Oral iron** (mnemonic FSG):
   - **Ferrous fumarate** 325 mg tablet = **106 mg** elemental iron.
   - **Ferrous sulphate** 325 mg = **65 mg**.
   - **Ferrous gluconate** 325 mg = **36 mg**.
   - More elemental iron means more effect but more GI side effects (gastric upset, constipation).
2. **Parenteral iron** if oral iron is not tolerated or blood loss exceeds what oral iron can replace.
3. **Transfusion** only if severe anaemia or cardiopulmonary disease.
4. Find and treat the cause.

### 5.2 Thalassaemias

- Inherited disorders with **inadequate production of alpha or beta globin chains**.
- **Ineffective erythropoiesis** (active erythropoiesis with premature red cell death in the marrow) leads to iron accumulation, so **ferritin is raised**.

**Alpha-thalassaemia** (reduced alpha chains; severity depends on number of genes lost). Excess beta chains form tetramers (HbH) that damage the membrane.

| Genes lost | Name | Features |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Silent carrier** | Asymptomatic, normal Hb |
| 2 | **Alpha-thalassaemia trait (minor)** | Mild microcytic hypochromic anaemia; no treatment |
| 3 | **HbH disease** | Severe anaemia; the only form needing treatment |
| 4 | **Hb Bart's (4 gamma chains)** | Hydrops fetalis, stillbirth |

**Beta-thalassaemia** (reduced beta chains; excess alpha chains form tetramers that damage the membrane). Common in Mediterranean, Middle Eastern and Indian ancestry.

| Type | Features |
|---|---|
| **Major (Cooley anaemia)** | Severe microcytic hypochromic anaemia; marrow expansion causes bone distortion (**"crew-cut" skull X-ray**, prominent malar bones, dental malalignment); massive hepatosplenomegaly; growth failure; death in early childhood from heart failure if not transfused. **Hb electrophoresis: raised HbF and HbA2.** Film: microcytic hypochromic cells, target cells. Treatment: **regular red cell transfusions** |
| **Minor (trait)** | Usually asymptomatic, mild microcytic hypochromic anaemia; diagnosed by electrophoresis; no treatment |
| **Intermedia** | Intermediate severity; usually both beta genes involved; usually not transfusion-dependent |

- **Iron overload** in transfusion-dependent patients can cause heart failure and a haemochromatosis-like picture; treat with the chelator **desferrioxamine**.

> **Exam tip:** to tell **IDA from thalassaemia** on the CBC, in thalassaemia the **MCV is disproportionately low for the Hb**, the RBC count is high-normal, and **ferritin is normal or raised**. If "iron deficiency" does not respond to iron, do Hb electrophoresis.

### 5.3 Anaemia of chronic disease (ACD)

- Occurs with **chronic infection** (TB, lung abscess), **cancer** (lung, breast, Hodgkin lymphoma), **inflammation** (RA, SLE) or trauma.
- **Hepcidin**, an acute phase protein from the liver, degrades **ferroportin** (the iron exporter), so iron is trapped inside cells (iron restriction) and too little is available for erythropoiesis. Inflammatory cytokines also inhibit erythropoiesis.
- **Lab:** serum iron **low**, TIBC **low**, transferrin saturation normal or low, **ferritin raised**.
- Mostly **normocytic normochromic**, but can be microcytic and hypochromic.
- Treat the underlying disease. **Do not give iron.** The anaemia is usually mild and well tolerated.

## 6. Normocytic anaemias

**Differential diagnosis:**

1. Aplastic anaemia.
2. Acute bleeding.
3. Early nutritional anaemia (iron, B12, folate deficiency).
4. Anaemia of renal insufficiency (reduced erythropoietin).
5. Anaemia of chronic disease.
6. Some haemolytic anaemias.
7. Primary bone marrow disorders.
8. Endocrine disorders (hypopituitarism, hypothyroidism, hypoadrenalism).

An **acute drop in Hb** is either haemolysis or acute bleeding, so do a haemolysis work-up.

### 6.1 Aplastic anaemia (extra reading)

- Bone marrow failure with **pancytopenia**.
- **Causes:** idiopathic (the majority); radiation; drugs (**chloramphenicol, sulphonamides, gold, carbamazepine**); viruses (parvovirus, hepatitis B and C, EBV, CMV, varicella-zoster, HIV); chemicals (benzene, insecticides).
- **Features:** anaemia symptoms, bruising and petechiae (thrombocytopenia), infections (neutropenia); can transform into acute leukaemia.
- **Diagnosis:** normocytic normochromic anaemia; **bone marrow biopsy**: hypocellular marrow with absence of progenitors of all three lines.
- **Treatment:** bone marrow transplantation; transfuse red cells and platelets judiciously; treat underlying causes (immunosuppression is used for those without a donor).

### 6.2 Haemolytic anaemias

Red cells normally survive about 120 days; in haemolysis survival is shortened. The marrow can increase output six- to eightfold (more cells, more active marrow, early release of reticulocytes). **Anaemia only develops if destruction exceeds this increased production.**

**Classification:**

| Basis | Groups |
|---|---|
| Cause | **Extracorpuscular** (mostly acquired): immune, mechanical (prosthetic valves, MAHA), drugs, burns, toxins, snake bites, infection (**malaria**, Clostridium) |
| | **Intracorpuscular** (mostly inherited): haemoglobin (sickle cell, HbC, thalassaemias), membrane (hereditary spherocytosis, **PNH**), enzyme (**G6PD**, pyruvate kinase deficiency) |
| Site | **Intravascular** (in the circulation) or **extravascular** (reticuloendothelial system, mainly the spleen; the commonest) |

> **Remember:** all intrinsic defects are hereditary **except PNH and spur cell anaemia**, which are acquired. All extrinsic causes are acquired.

**Clinical features:** anaemia; signs of the underlying disease (for example bone crises in sickle cell disease); **jaundice** and **pigment gallstones**; **dark urine** (haemoglobinuria, not bilirubin; indicates intravascular haemolysis); hepatosplenomegaly and lymphadenopathy in chronic cases.

**Laboratory hallmarks (both types):**

- Low Hb or Hct
- Raised **unconjugated bilirubin**
- Raised **LDH**
- Raised **reticulocytes**
- Raised urinary urobilinogen

**Extra features of intravascular haemolysis:** low **haptoglobin**, raised methaemalbumin, positive urinary haemosiderin, haemoglobinuria.

**Blood film clues:** schistocytes (mechanical/intravascular), spherocytes (extravascular, e.g. hereditary spherocytosis or AIHA), sickled cells (sickle cell), **Heinz bodies** (G6PD deficiency). **Direct Coombs test** detects antibody or complement on the red cell: positive in AIHA.

**Treatment:** treat the cause; transfuse if severe or unstable; **give folic acid** (folate is consumed in haemolysis).

#### Sickle cell anaemia

- **Autosomal recessive.** Hb S replaces Hb A through a **glutamic acid to valine substitution at position 6 of the beta globin chain**. Disease needs **two HbS genes (homozygous)**; one gene = **trait** (no anaemia). Trait gives relative protection against **severe falciparum malaria in early childhood**.
- Under low oxygen, acidosis, dehydration, infection or temperature change, HbS polymerises and cells sickle, obstructing small vessels (ischaemia).

**Clinical features:**

- Haemolytic anaemia features (gallstones very common); high-output heart failure over time.
- **Aplastic crisis** (usually parvovirus B19); transfuse; recovery in 7 to 10 days.
- **Vaso-occlusive:** bone pain from infarction (most common manifestation), avascular necrosis (hip commonest), **hand-foot syndrome (dactylitis; often the first sign)**, **acute chest syndrome** (looks like pneumonia), splenic infarcts, **priapism**, chronic leg ulcers over the lateral malleoli.
- **Infections:** functional asplenia leads to infection with encapsulated organisms (**Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae**, Neisseria meningitidis) and **Salmonella osteomyelitis**.

**Diagnosis:** anaemia; sickled cells on film; **Hb electrophoresis** (no HbA, 2 to 20% HbF, HbS about 80%); usually picked up by newborn screening.

**Treatment:**

1. Avoid high altitude; maintain hydration; treat infections early; vaccinate against capsulated organisms; **penicillin prophylaxis in children under 6 years**.
2. **Folic acid.**
3. Painful crises: analgesia (morphine), oral or IV (normal saline) hydration.
4. **Hydroxyurea:** raises HbF, reduces painful crises, helps leg ulcer healing.
5. **Transfusion only when necessary:** acute chest syndrome, stroke, priapism not responding to fluids and analgesia, cardiac decompensation (not based on Hb level).
6. Bone marrow transplantation; gene therapy is a future hope.

#### Hereditary spherocytosis

- **Autosomal dominant** defect in **spectrin** and other membrane proteins; loss of membrane surface without loss of volume gives spherical cells that are trapped and destroyed in the spleen (extravascular haemolysis).
- Features: haemolytic anaemia, jaundice, splenomegaly, gallstones, haemolytic crises.
- Diagnosis: spherocytes on film, raised retics, **raised MCHC**, **increased osmotic fragility** (lysis at higher saline concentrations), **negative direct Coombs test** (separates it from AIHA).
- **Treatment: splenectomy.**

#### G6PD deficiency

- **X-linked recessive** (mainly males). Cells cannot neutralise hydrogen peroxide, so Hb denatures into **Heinz bodies**; these cells are pitted by the spleen to give **bite cells**.
- **Triggers:** sulphonamides (for example cotrimoxazole), nitrofurantoin, **primaquine**, dimercaprol, **fava beans**, infection.
- Mild form (**A-variant**, about 10% of African-American men): self-limited haemolysis. Severe form (Mediterranean): severe haemolysis, for example with fava beans.
- Diagnosis: Heinz bodies and bite cells; **G6PD assay** (can be falsely normal during a crisis because the most deficient cells have already been destroyed, so **repeat later**).
- Treatment: avoid triggers, hydrate, transfuse in severe cases.

#### Autoimmune haemolytic anaemia (AIHA)

Autoantibodies against red cell antigens; the immunoglobulin class determines site of destruction, prognosis and treatment. The course is more fulminant in children.

| Feature | Warm AIHA | Cold AIHA |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency | More common | Less common |
| Antibody | **IgG**, binds at 37°C | **IgM**, binds at 0 to 5°C |
| Haemolysis | Extravascular (spleen) | Complement activation, intravascular; sequestration in liver |
| Causes | Idiopathic; lymphoma, CLL, **SLE**, **alpha-methyldopa** | Idiopathic (elderly); **Mycoplasma pneumoniae**, infectious mononucleosis |
| Tests | Direct Coombs positive (IgG on cells); spherocytes | Coombs positive for complement only; **cold agglutinin titre** positive |
| Treatment | **Glucocorticoids** first-line; splenectomy if refractory; azathioprine or cyclophosphamide; transfusion only if necessary; folic acid | **Avoid cold**; steroids not helpful |

#### Paroxysmal nocturnal haemoglobinuria (PNH)

- **Acquired** stem-cell disorder; deficiency of anchor proteins (**CD55, CD59**) that protect cells from complement, giving complement-mediated lysis of red cells, white cells and platelets.
- Features: chronic intravascular haemolysis with haemoglobinuria, raised LDH; normocytic anaemia; pancytopenia; **venous thrombosis** (for example **Budd-Chiari syndrome**); may evolve to aplastic anaemia, MDS, myelofibrosis or acute leukaemia; abdominal, back and musculoskeletal pain.
- Diagnosis: **flow cytometry for CD55 and CD59** (most sensitive and specific); Ham test (acidified serum) and sugar water test (older).
- Treatment: glucocorticoids (many do not respond); bone marrow transplantation (complement inhibitors such as eculizumab are used where available).

## 7. Macrocytic anaemias

### 7.1 Megaloblastic (impaired DNA synthesis)

Causes: **vitamin B12 deficiency, folate deficiency, drugs (methotrexate)**.

#### Vitamin B12 deficiency

- B12 is a cofactor for **homocysteine to methionine** and **methylmalonyl CoA to succinyl CoA**.
- Liver stores last **3 or more years**. Dietary sources: meat and fish.
- B12 binds **intrinsic factor** (from gastric parietal cells) and is absorbed in the **terminal ileum**.

**Causes (almost all are impaired absorption):**

- **Pernicious anaemia** (autoimmune lack of intrinsic factor): commonest cause in the West.
- Gastrectomy.
- Poor diet (strict vegetarianism), alcoholism.
- Crohn disease, ileal resection (last about 100 cm of terminal ileum).
- Competition for B12: fish tapeworm (**Diphyllobothrium latum**), blind loop syndrome (bacterial overgrowth).

**Clinical features:** anaemia; sore tongue (glossitis, stomatitis); **neuropathy**, which distinguishes B12 from folate deficiency. Untreated, it becomes irreversible: **demyelination of the posterior columns, lateral corticospinal and spinocerebellar tracts (subacute combined degeneration of the cord)** gives loss of position and vibration sense in the legs, ataxia, and upper motor neurone signs (brisk reflexes, spasticity, weakness, Babinski sign); incontinence, impotence and dementia may follow.

**Diagnosis:**

1. CBC: low Hb, **MCV above 100 fL**.
2. Film: macro-ovalocytes and **hypersegmented neutrophils**.
3. **Low serum B12.**
4. **Raised methylmalonic acid and homocysteine** (useful if B12 is borderline).
5. **Anti-intrinsic factor antibodies** (pernicious anaemia).
6. **Schilling test** (historical, not routine): unlabelled IM B12 to saturate binding sites; oral radioactive B12 with urine and plasma measurement; repeat with intrinsic factor. If intrinsic factor corrects absorption, it is pernicious anaemia.

**Treatment:** parenteral **cyanocobalamin (vitamin B12) IM**. (Standard regimen: loading doses then maintenance every one to three months; the slide quotes monthly.)

> **Exam tip:** never give folic acid alone to a patient with B12 deficiency; it corrects the anaemia but allows the neurological damage to progress.

#### Folate deficiency

- Stores are limited: **3 months of poor intake** can cause deficiency. Source: green vegetables (lost by overcooking).
- **Causes:** poor diet ("tea and toast", the commonest), **alcoholism**, long-term oral antibiotics, increased demand (**pregnancy**, **haemolysis**), antagonists (**methotrexate**, **phenytoin**), haemodialysis.
- Features: as for B12 but **without neurological symptoms**.
- Treatment: **daily oral folic acid**.

### 7.2 Non-megaloblastic macrocytosis

Liver disease and alcohol, myelodysplasia (MDS), hypothyroidism, myeloma, congenital marrow failure syndromes.

#### Myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS)

- Malignant clonal stem-cell disorders with **dysplasia**, variable **cytopenias** and variable risk of transformation to **AML**; disease of the elderly (median age above 65).
- Treatment: supportive (transfusion, G-CSF, antibiotics, erythropoietin); **hypomethylating agents (azacitidine)**; stem cell transplant for younger fit patients.

## 8. Worked cases

| Case | Findings | Diagnosis |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Low Hb, low MCV, low RBC count, high RDW | Iron deficiency anaemia |
| 2 | Very low MCV out of proportion to Hb, high-normal RBC count, slightly raised RDW, normal ferritin | Thalassaemia |
| 3 | Macrocytosis, high RDW | B12 or folate deficiency |
| 4 | Macrocytosis and low platelets | Marrow disorder (MDS) |
| 5 | Hb 5, MCV 85, retic 300 (3%) | Normocytic with high retic: **bleeding, haemolysis or treated nutritional deficiency** (healthy marrow) |

**MCQ answers from the lecture:**

1. Alcoholic with macrocytosis and neuropathy: **peripheral blood smear** (hypersegmented neutrophils). Both B12 and folate are possible, so the film is the next step.
2. Man of 55 with constipation, MCV 72, ferritin 10: **colonoscopy** (IDA in a man means occult GI loss, possible cancer).
3. Woman of 50 with rheumatoid arthritis, low iron, low TIBC, MCV 80: **anaemia of chronic disease**.
4. Teenage girl, hypochromic microcytic cells: **iron deficiency anaemia**.

## Key points to revise

1. Anaemia is Hb below 12 g/dL (women) or 13 g/dL (men); approach with Hb, MCV, retic count and film.
2. Microcytic: IDA (ferritin low, TIBC high), thalassaemia (MCV very low for the Hb, ferritin normal or high), ACD (ferritin high, TIBC low).
3. Men and post-menopausal women with IDA need a GI work-up; in Kenya think hookworm and schistosomiasis.
4. Haemolysis: raised unconjugated bilirubin, LDH and retics; intravascular adds low haptoglobin and haemoglobinuria.
5. Sickle cell: Glu6Val on the beta chain; hydroxyurea, hydration, vaccines, penicillin prophylaxis; transfuse only for specific complications.
6. B12 deficiency causes neurological damage (subacute combined degeneration); folate deficiency does not; always exclude B12 before giving folate.
7. Schistocytes with low platelets and anaemia = TTP, treated with urgent plasma exchange.
8. Transfuse at Hb below 7 g/dL or when cardiopulmonary disease requires it; do not give iron in ACD.
