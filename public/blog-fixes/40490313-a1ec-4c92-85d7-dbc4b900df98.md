# COVID-19

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Infectious diseases and respiratory medicine**

> **In one line:** COVID-19 is the disease caused by the enveloped beta-coronavirus **SARS-CoV-2**, which enters cells through the **ACE2 receptor** and spreads by droplets and aerosols. About 80% have mild upper-airway disease; about 20% progress to pneumonia, hypoxia and ARDS because of viral damage to alveolar type II cells and an excessive inflammatory response. Diagnosis is by NAAT (RT-PCR) or antigen test, treatment is graded by severity (oxygen, steroids, anticoagulation), and a subset develop prolonged "long COVID".

## What you must know

- **SARS-CoV-2** is an enveloped β-coronavirus, genetically very similar to SARS-CoV-1; **ACE2** is its main receptor.
- Transmission: **airborne and droplet**; faeco-oral spread is suggested by some studies.
- **Incubation 2 to 14 days.** About **80% mild**, about **20% progress** to pulmonary infiltrates; initial estimated fatality about 2%, strongly age-dependent.
- Diagnosis: **NAAT/RT-PCR** (most sensitive) or **antigen test** (faster, less sensitive; a negative result in a symptomatic patient needs repeating).
- Severity: **mild** (no dyspnoea or hypoxia), **moderate** (RR above 24, SpO2 90 to below 93%), **severe** (RR above 30, SpO2 below 90%).
- **Steroids** (dexamethasone or methylprednisolone) and **anticoagulation** are the key drugs for moderate to severe disease; oxygen target SpO2 92 to 96%.
- **Long COVID:** new or continuing symptoms 3 months after infection lasting at least 2 months, with no other explanation; diagnosis of exclusion.
- Diabetes worsens COVID-19 outcomes, and COVID-19 worsens glycaemic control (a bidirectional relationship).

## 1. Aetiology and transmission

- Causative agent: SARS-CoV-2, an enveloped β-coronavirus with a single-stranded RNA genome.
- Spread: airborne and droplet; some research suggests faeco-oral transmission.
- Based on the cells infected, the disease is divided into **three phases** that match clinical stages.

## 2. Pathogenesis: three stages

| Stage | Site and events | Clinical correlate |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Asymptomatic** (first 1 to 2 days) | Inhaled virus binds epithelial cells of the **nasal cavity** via ACE2 and starts replicating. Ciliated cells are the primary target; ACE2 expression in conducting airways is low. Local spread, **limited innate immune response**. | Patient is **infectious** even with a low viral load. Nasal swabs are more sensitive than throat swabs. RT-PCR cycle value may predict viral load, infectivity and course. |
| **2. Upper and conducting airway response** (next few days) | Virus spreads down the conducting airways; a **more robust innate response** (interferons beta and lambda from infected epithelium; CXCL10 may predict course). | COVID-19 becomes clinically manifest. In about **80%** disease stays mild and mostly restricted to the upper airways: home care, symptomatic treatment. |
| **3. Hypoxia, ground-glass infiltrates, ARDS** | About **20%** progress. Virus reaches the gas-exchange units and infects **alveolar type II cells** (peripheral and subpleural). The cells release many viral particles and die by apoptosis, a "self-replicating pulmonary toxin" infecting adjacent alveoli. | Hypoxia, bilateral infiltrates, ARDS. |

- Type II cells are the precursors of type I cells; their loss triggers secondary epithelial regeneration.
- Pathology: **diffuse alveolar damage with fibrin-rich hyaline membranes** and a few multinucleated giant cells. Aberrant healing may cause more scarring and fibrosis than other ARDS.
- **Elderly** are at particular risk: weaker immune response, poorer epithelial repair and reduced mucociliary clearance, which lets the virus reach the alveoli.
- Recovery needs a vigorous innate and adaptive immune response plus epithelial regeneration.

> **Remember:** disease confined to the conducting airways is mild and managed at home; disease that has reached the alveoli needs careful monitoring and support.

## 3. Clinical features of acute COVID-19

- Incubation 2 to 14 days; symptoms mark the acute stage.
- **Symptoms:** fever, lethargy, myalgia, fatigue, headache, rhinorrhoea, dry cough, dyspnoea, **loss of smell or taste**, nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea.
- Mild to moderate illness lasts 1 to 2 weeks; severe cases can last months. Some patients are **asymptomatic** but infectious.
- Neurological symptoms such as headache and anosmia are present in more than 80% of hospitalised patients (as reported in the slide series).

## 4. Diagnosis

### Whom to test

- **Symptomatic patients.**
- **Asymptomatic close contacts** (including neonates of infected mothers): test **5 to 7 days after exposure** (at least 5 days since last exposure).
- Screening: congregate living facilities (long-term care, prisons, shelters), hospitalised patients, before surgery or **aerosol-generating procedures**, before immunosuppressive therapy including transplantation.

### When to avoid testing

- Asymptomatic people with a diagnosis in the previous **month**.
- **NAAT should be avoided within 3 months** of prior infection because a repeat positive probably reflects residual RNA, not reinfection.

### Choice of test

| Test | Features |
|---|---|
| **NAAT (RT-PCR)** | detects viral RNA; **preferred** because of superior sensitivity; usually lab-based (turnaround ideally within 24 to 48 hours); rapid point-of-care RT-PCR performs comparably. Sample: **nasopharyngeal and oropharyngeal swabs** (preferred), tracheal aspirate or BAL. |
| **Antigen test** | rapid, cheap, point-of-care or home use, but **less sensitive**. Best in the **first 5 to 7 days** of symptoms. A positive result is reliable (very low false-positive rate); a negative result in a symptomatic person may be false and needs further testing (repeat at 48 hours). |
| **Serology** | detects antibodies; useful for **past infection** or for patients symptomatic for 3 to 4 weeks; little use in acute diagnosis. Most target nucleocapsid; only spike-targeted tests detect vaccine response and cannot separate it from infection. |

- **Bronchoscopy is not recommended** for diagnosis (aerosol risk); consider only in intubated patients when upper samples are negative and the result would change management.
- **Interpretation of NAAT:** a positive result confirms COVID-19. A single negative usually excludes it, but **false negatives** from upper respiratory samples occur; if suspicion remains, repeat after **24 to 48 hours** (not within 24 hours). In hospitalised patients with negative NAAT, characteristic laboratory and imaging findings support the diagnosis.
- Standard tests cannot identify the variant; **sequencing** is needed.
- **Omicron:** same testing approach. Some subvariants give **S-gene target failure** on some NAATs, but most NAATs have more than one target; antigen tests target the nucleoprotein and still detect it. Vaccination status does not change interpretation of viral tests.

### Laboratory findings (hospitalised pneumonia)

- Leucopenia (9 to 25%) or leucocytosis (24 to 30%); **lymphopenia (63%)**.
- Raised ALT and AST (37%).
- Raised inflammatory markers: **ferritin, CRP, ESR**; CRP is associated with severity, and procalcitonin is usually low (high suggests bacterial coinfection).
- **High D-dimer and ferritin** in hospitalised patients.

### Imaging

**Chest radiograph:** peripheral **ground-glass opacities** in the mid and lower zones of both lungs; with progression, dense consolidation and loss of lung markings.

**Chest CT (most common findings)**

| Finding | Frequency |
|---|---|
| Ground-glass opacification | 83% |
| Ground-glass with mixed consolidation | 58% |
| Adjacent pleural thickening | 52% |
| Interlobular septal thickening | 48% |
| Air bronchograms | 46% |

- Less common: **crazy paving** (ground-glass with septal thickening), bronchiectasis, pleural and pericardial effusion, lymphadenopathy.
- Typically **bilateral, peripheral and lower lobe**.

## 5. Management

### Mild disease (home isolation)

- Upper respiratory symptoms or fever **without breathlessness or hypoxia**.
- Physical distancing, indoor masks, strict hand hygiene, adequate fluids and diet, awake proning.
- Symptomatic treatment: hydration, antipyretic, antitussive, multivitamins.
- **Monitor temperature and oxygen saturation 3 to 4 times daily.**
- **Seek medical help** if: persistent fever, severe weakness or prostration, persistent vomiting, palpitations, breathlessness on activity or at rest, severe cough, **saturation below 95%**, drowsiness or altered behaviour in the elderly. Closer monitoring for comorbidities.
- **Drugs:** paracetamol 500 mg four times daily; if worsening, naproxen 250 mg twice daily; antibiotics such as azithromycin or doxycycline (as per the lecture, only if bacterial infection is suspected); bed rest, vitamins, electrolyte solutions, PPIs. **Inhaled budesonide** 800 mcg twice daily by spacer for 5 to 7 days if fever and cough persist beyond 5 days.

### Moderate disease

- **RR above 24/min, breathlessness, SpO2 90% to below 93%** on room air. **Admit.**
- Paracetamol, antitussive, hydration.
- **Oxygen** to a target SpO2 of **92 to 96%**: nasal prongs, simple face mask or non-rebreathing mask according to need.
- Injectable antibiotics (e.g. ceftriaxone) where bacterial infection is possible.
- **Anti-inflammatory:** methylprednisolone 0.5 to 1 (mg/kg) in 2 divided doses for 5 to 10 days, **or IV dexamethasone 0.1 to 0.2 mg/kg** for 5 to 10 days; switch to oral when stable.
- **Anticoagulation:** unfractionated or low molecular weight heparin, weight-based (e.g. enoxaparin).
- **Monitor** work of breathing, haemodynamics, oxygen requirement; chest X-ray; HRCT if worsening; CBC, renal and liver function every 24 to 48 hours; CRP and D-dimer every 48 to 72 hours.

> **Check:** the slide writes methylprednisolone "0.5 to 1 mg" and enoxaparin "0.5 mg/kg per day"; the dose is weight-based (mg/kg/day), and the same slide set uses enoxaparin 0.5 mg/kg twice daily for intermediate dosing in severe disease. Follow the current MoH guideline for exact doses.

### Severe disease

- **RR above 30/min, breathlessness, SpO2 below 90%** on room air. **Admit to ICU.**
- Continue symptomatic treatment; **conservative fluid management** (maintain euvolaemia) when there is no shock.
- Immediate respiratory support: start oxygen at **5 L/min** and titrate to SpO2 above 92 to 96% (in pregnancy too).
- Escalation: face mask (low work of breathing), **high-flow nasal cannula** if oxygen need increases, **intubation** if work of breathing is high; follow the **conventional ARDS ventilatory protocol**.
- **Steroids:** methylprednisolone 1 to 2 mg/kg IV in 2 divided doses, or dexamethasone 0.2 to 0.4 mg/kg, for 5 to 10 days.
- **Anticoagulation:** weight-based intermediate-dose prophylactic UFH or LMWH, e.g. enoxaparin 0.5 mg/kg per dose twice daily.
- Septic shock: manage by protocol and local antibiogram.
- Monitoring: serial chest X-ray (HRCT if worsening); CRP and D-dimer every 24 to 48 hours; CBC, kidney and liver function daily.

### Oxygen delivery devices

| Device | Flow rate |
|---|---|
| Nasal prongs | 1 to 5 L/min |
| Simple face mask | 6 to 8 L/min |
| Partial rebreathing mask | 6 to 10 L/min |
| Non-rebreathing mask | 10 to 15 L/min |

## 6. Long COVID (post-COVID-19 condition)

**Definition:** continuation or new development of symptoms **3 months after** the initial infection, lasting **at least 2 months**, with no other explanation. Most people with mild disease recover within 1 to 2 weeks, and fully within 3 months.

**Epidemiology**
- About **10 to 20%** of infected people develop long COVID.
- More than 17 million in the WHO European Region in the first two years; at least **65 million worldwide** by March 2023.
- Affects all ages, with the highest diagnosis rate at **36 to 50 years**; most cases follow mild, non-hospitalised illness (because they are the majority of cases).

**Causes (not established)**
- Persistent or reactivated virus particles.
- Overactive immune cells releasing inflammatory mediators that injure tissue.
- **Autoantibodies** against own tissues.
- A combination of these.

**Immune findings (at a median of 14 months):** more non-classical monocytes, activated and double-negative B cells, IL-4 and IL-6-secreting CD4 T cells; fewer conventional dendritic cells and exhausted T cells; **low cortisol**; reduced **memory T cells**; raised antibody-secreting B cells and activated NK cells; increased checkpoint molecules **TIGIT and PD-L1** (suggesting immune exhaustion). This suggests persistent infection or an aberrant immune response. Low or absent antibody response in the acute stage predicts long COVID at 6 to 7 months.

**Risk factors**
- Female sex, older age, higher BMI, smoking.
- Underlying conditions: diabetes, asthma, autoimmune disease.
- Severe COVID-19 (but anyone can be affected); **unvaccinated** status.
- Reinfection (risk each time); multisystem inflammatory syndrome.
- Health inequities, lower income, inability to rest in the first weeks.

**Children:** can have long COVID with or without symptoms. **MIS-C** is a serious delayed complication with inflammation of heart, lungs, kidneys, brain, skin, eyes and GI tract; most often ages 5 to 11.

**Features:** more than 200 possible symptoms.

| System | Symptoms |
|---|---|
| General | fatigue, **post-exertional malaise**, fever |
| Respiratory and cardiac | dyspnoea, cough, chest pain, palpitations |
| Neurological and psychiatric | "brain fog", headache, sleep problems, light-headedness on standing, pins and needles, altered smell or taste, depression, anxiety |
| Digestive | diarrhoea, abdominal pain |
| Other | joint and muscle pain, rash, menstrual changes |

**Organ-specific problems**
- **Lung:** about one third of hospitalised patients had abnormal chest CT at 12 months (residual bands, fibrosis, air trapping, bronchiectasis); histology of mixed organising and chronic fibrosing patterns; a few have a persistently raised risk of venous thromboembolism.
- **Heart:** myocarditis; one study found about 60% of recovered patients with signs of ongoing cardiac inflammation, even after mild illness.
- **POTS (postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome):** abnormal autonomic response to standing with excessive tachycardia **without hypotension**; may appear 6 to 8 months after infection. Tilt-table and Valsalva tests show poor heart rate and blood pressure control, with lower skin temperature (vasoconstriction). "**COVID-somnia**" (insomnia) is common.
- **Kidney:** reduced GFR in about **35% at 6 months**; raised risk of CKD and dialysis.
- **Smell and taste:** about a quarter recover within weeks; most persist; 60 to 80% improve within a year. Can cause poor appetite, anxiety and depression.
- **Chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS, SEID):** overlaps with long COVID (fatigue, cognitive problems, muscle pain); some meet diagnostic criteria.

**Diagnosis:** no single test; a **diagnosis of exclusion** based on history and ruling out other causes. Targeted tests by symptom (chest X-ray for respiratory symptoms, ECG for cardiac).

**Management**
- No established drug treatment (as of 2022); trials ongoing. **Nirmatrelvir within 5 days** of infection reduced the risk of long COVID (March 2023 data).
- **Multidisciplinary rehabilitation**; rest, planning and prioritising for fatigue; **pacing** for post-exertional symptom exacerbation.
- Antihistamines for allergic-type symptoms such as rash.
- **Screen for mental health problems**, including post-traumatic stress.
- Persistent olfactory dysfunction: oral or intranasal corticosteroids, palmitoylethanolamide and luteolin (effectiveness unclear); olfactory training.

## 7. COVID-19 and diabetes mellitus

Diabetes is a risk factor for worse outcomes, and the relationship is **bidirectional**.

**Effects of COVID-19 on glucose control**
- **Direct:** cytokines and inflammatory mediators raise insulin resistance and glucose; the virus may bind ACE2 in pancreatic islets and cause **acute diabetes** through pancreatic injury.
- **Indirect:** disrupted diabetes care and treatments that alter glucose. **Glucocorticoids** lower mortality in critical COVID-19 but increase gluconeogenesis and insulin resistance, so glucose rises and insulin needs increase.

**Why diabetics do worse (a "flywheel")**
1. **Amplification:** hyperglycaemia increases ACE2 expression and glycosylation on beta cells, increasing viral attachment, viral load and beta-cell destruction.
2. **Vascular perfusion:** non-enzymatic glycation of endothelium and atherosclerosis narrow vessels, reducing blood and immune-cell delivery to infected organs.
3. **Insulin resistance:** free fatty acids from adipocytes worsen resistance, causing reactive oxygen species and cell damage that multiplies during infection.
4. **Immune dysregulation:** acidosis and ketones impair enzymes; macrophages, neutrophils and CD4 cells are weakened; **IL-17 and Th17** promote harmful inflammation and a cytokine storm. Impaired chemotaxis and diapedesis, calcium influx and low ATP reduce polymorph and macrophage function.
5. Other: impaired neutrophil degranulation and complement activation, higher glucose in airway secretions (more viral replication), exaggerated cytokine response, reduced viral clearance and associated comorbidities.

## 8. COVID-19 and dementia

- Dementia (a major neurocognitive disorder in DSM-5) shares mechanisms with COVID-19 injury: hypoxia and oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, neurodegeneration, and blood-brain barrier (BBB) permeability. Related conditions: Alzheimer's disease, vascular dementia, Lewy body dementia.
- Neurological sequelae: dizziness, headache, myalgia, hypogeusia, hyposmia, polyneuropathy, myositis, cerebrovascular disease, encephalitis, encephalopathy.
- Risk is bidirectional but **correlation is not causation**. Older adults with COVID-19 had a higher risk of **new Alzheimer's diagnosis** (highest at 85 years and above, and in women). A Danish study found Alzheimer's 3.4 times and Parkinson's 2.2 times more frequent 12 months after a positive test. Increased risk was also seen for vascular dementia, Parkinson's disease dementia and behavioural variant frontotemporal dementia.
- Proposed mechanisms: entry via the **olfactory system**; entry across the **BBB** through endothelial ACE2 and cytokines (IL-6, IL-1β, TNF, IL-17); inflammation driving accumulation of **β-amyloid and α-synuclein** (high β-amyloid found at autopsy in young COVID-19 patients).

## 9. Autoimmunity after COVID-19

| Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Molecular mimicry** | spike motif TQLPP resembles thrombopoietin, so cross-reacting antibodies may cause **thrombocytopenia**; motif ELDKY is shared with PRKG1 (platelet activation) and tropomyosin, possibly explaining clotting disorders and cardiac disease |
| **Dysregulated immune response** (cytokine storm) | **MIS-C**; **Guillain-Barré syndrome**; rheumatological manifestations (joint pain, myalgia, rash, RA- or lupus-like illness) |
| **Activation of self-reactive cells** | SLE onset or flare, **antiphospholipid syndrome**, RA, autoimmune hepatitis |
| **Disruption of immune regulation** | type 1 diabetes, IBD, multiple sclerosis, psoriasis |

## 10. Vaccines

### Development and types

- **Preclinical** (antigen selection), **Phase 1** (small number: safety, immune response, dose), **Phase 2** (hundreds: safety, immunogenicity in age groups and formulations), **Phase 3** (thousands compared with a control group: efficacy and safety).
- Approaches: **whole microbe** (inactivated: flu, polio; live attenuated: MMR; viral vector: Ebola), **subunit** (DTaP), **genetic/nucleic acid** (mRNA or DNA).
- **mRNA vaccines** encode the spike antigen; the cell makes the protein, which is displayed and triggers **killer T cells, B cells and helper T cells**. RNA is transient and does **not integrate** into the genome; the vaccine contains no live virus and cannot cause COVID-19.

### Vaccine comparison

| | **Moderna mRNA-1273** | **Pfizer-BioNTech BNT162b2** | **Oxford-AstraZeneca ChAdOx1-S** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Type | mRNA | mRNA | viral vector (not live replicating) |
| Age | 6 months and above | 6 months and above | 18 years and above |
| Primary schedule | adults: 2 doses 100 µg (0.5 ml), 8 weeks apart; 12 to 17 years: 2 doses 100 µg, 4 weeks apart; 6 to 11 years: 2 doses 50 µg (0.25 ml), 4 weeks apart; 6 months to 5 years: 2 doses 25 µg (0.25 ml), 4 weeks apart. WHO allows 4 to 8 weeks between doses; 8 weeks preferred (higher effectiveness, lower myocarditis risk) | 12 years and above: 2 doses 30 µg (0.3 ml), 4 to 8 weeks apart (deltoid); 5 to 11 years: 2 doses 10 µg (0.2 ml), 4 to 8 weeks (preferably 8); 6 months to 4 years: 3 doses 3 µg (0.2 ml) (2 doses 3 weeks apart, third at least 8 weeks after the second) | 2 IM doses (0.5 ml), 8 to 12 weeks apart; extend interval to maximise first-dose coverage; immunocompromised should be offered an additional dose |
| Pregnancy | WHO recommends; no pregnancy test or delay needed; breastfeeding as other adults (antibodies in breast milk) | WHO recommends; antibodies in breast milk | only when benefit outweighs risk; unlikely to harm the breastfed child |
| Do not give | severe allergy to a component; myocarditis or pericarditis after dose 1 (no more doses unless advised) | severe allergy to a component; **fever above 38.5 °C** (postpone) | severe allergy to a component; under 18 years |
| Special side effects | rare **myocarditis** (young males 18 to 35, after dose 2) | rare **myocarditis** | **thrombosis with thrombocytopenia syndrome (TTS)**; very rare Guillain-Barré syndrome (causal link not confirmed) |

**Notes**
- Moderna bivalent vaccine (original strain plus Omicron BA.1) gave a modest improvement in immune response; both versions protect against severe Omicron disease. Vaccination can be offered after past infection (individuals may delay 3 months; AstraZeneca at least 3 months after infection).
- **Moderna summary:** very high effectiveness against severe disease, hospitalisation and death after two doses plus a booster; modest effectiveness against symptomatic illness; modest effect on mild infection and transmission, particularly with Omicron; vaccine effectiveness against Omicron is lower than against Delta, and wanes faster. A third dose (first booster) is recommended for all adults and a second booster for the highest priority groups.
- **Priority groups** (WHO roadmap): older adults, immunocompromised, health workers. In general children are at lower risk, so higher-risk groups are vaccinated first, but children aged 6 months to 17 years with comorbidities should be offered it.
- **Boosters:** first booster 4 to 6 months after the primary series for highest-priority groups; second booster 4 to 6 months after the first; for those aged 12 and above, booster dose is **half** the primary dose (50 µg in 0.25 ml for Moderna). No routine boosters for children under 12 except immunocompromised.
- **Myocarditis after mRNA vaccines:** very rare, mainly young males 18 to 35 after dose 2, within a few days, usually mild, responds to conservative treatment and has better outcomes than classical or COVID-19 myocarditis.
- **AstraZeneca:** efficacy about 72% against symptomatic infection, higher with longer dose interval; either mRNA vaccine can be used as the second dose after a first AstraZeneca dose, and AstraZeneca can follow an inactivated vaccine (Sinopharm, Sinovac, Bharat) or be a heterologous booster.

> **Check:** the slide gives the adult Moderna age as "17 and above"; WHO's adult category is 18 and above (12 to 17 listed separately).

### Variants

- Viruses mutate as they replicate; widespread circulation gives more opportunities to mutate. Most mutations have little effect, but some change **transmissibility or severity**.
- Vaccines elicit a broad immune response (many antibodies and cells), so variants should not make them completely ineffective; vaccine composition can be updated.

## 11. Complications

- Pneumonia, **ARDS**, respiratory failure, pulmonary fibrosis.
- Thromboembolism (DVT, PE, stroke), myocarditis, arrhythmias.
- Acute kidney injury; new hyperglycaemia or diabetic ketoacidosis.
- Cytokine storm; secondary bacterial infection and sepsis.
- Neurological (GBS, encephalopathy, stroke); MIS-C in children.
- Long COVID and autoimmune disease.

## Key points to revise

1. SARS-CoV-2 uses ACE2; spread is by droplets and aerosols; incubation 2 to 14 days.
2. Three stages: nasal infection, conducting airway spread (80% stop here), alveolar type II cell infection and ARDS (20%).
3. NAAT/RT-PCR is the preferred test; antigen tests are faster but less sensitive, so repeat a negative symptomatic result; avoid NAAT within 3 months of infection.
4. Severity: mild (no hypoxia), moderate (RR above 24, SpO2 90 to below 93%), severe (RR above 30, SpO2 below 90%); target SpO2 92 to 96%.
5. Moderate and severe disease: oxygen, dexamethasone or methylprednisolone for 5 to 10 days, and weight-based heparin; HFNC then intubation with ARDS protocol if worsening.
6. Long COVID: symptoms starting or lasting 3 months after infection for at least 2 months; diagnosis of exclusion; treat with rehabilitation and pacing.
7. Diabetes and COVID-19 worsen each other through ACE2, glycation, insulin resistance and immune dysregulation; steroids raise glucose.
8. mRNA vaccines (Moderna, Pfizer) risk rare myocarditis in young men; AstraZeneca vector vaccine carries a TTS risk.
