Bone & Soft Tissue Pathology — Must-Knows

## Soft Tissue Tumours — Fat, Fibrous, Muscle**Lipoma vs liposarcoma**
- Lipoma: mature fat cells, thin capsule, benign — the most common soft tissue tumour overall
- Well-differentiated liposarcoma: looks almost identical to lipoma under the microscope, but is malignant — the only way to tell them apart is a genetic test showing MDM2/CDK4 gene amplification (region 12q13-15)
- Myxoid liposarcoma: has a jelly-like (myxoid) background plus immature fat cells called lipoblasts; caused by a specific gene fusion, t(12;16) FUS-DDIT3
- Pleomorphic liposarcoma: the most aggressive type, cells look wildly different from each other (pleomorphic)
-
**Nodular fasciitis — the great impersonator**

- Grows fast, has lots of dividing cells (mitoses) — looks alarming, like cancer
- But it's actually benign and self-limiting (goes away on its own)
- Caused by a gene fusion: t(17;22) MYH9-USP6
- Exam trap: don't call this a sarcoma just because it grows fast and has mitoses
-
**Desmoid tumour (deep fibromatosis)**

- Grows into surrounding tissue (locally invasive) but never spreads to distant organs (no metastasis)
- Linked to a faulty APC gene — same gene that's faulty in familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP), a condition causing hundreds of colon polyps
- On biopsy: bland-looking fibroblasts, but with abnormal build-up of a protein called beta-catenin inside the nucleus (because APC normally breaks beta-catenin down, and when APC is broken, beta-catenin piles up)
-
**Rhabdomyosarcoma (skeletal muscle cancer)**

- Embryonal type — most common type in children; the "botryoid" (grape-like) variant grows as clusters in the vagina or bladder of young children
- Look for cross-striations on histology — proof the cells are trying to become muscle
-
**Leiomyoma vs leiomyosarcoma**

- Leiomyoma: benign smooth muscle tumour, very common in the uterus (fibroids); cells have blunt-ended nuclei and minimal atypia
- Leiomyosarcoma: malignant version, seen in deep sites like the retroperitoneum; markers SMA and desmin (both smooth muscle markers) are positive, but the cells look atypical and hyperchromatic
-
**Synovial sarcoma**

- Confusing name — it does NOT come from the joint synovium
- Occurs near joints in young adults, grows slowly over years
- Defining feature: a translocation, t(X;18) SS18-SSX
-
**Undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma (UPS)**

- This is a "leftover" diagnosis — given only when a high-grade sarcoma doesn't show any specific line of differentiation on special stains (not fat, not muscle, not anything specific)
-

## Nerve Sheath Tumours**Schwannoma**
- Comes from Schwann cells (which make the myelin sheath around peripheral nerves)
- Encapsulated (has a clear boundary), painless
- Classic histology: Antoni A areas (densely packed cells) and Antoni B areas (loose, myxoid) — plus Verocay bodies (rows of nuclei lined up around a pale zone)
- Positive for S100 stain
-
**Neurofibroma types — and how they link to NF1 vs NF2**

- Localised cutaneous neurofibroma: a single small nodule in the skin
- Diffuse neurofibroma: spreads more broadly through the dermis
- Plexiform neurofibroma: grows along the length of a nerve, looks like a "bag of worms" — this type is specifically linked to NF1, and carries a 15-20% lifetime risk of turning into a malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour (MPNST)
- NF1 (neurofibromatosis type 1) → neurofibromas are the hallmark
- NF2 (neurofibromatosis type 2) → bilateral schwannomas (especially in the internal auditory canal, causing hearing loss) are the hallmark instead — don't mix these two up
-

## Bone Tumours**Osteosarcoma**
- Most common in teenagers, at the distal femur (metaphysis, near the knee)
- Look for: a Codman triangle on X-ray (a triangular shadow where the tumour lifts the periosteum off the bone) and a mixed lytic (bone-destroying) and sclerotic (bone-forming) pattern
-
**Osteoid osteoma vs osteoblastoma — same family, different size**

- Osteoid osteoma: small (under 2cm), causes severe night pain that is dramatically relieved by aspirin (because the tumour produces prostaglandins, which aspirin blocks) — commonly in the proximal tibia
- Osteoblastoma: essentially a bigger version (over 2cm), often in the posterior elements of the spine, but does NOT respond dramatically to aspirin the way osteoid osteoma does
-
**Osteochondroma**

- A painless bony bump that grows outward from the metaphysis
- Key finding: the medullary cavity (the hollow center of the bone) is continuous with the tumour's own cavity — proving it's really just an outgrowth of normal bone with a cartilage cap on top
-
**Chondrosarcoma**

- A malignant tumour made of cartilage-producing cells
- Classic site: pelvis or axial skeleton (unlike osteosarcoma, which favours long bone metaphyses)
-
**Ewing sarcoma**

- Occurs in adolescents, made of small round blue cells (very immature-looking cells with little cytoplasm)
- Defining translocation: t(11;22) EWS-FLI1
- Positive for CD99 marker
-
**Fibrous dysplasia**

- Caused by a GNAS gene mutation
- On X-ray/histology, the bone trabeculae look like "Chinese letters" — an odd, curved shape
-

## Bone Infection and Death**Osteomyelitis**
- Bacterial infection of bone, most often caused by Staphylococcus aureus
- In children, it typically starts in the metaphysis (because blood flow there is sluggish, letting bacteria settle)
- Chronic osteomyelitis can form a sinus tract (a channel to the skin surface) containing a piece of dead bone
- Sequestrum = the dead piece of bone
- Involucrum = new bone that grows around the sequestrum to try to wall it off
- Easy to confuse these two — sequestrum is dead, involucrum is the new living shell around it
-
**Osteonecrosis (avascular necrosis)**

- Death of bone due to loss of blood supply
- Classic cause: prolonged high-dose corticosteroid use
- Classic site: femoral head, seen on MRI as subchondral collapse (the bone surface just under the joint caves in)
-

## Joint Disease**Osteoarthritis**
- "Wear and tear" — pain worsens with activity, improves with rest
- X-ray triad: joint space narrowing (cartilage loss) + osteophytes (bone spurs) + subchondral sclerosis (dense bone just under the joint surface)
-
**Rheumatoid arthritis**

- Autoimmune, affects small joints symmetrically (both hands, MCP and PIP joints)
- Blood test: positive anti-citrullinated peptide antibodies (ACPA), raised ESR
-
**Ankylosing spondylitis**

- Affects the spine, causes stiffness and reduced mobility
- Strongly linked to the HLA-B27 gene
-
**Gout vs pseudogout — the crystal difference is everything**

- Gout: caused by monosodium urate crystals, which look needle-shaped and are negatively birefringent under polarized light; classic site is the big toe (first MTP joint), often in obese men
- Pseudogout: caused by calcium pyrophosphate (CPPD) crystals, which look rhomboid-shaped and are positively birefringent — easy to mix up with gout if you don't remember the crystal shape/birefringence difference

## Nerve and Muscle Disease**Guillain-Barré syndrome**
- Ascending weakness (starts in the legs, moves upward) and loss of reflexes
- Often follows a Campylobacter jejuni gut infection
- CSF shows raised protein but a normal cell count — this specific combo is called albuminocytologic dissociation
-
**Axonal vs demyelinating neuropathy — read the nerve conduction study**

- Axonal damage → reduced amplitude (fewer working axons), but conduction velocity stays roughly normal
- Demyelinating damage → reduced velocity (signal travels slower without myelin), amplitude may be preserved
- Diabetic neuropathy is a classic example of axonal damage
-
**Myasthenia gravis vs Lambert-Eaton — opposite mechanisms**

- Myasthenia gravis: antibodies attack acetylcholine receptors AFTER the nerve-muscle junction (postsynaptic); weakness gets WORSE with repeated muscle use
- Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome: antibodies attack calcium channels BEFORE the junction (presynaptic), often as a paraneoplastic syndrome from small cell lung cancer; weakness actually IMPROVES briefly with repeated use, because each contraction pushes a bit more calcium into the nerve terminal
- Learn them as mirror opposites — that's exactly how they're tested
-
**Dermatomyositis vs polymyositis vs inclusion body myositis**

- Dermatomyositis: inflammation clusters around blood vessels and at the edges of muscle bundles (perifascicular atrophy) — thought to be an antibody-driven, vessel-based process; often paraneoplastic (linked to an underlying cancer) in adults
- Inclusion body myositis: seen in older patients (over 50), affects the quadriceps and wrist flexors, shows rimmed vacuoles on biopsy
- Polymyositis: inflammation is inside the muscle fascicle (endomysial), driven directly by T-cells
-
**Botulinum toxin**

- Blocks the release of acetylcholine from the nerve terminal (presynaptic) — this is why it paralyzes muscle
-
**Leprosy**

- Mycobacterium leprae specifically invades Schwann cells, causing segmental demyelination — this is why leprosy causes nerve damage and sensory loss in the skin
-

## Histopathology Technique (comes up almost every CAT)**Tissue processing order — memorize this sequence exactly**
- Fixation (10% formalin — stops decay, preserves structure)
- Dehydration (increasing strengths of alcohol, removes water)
- Clearing (xylene — replaces alcohol with something wax-compatible)
- Embedding (paraffin wax — gives the tissue a firm block to cut)
- Sectioning (microtome, usually 4-5 μm thick)
- Staining
- Mounting
-
**Why fixation matters**

- Stops autolysis (the tissue's own enzymes digesting itself) — this is different from putrefaction, which is bacterial decomposition, not the tissue's own enzymes
-
**Special stains — match the stain to what it shows**

- H&E: routine stain — nuclei blue-purple, cytoplasm pink
- PAS (Periodic Acid-Schiff): basement membranes, glycogen — bright magenta
- Masson's trichrome: collagen/fibrosis — shows up blue/green against red muscle
- Congo red: amyloid — shows apple-green birefringence under polarized light (this is the gold-standard test for amyloidosis)
- Ziehl-Neelsen: acid-fast bacilli (TB)

## Oral Pathology- Oral squamous cell carcinoma (HPV-negative type, linked to smoking/alcohol): classic site is the ventral tongue and floor of mouth, where carcinogens pool in saliva
- Erythroplakia (red patch) carries a much higher cancer risk than leukoplakia (white patch) — red lesions are more likely to already show severe dysplasia
- Pyogenic granuloma: a fast-growing, bleeding gum mass in pregnant women (nicknamed "pregnancy tumour") — hormone-driven
- Aphthous ulcers: shallow, painful, self-healing within about 10 days
- Oral candidiasis: white plaques that scrape off to reveal red, raw mucosa underneath — common in chemotherapy patients
- Keratocystic odontogenic tumour: a jaw cyst with a corrugated keratin lining, tends to recur after removal
