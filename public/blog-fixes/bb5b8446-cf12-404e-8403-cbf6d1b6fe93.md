# Leishmaniasis

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Infectious Diseases (tropical)**

> **In one line:** leishmaniasis is infection by obligate intracellular *Leishmania* protozoa, spread by phlebotomine sandflies, which live as amastigotes inside macrophages. Disease ranges from a self-healing skin ulcer (cutaneous) through destructive mucosal disease to fatal visceral leishmaniasis (kala-azar), which presents with fever, massive splenomegaly and pancytopenia and is diagnosed by finding amastigotes in a splenic, marrow or node aspirate.

## What you must know

- Three syndromes: **visceral (VL, kala-azar)**, **cutaneous (CL)** and **mucosal (ML)**.
- Vector: female **sandfly**, *Phlebotomus* (Old World: Asia, Africa, Europe) or *Lutzomyia* (New World: Americas).
- Two forms: **promastigote** (extracellular, flagellate, in the sandfly) and **amastigote** (intracellular, non-flagellate, in macrophages of the vertebrate host).
- VL is caused by the ***L. donovani* complex**: *L. donovani* (anthroponotic) and *L. infantum* (zoonotic). **Kenya**, Uganda, Somalia, Sudan, South Sudan, Ethiopia, India, Bangladesh and Brazil are foci.
- VL: **fever, splenomegaly, hepatomegaly, weight loss, pancytopenia, hypergammaglobulinaemia**. Gold-standard diagnosis: **amastigotes on Giemsa smear** of tissue aspirate (splenic smear sensitivity over 95%).
- First-line VL drug in most regions: **pentavalent antimonial** (sodium stibogluconate) 20 mg/kg daily for 28 to 30 days; **liposomal amphotericin B** is the least toxic and the drug of choice in HIV co-infection.
- **PKDL** follows VL treatment in 2 to 50% of patients.
- No vaccine; control is by vector control.

## 1. Introduction

- Caused by unicellular eukaryotic **obligate intracellular protozoa** of the genus *Leishmania*, which primarily affect the **reticuloendothelial system**.
- Clinical range: self-healing cutaneous ulcers to fatal visceral disease.
- About **20 species** cause human disease. It occurs in **98 countries**, most of them developing, in tropical and temperate regions.
- Transmission may be **anthroponotic** (human to human via sandfly) or **zoonotic** (animal reservoir, e.g. dogs, hyraxes).

## 2. Life cycle

1. A female sandfly injects **promastigotes** (10 to 20 µm, flagellate) through the proboscis into the skin.
2. **Neutrophils** are the first host cells to take up the promastigotes. Infected neutrophils may undergo apoptosis and release viable parasites.
3. Parasites are taken up by **macrophages** and dendritic cells (directly or inside apoptotic neutrophils).
4. Inside macrophages the parasites become **amastigotes** and multiply, rupturing the cell and invading further macrophages.
5. A sandfly feeding on an infected host takes up amastigotes, which transform into flagellated promastigotes in the **posterior midgut** and multiply by **binary fission**.
6. Promastigotes migrate to the **anterior midgut** and infect the next host at the next blood meal.

## 3. Visceral leishmaniasis (kala-azar)

### Epidemiology

- Caused by the *L. donovani* complex. India, Bangladesh, Sudan, South Sudan, Ethiopia and Brazil account for **90% of world VL**. Also Kenya, Uganda, Somalia, the Middle East, Pakistan and China. VL is rare in West and the rest of sub-Saharan Africa.

### Immunopathogenesis

- The **leishmanin (Montenegro) skin test** is positive (delayed-type hypersensitivity, read at 48 hours after intradermal killed promastigotes) in people who have recovered from infection with *L. donovani* or *L. infantum*; it is negative during active disease.
- Organs of the reticuloendothelial system are chiefly affected: **spleen, liver and lymph nodes** enlarge; tonsils and intestinal submucosa are heavily infiltrated. **Bone marrow dysfunction causes pancytopenia.**

### Clinical features

- Abrupt onset of moderate to high-grade **fever** with rigors and chills.
- **Splenomegaly** (palpable by the second week), hepatomegaly, lymphadenopathy.
- Weight loss and malaise.
- **Skin darkening** (the name kala-azar means "black fever").
- Pedal oedema and ascites in advanced disease with hypoalbuminaemia.
- **Anaemia**; epistaxis, retinal haemorrhage and GI bleeding from thrombocytopenia.
- Secondary infections are common.

### Diagnosis

| Test | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Amastigotes on Giemsa-stained smears of tissue aspirate** | **Gold standard.** Splenic smear sensitivity over 95%; bone marrow 60 to 85%; lymph node aspirate about 50%. Splenic aspiration carries a risk of haemorrhage |
| Culture of aspirates | Increases sensitivity |
| ELISA, IFAT | In specialised laboratories |
| **Rapid immunochromatographic test (rK39)** | Used in the field; lower sensitivity and specificity in East Africa |
| Other | Pancytopenia, **hypergammaglobulinaemia**, hypoalbuminaemia |

> **Exam tip:** fever with **neutropenia or pancytopenia** in a patient from an endemic area strongly suggests VL; hypergammaglobulinaemia in long-standing illness supports it.

### Differential diagnosis

Malaria, typhoid fever, TB, brucellosis, schistosomiasis, portal hypertension, CML, tropical splenomegaly syndrome (TSS), histoplasmosis.

## 4. Treatment of visceral leishmaniasis

### General

- Blood transfusion for severe anaemia.
- Some patients relapse despite completing treatment.
- Treat malnutrition and concurrent illness (HIV, TB); antibiotics for superimposed infections.
- Supportive care: rest, **high-protein, high-calorie diet**, transfusions, wound care.
- The earliest sign of improvement is symptomatic; **regression of splenomegaly takes months**.

### Drugs

| Drug | Dose and notes |
|---|---|
| **Pentavalent antimonials** (drug of choice in most endemic regions): sodium stibogluconate (100 mg SbV/mL; painful IM) or meglumine antimoniate (85 mg SbV/mL) | **20 mg/kg daily** IV or IM for **28 to 30 days**. Cure rates over 90% in Africa, the Americas and most of the Old World but **under 50% in Bihar, India** (resistance). Adverse effects: arthralgia, myalgia, raised AST/ALT, **ECG changes** |
| **Amphotericin B deoxycholate** | First-line in Bihar, India; elsewhere used when antimonials fail. **0.75 to 1.0 mg/kg on alternate days for 15 infusions.** Adverse effects: fever, chills, nausea and vomiting, thrombophlebitis. Pre-treat with an antihistamine (chlorpheniramine) and an antipyretic (paracetamol) |
| **Liposomal amphotericin B** | **3 mg/kg daily on days 1 to 5, 14 and 21 (total 21 mg/kg).** Total dose varies: Asia 10 to 15 mg/kg, Africa about 18 mg/kg, Mediterranean / Americas 20 mg/kg or more. Less toxic |
| **Paromomycin (aminosidine)** | Aminocyclitol-aminoglycoside. **11 mg base/kg IM daily for 21 days** (approved in India) |
| **Miltefosine** | First **oral** drug for leishmaniasis. Long half-life promotes resistance. Adverse effects: mild vomiting, diarrhoea, allergic dermatitis, hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity. Teratogenic |
| **Pentamidine** | Also used |

- There is a role for **multidrug therapy**.

### Prognosis

- Within a week of treatment: defervescence, regression of splenomegaly, weight gain, recovery of blood counts.
- **Continued improvement over 6 to 12 months** suggests cure.

## 5. VL and HIV co-infection

- Where both are endemic, VL behaves as an **opportunistic infection**.
- **Serological tests are often negative.** Parasites may be found in unusual sites such as bronchoalveolar lavage fluid and buffy coat.
- **Liposomal amphotericin B** is the drug of choice. Most patients **relapse within 1 year**.

## 6. Post-kala-azar dermal leishmaniasis (PKDL)

- Occurs in **2 to 50%** during or after cure of VL.
- Lesions: **hypopigmented macules, papules, nodules** or diffuse infiltration of the skin, sometimes the oral mucosa.
- Indian PKDL: antimonials for **60 to 120 days**. In **East Africa most patients heal spontaneously.**

## 7. Cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL)

![Leishmaniasis · Image from the Wikipedia article, Wikimedia Commons](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/67/Skin_ulcer_due_to_leishmaniasis%2C_hand_of_Central_American_adult_3MG0037_lores.jpg/960px-Skin_ulcer_due_to_leishmaniasis%2C_hand_of_Central_American_adult_3MG0037_lores.jpg)

### Species

| Region | Species |
|---|---|
| **Old World, anthroponotic** | *L. tropica*: urban and suburban areas |
| **Old World, zoonotic** | *L. major* (commonest) |
| **Highlands of Ethiopia, Kenya, Uganda** | *L. aethiopica* (natural host: hyraxes) |
| **New World (mainly zoonotic)** | *L. mexicana*, *L. (Viannia) panamensis*, *L. amazonensis* |

### Clinical features

- After a sandfly bite a **papule** forms, grows into a **nodule**, then **ulcerates** over weeks to months.
- The ulcer is **painless**, with a base of necrotic tissue and crusted serum; secondary bacterial infection can occur.
- Lymphadenopathy may precede the skin lesion; **satellite lesions** are common with *L. major* and *L. tropica*.
- Lesions **heal spontaneously after 2 to 15 months**. *L. major* and *L. mexicana* heal quickly; *L. tropica* and *Viannia* species heal more slowly.
- Differential: cutaneous TB, fungal infections, leprosy, sarcoidosis, malignant ulcers.

### Diagnosis

- Amastigotes on **slit-skin smears, aspirates or biopsies** (gold standard); culture; **PCR**; isoenzyme profiling.

### Treatment

- Most heal spontaneously. **Systemic treatment** is needed for: lesions on the face, hands or joints; multiple lesions; large ulcers; lymphatic spread; New World CL with risk of mucosal disease; CL with HIV.
- **Pentavalent antimonial** first line: 20 mg/kg for 20 days.

| Situation | Treatment |
|---|---|
| *L. (Viannia) guyanensis* | Pentamidine isethionate: 2 injections of 4 mg salt/kg, 48 hours apart |
| ***L. aethiopica*** | **Paromomycin 16 mg/kg daily**; does not respond to antimonials |
| *L. major* | Oral **fluconazole 200 mg daily for 6 weeks**; miltefosine |
| *L. (V.) panamensis*, *L. mexicana* | Ketoconazole 600 mg daily for 28 days (76 to 90% effective in Panama and Guatemala) |
| **Local therapy** | Cryotherapy (liquid nitrogen); local heat 40 to 42°C (radiofrequency generator); intralesional sodium stibogluconate 0.3 to 0.8 mL; topical paromomycin |

## 8. Diffuse cutaneous leishmaniasis (DCL)

- Caused by *L. amazonensis* and *L. mexicana* (Americas) and ***L. aethiopica*** (Ethiopia and **Kenya**).
- Marked by **absent cell-mediated immunity** to the parasite, so it multiplies unchecked.
- Widespread papules, nodules, plaques and diffuse infiltration, symmetrical or not. Lesions **do not ulcerate**, **do not heal spontaneously** and are difficult to treat; 20-day antimonial courses are given.

## 9. Mucosal leishmaniasis (ML)

- Typically caused by *L. (V.) braziliensis*; rarely *L. amazonensis*, *L. guyanensis*, *L. panamensis*. Almost entirely confined to the **Americas**.
- Parasites spread by lymphatics or blood to the upper respiratory mucosa.
- Presents with nasal stuffiness and bleeding, then **destruction of nasal cartilage, septal perforation and collapse of the nasal bridge**.
- **Tissue biopsy** is essential to find parasites.
- Treatment of choice: **pentavalent antimonial 20 mg SbV/kg for 30 days**.

## 10. Prevention

- **No vaccine.**
- Vector control: **insecticide-impregnated bed nets and curtains**, residual insecticide spraying.
- Treatment of infected domestic dogs (zoonotic foci).

## Key points to revise

1. *Leishmania* amastigotes live in macrophages; promastigotes are in the sandfly.
2. VL (kala-azar): fever, splenomegaly, pancytopenia, hypergammaglobulinaemia, dark skin; common in Kenya.
3. Diagnosis: amastigotes in splenic (over 95%), marrow (60 to 85%) or node (50%) aspirate; rK39 in the field.
4. Treatment of VL: sodium stibogluconate 20 mg/kg for 28 to 30 days, or liposomal amphotericin B; miltefosine is oral.
5. HIV co-infection: opportunistic, serology often negative, relapse within 1 year.
6. PKDL: skin lesions after VL, heals spontaneously in East Africa.
7. CL: painless ulcer that self-heals; *L. aethiopica* (Kenya highlands, hyraxes) responds to paromomycin, not antimonials, and causes DCL.
8. ML (Americas): nasal destruction, antimonials for 30 days.
