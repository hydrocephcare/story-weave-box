# Neonatal Jaundice

**Year 4 · Paediatrics · Neonatology**

> **In one line:** jaundice in the newborn is the yellow discolouration of skin and sclera from accumulated bilirubin, usually unconjugated; most is physiological (high bilirubin production plus an immature liver), but unconjugated bilirubin is neurotoxic and can cause **kernicterus**, so pathological jaundice must be recognised early and treated with **phototherapy**, IVIG or **exchange transfusion**.

## What you must know

- Jaundice is the commonest reason for readmission of newborns. Visible jaundice means **unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia** in most cases.
- **Physiological jaundice** appears on day 2 to 3 and results from increased red-cell breakdown plus low **ligandin** and low **glucuronyl transferase** activity.
- **Jaundice in the first 24 hours is pathological** until proven otherwise; so is jaundice beyond 1 to 2 weeks (cholestasis, galactosaemia, hypothyroidism, biliary atresia).
- Jaundice spreads **head to toe** (face and forehead first); visible jaundice on the legs signals the need to measure bilirubin.
- **Kernicterus** (bilirubin encephalopathy) is a preventable cause of death and lifelong neurological damage.
- Treatment ladder: **phototherapy** (first line), **IVIG** (Rh and ABO incompatibility), **exchange transfusion** (failure of phototherapy or severe disease).
- Prevent Rh haemolytic disease with **anti-D immunoglobulin** given to Rh-negative mothers.

## 1. Definition and bilirubin metabolism

- Newborn jaundice is the yellow colour of skin and sclerae caused by **accumulation of unconjugated bilirubin**.
- In most infants it is a **normal transitional phenomenon**. In some, bilirubin rises excessively and becomes dangerous.
- **Pathway**: haem from red cells becomes unconjugated (lipid-soluble) bilirubin, carried on albumin to the liver, **conjugated by glucuronyl transferase** (UDPGT) to water-soluble bilirubin, excreted in bile. Gut bacteria convert it to stercobilin; in the neonatal gut **beta-glucuronidase** deconjugates some, which is reabsorbed (**enterohepatic circulation**).

## 2. Causes

### 2.1 Physiological jaundice

Two things happen together:

1. **High bilirubin production** from rapid breakdown of fetal red cells (shorter red-cell lifespan, high haematocrit).
2. **Low hepatic excretory capacity**: low concentration of the binding protein **ligandin** in hepatocytes, and low activity of **glucuronyl transferase**, the enzyme that conjugates bilirubin with glucuronic acid to make it water soluble.

Increased enterohepatic circulation adds to the load.

### 2.2 Breastfeeding jaundice versus breast milk jaundice

| Feature | Breastfeeding jaundice | Breast milk jaundice |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | Poor feeding establishment, low intake, weight loss, increased enterohepatic circulation | Factors in breast milk (e.g. beta-glucuronidase) |
| Onset | First week | After the first week, may last weeks |
| Management | Improve breastfeeding, supplement if needed | Usually reassurance; interrupt breastfeeding 24 to 48 hours if bilirubin high |

### 2.3 Risk factors for severe jaundice

- **Race**: higher in East Asians and American Indians; lower in Africans and African Americans.
- **Geography**: higher at high altitude; Greeks in Greece have a higher incidence.
- **Genetics and family**: siblings with significant jaundice (especially treated); polymorphisms in genes for bilirubin-metabolising enzymes; **G6PD deficiency** (homozygous or heterozygous) and other hereditary haemolytic anaemias.
- **Nutrition**: breastfed infants or those with inadequate intake (increased enterohepatic circulation prolongs jaundice).
- **Maternal**: diabetes (higher risk); some drugs increase or reduce risk; some herbal remedies taken by a breastfeeding mother may worsen jaundice.
- **Prematurity and low birthweight**.
- **Congenital infection**.

### 2.4 Pathological causes (add to the list)

| Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|
| Haemolysis | **Rh and ABO incompatibility**, G6PD deficiency, spherocytosis, bruising or cephalhaematoma |
| Infection | Sepsis, TORCH, urinary tract infection |
| Metabolic or endocrine | Hypothyroidism, galactosaemia |
| Reduced conjugation | Crigler-Najjar, Gilbert syndrome |
| Conjugated (cholestatic) | **Biliary atresia**, neonatal hepatitis, choledochal cyst, TPN cholestasis |

> **Exam tip:** conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia (direct bilirubin above 20% of total or more than 25 to 34 micromol/L), pale stools and dark urine always mean a cholestatic cause such as biliary atresia and need urgent referral (best outcome if surgery before 8 weeks).

## 3. Clinical presentation

- Typically appears on the **second or third day**.
- **Jaundice visible in the first 24 hours** is likely non-physiological and needs evaluation.
- Appearing after **3 to 4 days** also needs closer scrutiny and monitoring.
- Severe jaundice or jaundice beyond **1 to 2 weeks**: check the newborn screen for **galactosaemia and congenital hypothyroidism**, explore family history, review the weight curve, ask the mother about breastfeeding adequacy, and check **stool colour**.

### 3.1 History

**Family**: previous sibling with neonatal jaundice (especially if treated); family jaundice or Gilbert syndrome; anaemia, splenectomy or gallstones (haemolytic disorder); liver disease.

**Pregnancy and delivery**: maternal infection; maternal drugs including herbal remedies; delayed cord clamping; birth trauma with bruising or fractures.

**Postnatal**: pale stools; breastfeeding pattern; drugs or herbs taken by the lactating mother; greater than average weight loss; symptoms of hypothyroidism; symptoms of metabolic disease such as galactosaemia; exposure to total parenteral nutrition.

### 3.2 Examination

- Jaundice is first visible on the **face and forehead**. Press on the skin to blanch it and reveal the colour (daylight).
- It then spreads to the trunk and extremities; it fades in the opposite direction.
- Visible jaundice on the **lower limbs** strongly suggests the need to measure bilirubin (serum or by transcutaneous bilirubinometer).
- Look for pallor, hepatosplenomegaly, bruising, cephalhaematoma, signs of sepsis, hypotonia, and neurological signs of bilirubin encephalopathy.

## 4. Investigations

**First-line**

- **Transcutaneous bilirubinometry** (handheld) reduces the need for blood sampling.
- **Total serum bilirubin** (split into conjugated and unconjugated).

**Additional tests are indicated when:**

- Jaundice appears in the first day or after the third day.
- The infant is anaemic at birth, appears ill, or the bilirubin is high enough to need treatment.
- Jaundice lasts more than 2 weeks.
- History or examination suggests pathology not explained by simple physiological jaundice.

**Other suggested tests**

| Test | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Maternal and infant **blood group and Rh** | Isoimmunisation |
| **Direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test** | Immune haemolysis |
| Haemoglobin and haematocrit | Anaemia or polycythaemia |
| Serum albumin | Binding capacity for bilirubin |
| End-tidal carbon monoxide | Index of bilirubin production (haemolysis) |
| Peripheral blood film | Red-cell morphology (spherocytes, G6PD) |
| Reticulocyte count | Haemolysis |
| **LFTs**: ASAT and ALAT | Raised in hepatocellular disease |
| Alkaline phosphatase and GGT | Raised in cholestasis |
| Viral and parasitic infection tests | With hepatosplenomegaly, petechiae, thrombocytopenia |
| **Urine reducing substances** | Screens for galactosaemia (infant must be on milk) |
| Blood gas | Acidosis, especially respiratory, increases risk of bilirubin CNS toxicity |
| Thyroid function tests | Hypothyroidism |

**Imaging**

- **Ultrasound** of liver and bile ducts if cholestasis suspected.
- **Radionuclide (HIDA) scan** if **extrahepatic biliary atresia** is suspected.

## 5. Management

Phototherapy, IVIG and exchange transfusion are the main treatments. Choose according to gestation, age in hours and bilirubin level on treatment charts (for example the NICE or local protocol).

### 5.1 Phototherapy

- **Primary treatment** of unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia.
- Three reactions occur when bilirubin is exposed to light:
  1. **Photo-oxidation**.
  2. **Configurational isomerisation**: very rapid; converts the usual 4Z,15Z bilirubin to water-soluble isomers in which one or both internal bonds are opened.
  3. **Structural isomerisation**: intramolecular cyclisation forming **lumirubin**; enhanced by higher light intensity.
- The products are excreted without needing conjugation.

**Physics**

- Bilirubin absorbs light mainly around **450 to 460 nm**; longer wavelengths penetrate skin better.
- Lamps with output in the **blue region (460 to 490 nm)** are most effective. In practice white, blue, turquoise and green light are used.
- Energy falls with distance: keep the light **no more than 50 cm** (20 in) away, as close as 10 cm if the infant's temperature is monitored.
- Efficiency rises with **more skin area exposed** and with **higher serum bilirubin**.
- Green light penetrates better in theory but is not clearly more effective, makes babies look sick and is unpleasant for staff, so is little used.
- **Special narrow-spectrum blue lamps** work best; ordinary blue fluorescent tubes are about equal to white daylight tubes. White tubes are less efficient but moving them closer compensates.
- **Double or triple phototherapy** (two or three units at once) is used for very high bilirubin.

**Practical care**

- Infant naked except for a nappy; **cover the eyes** to protect the retina.
- Check distance (50 cm or less with fluorescent lamps).
- Line the cot with reflecting material (white linen) and hang a white curtain around: these can multiply energy delivery several-fold.
- With spotlights, place the infant at the centre of the light circle because energy drops off towards the edge.
- Continue **milk feeds** (breast milk or formula): milk carries bilirubin out of the gut.
- **Stopping**: when bilirubin falls **25 to 50 micromol/L (1.5 to 3 mg/dL)** below the level that triggered treatment. Check for **rebound** within **6 to 12 hours** of stopping; timing of follow-up is individualised.

**Side effects**

- Increased **insensible water loss** and loose stools (consider fluid supplementation).
- **Retinal damage** if the eyes are uncovered.
- DNA strand breakage and other effects on genetic material (seen with hyperbilirubinaemia plus phototherapy).
- Redistribution of skin blood flow in small premature infants, with an increased incidence of **patent ductus arteriosus**.
- **Hypocalcaemia** in preterm infants (suggested to be mediated by altered melatonin metabolism); a head covering may prevent it under 35 weeks' gestation.
- Amino acids in TPN solutions degrade in light: **shield TPN** from the lamps.
- Equipment accidents, including burns when UV filters are not replaced; regular maintenance is needed.
- Others: skin rash, "bronze baby" syndrome with conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia, hyperthermia, separation of mother and baby.

### 5.2 Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG)

- In **Rh, ABO or other blood group incompatibility** with significant jaundice, IVIG significantly reduces the need for exchange transfusion (it blocks Fc receptors and reduces haemolysis).

### 5.3 Exchange transfusion

- Second-line treatment when phototherapy fails to control bilirubin; indicated to **avoid bilirubin neurotoxicity** when other measures have failed or are not enough.
- May be needed in **erythroblastosis** with severe anaemia, hydrops, or both, even without a high bilirubin.
- Early exchange has been done for **cord haemoglobin below 11 g/dL**, **cord bilirubin above 70 micromol/L (4.5 mg/dL)**, or both.
- If phototherapy does not lower the bilirubin significantly, perform exchange transfusion.
- Risks (standard): infection, electrolyte disturbance (hypocalcaemia, hyperkalaemia), thrombocytopenia, necrotising enterocolitis, portal vein thrombosis, cardiac arrhythmia.

### 5.4 Other therapies

- **Breast milk jaundice**: interrupt breastfeeding for **24 to 48 hours** and give a breast milk substitute.
- **Supplementing** breast feeds with **5 mL of breast milk substitute** reduces the level and duration of jaundice.
- **Oral bilirubin oxidase** and **agar or charcoal feeds** bind or break down bilirubin in the gut and reduce enterohepatic circulation, but have not become popular; bilirubin oxidase is not available as a drug and is restricted to research protocols.
- **Prevention of Rh disease**: **Rh immunoglobulin (anti-D)** to Rh-negative women has greatly reduced the incidence and severity of Rh haemolytic disease.

## 6. Complications and prognosis

- **Kernicterus** (chronic bilirubin encephalopathy): unconjugated bilirubin crosses the blood-brain barrier and deposits in the basal ganglia, hippocampus, brainstem nuclei. Risk is higher with acidosis (especially respiratory), low albumin, sepsis, prematurity and haemolysis.
- Acute phase: lethargy, poor feeding, hypotonia, then hypertonia, **opisthotonus**, high-pitched cry, seizures, apnoea, death.
- Chronic: **athetoid cerebral palsy**, sensorineural hearing loss, **upward gaze palsy**, dental enamel hypoplasia, intellectual disability.
- **Prognosis is excellent** if the infant is treated according to accepted guidelines.
- Kernicterus remains a true risk. Its recent increase may reflect the **misconception that jaundice in a healthy term infant can be ignored**.

> **Remember:** every jaundiced newborn needs a risk assessment. Time of onset, rate of rise and gestation determine action, not the colour alone.

## Key points to revise

1. Physiological jaundice results from more bilirubin production plus low ligandin and glucuronyl transferase; it starts on day 2 to 3.
2. Jaundice in the first 24 hours, or lasting beyond 2 weeks, is pathological; check haemolysis, sepsis, TFTs, galactosaemia and cholestasis.
3. Visible jaundice spreads head to toe; bilirubin check if the legs are yellow.
4. Key tests: bilirubin, blood group and Rh, direct Coombs, FBC, retics, G6PD, LFTs, TFTs, urine reducing substances.
5. Phototherapy works by photo-oxidation, configurational and structural isomerisation (lumirubin); blue light 460 to 490 nm, distance 50 cm or less, eyes covered.
6. Stop phototherapy 25 to 50 micromol/L below the starting threshold and recheck in 6 to 12 hours.
7. IVIG reduces exchange transfusion in Rh/ABO disease; exchange transfusion is for failure of phototherapy or severe haemolysis.
8. Kernicterus is preventable; give anti-D to Rh-negative mothers.
