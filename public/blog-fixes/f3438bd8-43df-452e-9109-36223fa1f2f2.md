## SummaryThis document provides a comprehensive overview of respiratory system pathology, designed for exam preparation covering multiple-choice questions (MCQs), short answer questions (SAQs), and essays. It systematically breaks down common and critical conditions affecting the upper and lower respiratory tracts, including infections, tumors, airway diseases (obstruction, restriction), interstitial lung diseases, and vascular pathologies. The content emphasizes key definitions, epidemiological data, pathogenesis, characteristic morphology, and clinical features for each condition.
The material is structured topic by topic, starting with upper respiratory tract infections and neoplasms, then moving to atelectasis, acute lung injury, obstructive versus restrictive diseases, specific conditions like emphysema, chronic bronchitis, asthma, bronchiectasis, and various chronic interstitial lung diseases (IPF, NSIP, COP, pneumoconioses, sarcoidosis). It also details pulmonary vascular diseases such as pulmonary embolism and hypertension, and diffuse alveolar hemorrhage syndromes. Finally, it covers infectious diseases like pneumonia and tuberculosis, lung cancers, and pleural lesions, concluding with a rapid-fire MCQ anchor section for quick review.
## Key Points- **Rhinovirus**: Most common pathogen in the common cold.
- **S. pneumoniae**: Most common cause of lobar pneumonia.
- **Adenocarcinoma**: Most common lung cancer subtype overall, and most common in women and nonsmokers.
- **Small Cell Lung Cancer (SCLC)**: Almost always metastatic at presentation, treated with chemotherapy, and has a median survival of approximately 1 year.
- **Panacinar emphysema**: Associated with α1-antitrypsin deficiency and primarily affects the lower lobes.
- **Centriacinar emphysema**: Associated with cigarette smoking and primarily affects the upper lobes.
- **Distal acinar emphysema**: Associated with spontaneous pneumothorax and bullae.
- **Reid Index**: Measures submucosal gland hypertrophy in chronic bronchitis; >0.4 indicates significant enlargement.
- **Hyaline membranes**: Hallmark microscopic finding in Diffuse Alveolar Damage (DAD), characteristic of ARDS.
- **Usual Interstitial Pneumonia (UIP)**: The histologic pattern required for the diagnosis of Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (IPF), characterized by patchy fibrosis with temporal heterogeneity.
- **IPF Survival**: Mean survival is 3 years or less.
- **NSIP vs. IPF Prognosis**: Non-specific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP) has a significantly better prognosis than IPF.
- **Sarcoidosis**: Characterized by noncaseating granulomas in multiple organs; diagnosis of exclusion.
- **Erythema nodosum**: A skin sign associated with acute sarcoidosis.
- **Sarcoidosis Hypercalcemia**: Caused by active Vitamin D production by macrophages within granulomas.
- **Pleural plaques**: The most common manifestation of asbestos exposure, usually asymptomatic.
- **Asbestosis Location**: Primarily affects the lower lobes of the lungs.
- **Silicosis Lymph Nodes**: May show "eggshell calcification".
- **Silicotuberculosis**: The association of silicosis with tuberculosis, due to impaired cell-mediated immunity.
- **CWP vs. Silicosis vs. Asbestos Lung Cancer Risk**: Coal Worker's Pneumoconiosis (CWP) does not increase lung cancer risk; silicosis has a contentious relationship; asbestos significantly increases lung cancer risk (5x) and risk of malignant mesothelioma (>1000x).
- **Kartagener Syndrome**: A rare autosomal recessive condition characterized by ciliary structural defects leading to bronchiectasis and male sterility.
- **Pulmonary Emboli Source**: Most commonly arise from deep veins of the lower legs.
- **Pulmonary Infarction in PE**: Occurs in only about 10% of cases due to the dual blood supply of the lungs.
- **Sudden Death in PE**: Occurs when >60% of the pulmonary vasculature is obstructed.
- **BMPR-2 Mutations**: Found in 50% of familial primary pulmonary hypertension cases.
- **Goodpasture Antigen**: Non-collagenous domain of the α3 chain of collagen IV, found in lung and kidney basement membranes.
- **Goodpasture Immunofluorescence**: Linear IgG deposition along alveolar and glomerular basement membranes.
- **Diphtheria Pseudomembrane**: Caused by *Corynebacterium diphtheriae*.
- **Recurrent Respiratory Papillomatosis (RRP)**: Caused by HPV types 6 and 11.
- **Laryngeal Cancer M:F Ratio**: 7:1.
- **Glottic Tumor Location**: Approximately 90% are confined to the larynx at diagnosis due to early symptoms.
- **Asthma Eosinophil Mediator**: IL-5 is key for eosinophil activation.
- **Asthma Mucus Mediator**: IL-13 stimulates mucus production.
- **Curschmann Spirals**: Whorls of shed epithelium found in mucus plugs in asthma.
- **Charcot-Leyden Crystals**: Eosinophil protein crystalloids found in sputum in asthma.
- **Aspirin-Induced Asthma**: Mechanism involves COX inhibition, shifting metabolism to leukotriene production.
-

## 1. UPPER RESPIRATORY TRACT### ACUTE INFECTIONS — MUST KNOWS**Common Cold:**
- Most common pathogen: **Rhinovirus**
- Others: coronavirus, RSV, parainfluenza, influenza, adenovirus, enteroviruses, Group A β-hemolytic streptococci
- ~40% of cases have no identifiable cause
- Self-limiting, usually <1 week
- Complications: bacterial **otitis media** or **sinusitis**
-

**Acute Pharyngitis:**
- Most common form = mild, accompanies common cold
- Severe form with exudate = **β-hemolytic streptococci** or adenovirus
- Streptococcal tonsillitis → risk of **peritonsillar abscess (quinsy)**, **poststreptococcal glomerulonephritis**, **acute rheumatic fever**
- Coxsackievirus A → **herpangina** (pharyngeal vesicles/ulcers)
- EBV → **infectious mononucleosis** ("kissing disease") — important cause of pharyngitis
-

**Acute Epiglottitis:**
- Agent: **H. influenzae** (mainly in children)
- Features: **abrupt onset**, pain, **airway obstruction** → can be fatal
- Prevented by **H. influenzae vaccination**
-

**Laryngitis:**
- Common cold agents + inhalants + allergens
- Uncommon but important: **tuberculous laryngitis** (from infected sputum) and **diphtheritic laryngitis**
- Diphtheria: **Corynebacterium diphtheriae** → necrosis + fibrinopurulent exudate → **dirty-gray pseudomembrane**
- Hazards of diphtheria: pseudomembrane aspiration (airway obstruction) + toxin absorption (myocarditis, peripheral neuropathy)
-

**Croup (Laryngotracheobronchitis):**
- Most common cause: **parainfluenza virus** (children)
- Also: RSV
- Features: **inspiratory stridor**, harsh persistent cough
- Self-limited but can cause respiratory failure
-

### NASOPHARYNGEAL CARCINOMA — MUST KNOWS- Strongly linked to **EBV** (genome found in virtually ALL cases)
- High incidence in **Chinese populations** → viral oncogenesis + genetic susceptibility
- EBV: first replicates in nasopharyngeal epithelium → infects tonsillar B lymphocytes → epithelial transformation
- **3 histologic variants:**
-
 1. Keratinizing squamous cell carcinoma
2. Non-keratinizing squamous cell carcinoma
3. **Undifferentiated carcinoma** — most common, most closely linked to EBV
- Undifferentiated type: large epithelial cells, indistinct borders ("syncytial" growth), prominent **eosinophilic nucleoli**
- Background of **reactive lymphocytes** → can mimic non-Hodgkin lymphoma → need **immunohistochemical stains** to confirm epithelial origin
- Behavior: locally invasive → cervical lymph nodes → distant metastasis
- **Radiosensitive** — 5-year survival ~50% even in advanced disease
-

### LARYNGEAL TUMORS — MUST KNOWS**Most common presenting symptom of ALL laryngeal lesions:** HOARSENESS
**Vocal Cord Nodules ("Polyps" / Singer's Nodes):**
- <0.5 cm, smooth, hemispherical, on true vocal cords
- Fibrous tissue + stratified squamous mucosa
- Associated with **heavy smokers** and **singers** (chronic irritation)
-

**Laryngeal Papilloma:**
- Benign, on true vocal cords, raspberry-like, <1 cm
- Histology: finger-like projections with fibrovascular cores + squamous epithelium
- Caused by **HPV types 6 and 11**
- In **children** → **Recurrent Respiratory Papillomatosis (RRP)** — multiple, recur after excision
- Transmission in children: **vertical transmission during delivery**
- Do NOT become malignant, often regress at puberty
- **HPV vaccine** (types 6 & 11) can prevent RRP
-

**Carcinoma of the Larynx:**
- Only **2% of all cancers**
- More common after age 40, **M:F ratio = 7:1**
- Risk factors: **cigarette smoking** (almost all cases), alcohol, asbestos exposure
- HPV sequences in ~15% → **better prognosis**
- **95% are squamous cell carcinomas**
- Location breakdown:
-
 - **Glottic (vocal cord): 60–75%** — best prognosis (early symptoms + sparse lymphatics)
- Supraglottic: 25–40% — ~1/3 metastasize to cervical lymph nodes
- Subglottic: <5% — clinically silent until advanced
- Begins as in situ lesion → pearly gray wrinkled plaques → ulceration → fungation
- Adjacent mucosa may show squamous dysplasia or carcinoma in situ (field effect from carcinogen exposure)
- **Glottic tumors: ~90% confined to larynx at diagnosis** (early hoarseness saves lives)
- Death: infection of distal respiratory passages OR widespread metastases + cachexia
-

## 2. ATELECTASIS (COLLAPSE)### MUST KNOWS- Definition: loss of lung volume from inadequate expansion → **ventilation-perfusion imbalance** → **hypoxia**
- Blood shunting: inadequately oxygenated blood from pulmonary arteries → veins
-

**3 Types:**
| Type | Mechanism | Key Cause |
|------|-----------|-----------|
| **Resorption** | Obstruction → air absorbed distally | Mucous plug, foreign body, tumor, asthma |
| **Compression** | Fluid/blood/air in pleural space compresses lung | Pleural effusion, pneumothorax (CHF common) |
| **Contraction** | Fibrosis prevents expansion + increases elastic recoil | Local or generalized lung/pleural fibrosis |
- Resorption atelectasis — most common cause = **mucous/mucopurulent plug**
- Basal atelectasis: bedridden patients, ascites, post-surgery (elevated diaphragm)
- **Contraction atelectasis is irreversible**; the other two are potentially reversible
- Treat promptly → prevent hypoxemia and superimposed infection
-

## 3. ACUTE LUNG INJURY (ALI) & ARDS### MUST KNOWS**ALI Clinical Triad (no primary left-sided heart failure):**
- Acute onset of dyspnea
- Decreased arterial oxygen pressure (hypoxemia)
- Bilateral pulmonary infiltrates on chest radiograph
-

- Infiltrates = **noncardiogenic pulmonary edema** (damage to alveolar capillary membrane, NOT cardiac failure)
- ALI can progress → **ARDS**
-

**ARDS:**
- Diffuse alveolar capillary AND epithelial damage
- Features: rapid onset respiratory insufficiency, **cyanosis**, severe **arterial hypoxemia refractory to oxygen therapy**, can → multisystem organ failure
- Histologic manifestation = **Diffuse Alveolar Damage (DAD)**
- Distinct from neonatal RDS (which is primary surfactant deficiency)
-

**Pathogenesis — KEY STEPS:**
- Insult → pulmonary macrophages release **IL-8** (within 30 minutes) → neutrophil chemotaxis
- Also IL-1, TNF → endothelial activation + neutrophil sequestration
- Activated neutrophils release oxidants, proteases, PAF, leukotrienes → damage epithelium + endothelium
- → Increased vascular permeability, alveolar flooding, loss of diffusion capacity, surfactant loss (type II pneumocyte damage)
- Balance between destructive forces vs. antiproteases, antioxidants, IL-10 determines severity
-

**Morphology:**
- Acute phase: dark red, firm, airless, heavy lungs
- Microscopy: capillary congestion, alveolar epithelial necrosis, interstitial + intra-alveolar edema/hemorrhage, neutrophils in capillaries (sepsis)
- **HALLMARK:** **Hyaline membranes** lining distended alveolar ducts (fibrin-rich edema + necrotic epithelial cells)
- Organizing stage: type II pneumocyte proliferation + intra-alveolar fibrosis + alveolar septal thickening
-

**Clinical:**
- 85% develop within **72 hours** of insult
- Mortality: decreased from 60% → **40%** with improved supportive therapy
- Poor prognosis: advanced age, bacteremia (sepsis), multisystem (cardiac, renal, hepatic) failure
- Survivors may develop **diffuse interstitial fibrosis**
-

## 4. OBSTRUCTIVE vs. RESTRICTIVE DISEASE### MUST KNOWS — HIGH YIELD TABLE| Feature | Obstructive | Restrictive |
|---------|-------------|-------------|
| Problem | Airflow limitation | Reduced expansion |
| FVC | Normal or slightly ↓ | ↓ |
| FEV1 | Significantly ↓ | Normal or proportionately ↓ |
| FEV1/FVC ratio | **↓ (characteristic)** | Near normal |
| Diseases | Emphysema, chronic bronchitis, asthma, bronchiectasis | ARDS (acute), pneumoconioses, IPF, NSIP, sarcoidosis |
**Restrictive conditions:**
- Chest wall disorders with normal lungs (severe obesity, pleural disease, Guillain-Barré)
- Acute or chronic interstitial lung diseases
-

## 5. EMPHYSEMA### MUST KNOWS- **Definition:** Abnormal **permanent** enlargement of air spaces **distal to terminal bronchioles** + destruction of walls **without significant fibrosis**
-

**4 Types:**
| Type | Location | Association |
|------|----------|-------------|
| **Centriacinar (Centrilobular)** | Central/proximal acinus (respiratory bronchioles) — distal alveoli spared | Cigarette smoking; upper lobes |
| **Panacinar (Panlobular)** | Entire acinus uniformly enlarged | **α1-antitrypsin deficiency**; lower lobes |
| **Distal Acinar (Paraseptal)** | Distal acinus; adjacent to pleura/septa | Unknown cause; **spontaneous pneumothorax in young adults**; bullae |
| **Irregular** | Irregular acinar involvement, with scarring | Healed inflammatory disease; most common but **asymptomatic** |
- Clinically significant obstruction: only **centriacinar** and **panacinar**
- Centriacinar is **20x more common** than panacinar
-

**Pathogenesis — PROTEASE-ANTIPROTEASE IMBALANCE:**
- Tobacco/pollutants → inflammation (neutrophils, macrophages, lymphocytes) → release elastases, IL-8, oxidants
- Elastin degradation → more inflammation (positive feedback)
- Normally checked by **α1-antitrypsin** (antielastase) and antioxidants
- **>80% of α1-antitrypsin deficiency** patients develop symptomatic **panacinar emphysema**
- MMPs (especially **MMP-9 and MMP-12**) have pathogenic roles
- TGFB gene polymorphisms → reduced mesenchymal repair → inadequate elastin repair
- Key concept: emphysema = **insufficient wound repair** (loss of epithelial, endothelial, AND mesenchymal cells → no ECM scaffold)
-

**Morphology:**
- Panacinar: pale, voluminous lungs; may obscure heart at autopsy
- Centriacinar: deeper pink, less voluminous; upper 2/3 more affected
- Histology: alveolar wall destruction without fibrosis; reduced alveolar capillaries; airway collapse on expiration (loss of elastic tethering)
-

**Clinical:**
- Dyspnea — first symptom, insidious, progressive
- Reduced FEV1 with normal/near-normal FVC; ↓ FEV1/FVC ratio
- **"Pink Puffer"** — pure emphysema: barrel chest, dyspnea, prolonged expiration, hunched forward, low diffusing capacity; adequate oxygenation until late
- **"Blue Bloater"** — emphysema + chronic bronchitis: diminished respiratory drive, CO2 retention, hypoxia, cyanosis, often obese, cor pulmonale + edema
- Secondary pulmonary hypertension → cor pulmonale
- Death: pulmonary failure (respiratory acidosis, hypoxia, coma) OR right heart failure (cor pulmonale)
-

**Related Conditions (NOT true emphysema but called such):**
- **Compensatory emphysema** — alveolar dilation after surgical lung removal (no wall destruction)
- **Obstructive over-inflation** — air trapping by subtotal obstruction (tumor/foreign body) — medical emergency
- **Bullous emphysema** — subpleural blebs/bullae (>1 cm); can rupture → pneumothorax
- **Mediastinal (interstitial) emphysema** — air in connective tissue stroma/mediastinum/subcutaneous tissue; from sudden ↑ intra-alveolar pressure (vomiting, coughing)
-

## 6. CHRONIC BRONCHITIS### MUST KNOWS- **Clinical definition:** Persistent productive cough for **≥3 consecutive months in ≥2 consecutive years**
- Prevalence: 20–25% of men aged 40–65 years
- Major cause: **cigarette smoking**; also SO2, NO2 air pollutants
-

**Pathogenesis:**
- Hallmark: **mucus hypersecretion** beginning in large airways
- Irritants → **hypertrophy of mucous glands** (trachea/main bronchi) + **goblet cell metaplasia** in smaller airways
- Also: CD8+ lymphocytes, macrophages, neutrophils infiltrate (NO eosinophils — unlike asthma)
- **Airflow obstruction** (peripheral/small airways) due to:
-
 1. Small airway disease (goblet cell metaplasia + mucus plugging + bronchiolar wall fibrosis)
2. Coexistent emphysema
- **IL-13** mediates many respiratory epithelial effects
- **MUC5AC** mucin gene transcription increased by tobacco smoke
- Neutrophil elastase production increased by tobacco smoke
- Microbial infection = secondary role (maintains inflammation)
-

**Reid Index:**
- Ratio of submucosal gland thickness to bronchial wall thickness
- Normal: **≤0.4**
- Chronic bronchitis: **>0.4** (gland enlargement)
-

**Morphology:**
- Large airways: hyperemic, edematous mucosa covered by mucinous/mucopurulent secretions
- Histology: enlarged mucus-secreting glands; inflammatory cells (mononuclear ± neutrophils)
- **Chronic bronchiolitis** = goblet cell metaplasia + mucous plugging + inflammation + fibrosis
- Severe: **bronchiolitis obliterans** (complete fibrotic obliteration of lumen)
-

**Clinical:**
- "Blue Bloater" — hypercapnia, hypoxemia, cyanosis
- Progression → pulmonary hypertension, right heart failure
- Recurrent infections + respiratory failure are constant threats
-

## 7. ASTHMA### MUST KNOWS- **Definition:** Chronic inflammatory disorder → **reversible** episodic airway obstruction + wheezing + breathlessness + chest tightness + cough (especially nocturnal/early morning)
- **Hallmarks:** Reversible airway obstruction + chronic eosinophilic bronchial inflammation + bronchial smooth muscle hypertrophy + hyperreactivity + increased mucus secretion
-

**Pathogenesis (Atopic Asthma):**
- **TH2 cells** are central
- IL-4 → **IgE production**
- IL-5 → **eosinophil activation**
- IL-13 → **mucus production** + IgE production by B cells
- IgE coats mast cells → allergen exposure → **two-wave reaction:**
-

**Phase 1 (Early/Immediate):**
- Bronchoconstriction (vagal receptor stimulation) + increased mucus + vasodilation
-

**Phase 2 (Late):**
- Inflammation: eosinophils, neutrophils, T cells activated
- Epithelial cells release chemokines (including **eotaxin** — potent eosinophil chemoattractant)
- Amplifies TH2 cell and eosinophil recruitment
-

**Airway Remodeling (repeated bouts):**
- Smooth muscle hypertrophy + mucus gland enlargement
- ↑ vascularity + subepithelial collagen deposition
-

**Genetics:** Multiple susceptibility genes
- Chromosome **5q**: IL13, CD14, HLA class II, β2-adrenergic receptor gene, IL-4 receptor gene
- Chromosome **20q**: **ADAM-33** (regulates bronchial smooth muscle/fibroblast proliferation → airway remodeling)
- **YKL-40** (chitinase family) serum levels correlate with severity
-

**"Hygiene Hypothesis":** Eradication of infections → altered immune homeostasis → ↑ allergic responses (explains ↑ asthma incidence in Western world)
**4 Types of Asthma:**
| Type | Key Feature |
|------|-------------|
| **Atopic** | Most common; IgE-mediated type I hypersensitivity; positive family history + skin test; triggered by environmental antigens |
| **Non-Atopic** | No allergen sensitization; skin test negative; triggered by viral infections + air pollutants |
| **Drug-Induced** | Aspirin most common — blocks COX → shifts to lipoxygenase → leukotrienes → bronchospasm; also nasal polyps + urticaria |
| **Occupational** | Fumes, organic/chemical dusts, gases; repeated exposure required |
**Morphology (Status Asthmaticus/Fatal Cases):**
- Overdistended lungs; small areas of atelectasis
- **Bronchi/bronchioles occluded by thick mucous plugs**
- Histology: **Curschmann spirals** (whorls of shed epithelium in mucus)
- **Charcot-Leyden crystals** (eosinophil protein crystalloids)
- **Airway remodeling:** thickened airway wall, sub-basement membrane fibrosis, ↑ vascularity, enlarged submucosal glands, goblet cell metaplasia, smooth muscle hypertrophy/hyperplasia
- **Bronchial thermoplasty:** controlled thermal energy via bronchoscopy → reduces smooth muscle mass → reduces airway hyperresponsiveness
-

**Clinical:**
- Chief difficulty in **expiration** (bronchi constricted + filled with mucus)
- Progressive hyperinflation with trapped air
- **Status asthmaticus:** prolonged attack unresponsive to therapy (days/weeks) → hypercapnia + acidosis + severe hypoxia → can be fatal
-

## 8. BRONCHIECTASIS### MUST KNOWS- **Definition:** Permanent dilation of bronchi/bronchioles from destruction of muscle and elastic tissue due to chronic necrotizing infections
- NOT a primary disease — always secondary
-

**Predisposing Conditions:**
- Bronchial obstruction (tumor, foreign body, mucus impaction, atopic asthma, chronic bronchitis)
- Congenital/hereditary:
-
 - **Cystic fibrosis** — most important (viscid mucus → obstruction → infection)
- Immunodeficiency states (especially **immunoglobulin deficiency**)
- **Kartagener syndrome** — rare autosomal recessive; structural ciliary abnormalities → impaired mucociliary clearance + infections + male sterility (immotile spermatozoa)
- Necrotizing pneumonia (S. aureus, Klebsiella) — post-TB bronchiectasis significant in endemic areas
-

**Pathogenesis — Two Intertwined Processes:**
- **Obstruction** + **chronic persistent infection** (either can come first)
- Obstruction → impaired clearance → secondary infection → inflammatory damage → airway dilation (irreversible)
- Chronic necrotizing inflammation → obstructive secretions → peribronchial fibrosis + traction → dilation
-

**Morphology:**
- Usually **lower lobes bilaterally** (most vertical airways)
- Localized = tumor/foreign body
- Airways dilated up to **4x normal diameter** — can be traced almost to pleural surface (normal bronchioles not visible within 2–3 cm of pleura)
- Histology (active): intense acute + chronic inflammatory exudate in bronchial/bronchiolar walls + epithelial desquamation/ulceration
- Mixed flora: staphylococci, streptococci, pneumococci, enteric organisms, anaerobes, H. influenzae, **Pseudomonas aeruginosa** (especially in children)
- Chronic: fibrosis of bronchial/bronchiolar walls; peribronchiolar fibrosis; abscess cavities
-

**Clinical:**
- Severe persistent cough + copious mucopurulent (sometimes fetid) sputum
- Hemoptysis (flecks or frank)
- Episodic, precipitated by URTI or new pathogens
- **Digital clubbing**
- Severe/widespread: obstructive ventilatory defects + hypoxemia + hypercapnia + pulmonary hypertension + cor pulmonale
- Complications: **metastatic brain abscesses** + **reactive amyloidosis**
-

## 9. CHRONIC INTERSTITIAL (RESTRICTIVE) LUNG DISEASES### OVERVIEW MUST KNOWS- Heterogeneous group; bilateral, often patchy, chronic involvement of pulmonary connective tissue (alveolar walls)
- Hallmark: **reduced compliance** (stiff lungs → more pressure needed to expand → dyspnea)
- Alveolar epithelial + interstitial vascular damage → V/Q mismatch → hypoxia
- CXR: diffuse infiltration — small nodules, irregular lines, "ground-glass shadows"
- End-stage: **"honeycomb lung"** (fibrosis + gross destruction)
-

### IDIOPATHIC PULMONARY FIBROSIS (IPF) — MUST KNOWS- Also called: **Cryptogenic Fibrosing Alveolitis**
- Males > females; **~2/3 patients >60 years** at presentation
- Patchy progressive bilateral interstitial fibrosis → severe hypoxemia + cyanosis
- Histologic/radiologic pattern = **Usual Interstitial Pneumonia (UIP)** — required for IPF diagnosis
- Must exclude known causes (asbestosis, collagen vascular diseases) before calling it "idiopathic"
-

**Pathogenesis:**
- Repeated cycles of epithelial injury (unknown agent)
- TH2 response with eosinophils, mast cells, IL-4, IL-13
- "Alternatively activated macrophages" important
- Abnormal repair → exuberant fibroblastic/myofibroblastic proliferation → **fibroblastic foci** (hallmark early lesion)
- **TGF-β1** (from injured type I pneumocytes) → fibroblast → myofibroblast transformation → excess collagen + ECM
- TGF-β1 also downregulates **caveolin-1** (endogenous inhibitor of fibrosis)
- Familial IPF: mutations shortening **telomeres** → rapid pneumocyte senescence/apoptosis
-

**Morphology:**
- Gross: cobblestone pleural surface (retraction along interlobular septa)
- Lower lobe predominance + subpleural/interlobular septal distribution
- Histologic hallmark: **patchy interstitial fibrosis with temporal heterogeneity** (early fibroblastic foci + late collagenous scars in same biopsy)
- **Honeycomb fibrosis:** cystic spaces lined by hyperplastic type II pneumocytes or bronchiolar epithelium
- Secondary pulmonary hypertensive changes
-

**Clinical:**
- Insidious onset: nonproductive cough + progressive dyspnea
- Characteristic: **"Velcro" crackles** on inspiration
- Late: cyanosis, cor pulmonale, peripheral edema
- Prognosis: **mean survival 3 years or less** — relentless progression despite therapy
- Only definitive therapy: **lung transplantation**
-

### NONSPECIFIC INTERSTITIAL PNEUMONIA (NSIP) — MUST KNOWS- Chronic bilateral ILD; unknown etiology
- Important: **much better prognosis than IPF**
- Two patterns:
-
 - **Cellular pattern:** mild-moderate chronic interstitial inflammation (lymphocytes + few plasma cells); uniform/patchy; better outcome
- **Fibrosing pattern:** diffuse/patchy interstitial fibrosis; NO temporal heterogeneity (unlike UIP); fibroblastic foci and honeycombing absent
- Presentation: dyspnea + cough over months
-

### CRYPTOGENIC ORGANIZING PNEUMONIA (COP) — MUST KNOWS- Previously called: **BOOP** (Bronchiolitis Obliterans Organizing Pneumonia) — now COP preferred
- CXR: subpleural or peribronchial patchy areas of air-space consolidation
- Histology: **polypoid plugs of loose organizing connective tissue** within alveolar ducts, alveoli, and bronchioles; same age (no temporal heterogeneity); normal underlying lung architecture
- Treatment: oral steroids for 6+ months; some resolve spontaneously
- Note: organizing pneumonia can also occur secondary to infection, collagen vascular disease, transplant injury (then NOT cryptogenic)
-

### PNEUMOCONIOSES — MUST KNOWS**Pathogenesis principles:**
- Particles 1–5 µm diameter = most dangerous (lodge at distal airway bifurcations)
- >5–10 µm = don't reach distal airways; <0.5 µm = move in/out without deposition
- Alveolar macrophage = key cell (inflammasome activation → IL-1 production → inflammation → fibrosis)
- Tobacco smoking worsens ALL inhaled mineral dusts (most so with **asbestos**)
-

**Coal Worker's Pneumoconiosis (CWP):**
- Spectrum: asymptomatic anthracosis → simple CWP → complicated CWP (**Progressive Massive Fibrosis/PMF**)
- <10% of simple CWP progresses to PMF
- Simple CWP: coal macules (dust-laden macrophages + delicate collagen) + coal nodules; upper lobes
- PMF: multiple intensely black scars **>2 cm** (up to 10 cm); dense collagen + pigment
- CWP: benign disease; PMF → pulmonary dysfunction + pulmonary hypertension + cor pulmonale
- **No increased lung carcinoma risk** in coal miners (unlike silica + asbestos)
-

**Silicosis:**
- Most prevalent **chronic occupational disease in the world**
- Crystalline silica (quartz most common); sandblasting + hard-rock mining highest risk
- Crystalline forms (quartz, cristobalite, tridymite) most toxic; amorphous forms less so
- Mechanism: macrophage activation → IL-1, TNF, fibronectin, oxygen free radicals, fibrogenic cytokines
- TNF key: anti-TNF antibodies block silica-induced fibrosis in mice
- Morphology: silicotic nodules — **concentrically arranged hyalinized collagen** with amorphous center; **"whorled" collagen** (distinctive); weakly birefringent silica particles by polarized microscopy
- Advanced: nodule coalescence → PMF; hilar lymph node "**eggshell calcification**"
- Clinical: detected on CXR (upper zone fine nodularity) before symptoms; PMF → pulmonary hypertension + cor pulmonale
- Key associations: **↑ susceptibility to tuberculosis** (silicosis depresses cell-mediated immunity + macrophages can't kill mycobacteria); **silicotuberculosis** nodules have central caseation
- Relationship with lung cancer: contentious/unresolved
-

**Asbestosis & Asbestos-Related Diseases:**
Asbestos exposure linked to:
- **Asbestosis** (parenchymal interstitial fibrosis)
- Pleural plaques / diffuse pleural fibrosis
- Pleural effusions
- **Lung carcinoma** (5x increased risk)
- **Malignant mesothelioma** (>1000x increased risk — normally 2–17/million)
- Laryngeal carcinoma
-

**2 forms of asbestos:**
- **Serpentine (chrysotile):** curly, flexible; most used in industry; impacted in upper respiratory tract; gradually leached from tissues; less pathogenic
- **Amphibole:** straight, stiff, brittle; less prevalent but **more pathogenic**; align in airstream → deeper lung penetration → stay longer (less soluble)
- Both forms cause asbestosis + lung cancer + mesothelioma
-

**Asbestos bodies:** golden-brown, fusiform/beaded rods with translucent center; asbestos fibers coated with iron-containing proteinaceous material (from phagocyte **ferritin**)
**Morphology of asbestosis:**
- Diffuse pulmonary interstitial fibrosis indistinguishable from UIP — except for **asbestos bodies**
- Starts **lower lobes + subpleural** (opposite of CWP/silicosis which are upper lobe)
- Progresses → honeycombing + pulmonary hypertension + cor pulmonale
- **Pleural plaques:** most common manifestation of asbestos exposure; well-circumscribed dense collagen, often calcified; anterior/posterolateral parietal pleura + diaphragm; NO asbestos bodies in plaques; usually asymptomatic
-

**Smoking + asbestos = remarkable synergy for lung carcinoma** (smoking does NOT increase mesothelioma risk)

### SARCOIDOSIS — MUST KNOWS- Multisystem disease of **unknown etiology**; noncaseating granulomas in many tissues
- Diagnosis of **exclusion** (must rule out mycobacterial/fungal infections, berylliosis)
- Bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy = major presenting manifestation
- Eye and skin involvement: ~25% each
-

**Epidemiology:**
- All races, ages, both genders; adults <40 years predominate
- High incidence: Danish/Swedish populations + **African-Americans** (10x higher than whites)
- Higher prevalence in **non-smokers** (rare among lung diseases)
-

**Pathogenesis:**
- Disordered immune regulation; genetically predisposed + environmental agents
- **CD4+ TH1 cell-driven** response
- Key abnormalities:
-
 - ↑ CD4+ TH1 cells in alveoli and interstitium
- ↑ IL-2 and IFN-γ (T cell expansion + macrophage activation)
- ↑ IL-8, TNF, MIP-1α (granuloma formation)
- **Anergy** to skin test antigens (PPD, Candida) — CD4+ T cells recruited to lung
- Polyclonal hypergammaglobulinemia
- Genetic: HLA class I (HLA-A1, HLA-B8) association
- Sarcoidosis **recurs in transplanted lungs in 75% of patients**
-

**Morphology:**
- **Noncaseating epithelioid granuloma** = diagnostic hallmark (any organ)
- Compact collection of epithelioid cells (abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm, vesicular nuclei) rimmed by CD4+ T cells
- Multinucleate giant cells common (macrophage fusion)
- Peripheral fibroblastic layer → eventual hyalinized scar
- Special inclusions (NOT required for diagnosis):
-
 - **Schaumann bodies:** laminated calcium + protein concretions
- **Asteroid bodies:** stellate inclusions in giant cells
- **Rarely:** central foci of necrosis (NOT caseation — no caseating necrosis)
- Lungs: 90% involved; granulomas in interstitium with "lymphangitic" distribution (around bronchioles, pulmonary venules, pleura)
- 5–15% → **diffuse interstitial fibrosis** ("honeycomb lung")
- Hilar + paratracheal lymph nodes enlarged in **75–90%** of patients; lymph nodes are "**nonmatted**" (nonadherent, don't ulcerate) — unlike TB
- Skin: **Erythema nodosum** = hallmark of acute sarcoidosis (raised, red, tender nodules on anterior legs); sarcoidal granulomas uncommon in these
- Eye: iritis/iridocyclitis → corneal opacities, glaucoma, vision loss; lacrimal gland involvement → **sicca syndrome**
- **Mikulicz syndrome:** combined uveoparotid involvement
- **Hypercalcemia + hypercalciuria:** not from bone destruction — from **active vitamin D production by mononuclear phagocytes** in granulomas (increased calcium absorption)
-

**Clinical:**
- Many asymptomatic (found on CXR or autopsy)
- Symptomatic: respiratory symptoms (dyspnea, dry cough, substernal discomfort) OR constitutional (fever, fatigue, weight loss, night sweats)
- Diagnosis: noncaseating granuloma on biopsy (after exclusion of other causes)
- Course: unpredictable; spontaneous remissions common; steroid therapy
- **65–70% recover** with minimal/no residual disease
- 20% → permanent lung dysfunction/visual impairment
- 10–15% → progressive pulmonary fibrosis + cor pulmonale (die)
-

## 10. PULMONARY VASCULAR DISEASES### PULMONARY EMBOLISM — MUST KNOWS- **>95% of pulmonary emboli** arise from thrombi in **deep veins of lower legs** (popliteal + larger veins above)
- Among hospitalized patients: <1/3 diagnosed before death
-

**Risk factors:**
- Prolonged bed rest (immobilized legs)
- Orthopedic surgery (knee/hip)
- Severe trauma (burns, multiple fractures)
- Congestive heart failure
- Peri-parturition / oral contraceptives (high-estrogen)
- Disseminated cancer (Virchow's triad)
-

**Consequences depend on:** size of embolus + cardiopulmonary status
**Two key consequences of pulmonary arterial occlusion:**
- ↑ pulmonary artery pressure (blockage + vasospasm from thromboxane A2, serotonin)
- Ischemia of downstream parenchyma
-

**Pulmonary infarction:** only in **10% of patients** (because of dual blood supply: pulmonary arteries + bronchial arteries + direct from alveolar air)
- Occurs when: compromised cardiac function OR compromised bronchial circulation OR underlying pulmonary disease
-

**Morphology:**
- Large embolus → saddle embolus at bifurcation → instant death (acute cor pulmonale/hypoxia)
- Smaller emboli → medium/small pulmonary arteries
- Infarcts: **wedge-shaped** (base at pleura, apex toward hilum); **hemorrhagic**; raised, red-blue early → pales → red-brown (hemosiderin) → fibrotic scar
- **~3/4 infarcts affect lower lobes**; >half are **multiple**
- Fibrinous exudate on adjacent pleura
-

**Clinical outcomes (4 patterns):**
- **60–80%** clinically silent (small, removed by fibrinolysis)
- **5%** sudden death/acute cor pulmonale/shock (>60% vasculature obstructed)
- **10–15%** pulmonary infarction (medium vessel + cardiac insufficiency present) → dyspnea
- **<3%** recurrent multiple emboli → pulmonary hypertension + chronic cor pulmonale + pulmonary vascular sclerosis
-

- Patients with one PE have **30% chance of developing a second**
- Treatment: anticoagulation; massive PE → **thrombolytic therapy**
-

**Nonthrombotic emboli:** air, fat, amniotic fluid; IV drug abuse → **talc granulomatosis** → interstitial fibrosis + pulmonary hypertension

### PULMONARY HYPERTENSION — MUST KNOWS- Normal pulmonary pressure = ~1/8 of systemic
- Pulmonary hypertension: mean pulmonary pressure ≥1/4 of systemic
-

**Secondary PH causes:**
- COPD/interstitial lung disease → loss of alveolar capillaries → ↑ vascular resistance
- Recurrent pulmonary emboli → reduced functional cross-sectional area
- Antecedent heart disease (mitral stenosis → ↑ left atrial pressure → PH); left-to-right shunts
-

**Primary (Idiopathic) Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension:**
- All known causes excluded
- **Majority sporadic; 6% familial** (autosomal dominant)
-

**Pathogenesis:**
- Endothelial/smooth muscle dysfunction → ↓ vasodilators (NO, prostacyclin) + ↑ vasoconstrictors (endothelin) + growth factors → smooth muscle proliferation
- Familial: **BMPR-2** (bone morphogenetic protein receptor type 2) germline mutations in **50% of familial cases**; BMPR-2 normally inhibits proliferation; loss-of-function → abnormal endothelial + smooth muscle proliferation
- Sporadic: **serotonin transporter gene (5HTT)** polymorphisms → enhanced vascular smooth muscle proliferation
-

**Morphology — affects entire arterial tree:**
- Atheromas in main elastic arteries
- Intimal + medial thickening → narrowed lumina (medium arteries)
- Medial hypertrophy + elastic membrane reduplication in small arteries/arterioles
- **Plexiform lesions** (idiopathic PAH): endothelial proliferation forming multiple lumina in small arteries
-

**Clinical:**
- Primary PAH: young adults, **women > men**; fatigue, syncope on exercise, dyspnea on exertion, chest pain
- Death from **right heart failure (decompensated cor pulmonale)** within **2–5 years**
- Treatment: vasodilators, antithrombotics, prostacyclin infusions; definitive = lung transplant
-

### GOODPASTURE SYNDROME — MUST KNOWS- Prototype diffuse alveolar hemorrhage syndrome
- Triad: **hemoptysis + anemia + diffuse pulmonary infiltrates**
- Also: rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis
- Pathogenesis: antibodies against **noncollagenous domain of α3 chain of collagen IV** (affects lung + kidney basement membranes)
- Anti-GBM antibodies detectable in serum of **>90% of cases**
- Morphology: diffuse alveolar hemorrhage; hemosiderin (intra- and extracellular); **linear IgG deposition along alveolar septa** (also along glomerular BM — hallmark on renal biopsy)
- Treatment: **plasmapheresis** (removes antibodies) + **immunosuppressive drugs** (inhibit antibody production)
- Severe renal disease → renal transplantation
-

## 11. PNEUMONIA### CLASSIFICATION MUST KNOWS| Setting | Key Pathogens |

| Nosocomial (hospital-acquired) | Gram-negative rods (Klebsiella, Serratia, E. coli), Pseudomonas, **MRSA** |

| Immunocompromised | CMV, Pneumocystis jiroveci, MAC, Aspergillus, Candida, plus usual organisms |
| Chronic | Nocardia, Actinomyces, Mycobacteria, Histoplasma, Coccidioides, Blastomyces |
**Special associations:**
- **K. pneumoniae** → chronic alcoholics
- **P. aeruginosa** → cystic fibrosis, burn victims, neutropenia
- **L. pneumophila** → organ transplant recipients
- **S. aureus pneumonia** → usually secondary to viral respiratory infections
-

### LOBAR vs. BRONCHOPNEUMONIA| Feature | Lobar | Bronchopneumonia |
|---------|-------|-----------------|
| Distribution | Contiguous air spaces of part/all of a lobe | Patchy, multiple lobes |
| Organism | S. pneumoniae >90% | Various organisms |
| Age | Any | More common at extremes of age |
**4 Stages of Lobar Pneumonia (pre-antibiotic era):**
- **Congestion:** heavy, red, boggy lung; vascular congestion + protein-rich fluid + neutrophils + many bacteria in alveoli
- **Red hepatization:** liver-like consistency; alveoli packed with neutrophils + RBCs + fibrin
- **Gray hepatization:** dry, gray, firm; RBCs lysed; fibrinosuppurative exudate persists
- **Resolution:** enzymatic digestion of exudate → granular semifluid debris → resorption/macrophage ingestion/coughed up/organized by fibroblasts; pleural reaction may → fibrous thickening/adhesions
-

**Complications of pneumococcal pneumonia** (especially serotype 3):
- Abscess formation (tissue necrosis)
- **Empyema** (suppurative pleural accumulation)
- Organization → solid fibrous tissue
- Bacteremic dissemination → meningitis, arthritis, infective endocarditis
-

**Atypical Pneumonia:**
- Respiratory distress **out of proportion** to clinical/radiologic signs
- Inflammation predominantly confined to **alveolar septa** (interstitial); alveolar spaces generally clear
- Septa widened + edematous; mononuclear infiltrate (lymphocytes, histiocytes, plasma cells)
- Severe cases → diffuse alveolar damage with hyaline membranes
- Macroscopically: red-blue, congested, subcrepitant areas
-

## 12. TUBERCULOSIS### MUST KNOWS- Caused by **Mycobacterium tuberculosis**; M. tuberculosis complex includes M. africanum, M. canettii, M. bovis, others
- MDR strains exist
- Globally: ~10 million new TB cases/year; 1.4 million deaths; **India accounts for 26%** of global cases
-

**Pathogenesis:**
- Inhalation → alveolar macrophages encounter bacteria
- If not eliminated → invasion of lung interstitium (direct or via infected macrophages)
- Dendritic cells transport MTB to lymph nodes → T cell priming → granuloma formation
- Granuloma may contain infection (**latent TB**)
- If granuloma fails → dissemination → bloodstream → other organs → re-entry into respiratory tract → **active TB**
-

**Primary TB:** first infection in uninfected host
**Secondary (reactivation) TB:** reactivation of latent TB
**Miliary TB:** hematogenous dissemination to lungs + other organs
**Morphology:**
- **Ghon focus:** subpleural nodule, often upper lobe
- **Ghon complex:** Ghon focus + lymphatic/hilar lymph node involvement
- **Ranke complex:** calcified Ghon complex (calcified Ghon lesion + calcified mediastinal lymph nodes) — represents old/healed primary TB
- **Caseous necrosis:** cheese-like, tan-to-white grumous material — pathognomonic
- Histologic hallmark: **necrotizing granulomatous inflammation** — central necrotic zone surrounded by epithelioid histiocytes + **Langhans giant cells** (nuclei in horseshoe pattern at cell periphery) + lymphocytes
- Langhans giant cells are NOT specific for TB (also seen in other granulomas)
- Organisms in central necrosis zone — seen on **special stains** (Ziehl-Neelsen/acid-fast)
- Complications: empyema, bronchiectasis, cavitary disease, tuberculomas
-

**Clinical features:**
- Chronic productive cough, weight loss, fever, **night sweats**, hemoptysis
- History of latent TB or exposure
-

## 13. LUNG CANCER### MUST KNOWS — VERY HIGH YIELD**4 Major Histologic Subtypes:**
| Type | Key Features |
|------|-------------|
| **Adenocarcinoma** | Most common overall; most common in **women + nonsmokers**; EGFR mutations + ALK mutations targetable |
| **Squamous Cell Carcinoma** | Strongly linked to smoking; central location; precursor = squamous dysplasia |
| **Large Cell Carcinoma** | Diagnosis of exclusion; aggressive |
| **Small Cell Carcinoma (SCLC)** | Neuroendocrine; almost always metastatic at presentation; **treat with chemotherapy**; NOT surgically resectable; very sensitive to chemo but invariably recurs; median survival **1 year** even with treatment |
- **Smoking = most important risk factor**
- NSCLCs (non-small cell) — may be curable by surgery if localized; combination chemo + targeted therapy (anti-EGFR for EGFR-mutant adenocarcinoma; ALK inhibitors for ALK-mutant)
-

**Precursor lesions:**
- **Squamous dysplasia** → squamous cell carcinoma
- **Atypical adenomatous hyperplasia** → some adenocarcinomas
- **Adenocarcinoma in situ** (formerly bronchioloalveolar carcinoma) → some adenocarcinomas; pure lepidic growth ≤3 cm without stromal invasion
-

**Symptoms:**
- Chronic cough, expectoration, hoarseness, chest pain
- Superior vena cava syndrome
- Pericardial/pleural effusion
- Persistent segmental atelectasis, pneumonitis
- Metastatic: brain (mental/neurologic changes), liver (hepatomegaly), bones (pain)
- SCLCs especially cause **paraneoplastic syndromes**
-

**Paraneoplastic Syndromes (3–10% of lung cancer patients):**
| Syndrome | Association |
|---------|-------------|
| Hypercalcemia (PTHrP secretion) | **Squamous cell carcinoma** most common |
| Cushing syndrome (ACTH) | SCLC most common |
| SIADH | SCLC most common |
| Neuromuscular (myasthenic syndrome, peripheral neuropathy, polymyositis) | SCLC |
| Clubbing + hypertrophic pulmonary osteoarthropathy | SCLC |
| Coagulation abnormalities (migratory thrombophlebitis, nonbacterial endocarditis, DIC) | Adenocarcinoma (hematologic syndromes) |
- Hypercalcemia from PTHrP = paraneoplastic (osteolytic metastases causing hypercalcemia = NOT paraneoplastic)
- Overall: **NSCLCs better prognosis than SCLCs**
-

## 14. PLEURAL LESIONS### MUST KNOWS**Pleural Effusion (≥15 cc fluid):**
- Causes: ↑ hydrostatic pressure (CHF), ↑ vascular permeability (pneumonia), ↓ oncotic pressure (nephrotic syndrome), ↑ intrapleural negative pressure (atelectasis), ↓ lymphatic drainage (carcinomatosis)
- Malignant effusions: usually **>500 mL**; often first evidence of malignancy
- For lung, breast, ovarian metastases: **92% ipsilateral** to primary lesion
-

**Types:**
- **Inflammatory:** serous/serofibrinous/fibrinous — from TB, pneumonia, infarct, abscess, bronchiectasis, collagen vascular disease, radiation
- **Empyema:** pus in pleural cavity; bacterial/fungal; usually from lung infection; organizes into dense adhesions (doesn't disappear)
- **Hydrothorax:** clear/straw-colored; CHF; not loculated, collects basally
- **Hemothorax:** aneurysm rupture/vascular trauma; large clots
- **Chylothorax:** milky white fluid (lymph + fat); usually **left-sided**; thoracic duct trauma/obstruction by malignancy
-

**Pneumothorax:**
- All types → compression atelectasis + respiratory distress
- **Spontaneous idiopathic** (young people): rupture of small peripheral apical subpleural blebs; usually resolves but may recur
- **Tension pneumothorax:** one-way valve → traps air during inhalation → compresses contralateral lung + mediastinal structures → emergency
-

**Malignant Mesothelioma:**
- Primary pleural malignancy
- Linked to **asbestos exposure** (>1000x increased risk vs. general population)
- Grim prognosis
-

## Practice Questions- A 55-year-old male presents with a chronic cough productive of purulent sputum for the past 3 months each year for the last 5 years. He has a significant smoking history. Which of the following is the most defining characteristic of chronic bronchitis in terms of airway pathology?
-
→ Hypertrophy of submucosal mucous glands and goblet cell metaplasia.

- A patient undergoing a routine chest X-ray is found to have bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy and small nodules scattered throughout the lung parenchyma. The patient is a non-smoker with no known occupational exposures. Biopsy reveals noncaseating granulomas. What is the most likely diagnosis?
-
→ Sarcoidosis.

- A 60-year-old coal miner presents with progressive dyspnea and a cough. Chest X-ray shows upper lobe nodularity and larger opacities consistent with progressive massive fibrosis. Which of the following is true regarding Coal Worker's Pneumoconiosis (CWP)?
-
→ CWP itself does not increase the risk of lung cancer.

- A patient with a history of recent orthopedic surgery develops sudden onset of dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, and tachycardia. A ventilation-perfusion scan is highly suggestive of pulmonary embolism. What is the most common source of these pulmonary emboli?
-
→ Deep veins of the lower legs.

- A young adult presents with hemoptysis, anemia, and diffuse pulmonary infiltrates on chest X-ray. Workup also reveals rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis. Immunofluorescence microscopy of a lung biopsy shows linear deposition of IgG along the alveolar septa. What is the most likely diagnosis?
-
→ Goodpasture syndrome.

- A patient with a history of heavy smoking is diagnosed with emphysema. Histologic examination reveals destruction of alveolar walls primarily in the respiratory bronchioles in the upper lobes, with relative sparing of the distal alveoli. Which type of emphysema is this?
-
→ Centriacinar (Centrilobular) emphysema.

- A 40-year-old female presents with wheezing, shortness of breath, and cough, particularly at night. Spirometry shows reversible airway obstruction and bronchial hyperreactivity. Histological examination of a fatal case might show which of the following findings?
-
→ Thickened basement membrane, eosinophilic infiltration, smooth muscle hypertrophy, and Curschmann spirals.

- A 70-year-old male with a history of asbestos exposure develops progressive interstitial fibrosis predominantly in the lower lobes of his lungs. Microscopic examination reveals interstitial fibrosis and characteristic asbestos bodies. What is the most likely diagnosis?
-
→ Asbestosis.
