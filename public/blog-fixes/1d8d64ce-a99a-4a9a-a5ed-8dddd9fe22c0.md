## TOPIC 1: INTRODUCTION TO MYCOLOGY (Lecture 1)

**Q1. Write a detailed account of the general properties and classification of fungi.**
General Properties:
- Eukaryotic — cells contain membrane-bound organelles
- Have a rigid cell wall made of chitin
- Lack chlorophyll — cannot photosynthesize
- Chemoheterotrophs — require organic compounds for carbon and energy
- Have ergosterols in their membranes — regulate permeability and fluidity
- Possess 80S ribosomes
- No obligate anaerobes — all require water and oxygen
- Obtain nutrients as saprophytes (decaying matter) or parasites (living matter)
- Reproduce asexually and/or sexually by producing spores
- Food storage in the form of lipids and glycogen
- Grow reproductively by budding or non-reproductively by hyphal tip elongation
Classification based on Sexual Reproduction:
- Zygomycetes — sexual spores called zygospores
- Ascomycetes — sexual spores called ascospores, produced in asci
- Basidiomycetes — sexual spores called basidiospores, produced on basidia
- Deuteromycetes (Fungi Imperfecti) — no known sexual spores demonstrated
Classification based on Morphology:
- Moulds — filamentous; made of hyphae; e.g. Aspergillus
- Yeasts — unicellular; reproduce by budding; e.g. Cryptococcus
- Yeast-like — produce pseudohyphae; e.g. Candida albicans
- Dimorphic — exist in two forms depending on temperature; yeast at 37°C, mould at 25°C; e.g. Histoplasma capsulatum

**Q2. Describe the morphology of moulds, yeasts, and dimorphic fungi.**
Moulds:
- Made of cylindrical tube-like structures called hyphae
- Mass of hyphae = mycelium
- Hyphae can be branched or unbranched
- Septate hyphae — have cross walls (septa) with pores; cytoplasm is continuous; damage to one compartment does not kill the whole strand
- Aseptate hyphae — no septa; more primitive; damage kills the entire strand
- All moulds are septate except Zygomycetes (Rhizopus, Mucor)
- All fungal hyphae tend to be coenocytic (multinucleate)
Yeasts:
- Unicellular; spherical or ellipsoid
- Reproduce by budding — forms blastospores (blastoconidia)
- Buds that fail to detach elongate to form pseudohyphae (e.g. Candida albicans)
- Candida albicans can also produce true hyphae (germ tube)
- Some yeasts produce a capsule e.g. Cryptococcus, Blastomyces
- True yeasts like Saccharomyces do not produce pseudohyphae
Dimorphic Fungi:
- Exist as yeast in infected tissue and when cultured at 37°C
- Exist as mould in natural habitat and when cultured at 25°C
- Examples: Blastomyces dermatitidis, Histoplasma species, Paracoccidioides brasiliensis, Coccidioides immitis

**Q3. Describe the types of asexual spores produced by fungi.**
- Conidia/Conidiospores — small single-celled microconidia or large multicellular macroconidia; formed at the tip of hyphae
- Sporangiospores — single-celled spores found within a sac called a sporangium at the tip of a sporangiophore
- Arthrospores — spores formed from fragmented hyphae
- Chlamydospores — spores with very thick walls; resistant forms
- Blastoconidia (Blastospores) — formed through a budding process; seen in yeasts

## TOPIC 2: FUNGAL PATHOGENESIS (Lecture 2)

**Q4. Describe the pathogenesis of fungal infections, including virulence factors and host defenses.**
Pathogenesis:
- Most fungi are saprophytic; infection in humans is a chance event when conditions are favourable
- Except for dimorphic fungi and dermatophytes (primary pathogens), most fungi are opportunistic
- The human body is generally hostile to fungi
- Interplay between fungal virulence factors and host defenses determines whether disease occurs
- Infection depends on inoculum size and host immunity
Virulence Factors of Fungi:
- Adhesion to host cells via cell wall glycoproteins
- Capsule production — resists phagocytosis (e.g. Cryptococcus)
- Secretion of enzymes — keratinase, elastase, collagenase — damage host tissue
- Ability to acquire iron from red blood cells (e.g. Candida albicans)
- Resistance to killing by phagocytes (e.g. dimorphic fungi)
- Secretion of mycotoxins
- Thermal dimorphism — allows survival and pathogenicity at body temperature
- Blocking cell-mediated immune defenses
Host Defense Factors:
- Physical barriers — skin and mucous membranes
- Fatty acid content of the skin — antifungal
- pH of skin, mucosal surfaces and body fluids
- Epithelial cell turnover — removes colonizing fungi
- Normal flora — competes with fungi, keeps them commensal
- Chemical barriers — secretions, serum factors
- Body temperature 37°C — most fungi are mesophilic and cannot grow at this temperature
- Phagocytic cells — PMNs (effector cells) and macrophages/monocytes (professional phagocytes)

**Q5. Describe immunity to fungal infections (innate and acquired).**
Innate (Non-specific) Immunity:
- Physical barriers — skin and mucous membranes
- pH and body temperature
- Serum factors
- Normal flora
- Phagocytic cells — PMNs, monocytes and macrophages
Acquired Immunity:
- Both humoral and cell-mediated immunity (CMI) are mounted
- CMI is the mainstay of host defense because fungi reside intracellularly
Cell-Mediated Immunity (CMI):
- Non-specific arm — PMNs and macrophages
- Specific arm — T-lymphocytes
- Granuloma formation — response to intracellular fungi represents delayed hypersensitivity
Humoral Immunity:
- Antibodies are produced but their role in protection is not very clear
- Antibodies assist via opsonization — important against Candida and Cryptococcus
- Complement acts as opsonin and can cause direct cell damage through the lytic pathway
- Antibodies are important for serodiagnosis of fungal infections
Hypersensitivity:
- Dermatophyte infections can produce "id reactions" — fungus-free skin lesions at distant sites due to hypersensitivity
- Kerion — inflamed boggy scalp lesion from strong immune response to dermatophyte
- Fungal spores are significant allergens — can cause allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA), farmer's lung, bronchial asthma

**Q6. Discuss the predisposing factors to fungal infections and the classification of mycoses.**
Predisposing Factors:
- Prolonged antibiotic therapy — disrupts normal flora, allows fungal overgrowth
- Underlying diseases — HIV/AIDS, cancer, diabetes, obesity
- Extremes of age
- Surgical procedures — compromise epithelial barriers
- Transplants and immunosuppressive drugs — reduce host immunity
- Irradiation therapy
- Indwelling catheters — bypass epithelial defenses
- Drug addiction; certain occupations
Classification of Mycoses:
- Superficial mycoses — outermost skin layer; no cellular response; cosmetic only; e.g. Pityriasis versicolor, Tinea nigra
- Cutaneous mycoses — invade keratinized tissues; evoke cellular immune response; caused by dermatophytes; e.g. Tinea pedis, Tinea capitis
- Subcutaneous mycoses — involve deeper skin and subcutaneous tissues
- Systemic (deep) mycoses — infect internal organs; caused by primary pathogens like dimorphic fungi
- Opportunistic mycoses — infect immunocompromised patients; caused by organisms like Candida, Aspergillus, Cryptococcus

## TOPIC 3: FUNGAL INFECTIONS / MYCOSES (Lecture 3)

**Q7. Write a detailed account of cutaneous mycoses (dermatophytoses), including their causative agents, clinical features, and laboratory diagnosis.**
Causative Agents — Dermatophytes:
- Keratinophilic fungi — love keratin (major protein in nails, hair, skin)
- Use keratin as nitrogen source; produce keratinases to break it down
- Three genera:
  - Microsporum — hair and skin; rarely nails; children mostly; large rough-walled spindle-shaped macroconidia
  - Trichophyton — hair, skin and nails; children and adults; large smooth pencil-shaped macroconidia; abundant microconidia
  - Epidermophyton — skin and nails; adults; smooth club-shaped macroconidia in clusters; no microconidia
Ecological Classification:
- Anthropophilic — humans only; person-to-person contact; e.g. T. rubrum
- Zoophilic — from animals to humans; e.g. M. canis
- Geophilic — from soil to humans; e.g. M. gypseum
Clinical Forms (named by site):
- Tinea capitis — scalp
- Tinea pedis — feet (most prevalent; starts between toes; vesicles, maceration, peeling; prone to secondary bacterial infection)
- Tinea corporis — body; annular ring-like lesions with active border
- Tinea cruris — groin (jock itch)
- Tinea unguium — nails (onychomycosis)
- Tinea barbae — beard
Laboratory Diagnosis:
- Specimen: skin scales, nail and hair clippings
- Wood's lamp examination — green/yellow fluorescence in infected hair
- Direct microscopy with KOH (10–20%) — branching hyphae; ectothrix or endothrix spore arrangement in hair; arthrospores in skin and nails
- Culture on SDA — identification by colony morphology; microscopy with LPCB stain

**Q8. Discuss Candida infections — pathogenesis, clinical manifestations, and laboratory diagnosis.**
The Organism:
- Candida albicans is the most common species
- Normal flora of skin, mouth, GIT and female genital tract
- Opportunistic — causes disease when host defenses are compromised
Pathogenesis:
- Adhesion to mucosal cells via mannoproteins (adhesins)
- Dimorphism — shift from yeast to hyphal form associated with pathogenicity
- Hyphae penetrate mucosal barriers
- Extracellular enzymes (proteases, elastases) digest epithelial cells
- Suppresses complement production; acquires iron from red blood cells
Clinical Manifestations:
Mucocutaneous:
- Oral thrush — common in HIV/AIDS patients; white plaques on oral mucosa
- Vaginal candidiasis — overgrowth in pregnancy, diabetics, after antibiotics
- Esophageal candidiasis
- Chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis (CMC) — in T-lymphocyte immunodeficiencies
Cutaneous:
- Skin folds (axilla, groin, umbilicus, vulva, glans penis)
- Diaper rash in infants
- Nail infections in those frequently in water
Systemic Infections (in neutropenic patients):
- Urinary tract, GIT, pulmonary, meningitis, carditis, ocular, osteoarticular
- Candidemia — disseminated candidiasis (severe, life-threatening)
Laboratory Diagnosis:
- Specimen: exudates, scrapings, aspirates, blood, biopsy
- Direct microscopy (KOH/Gram stain) — budding yeast and pseudohyphae
- Culture on SDA/Bird agar — off-white colonies; yeast smell
- Germ tube test — positive = presumptive diagnosis of C. albicans
- CHROM agar — colored colonies identify different Candida species
- API biochemical tests — carbohydrate assimilation and fermentation profiles
- PCR
Treatment:
- Superficial — topical nystatin or azole
- Chronic mucocutaneous — fluconazole
- Disseminated/severe — Amphotericin B + fluconazole +/- flucytosine

**Q9. Discuss Cryptococcus — its properties, pathogenesis, clinical features, and laboratory diagnosis.**
The Organism:
- Encapsulated yeast; reproduces by budding; round/ovoid; 4–6 um diameter
- Produces urease enzyme
- Whitish mucoid colonies in 2–3 days on culture
- 4 capsular serotypes: A, B, C, D — A and D occur worldwide; B and C mainly in tropics
Epidemiology:
- Reservoir: pigeon droppings, nesting places, contaminated soil (nitrogenous/creatine-rich soil)
- Transmission: inhalation of organism
- No human-to-human transmission
Virulence Factors:
- Capsule — inhibits phagocytosis
- Melanin production via phenol oxidase — antioxidant; protects from intracellular killing by phagocytes
Risk Factors:
- HIV/AIDS (defects in T-cell mediated immunity)
- Post-transplantation
- Corticosteroid therapy
- Hodgkin's disease
Pathogenesis:
- Inhalation of yeast from environment
- Engulfed and retained by lung macrophages
- Circulated to other body parts inside macrophages
- Particular attraction to CNS — lodge and multiply there
- Extreme immunosuppression — spreads to other organs
Clinical Manifestations:
- Cryptococcal meningitis/meningoencephalitis — most common; severe headache, fever, then signs of meningitis; most common meningitis in AIDS patients
- Pulmonary cryptococcosis — usually asymptomatic
- Other sites — skin, bone, oral cavity, prostate, kidneys
Laboratory Diagnosis:
- Specimen: CSF, blood, sputum, biopsy, pus, urine
- India ink / nigrosin staining of CSF — demonstrates encapsulated yeast (clear halo around cell)
- Latex agglutination — detects polysaccharide capsular antigen in CSF or serum; rapid and sensitive
- Culture on SDA — creamy mucoid colonies; Birdseed agar — brown (melanin) colonies
- Biochemical: urease test positive; API tests (carbohydrate assimilation)
Treatment:
- Amphotericin B + 5-Flucytosine OR Amphotericin B + Fluconazole
- Fluconazole — long-term suppression in AIDS patients

## TOPIC 4: MYCOTOXINS (Lecture 4)

**Q10. Write a detailed account of mycotoxins — their production, types, and effects.**
Definition:
- Mycotoxins — toxic secondary metabolites produced by fungi
- Low molecular weight; diverse chemical structures and biological activities
- Mycotoxicosis — disease caused by action of mycotoxins; affects liver, kidney, lungs, nervous, endocrine and immune systems
Main Producing Genera:
- Aspergillus, Fusarium, Penicillium
Factors Affecting Production:
- Temperature (optimal 24–28°C; T-2 toxin produced at 15°C)
- High moisture/water activity
- Crop damage (insect or physical damage)
- Nutritional status of crops
- Geographic and seasonal conditions
- Storage conditions — temperature, aeration (O2, CO2 levels), pH
Characteristics of Mycotoxin Disease:
- Not always transmitted between animals
- Pharmaceutical treatment does not alter disease course
- Usually presents as sub-acute or chronic condition
- No specific treatment for most mycotoxins
Aflatoxins:
- Produced by Aspergillus flavus and A. parasiticus
- Types: B1, B2 (blue fluorescence), G1, G2 (green fluorescence) under UV light
- AFM1 — metabolite of AFB1; found in milk of cows fed contaminated feed
- AFB1 — most toxic; potent carcinogen; main target is the liver (hepatocellular carcinoma)
- Affected crops: corn, peanuts, rice, oilseeds, dried fruits
- Favored by: warm temperature (28–33°C), high moisture
- Clinical signs: decreased growth rate, anemia, liver damage, increased susceptibility to infections
- Treatment: HSCAS (absorbs aflatoxins); Vitamin E and selenium (supportive); anhydrous ammonia treatment of grain (prevention)
Zearalenone:
- Produced by Fusarium species (mainly F. graminearum)
- Affected crops: corn, wheat, barley, oats
- Favored by: high moisture (22–25%), alternating high and low temperatures (7–21°C)
- Acts as a weak oestrogen — binds estrogen receptors; causes infertility, abortion, breeding problems
- Swine are most susceptible
Ergot Alkaloids:
- Produced by Claviceps purpurea — grows on rye and related plants
- Cause ergotism ("St. Anthony's fire")
- Two types of ergotism:
  - Gangrenous — cuts off blood supply to extremities
  - Convulsive — CNS involvement
- Favored by: warm and humid conditions

## TOPIC 5: DIAGNOSIS OF FUNGAL DISEASES (Lecture 5)

**Q11. Describe in detail the laboratory diagnosis of fungal infections.**
Specimen Collection:
Superficial Mycosis:
- Clean with 70% alcohol; collect in sterile paper or petri dish
- Scrape outward from edge of lesion with scalpel
- Scalp — blunt scalpel; include hair, scales, follicle contents
- Onychomycosis — stop antifungals one week before collection
Subcutaneous Mycosis:
- Scrapings/crusts from lesions, pus aspirates, biopsy
Systemic Mycosis:
- Pus, biopsy, feces, urine, sputum, CSF
Specimen Transport:
- Hair — collected with forceps
- Tissues — grind or mince before culturing
- Body fluids — centrifuge; use sediment for culture
- Urine — centrifuge; use sediment for culture
1. Direct Examination (Microscopy):
- KOH preparation (10–20%) — digests protein debris and keratin; DMSO can be added to hasten clearing
- Calcofluor white — fluorescent stain; binds to chitin and glucan in fungal cell wall; excellent morphology
- India ink — for capsulated fungi (e.g. Cryptococcus)
- Gram stain — fungi are Gram positive
- Histopathology: PAS (Periodic Acid-Schiff) and H&E stains; superficial infections show dermatitis; deeper infections show granulomatous reaction with fibrosis
Microscopy findings:
- Dermatophyte: branching hyphae, arthrospores, conidia, ectothrix/endothrix spores
- Yeast: budding yeast cells, pseudohyphae
2. Fungal Culture:
- Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (SDA) — 2% dextrose, antibiotics (gentamicin, chloramphenicol), cycloheximide; standard medium
- Selective media:
  - Corn Meal Agar — sporulation and chlamydospore formation
  - Birdseed agar — Cryptococcus (brown melanin colonies)
  - BHI agar — dimorphic and fastidious fungi
- Temperature: 37°C (most pathogens); 30°C (superficial); 25°C and 37°C (dimorphic)
- Incubation: at least 4 weeks; positive usually in 7–10 days; Candida/Aspergillus in 24–72 hours
- Specimens cultured on agar slants — safe, space-efficient, resistant to drying
Identification of Fungal Cultures:
- Colony morphology — color, texture, pigmentation
- LPCB stain microscopically — lactic acid (preserves structure), phenol (kills organisms), glycerol (prevents drying), cotton blue (stains structures)
- Special culture techniques — CHROM agar for Candida species identification
- Biochemical — carbohydrate assimilation, nitrogen assimilation, sugar fermentation (API system)
Interpretation:
- Established pathogen isolated (e.g. H. capsulatum, C. neoformans) = confirmed infection
- Commensal/opportunist (e.g. Candida) — consider repeated isolation, same strain from multiple sites, immune status, serological evidence
3. Serological Tests:
- Antibody detection — used for systemic/subcutaneous mycoses; assess prognosis and treatment response
- Antigen detection — preferred in early infection and immunocompromised patients
- Tests used: agglutination, immunodiffusion (most widely used), CIEP, indirect fluorescent antibody, ELISA, RIA
4. Molecular Methods:
- PCR — Polymerase Chain Reaction
- RFLP — Restriction Fragment Length Polymorphism
- Nucleic acid probes
- Protein electrophoresis

**Q12. Discuss the role of serology in the diagnosis of fungal infections.**
Purpose of Serology:
- Detects either antigen (Ag) or antibody (Ab) in serum or body fluids
Antibody Detection:
- Used in diagnosis of systemic and subcutaneous mycoses
- Assesses prognosis — monitors disease progress
- Assesses response to treatment
- Limitation: poor in immunocompromised patients (weak antibody response)
Antigen Detection:
- Preferred in early stages of infection (before antibody response develops)
- Preferred in immunocompromised patients — because antibody production is impaired
- Example: latex agglutination detects Cryptococcal polysaccharide capsular antigen in CSF — rapid and highly sensitive
Serological Tests Used:
- Agglutination
- Immunodiffusion — most widely used in mycology
- Counter immuno-electrophoresis (CIEP)
- Indirect fluorescent antibody detection
- ELISA (Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay)
- RIA (Radio Immuno Assay)
Role of Complement in Serology:
- Complement is a series of serum proteins that, when activated, produce opsonins (C3b) and anaphylatoxins (C3a, C5a)
- C3b acts as opsonin — enhances phagocytosis of fungi
- C5a attracts macrophages and neutrophils
- Complement can be activated via classical, alternative, or lectin pathways
