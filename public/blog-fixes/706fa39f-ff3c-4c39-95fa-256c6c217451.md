## 13. Burkitt Lymphoma (×2)

**Q: Most likely diagnosis — rapidly growing jaw mass in a 6-year-old Kenyan child?**

- Endemic Burkitt Lymphoma
- Highly aggressive B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma
- Classically presents as rapidly growing jaw/facial bone tumour in children from equatorial Africa

**Q: Primary infectious cause/risk factor?**
- Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV) — present in nearly 100% of endemic cases
- Chronic malaria is a major co-factor (reduces resistance to EBV)

**Q: Classic histological finding?**
- "Starry sky" appearance
- Rapidly dividing neoplastic uniform B lymphocytes (the "sky")
- Interspersed with large pale tingible body macrophages (the "stars") clearing apoptotic cells

**Q: Specific genetic finding?**
- Chromosomal translocation t(8;14)
- Moves c-MYC oncogene (chromosome 8) to immunoglobulin heavy chain promoter (chromosome 14)
- Leads to uncontrolled cell proliferation

## 14. Pituitary Adenoma

**Q: What is seen on the sagittal MRI?**
- Mass within the sella turcica of the sphenoid bone
- Pituitary adenoma — benign tumour of the anterior pituitary gland

**Q: Classic neurological/visual symptom?**
- Bitemporal hemianopsia (loss of outer visual fields in both eyes)
- Caused by macroadenoma expanding superiorly and compressing the optic chiasm

**Q: Most common hormone secreted by functioning pituitary adenoma?**
- Prolactin
- Prolactinoma presents with: galactorrhoea, amenorrhoea, infertility

## 15. Gangrenous Appendicitis

**Q: What is the most likely diagnosis?**
- Gangrenous appendicitis
- Blackening of tissue = transmural necrosis from compromised vascular supply

**Q: Gross pathological findings?**
- Diffuse dark-brown to black discoloration of serosal surface
- Extensive tissue necrosis and congestion
- Loss of blood supply

**Q: Clinical signs and complications?**
- Severe right lower quadrant (RLQ) pain
- Rebound tenderness
- High fever
- Leukocytosis
- High risk of perforation → abscess or generalised peritonitis

**Q: Expected microscopic findings?**
- Transmural neutrophilic infiltration
- Extensive liquefactive necrosis
- Haemorrhage
- Fibrinopurulent exudate on serosal surface

## 16. Haemoglobin Electrophoresis (×2)

**Q: Principle of haemoglobin electrophoresis?**
- Separation of different haemoglobin types (HbA, HbS, HbC etc.)
- Based on electrical charge and migration in an electric field

**Q: What does presence of HbS band indicate?**
- Sickle Cell Disease or Sickle Cell Trait

**Q: How does Hb M-Iwate behave on electrophoresis?**
- Alkaline gel: anodal to HbA
- Acid gel: indistinguishable from HbA
- Isoelectric focusing: appears brown before chemical application (HbA appears red)
- Dried gel: migrates between HbF and HbS

**Q: Haemoglobin patterns and their patients?**
- HbSS — Sickle Cell Disease
- HbAS — Sickle Cell Trait
- HbAA — Normal

## 17. FISH (Fluorescence In Situ Hybridisation)

**Q: What technique is shown?**
- FISH — Fluorescence In Situ Hybridisation

**Q: What do the colours represent?**
- Blue regions: cell nuclei stained with DAPI (binds to DNA)
- Pink/red spots: fluorescent probes hybridised to specific DNA sequences

**Q: Metaphase vs interphase FISH — which is shown?**
- Interphase FISH
- DNA contained within intact non-dividing nuclei (not condensed into chromosomes)

**Q: What does finding 3–4 red signals per nucleus indicate?**
- Polysomy or aneuploidy (e.g. trisomy or high-level copy number gains)

**Q: Clinical applications of interphase FISH?**
- Prenatal screening: detecting trisomies 21, 18, 13
- Oncology: gene amplifications (HER2 in breast cancer, MYCN in neuroblastoma)
- Haematopathology: diagnosing leukaemias/lymphomas via genetic rearrangements

**Q: Advantages over standard karyotyping?**
- Speed: results in 24–48 hours vs 1–2 weeks for karyotyping
- Can be performed on non-dividing cells including FFPE sections
- Detects small microdeletions and gene amplifications below light microscopy resolution

## 18. Glucometer / Blood Glucose Monitoring (×2)

**Q: What instrument is shown?**
- Glucometer (Wellion CALLA Light model)
- Portable device for self-monitoring of blood glucose (SMBG)

**Q: Principle of the glucometer?**
- Electrochemical (amperometric) principle
- Test strip contains glucose oxidase enzyme
- Reacts with blood glucose → produces gluconic acid + hydrogen peroxide
- Electrons generate electric current proportional to glucose concentration
- Meter converts current to digital reading

**Q: Normal blood glucose reference ranges?**

- Fasting normal: 3.9–5.6 mmol/L (70–100 mg/dL)
- Impaired fasting: 5.6–6.9 mmol/L (100–125 mg/dL)
- Diabetes fasting: ≥7.0 mmol/L (≥126 mg/dL)
- Postprandial normal (2h): <7.8 mmol/L (<140 mg/dL)
- Postprandial diabetes (2h): ≥11.1 mmol/L (≥200 mg/dL)

**Q: What does a reading of 7.0 mmol/L mean?**
- Fasting: 7.0 mmol/L or higher on two tests may indicate diabetes
- Post-meal (2 hours): up to 7.8 mmol/L is normal
- Conversion: 7.0 mmol/L × 18 = 126 mg/dL

**Q: What does 6.3 mmol/L postprandial mean?**
- Within normal postprandial range (<7.8 mmol/L)
- Apple symbol on display = postprandial (after-meal) reading

**Q: Diagnostic criteria for Diabetes Mellitus (ADA)?**
- Fasting plasma glucose ≥126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L), OR
- 2-hour post OGTT ≥200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L), OR
- HbA1c ≥6.5%, OR
- Random plasma glucose ≥200 mg/dL with classic symptoms

**Q: What is HbA1c?**
- Glycated haemoglobin — reflects average blood glucose over past ~3 months
- Normal: <5.7%
- Prediabetes: 5.7–6.4%
- Diabetes: ≥6.5%

**Q: Sources of error in glucometer readings?**
- Inadequate blood sample volume
- Expired or improperly stored test strips
- Wet/contaminated finger
- Extreme haematocrit values (anaemia → falsely high; polycythaemia → falsely low)
- Severe hypoxia, hypotension, shock
- Interfering substances: ascorbic acid, paracetamol, high bilirubin
- Improper calibration

**Q: Glucometer vs laboratory plasma glucose?**
- Glucometer: capillary whole blood, quick bedside result
- Lab: venous plasma, glucose oxidase-peroxidase (GOD-POD) or hexokinase method — more accurate
- Plasma glucose values ~11% higher than whole blood values

**Q: Hypo and Hyper alarm presets?**

- Hypo: 3.9 mmol/L
- Hyper: 13.9 mmol/L

## 19. Biliary Calculi (Gallstones) (×2)

**Q: What are these specimens?**
- Multiple biliary calculi (gallstones)

**Q: Gross description?**
- Numerous, dark brown to black
- Irregularly shaped, nodular, multi-faceted/mulberry-like
- Rough and granular surface
- Variable size (few mm to ~2.5 cm)
- Hard consistency

**Q: Type and composition?**
- Black pigment stones
- Primarily calcium bilirubinate polymers
- Smaller amounts of calcium phosphate and calcium carbonate

**Q: How do they differ from cholesterol stones?**
- Cholesterol stones: pale yellow to green-white, often solitary, larger, waxy cut surface, composed of cholesterol
- Brown pigment stones: softer, clay-like, dark brown, form in bile ducts, associated with bacterial infection/parasites

**Q: Risk factors for black pigment stones?**
- Chronic haemolytic states (sickle cell anaemia, hereditary spherocytosis)
- Cirrhosis/chronic liver disease
- Chronic biliary stasis
- Advanced age

**Q: Pathogenesis?**
- Excess unconjugated bilirubin in bile
- Precipitates with calcium ions → calcium bilirubinate
- Occurs in sterile environment of gallbladder

**Q: Clinical symptoms?**
- Biliary colic: episodic RUQ/epigastric pain, postprandial, radiates to back/shoulder, nausea/vomiting
- Many patients remain asymptomatic

**Q: Complications?**
- Acute cholecystitis (obstruction of cystic duct)
- Choledocholithiasis (stone in common bile duct → obstructive jaundice, cholangitis, pancreatitis)
- Acute pancreatitis (obstruction of ampulla of Vater)
- Cholangitis (bacterial infection of bile ducts)
- Gallstone ileus (large stone erodes into small bowel → obstruction)
- Mirizzi syndrome (external compression of common hepatic duct)

**Q: Gold standard imaging?**
- Abdominal ultrasound
- Black pigment stones: often radiopaque on plain X-ray
- Cholesterol stones: usually radiolucent

**Q: Definitive treatment?**
- Laparoscopic cholecystectomy

**Q: Microscopic changes in chronic cholecystitis?**
- Chronic inflammatory infiltrates (lymphocytes, plasma cells)
- Fibrosis
- Muscular hypertrophy
- Rokitansky-Aschoff sinuses (mucosal invaginations through muscularis propria)

## 20. Liver Function Tests (LFTs)

**Q: Which liver enzyme is elevated?**
- Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP) — 108 U/L (normal: 35–104 U/L)

**Q: What does mildly elevated ALP with normal GGT mean?**
- Often not clinically significant alone
- ALP can be elevated due to bone growth, healing fractures, pregnancy, medications
- If GGT also elevated → would point to biliary/liver cause

**Q: Is there evidence of jaundice or biliary obstruction?**
- No — all bilirubin markers within normal range

**Q: How is liver synthetic function assessed?**
- Albumin (normal: 39.7–49.4 g/L) and Total Protein (normal: 66–87 g/L)
- Both within normal range → liver synthetic function intact

**Q: Normal reference ranges for LFTs?**
- ALT (SGPT): <35 U/L
- AST (SGOT): <35 U/L
- GGT: <40 U/L
- ALP: 35–104 U/L
- Total Bilirubin: <21 µmol/L
- Direct Bilirubin: <5.1 µmol/L
- Indirect Bilirubin: <12 µmol/L
- Albumin: 39.7–49.4 g/L
- Total Protein: 66–87 g/L
- LDH: 135–214 U/L

## 21. TURP Chips (×2)

**Q: What procedure produced this specimen?**
- Transurethral Resection of the Prostate (TURP)
- Fragments are "chips" created by loop cautery

**Q: Most common histological finding?**
- Nodular Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH)
- Other findings: chronic prostatitis, High-Grade Prostatic Intraepithelial Neoplasia (HGPIN)

**Q: Gross features suggesting possible carcinoma?**
- Fragments that are firmer in consistency
- Yellow-to-orange colour — submit preferentially

**Q: CAP guidelines for submission?**
- Specimens ≤12g: submit entirely
- Additional cassette for every 5g of remaining tissue beyond 12g

## 22. Cushing's Syndrome

**Q: What is the diagnosis?**
- Cushing's Syndrome

**Q: Characteristic physical features?**
- Buffalo hump (fat pad on upper back/neck)
- Thin arms and legs (muscle wasting)
- Thin skin with bruising
- Purple stretch marks (striae) on abdomen
- Hirsutism (extra face and body hair)
- Moon face (round, full face)
- Thinning scalp hair
- Red cheeks
- Central obesity

**Q: Why thin limbs despite obesity?**
- Excess cortisol causes muscle wasting and central fat redistribution

**Q: Cause of buffalo hump?**

- Cortisol-induced lipogenesis causing fat accumulation on upper back and neck

**Q: Cause of stretch marks and bruising?**
- Excess cortisol → collagen breakdown → skin thinning → easy bruising and purple striae

**Q: Cause of hirsutism?**
- Hypercortisolism → increased adrenal androgen production

**Q: Primary hormone responsible?**
- Cortisol

**Q: Common causes of increased cortisol?**
- Pituitary adenoma (Cushing's disease)
- Adrenal adenoma or carcinoma
- Ectopic ACTH production (e.g. small cell lung cancer)
- Exogenous corticosteroid use

## 23. MacConkey Agar

**Q: What type of culture medium is MacConkey agar?**
- Selective and differential culture medium
- Used primarily for isolating Gram-negative enteric bacilli

**Q: What makes it selective?**
- Bile salts and crystal violet dye
- Inhibits growth of most Gram-positive bacteria

**Q: Two types of bacterial growth?**
- Lactose-fermenting (LF) colonies — pink/red
- Non-lactose fermenting (NLF) colonies — colourless/transparent

**Q: Why are LF colonies pink?**
- Bacteria ferment lactose → produce acid → neutral red pH indicator turns pink/red

**Q: Pink halo around colonies indicates?**
- Strong lactose fermentation → high acid → precipitation of bile salts

**Q: Examples of lactose fermenters?**
- Escherichia coli
- Klebsiella species
- Enterobacter species

**Q: Examples of non-lactose fermenters?**
- Salmonella
- Shigella
- Proteus
- Pseudomonas aeruginosa

## 24. Uterine Leiomyomas (Fibroids) (×2)

**Q: What is shown?**
- Bisected uterus with multiple well-circumscribed whorled whitish nodular masses
- Uterine leiomyomata (fibroids) — most common benign smooth muscle tumour of the uterus

**Q: Key gross features?**
- Multiple well-circumscribed rounded nodules within myometrium
- Whorled, white-tan cut surface
- Clear demarcation from surrounding myometrium (pseudocapsule)
- Distortion of uterine cavity
- Focal haemorrhage/necrosis possible

**Q: Classification by location?**
- Submucosal: beneath endometrium, projects into uterine cavity
- Intramural: within myometrial wall
- Subserosal: beneath serosa, projects outward
- Pedunculated: attached by a stalk

**Q: Clinical symptoms?**
- Menorrhagia or abnormal uterine bleeding
- Pelvic pain or pressure
- Dysmenorrhoea
- Infertility or recurrent pregnancy loss
- Urinary frequency (mass effect)
- Iron deficiency anaemia
- Many asymptomatic

**Q: Differential diagnoses?**
- Leiomyosarcoma (necrosis, haemorrhage, soft consistency, infiltrative borders)
- Adenomyosis (ill-defined myometrial thickening)
- STUMP (smooth muscle tumour of uncertain malignant potential)

**Q: Types of degeneration?**
- Hyaline degeneration (most common)
- Cystic degeneration
- Red (carneous) degeneration — especially during pregnancy
- Myxoid degeneration
- Calcification (older/postmenopausal fibroids)
- Fatty degeneration

**Q: Microscopic features confirming diagnosis?**
- Interlacing fascicles of uniform spindle-shaped smooth muscle cells
- Cigar-shaped nuclei
- Eosinophilic cytoplasm
- Low mitotic activity (<5/10 HPF)
- No cytologic atypia
- No tumour cell necrosis

**Q: Management?**
- Observation: asymptomatic small fibroids
- Medical: GnRH analogues, tranexamic acid, hormonal therapy
- Surgical: myomectomy (fertility-preserving) or hysterectomy (definitive)
- Minimally invasive: uterine artery embolisation, MRI-guided focused ultrasound

## 25. Malignant Melanoma (×2)

**Q: Most likely diagnosis?**
- Malignant melanoma (likely superficial spreading or nodular type)

**Q: ABCDE criteria?**
- A — Asymmetry
- B — Border irregularity
- C — Colour variation
- D — Diameter >6 mm
- E — Evolution (change over time)

**Q: Major risk factors?**
- Chronic UV radiation exposure/sunburns
- Fair skin, light hair and eyes (Fitzpatrick I–II)
- Family/personal history of melanoma
- Numerous or atypical (dysplastic) nevi
- Immunosuppression
- Genetic mutations (CDKN2A, BRAF, MC1R)

**Q: Histopathological hallmarks?**
- Atypical melanocytes with nuclear pleomorphism and prominent nucleoli
- Pagetoid spread of melanocytes through epidermis
- Loss of maturation with depth (lack of "zonation")
- Dermal invasion by nests of atypical melanocytes
- Mitotic figures (sometimes atypical)
- Associated inflammatory infiltrate and melanin-laden macrophages

**Q: Staging systems?**
- Breslow thickness — tumour depth in mm (most important prognostic factor)
- Clark's level — anatomical level of invasion (I–V)
- AJCC TNM staging

**Q: Breslow thickness and 5-year survival?**
- <1 mm: >90%
- 1–2 mm: ~80%
- 2–4 mm: ~65%
- >4 mm: ~50%
- Ulceration worsens prognosis at any thickness

**Q: Treatment options?**
- Wide local excision (margins 0.5–2 cm based on Breslow thickness)
- Sentinel lymph node biopsy for lesions >0.8 mm
- Immunotherapy: anti-PD-1 (pembrolizumab, nivolumab), anti-CTLA-4 (ipilimumab)
- Targeted therapy for BRAF-mutated tumours (vemurafenib + trametinib)
- Radiation therapy (palliation, brain metastases)

**Q: Common sites of metastasis?**
- Regional lymph nodes
- Skin and subcutaneous tissue
- Lungs
- Liver
- Brain
- Bone

**Q: Differential diagnoses?**

- Seborrhoeic keratosis
- Pigmented basal cell carcinoma
- Dysplastic nevus
- Blue nevus
- Dermatofibroma
- Pigmented actinic keratosis
- Traumatic haematoma

## 26. Angiosarcoma of the Breast

**Q: What is breast angiosarcoma?**
- Rare aggressive malignant vascular tumour (<0.05% of breast malignancies)
- Arises from endothelial cells

**Q: Classification?**
- Primary: de novo, within breast parenchyma — age 30–50 years
- Secondary: post-radiotherapy or chronic lymphoedema (Stewart-Treves syndrome) — age 60–70 years

**Q: Gross pathological findings?**
- Ill-defined, spongy, haemorrhagic mass with dark red-brown areas
- Variegated cut surface with necrosis and cystic blood-filled spaces
- Infiltrative borders blending into breast parenchyma

**Q: Key clinical features?**
- Rapidly growing, painless breast mass
- Skin discoloration (bluish-red) in secondary/cutaneous forms
- Often mistaken for haematoma or benign vascular lesion

**Q: Histologic grading (Rosen)?**
- Grade I (low): well-formed anastomosing vascular channels
- Grade II (intermediate): solid cellular areas without necrosis
- Grade III (high): solid spindle/epithelioid areas with necrosis, mitoses, "blood lakes"

**Q: Immunohistochemistry markers?**
- CD31 (most specific)
- CD34
- ERG
- Factor VIII/vWF
- D2-40 (in post-radiation cases)
- MYC amplification — characteristic of secondary angiosarcoma

**Q: Treatment and prognosis?**
- Mastectomy is mainstay
- Adjuvant chemotherapy (anthracycline/taxane-based) for high-grade tumours
- Poor prognosis: 5-year survival ~30–40%
- High rate of local recurrence and lung/liver/bone metastases

## 27. Testicular Torsion (×2)

**Q: What is the specimen?**
- Testis with attached spermatic cord
- Haemorrhagic infarction secondary to testicular torsion

**Q: What is testicular torsion?**
- Twisting of the spermatic cord
- Obstructs venous outflow first, then arterial compromise
- Results in ischaemia and haemorrhagic infarction

**Q: Types?**
- Intravaginal: most common; "bell-clapper" deformity; seen in adolescents
- Extravaginal: occurs in neonates

**Q: What is "bell-clapper" deformity?**
- Tunica vaginalis completely surrounds testis and epididymis
- Testis suspended freely → predisposed to torsion

**Q: Normal testis weight vs this specimen?**
- Normal: 15–25 grams
- Specimen: 60 grams — enlarged due to congestion, oedema, haemorrhage

**Q: Gross features?**
- Enlarged, swollen testis
- Dark red/purple/black discoloration
- Twisted spermatic cord
- Haemorrhagic and oedematous parenchyma on cut section

**Q: Microscopic features?**
- Coagulative necrosis of seminiferous tubules
- Interstitial haemorrhage and congestion
- Thrombosed vessels in spermatic cord
- Loss of spermatogenic cells
- Neutrophilic infiltrate (later stages)

**Q: Clinical presentation?**
- Sudden onset severe testicular pain
- Scrotal swelling and redness
- Nausea, vomiting
- Negative Prehn's sign
- Absent cremasteric reflex
- High-riding testis with horizontal lie

**Q: Investigation of choice?**
- Colour Doppler ultrasound — shows absent or reduced blood flow

**Q: Management?**
- Surgical emergency — detorsion within 6 hours
- Orchiopexy of both testes
- Orchiectomy if testis non-viable

**Q: Golden window for salvage?**
- <6 hours: ~90–100%
- 6–12 hours: ~50%
- 12–24 hours: ~10%
- >24 hours: near 0%

**Q: Why venous obstruction before arterial?**
- Veins have thinner walls and lower intraluminal pressure — compressed first

**Q: Complications?**
- Testicular infarction and loss
- Infertility (antisperm antibody formation)
- Atrophy of affected testis
- Recurrence or contralateral torsion if not fixed

## 28. Ovarian Cysts / Serous Cystadenoma (×2)

**Q: What is the specimen?**
- Salpingo-oophorectomy specimen
- Enlarged ovary with multilocular cystic mass and attached fallopian tube

**Q: Most likely diagnosis?**
- Serous cystadenoma of the ovary
- Multilocular cystic structure containing clear/amber watery serous fluid

**Q: WHO classification of ovarian tumours?**
- Surface epithelial-stromal tumours (65–70%): serous, mucinous, endometrioid, clear cell, Brenner
- Germ cell tumours (15–20%): teratoma, dysgerminoma, yolk sac tumour
- Sex cord-stromal tumours (5–10%): granulosa cell, Sertoli-Leydig, fibroma/thecoma
- Metastatic tumours: e.g. Krukenberg tumour

**Q: Serous vs mucinous cystadenoma — gross differences?**
- Serous: usually unilocular, clear watery amber fluid, smaller, bilateral in ~20%
- Mucinous: multilocular, thick viscous mucoid fluid, often very large, usually unilateral

**Q: Microscopic features of serous cystadenoma?**
- Single layer of cuboidal to columnar ciliated epithelium (tubal-type)
- Thin fibrous wall
- No atypia, no stratification, no stromal invasion
- Psammoma bodies may be present

**Q: Classification of serous tumours by behaviour?**
- Benign: serous cystadenoma
- Borderline: serous borderline tumour
- Malignant: low-grade and high-grade serous cystadenocarcinoma

**Q: Risk factors for ovarian tumours?**
- Nulliparity
- Early menarche, late menopause
- Family history (BRCA1/BRCA2 mutations)
- Lynch syndrome (HNPCC)
- Increasing age
- Smoking, obesity
- Use of fertility drugs

**Q: Clinical features?**
- Often asymptomatic until large
- Abdominal distension, pelvic pain/heaviness
- Menstrual irregularities
- Pressure symptoms (urinary frequency, constipation)
- Acute presentation: torsion, rupture, haemorrhage
- Ascites if malignant

**Q: Complications?**
- Torsion → infarction
- Rupture → haemoperitoneum or chemical peritonitis
- Haemorrhage into cyst
- Infection
- Malignant transformation
- Pressure effects on adjacent organs

**Q: Tumour markers?**
- CA-125: serous carcinoma
- CEA, CA 19-9: mucinous tumours
- AFP: yolk sac tumour
- β-hCG: choriocarcinoma, dysgerminoma
- LDH: dysgerminoma
- Inhibin: granulosa cell tumour

**Q: Management of benign serous cystadenoma?**
- Cystectomy in younger patients wishing to preserve fertility
- Unilateral salpingo-oophorectomy in older patients or large/complex cysts
- TAH-BSO if bilateral or in postmenopausal women

**Q: What is a Krukenberg tumour?**
- Metastatic ovarian tumour, usually bilateral
- Signet-ring mucin-secreting cells in cellular stroma
- Most commonly from gastric carcinoma (also colon, breast)

**Q: What is Meigs syndrome?**
- Triad of:
  1. Benign ovarian tumour (classically ovarian fibroma)
  2. Ascites
  3. Right-sided pleural effusion
- All resolve after tumour removal
