# Hypersensitivity Reactions

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Immunology and Allergy**

> **In one line:** hypersensitivity reactions are exaggerated or inappropriate immune responses that damage the host. Gell and Coombs split them into four types: I (IgE, immediate), II (antibody against cell-surface antigen), III (soluble immune complexes) and IV (T-cell mediated, delayed).

## What you must know

- Types I, II and III are **antibody mediated**; type IV is **cell mediated**.
- **Type I** = IgE on mast cells and basophils; onset within minutes to hours; atopy, anaphylaxis, asthma, allergic rhinitis.
- **Type II** = IgG or IgM against cell-membrane antigens with **complement activation**; mismatched transfusion, anti-GBM disease.
- **Type III** = soluble antigen-antibody complexes deposited in kidney, joints and vessels; SLE, rheumatoid arthritis, serum sickness.
- **Type IV** = CD4 (and sometimes CD8) T cells and activated macrophages; onset 2 to 4 days; contact dermatitis, TB granuloma, Mantoux test.
- Anaphylactoid reactions look like anaphylaxis but are **not IgE mediated** (contrast media, local anaesthetics).
- Diagnosis differs by type: skin prick test and specific IgE (I), immunofluorescence of biopsies (II, III), Mantoux and patch tests (IV).

## 1. Overview

- Hypersensitivity reactions are over-excessive, aberrant immune responses that can harm the body. They underlie **allergy, autoimmunity and chronic inflammation**.
- The Gell and Coombs classification groups them by the effector mechanism.

| Type | Name | Mediator | Onset | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | Immediate / anaphylactic | IgE, mast cells, basophils, eosinophils | Minutes to hours | Hay fever, asthma, food allergy, anaphylaxis |
| II | Antibody-mediated cytotoxic | IgG or IgM against cell-bound antigen, complement | Minutes to hours | Mismatched transfusion, anti-GBM nephritis |
| III | Immune complex | Soluble antigen plus IgG or IgM, complement | Hours (serum sickness 1 to 2 weeks) | SLE, rheumatoid arthritis, glomerulonephritis |
| IV | Delayed type (DTH) | T cells (CD4, some CD8), macrophages | 2 to 4 days | Contact dermatitis, TB granuloma, Mantoux |

## 2. Type I: immediate hypersensitivity

**Also called** immediate hypersensitivity, anaphylactic reaction or allergic reaction (occurs within minutes to hours).

### Features

- Most people do not react to common environmental antigens, or make IgG antibodies to them. **Atopic** individuals instead make large amounts of **IgE**.
- Antibody: **IgE**. Cells: **mast cells, basophils, eosinophils**.
- Allergens are typically **small, highly soluble** antigens.
- Common allergens: pollens, dust mites, animal dander, nuts, shellfish, various drugs (penicillin is the classic).

### Two phases

1. **Sensitisation phase:** first contact with the allergen. It is presented to Th2 cells, which drive B cells to make specific IgE. IgE binds the high-affinity Fc-epsilon receptors on mast cells and basophils. The patient is silent.
2. **Challenge (effector) phase:** subsequent exposure. The allergen cross-links IgE on the cell surface and the cell **degranulates**.

### Mediators

| Group | Examples | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary (preformed)** | Histamine, tryptase, proteases, heparin, eosinophil and neutrophil chemotactic factors | Vasodilatation, increased permeability, bronchoconstriction, itch |
| **Secondary (newly made)** | Leukotrienes (C4, D4, E4), prostaglandin D2, platelet-activating factor, cytokines (IL-4, IL-5, TNF) | Sustained bronchoconstriction, mucus, late-phase inflammation, eosinophil recruitment |

> **Remember:** the late-phase reaction (4 to 8 hours after exposure) is driven by secondary mediators and eosinophils. This is why anaphylaxis can recur (biphasic) and why steroids are given.

### Clinical expression: allergy is a systemic disorder

| System | Manifestations |
|---|---|
| Respiratory | Allergic rhinitis, asthma |
| Digestive | Food allergy (oral and pharyngeal itch, vomiting, diarrhoea) |
| Skin | Eczema, urticaria, allergic dermatitis, angioedema |

- **Injected allergens** (for example bee sting venom) enter the blood and cause systemic inflammation and **anaphylactic shock**, which is life threatening (airway oedema, bronchospasm, hypotension).
- **Anaphylactoid reactions** are **non-IgE mediated** (direct mast-cell triggering), for example from **contrast media** or **local anaesthetics**. They can be clinically identical and are treated the same way.

### Diagnosis of allergy

1. **Skin prick test (SPT):** drop of allergen on the forearm, pricked through; wheal and flare at 15 to 20 minutes is positive. Stop antihistamines first.
2. **Specific IgE measurement (RAST):** blood test; used where skin disease or antihistamines prevent SPT.
3. **Elimination and provocation (challenge) test:** for food allergy; done under supervision.

### Management in brief

- Avoid the allergen; antihistamines, nasal or inhaled steroids for chronic disease; immunotherapy for selected cases.
- **Anaphylaxis:** adrenaline 0.5 mg IM (adult) into the anterolateral thigh, airway and oxygen, IV fluids, then antihistamine and hydrocortisone as adjuncts.

> **Exam tip:** adrenaline IM is the first-line drug in anaphylaxis. Steroids and antihistamines do not treat shock.

## 3. Type II: antibody-mediated (cytotoxic) hypersensitivity

### Features

- Antibody: **IgG or IgM**.
- Antigen is **bound to a cell membrane**: self antigens, or exogenous (microbial or drug) antigens adsorbed on the cell.
- **Complement activation is invariable**. Cells are destroyed by complement lysis, opsonisation and phagocytosis, and antibody-dependent cell cytotoxicity (ADCC).
- Some type II reactions alter function without killing the cell (receptor stimulation or blockade).

### Clinical examples

| Example | Target |
|---|---|
| Glomerulonephritis (**anti-GBM disease**, Goodpasture) | Basement membrane collagen |
| **Mismatched blood transfusion** (ABO) | Red cell antigens |
| Haemolytic disease of the newborn | Rhesus D antigen |
| Autoimmune haemolytic anaemia, ITP | Red cells, platelets |
| Graves disease (stimulating), myasthenia gravis (blocking) | TSH receptor, acetylcholine receptor |

### Diagnosis

Detection of antibody and antigen by **immunofluorescence on tissue biopsy** (kidney, skin). In anti-GBM disease this shows **linear** IgG along the basement membrane. The direct Coombs test detects antibody on red cells.

## 4. Type III: immune complex hypersensitivity

- Antigen reacts with antibody to form an **immune complex**, which can trigger inflammation.
- Disease occurs when complexes are not cleared and are **deposited in tissues**: kidneys (nephritis), joints (arthritis), blood vessels (vasculitis).

### Sequence

1. Soluble antigen plus IgG or IgM forms complexes.
2. Complexes deposit in tissue (often at sites of high-pressure filtration).
3. **Complement activation** (C3a, C5a).
4. Attraction of **neutrophils** and other inflammatory cells, which release enzymes and cause tissue damage.

### Clinical examples

- **SLE** and lupus nephritis
- **Rheumatoid arthritis**
- Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis
- Serum sickness; Arthus reaction (local)

### Diagnosis

Demonstration of specific immune complexes in blood or tissue, by **immunofluorescence** (granular deposits in glomeruli), plus low complement (C3, C4) in active disease.

> **Exam tip:** linear immunofluorescence = type II (anti-GBM). Granular immunofluorescence = type III (immune complexes).

## 5. Type IV: delayed-type hypersensitivity

### Features

- **Cell-mediated** response, not antibody mediated.
- Antigen-dependent **T-cell activation** (CD4 mainly, CD8 occasionally) through MHC class II or class I.
- **Activated macrophages** are the final effectors.
- **Delayed onset: 2 to 4 days.**
- Abnormal cellular response with **granuloma formation** when the antigen persists.

### Development

| Phase | Time | Events |
|---|---|---|
| Sensitisation | 1 to 2 weeks | Antigen presented by APCs; T cells proliferate |
| Effector | 24 to 72 hours after re-exposure | T cells release cytokines (IFN-gamma, TNF, IL-2); macrophages activate and act non-specifically |

### Clinical examples

- **Allergic contact dermatitis** (nickel, latex, poison ivy): hapten binds skin proteins, presented to T cells, eczematous rash 2 to 4 days later.
- **TB granuloma** (persistent antigen).
- Also tuberculin reaction, leprosy, graft rejection, some drug rashes.

### Diagnosis

- **Delayed skin test (Mantoux test)**
- **Patch test** (contact dermatitis)
- **Lymphocyte transformation test**

> **Remember:** type IV is the only type that **cannot be transferred by serum**; it is transferred by T cells.

## Key points to revise

1. Type I (IgE), II (IgG/IgM) and III (immune complex) are antibody mediated; type IV is T-cell mediated.
2. Type I has a sensitisation phase (IgE made) and a challenge phase (mast cell degranulation); histamine is the key preformed mediator.
3. Anaphylaxis is treated with IM adrenaline; anaphylactoid reactions are non-IgE but look the same.
4. Type II needs cell-bound antigen and complement: mismatched transfusion, anti-GBM disease.
5. Type III is deposition of soluble complexes: SLE, rheumatoid arthritis, glomerulonephritis.
6. Type IV starts after 2 to 4 days; Mantoux and patch tests; granulomas in TB.
7. Allergy tests: skin prick, specific IgE (RAST), elimination or provocation.
