## Summary
This document provides a comprehensive overview of vascular pathology, covering venous disorders such as varicose veins and deep vein thrombosis, and lymphatic conditions including lymphangitis and lymphoedema. It thoroughly classifies and details various vascular tumors, from benign hemangiomas and lymphangiomas to intermediate-grade Kaposi Sarcoma and malignant angiosarcoma, highlighting their unique clinical features and associations. Furthermore, the notes address the pathology related to modern vascular interventions like endovascular stenting and different types of bypass grafts.

## Key Points
- Varicose veins are abnormally dilated, tortuous superficial veins caused by increased intraluminal pressure and incompetent valves, rarely leading to embolism unlike deep vein thrombosis (DVT).
- Lymphatic disorders encompass acute lymphangitis, often bacterial, and lymphoedema, which can be primary or secondary to lymphatic obstruction by tumors, surgery, or filariasis.
- Vascular tumors range from common benign hemangiomas, which often regress spontaneously, to aggressive malignant angiosarcomas and HHV8-associated Kaposi Sarcoma, particularly in immunocompromised individuals.
- Endovascular stenting effectively dilates arterial stenoses but carries risks of immediate thrombosis and long-term in-stent restenosis, with drug-eluting stents reducing restenosis but requiring prolonged anticoagulation.
- Vascular replacement for coronary bypass utilizes saphenous vein grafts with limited long-term patency due to atherosclerosis and intimal hyperplasia, while internal mammary artery grafts offer superior patency for specific applications.

### SECTION 1: VENOUS DISORDERS

### Varicose Veins
- Abnormally **dilated, tortuous veins** produced by **prolonged increased intraluminal pressure** with vessel dilation and **incompetence of venous valves**
- Superficial veins of the upper and lower leg most commonly involved — venous pressures in these sites can be elevated **up to 10 times normal** by prolonged dependent posture
- Affect **up to 20% of men** and **one third of women**

**Risk Factors:**
- **Obesity** — increases risk
- **Pregnancy** — gravid uterus compresses inferior vena cava ↑ venous pressure; explains higher incidence in women
- **Familial predilection** — reflects defective venous wall development

**Clinical Features:**
- Incompetence of venous valves → **stasis, congestion, oedema, pain, thrombosis**
- Secondary tissue ischaemia from chronic venous congestion and poor vessel drainage:
  - **Stasis dermatitis** (also called "brawny induration") — brawny colour from haemolysis of extravasated red cells
  - **Ulcerations** — poor wound healing; superimposed infections common
-  **Embolism from superficial varicose veins is very rare** — in contrast to relatively frequent thromboembolism from **deep vein thrombosis**

**Two other important varicosity sites:**
- **Oesophageal varices** — from portal hypertension (covered in GI pathology notes)
- **Haemorrhoids** — anal and perianal collaterals; from portal hypertension or straining

### Phlebothrombosis and Thrombophlebitis
- **Phlebothrombosis** — thrombosis of veins with only **minimal inflammatory response**
- **Thrombophlebitis** — venous thrombosis associated with **prominent venous wall inflammation**
- Together these account for **at least 90% of clinical venous disease**

**Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT):**
- Most often involves **deep leg veins** (popliteal, femoral, iliac)
- Major risk: **pulmonary embolism** — thrombi may propagate to involve larger veins, dislodge, and embolise
- Risk factors (**Virchow's triad**): stasis + hypercoagulability + endothelial injury
- Clinical: leg pain, swelling, warmth — but up to 50% are clinically silent
-  Unlike superficial varicosities, **deep vein thrombosis is a major source of pulmonary embolism**

**Migratory Thrombophlebitis (Trousseau Syndrome):**
- Repeated thrombophlebitis in different venous sites
- Associated with **occult visceral malignancy** (especially pancreatic, lung, GI carcinomas)
- Mechanism: tumour-derived mucins and other procoagulants activate clotting

### SECTION 2: LYMPHATIC DISORDERS

### Lymphangitis
- Acute inflammation from **spread of bacterial infections into lymphatics**
- Most common agent: **group A β-haemolytic Streptococcus**
- **Morphology:** affected lymphatics dilated and filled with exudate of neutrophils and monocytes; infiltrates can extend through vessel wall → **cellulitis or focal abscesses**
- **Clinical features:**
  - **Red, painful subcutaneous streaks** (inflamed lymphatics tracking proximally)
  - **Painful enlargement of draining lymph nodes (lymphadenitis)**
  - If bacteria not contained within lymph nodes → escape into venous circulation → **bacteraemia or sepsis**

### Lymphoedema

**Primary Lymphoedema:**
- Isolated congenital defect (simple congenital lymphoedema)
- **Milroy disease** (heredofamilial congenital lymphoedema) — lymphatic agenesis or hypoplasia

**Secondary (Obstructive) Lymphoedema — Causes:**
- **Tumours** obstructing lymphatic channels or regional lymph nodes
- **Surgical procedures** severing lymphatic connections (e.g. axillary lymph node resection in radical mastectomy)
- **Post-radiation fibrosis**
- **Filariasis** — parasitic obstruction (most common worldwide cause of massive lymphoedema)
- **Post-inflammatory thrombosis and scarring**

**Pathophysiology:**
- Blockage → increased hydrostatic pressure distal to obstruction → **oedema**
- Chronic oedema → ECM deposition + fibrosis → **brawny induration** or **peau d'orange** appearance of overlying skin
- Inadequate tissue perfusion → **skin ulceration**

**Rupture of dilated lymphatics:**
- **Chylous ascites** — abdomen
- **Chylothorax** — thorax
- **Chylopericardium** — pericardium

### SECTION 3: VASCULAR TUMOURS

### Classification Overview

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Benign** | Hemangioma (capillary, cavernous), pyogenic granuloma, lymphangioma, glomus tumour, vascular ectasias |
| **Reactive/Borderline** | Bacillary angiomatosis, Kaposi sarcoma, hemangioendothelioma |
| **Malignant** | Angiosarcoma, hemangiopericytoma |

**General Principles:**
- Vascular neoplasms arise from **endothelium** (hemangioma, lymphangioma, angiosarcoma) OR from **cells supporting blood vessels** (glomus tumour)
- **Benign tumours:** obvious vascular channels filled with blood cells, lined by a **monolayer of normal-appearing ECs**
- **Malignant tumours:** more solidly cellular, proliferative, exhibit **cytologic atypia**; usually do not form well-organised vessels
- Endothelial origin of malignant tumours confirmed by **immunohistochemical staining for CD31 or von Willebrand factor**

### Vascular Ectasias
- Generic term for **local dilation of existing vessels** — NOT true neoplasms
- **Telangiectasia** — permanent dilation of pre-existing small vessels (capillaries, venules, arterioles), usually in skin or mucous membranes

**Nevus Flammeus ("Birthmark"):**
- Most common form of vascular ectasia
- Light pink to deep purple flat lesion on head or neck composed of dilated vessels
- **Most ultimately regress spontaneously**
- **Port wine stain** — type of nevus flammeus that tends to **grow during childhood**, thickening skin, and **persists over time**
- Port wine stain in **trigeminal nerve distribution** → **Sturge-Weber syndrome** (encephalotrigeminal angiomatosis):
  - Facial port wine nevi
  - Ipsilateral venous angiomas in cortical leptomeninges
  - Intellectual disability, seizures, hemiplegia, skull radiopacities
  - Caused by **somatic GNAQ missense mutation** (Gα subunit)

**Spider Telangiectasias:**
- Nonneoplastic vascular malformations grossly resembling a spider
- Radial, **often pulsatile lesions** composed of dilated subcutaneous arteries/arterioles about a central core — **blanch with pressure**
- Commonly on face, neck, upper chest
- Most frequently associated with **hyperestrogenic states** (pregnant women, cirrhosis)

**Hereditary Haemorrhagic Telangiectasia (Osler-Weber-Rendu Disease):**
- **Autosomal dominant** — mutations in genes encoding components of **TGF-β signalling pathway** in ECs
- Telangiectasias = malformations of dilated capillaries and veins — **present at birth**
- Widely distributed: skin, oral mucous membranes, respiratory, GI, and urinary tracts
- Lesions can **spontaneously rupture** → serious **epistaxis, GI bleeding, haematuria**

### Benign Vascular Tumours

### Hemangiomas
- Very common tumours composed of **blood-filled vessels**
- Constitute **7% of all benign tumours of infancy and childhood**
- Most present from birth; many eventually **regress spontaneously**
- Typically confined to head, neck, and thoracic skin; can also arise internally
- **Nearly one third of internal lesions occur in the liver**
- Malignant transformation: **rare**

### Capillary Hemangiomas
- **Most common type**
- Occur in skin, subcutaneous tissues, and mucous membranes (oral cavities, lips, liver, spleen, kidneys)
- Histology: thin-walled capillaries with scant stroma

### Juvenile (Strawberry) Hemangiomas
- Extremely common in newborn skin — **1 in 200 births**; can be multiple
- **Grow rapidly for a few months** → fade by **1–3 years** → completely regress by **age 7** in most cases

### Cavernous Hemangiomas
- Composed of **large, dilated vascular channels**
- More **infiltrative** than capillary type; frequently involve deep structures; **do not spontaneously regress**
- Difficult to distinguish from malignant counterparts on imaging
- Histology: sharply delineated but **unencapsulated**; large cavernous blood-filled vascular spaces separated by connective tissue stroma
- **Intravascular thrombosis with dystrophic calcification** common
- Can be locally destructive; often of little clinical significance except cosmetic or traumatic bleeding risk
- **Brain hemangiomas** — problematic (compression or rupture)
- **Component of von Hippel-Lindau disease** — vascular lesions in cerebellum, brain stem, retina, pancreas, liver

### Pyogenic Granulomas
- Capillary hemangiomas presenting as **rapidly growing red pedunculated lesions** on skin, gingiva, or oral mucosa
- Bleed easily; often ulcerated
- Roughly **a quarter follow trauma** — reach 1–2 cm within weeks
- **Granuloma gravidarum** — occurs in gingiva of ~1% of pregnant women; may regress after pregnancy or require excision
- Treatment: **curettage and cautery** usually curative

### Lymphangiomas
- Benign lymphatic counterparts of hemangiomas

### Simple (Capillary) Lymphangiomas
- Slightly elevated or pedunculated lesions up to **1–2 cm**
- Occur predominantly in **head, neck, and axillary** subcutaneous tissues
- Histology: networks of endothelium-lined spaces — distinguished from capillary hemangiomas by **lymphatic endothelial markers** (VEGFR-3, LYVE-1) or absence of erythrocytes

### Cavernous Lymphangiomas (Cystic Hygromas)
- Typically found in **neck or axilla of children**; rarely in retroperitoneum
- Can be enormous — up to **15 cm**; filling axilla or producing gross deformities
- **Common in Turner syndrome**
- Composed of massively dilated lymphatic spaces; **indistinct, unencapsulated margins** → resection difficult

### Glomus Tumour (Glomangioma)
- Benign but **exquisitely painful tumour** arising from modified SMCs of **glomus bodies** (arteriovenous structures involved in thermoregulation)
- Arise from **smooth muscle cells, NOT endothelial cells** — superficially resemble hemangiomas
- Most commonly found in **distal digits, especially under fingernails**
- Treatment: **excision is curative**

### Reactive Vascular Proliferations

### Bacillary Angiomatosis
- Vascular proliferation in **immunocompromised hosts** (especially **AIDS patients**)
- Caused by opportunistic bacilli of the **Bartonella family**
- Two species:
  - **Bartonella henselae** — reservoir = domestic cat; causes **cat-scratch disease** (necrotising granulomatous lymphadenopathy) in immunocompetent hosts
  - **Bartonella quintana** — transmitted by human body lice; caused **trench fever** in World War I
- Lesions involve skin, bone, brain, and other organs

**Pathogenesis:**
- Bacteria induce host tissues to produce **HIF-1α** → drives **VEGF production** → vascular proliferation
- Infections and lesions **cleared by antibiotics**

**Morphology:**
- Skin: red papules and nodules or rounded subcutaneous masses
- Histology: capillary proliferation with prominent **epithelioid ECs with nuclear atypia and mitoses** + scattered stromal neutrophils + causal bacteria (visible on **Warthin-Starry silver stain**)

### Intermediate-Grade Tumours

### Kaposi Sarcoma (KS)
- Vascular neoplasm caused by **human herpesvirus 8 (HHV8)** — also known as Kaposi sarcoma herpesvirus (KSHV)
- By far most common in **AIDS patients**; its presence is used as a criterion for **AIDS diagnosis**

**Four Forms:**

| Form | Features |
|---|---|
| **Classic KS** | Older men of **Mediterranean, Middle Eastern, or Eastern European** descent; NOT HIV-associated; multiple red-purple skin plaques/nodules on **distal lower extremities**; slowly progressive; localised |
| **Endemic African KS** | HIV-seronegative individuals **<40 years**; aggressive lymph node involvement; **severe form in prepubertal children** with lymph node and visceral involvement — **nearly 100% mortality within 3 years** |
| **Transplant-associated KS** | Solid-organ transplant recipients receiving **T-cell immunosuppression**; risk **100-fold greater** than immunocompetent; aggressive; involves lymph nodes, mucosa, viscera; **may regress with reduced immunosuppression** |
| **AIDS-associated (Epidemic) KS** | Most common HIV-related malignancy worldwide; occurs **300-fold more** than in transplant recipients; **1000-fold greater** than in general population; lymph node and visceral dissemination early; **incidence fell >80%** with antiretroviral therapy |

**Pathogenesis:**
- Virtually all KS lesions infected by HHV8 — a **γ-herpesvirus** like EBV
- Transmitted sexually and via oral secretions/cutaneous exposures
- **HHV8 lytic infection:** virally encoded G protein induces VEGF → endothelial growth; cytokines from inflammatory cells create proliferative milieu
- **HHV8 latent infection:** viral proteins disrupt proliferation controls (viral cyclin D homologue) and prevent apoptosis (inhibits p53)
- HHV8 + **altered T-cell immunity** both required; acquired somatic mutations also contribute

**Morphology — Three Stages (Classic KS):**
1.  **Patches** — red-purple macules; dilated irregular EC-lined vascular spaces with interspersed lymphocytes, plasma cells, macrophages; can resemble granulation tissue
2.  **Plaques** — larger, violaceous, raised; dermal accumulations of dilated jagged vascular channels lined by plump spindle cells; extravasated erythrocytes, haemosiderin-laden macrophages
3.  **Nodules** — distinctly neoplastic; sheets of plump proliferating spindle cells; small vessels and slitlike spaces with red cells; marked haemorrhage, haemosiderin; mitotic figures common; **round pink cytoplasmic globules** (degenerating erythrocytes in phagolysosomes); nodular stage heralds lymph node and visceral involvement

**Clinical Features:**
- Classic KS: largely restricted to skin → surgical resection usually adequate
- Transplant-associated: withdrawal of immunosuppression often effective
- AIDS-associated: antiretroviral therapy generally beneficial; interferon-γ and angiogenesis inhibitors also used

### Malignant Vascular Tumours

### Angiosarcoma
- **Malignant endothelial neoplasm** — histology varies from well-differentiated (resembling hemangioma) to profoundly anaplastic
- Older adults; males and females; **any site** — most often **skin, soft tissue, breast, liver**
- **5-year survival approximately 30%** — locally invasive, readily metastasise

**Special Associations:**
- **Post-mastectomy lymphangiosarcoma** — arises in ipsilateral upper extremity several years after radical mastectomy with lymph node resection/radiation; presumably from lymphatic vessels
- **Hepatic angiosarcoma** — associated with **arsenic** (pesticides), **Thorotrast** (radioactive contrast agent), **polyvinyl chloride** (classic human chemical carcinogenesis example) — all with **long latency** between exposure and tumour development
- Can also arise from radiation and rarely from prolonged insertion of foreign material (prosthetic devices)

**Morphology:**
- Cutaneous angiosarcomas: begin as multiple deceptively small **red papules/nodules** → large fleshy masses of red-tan to grey-white tissue with blurring margins
- Necrosis and haemorrhage common
- Microscopy: all degrees of differentiation — from plump atypical ECs forming vascular channels → wildly undifferentiated tumours with no discernible blood vessels
- Endothelial origin confirmed by: **CD31 or von Willebrand factor immunostaining**

### SECTION 4: PATHOLOGY OF VASCULAR INTERVENTION

### Endovascular Stenting
- Arterial stenoses (especially coronary) dilated by **balloon angioplasty** → more than **95% of endovascular coronary procedures** now combine angioplasty with **coronary stent placement**
- **Coronary stents** — expandable metallic mesh tubes; provide larger, more regular lumen; tack down intimal flaps; limit vascular spasm
- **Immediate complication:** thrombosis from endothelial injury → patients must receive **potent antithrombotic agents** (primarily platelet antagonists)

**In-Stent Restenosis:**
- Long-term limitation of angioplasty
- **Proliferative intimal thickening** from SMC ingrowth, proliferation, and ECM synthesis — all driven by initial vascular wall injury
- Can cause clinically significant luminal occlusion in **up to one third of patients** within **6–12 months** of stenting

**Drug-Eluting Stents:**
- Leach **antiproliferative drugs** (paclitaxel or sirolimus) into adjacent vessel wall → block SMC activation
- Duration of drug elution: short (**days to weeks**)
- Reduce restenosis incidence at 1 year by **50–80%**
-  Antiproliferative effect prolongs time to **reendothelialisation** → patients require **extended anticoagulation** to prevent stent thrombosis

### Vascular Replacement

**Large-bore synthetic conduits (12–18 mm):**
- Function well in **high-flow locations** (aorta)

**Small-diameter artificial grafts (≤8 mm):**
- Generally **fail** due to early thrombosis or late intimal hyperplasia (at the junction with native vasculature)

**Small-bore vessel replacement:**
- Required for coronary bypass → grafts fashioned from **saphenous veins** (from patient's own leg) or **left internal mammary artery**

### Saphenous Vein Grafts
- Long-term patency: **only ~50% at 10 years**
- Occlude due to: thrombosis (early) + intimal thickening (months to years post-op) + **vein graft atherosclerosis** — sometimes with plaque rupture, thrombosis, or aneurysms

### Internal Mammary Artery Grafts
- **>90% patent at 10 years** — far superior to saphenous vein grafts
- Can only be used to bypass the **left anterior descending artery**

## Practice Questions
1.  **Question:** What is a key difference in embolism risk between superficial varicose veins and deep vein thrombosis (DVT)? → **Answer:** Embolism from superficial varicose veins is very rare, in contrast to the relatively frequent thromboembolism from deep vein thrombosis (DVT).
2.  **Question:** What clinical syndrome involves migratory thrombophlebitis and is associated with occult visceral malignancy? → **Answer:** Trousseau syndrome.
3.  **Question:** What is the most common bacterial agent causing lymphangitis? → **Answer:** Group A β-haemolytic Streptococcus.
4.  **Question:** What is the most common worldwide cause of massive lymphoedema? → **Answer:** Filariasis (parasitic obstruction).
5.  **Question:** What are the characteristic features of Sturge-Weber syndrome? → **Answer:** Facial port wine nevi in a trigeminal nerve distribution, ipsilateral venous angiomas in cortical leptomeninges, intellectual disability, seizures, hemiplegia, and skull radiopacities, caused by a somatic GNAQ missense mutation.
6.  **Question:** What is the typical clinical course of juvenile (strawberry) hemangiomas in newborns? → **Answer:** They grow rapidly for a few months, then fade by 1–3 years, and completely regress by age 7 in most cases.
7.  **Question:** What is a characteristic clinical symptom and common location of a glomus tumour? → **Answer:** It is an exquisitely painful tumour most commonly found in distal digits, especially under fingernails.
8.  **Question:** What three chemical exposures are specifically associated with hepatic angiosarcoma? → **Answer:** Arsenic (pesticides), Thorotrast (radioactive contrast agent), and polyvinyl chloride.
