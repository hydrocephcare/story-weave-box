# Osteoarthritis

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Rheumatology**

> **In one line:** osteoarthritis (OA) is a chronic, degenerative joint disease in which articular cartilage breaks down and the underlying bone and joint margins respond with sclerosis and osteophytes, giving mechanical pain, stiffness and loss of movement. It is the commonest arthritis; it is not simply "wear and tear" but an interplay of mechanical, genetic, inflammatory and biochemical factors. Management is stepwise: non-drug measures first, then paracetamol, NSAIDs, topical agents and joint injections, with joint replacement for end-stage disease.

## What you must know

- OA is the **commonest type of arthritis**; it was once thought a normal consequence of ageing but results from multiple interacting factors.
- Prevalence rises with age: about **27% in people under 60** and **44% above 70** in the slide series; almost everyone has symptoms by 70.
- Before age 55 men and women are affected equally; **after 55 it is commoner in women**.
- It is a **non-inflammatory (mechanical)** arthritis: pain worse with use and better with rest, **short morning stiffness** (under 30 minutes), bony enlargement, crepitus.
- Radiographic hallmarks: **joint space narrowing, osteophytes, subchondral sclerosis, subchondral cysts**.
- ESR and rheumatoid factor are used mainly to **exclude other arthritis**; OA itself has normal inflammatory markers.
- Treatment ladder: exercise, weight loss and physiotherapy; **paracetamol**; **NSAIDs** (lowest dose, shortest time); topical NSAID or capsaicin; **intra-articular steroid**; surgery.
- **Avoid NSAIDs** in the elderly, peptic ulcer, renal impairment, anticoagulation and cardiovascular risk.

## 1. Definition and epidemiology

- A chronic condition characterised by **breakdown of the joint's cartilage**, so that bone surfaces rub on each other, causing pain, stiffness and loss of movement.
- OA is not purely age-related. It reflects **joint integrity, genetic predisposition, local inflammation, mechanical forces, and cellular and biochemical processes**.
- OA dates back to ancient humans; evidence has been found in ice-age skeletons.
- Symptoms usually appear in **middle age**.

## 2. Aetiology and risk factors

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Primary (idiopathic)** | age, female sex after menopause, genetic (nodal generalised OA of the hands), obesity |
| **Secondary** | previous joint trauma or fracture, developmental dysplasia of the hip, slipped femoral epiphysis, Perthes' disease, previous septic or inflammatory arthritis (RA, gout), avascular necrosis, haemochromatosis, acromegaly, haemophilic joints, neuropathic (Charcot) joints, occupational overuse |

> **Remember:** obesity is the main **modifiable** risk factor, especially for the knee, because each kg of body weight adds several kg of load across the joint.

## 3. Pathophysiology

1. Chondrocytes fail to maintain the cartilage matrix; proteoglycan content falls and water content rises, so the cartilage softens.
2. Collagen network is damaged (matrix metalloproteinases, IL-1 and TNF), and cartilage fibrillates and erodes.
3. Underlying bone responds with **sclerosis** (eburnation) and **cysts**.
4. At the joint margins, new bone forms as **osteophytes**.
5. Mild synovitis and capsular thickening develop; the muscle around the joint weakens.

## 4. Clinical features

### Symptoms

- **Pain** on use, relieved by rest; later pain at rest and at night.
- **Stiffness** after inactivity ("gelling"), lasting under 30 minutes, and morning stiffness short.
- Loss of function: difficulty with stairs, walking, dressing.
- Instability or giving way (knee), locking if loose bodies.

### Signs

- Bony swelling (osteophytes), crepitus, tenderness along the joint line.
- Reduced and painful range of movement; deformity (varus knee, fixed flexion).
- Small effusion; muscle wasting (quadriceps).
- **Hand OA:** Heberden's nodes (DIP) and Bouchard's nodes (PIP); squaring of the thumb base (first carpometacarpal joint). MCP joints are usually spared.

### Commonly involved joints

Knee, hip, first carpometacarpal joint, DIPs and PIPs, cervical and lumbar spine, first metatarsophalangeal joint. Wrist, elbow and ankle are unusual unless secondary.

> **Exam tip:** MCP, wrist and elbow involvement in a suspected OA should raise the suspicion of another cause (RA, haemochromatosis, calcium pyrophosphate disease).

## 5. Investigations

If possible, in suspected OA, particularly of the knee or hip:

- **ESR** (normal or mildly raised in OA; markedly raised suggests inflammatory disease).
- **Rheumatoid factor** (to exclude RA).
- **Synovial fluid evaluation** (clear, viscous, WBC under 2000/mm3; look for crystals).
- **Radiographs** of the affected joints.

| Radiographic feature (LOSS) | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **L**oss of joint space | cartilage loss, asymmetric |
| **O**steophytes | marginal new bone |
| **S**ubchondral sclerosis | bone thickening |
| **S**ubchondral cysts | cystic change beneath cartilage |

- Radiographic change and symptoms correlate poorly.
- MRI is not routinely needed.

## 6. Differential diagnosis

Rheumatoid arthritis, gout and pseudogout (calcium pyrophosphate), septic arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, reactive arthritis, avascular necrosis, tuberculous arthritis (important in Kenya, usually monoarticular and destructive).

## 7. Management

### Goals

- Control pain and swelling.
- Prevent disability.
- Improve quality of life.
- Prevent progression.
- Educate the patient about his or her role in the management team.

### Non-pharmacological

- **Weight loss.**
- **Rest** of the painful joint (relative), with canes and walkers as aids.
- **Physical therapy and orthoses:** braces, splints, therapeutic shoes, knee taping.
- **Exercise:** programmes for symptom relief, joint protection, disability prevention; aerobic versus resistance; adherence matters.
- Psychosocial support and patient education.
- All patients should see their doctor for a careful history and examination **before starting an exercise programme**. A comprehensive evaluation is the first step in designing an individualised programme, assessing:
  - **arthritis-related factors:** current medications, joint pain, inflammation, stability, range of movement
  - **impairments of inactivity:** altered body composition, muscle weakness, poor cardiovascular fitness

### Drug therapy

- Used **only if non-drug measures fail**, and not needed when symptoms are absent or minimal.
- The major drugs are analgesics: **paracetamol (acetaminophen), NSAIDs and intra-articular glucocorticoids**.
- Most drugs relieve pain; some target other symptoms or slowing progression (no proven disease-modifying drug).

| Step | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| **1. Paracetamol** | initial treatment in non-inflammatory OA, especially mild to moderate pain (Grade 1A) |
| **2. Oral NSAID** | if paracetamol is inadequate, inflammatory OA, or severe pain: a non-selective NSAID or COX-2 selective NSAID (coxib), at the **lowest dose for the shortest time** (Grade 1B) |
| **3. Topical NSAID** | mild to moderate pain with intolerance or contraindication to oral NSAIDs: e.g. **diclofenac gel**, in preference to an opioid or capsaicin (Grade 2C) |
| **4. Capsaicin (topical)** | if oral and topical NSAIDs give inadequate benefit, as an adjunct or alternative, rather than an opioid (Grade 2C) |
| **5. Intra-articular glucocorticoid** | moderate to severe pain in one or a few joints not relieved by oral drugs, or inflammatory OA with NSAID contraindication, rather than opioids or other injections (Grade 2B) |
| **6. Resistant disease** | opioid analgesics, intra-articular hyaluronans, glucosamine and chondroitin, other agents including colchicine |

- **Recurring swelling or inflammation** should prompt **synovial fluid aspiration (arthrocentesis)** to look for crystal disease or other arthropathy.

### Using NSAIDs safely

- **Adults over 60 or with CVS or GI risk should avoid NSAIDs**; the patient must be told about absolute and relative contraindications, especially cardiovascular and GI.
- Short to medium term: **naproxen 220 to 375 mg once or twice daily** (chosen for the lowest cardiovascular risk even at maximum dose). If it is contraindicated and no CVS risk: **ibuprofen 200 to 400 mg two or three times daily**.
- Allow **2 to 4 weeks** to judge efficacy. If the starting dose fails, increase gradually to the maximum while warning the patient to watch for GI side effects and rash.
- If there is no relief after 2 to 4 weeks at maximum dose, **change to a different NSAID**. After **three different NSAIDs** without relief, try additional therapy.

**Contraindications / cautions for NSAIDs**
- Peptic ulcer or high GI risk.
- Aspirin use for cardiovascular prevention.
- Warfarin or other anticoagulation.
- Increased cardiovascular risk.
- Risk of renal insufficiency.
- Aspirin-sensitivity syndrome.

> **Remember:** give a gastroprotective drug (PPI) if an NSAID is unavoidable in a patient at GI risk, and monitor blood pressure and renal function.

### Indications for needle aspiration or injection, stronger analgesia or surgery

- Uncertain diagnosis (aspirate).
- Needle aspiration or injection.
- Narcotic analgesics and other alternative therapy.
- **Severe OA unrelieved by oral, topical and intra-articular medication:** consider **surgery**, such as arthroplasty (total hip or knee replacement), osteotomy, or arthrodesis.

## 8. Complications and prognosis

- Progressive pain and disability, loss of independence.
- Secondary effects: depression, weight gain from inactivity, falls.
- Drug complications: GI bleeding, renal failure, hypertension with NSAIDs.
- Course is slow and variable; many patients stabilise for years.

## Key points to revise

1. OA is the commonest arthritis, a mechanical, non-inflammatory disease of cartilage with secondary bone change.
2. After age 55 it is commoner in women; prevalence rises steeply after 60.
3. Features: pain on use, short stiffness, crepitus, bony swelling, Heberden's and Bouchard's nodes.
4. X-ray shows loss of joint space, osteophytes, sclerosis and cysts; ESR is normal.
5. Treat first with exercise, weight loss, physiotherapy and aids; then paracetamol.
6. Use NSAIDs at the lowest dose for the shortest time and avoid them in the elderly and in GI, renal and CV risk; naproxen has the lowest CV risk.
7. Topical diclofenac or capsaicin, then intra-articular steroid for one or a few joints.
8. Recurrent effusions need arthrocentesis for crystals; severe disease needs joint replacement.
