### 1. Attenuated Vaccine
**Question:** A child born in Muranga was vaccinated with an attenuated vaccine. Explain briefly how it is supposed to protect the child from the particular viral infection
- Attenuated vaccine contains **live but weakened** virus — unable to cause disease but able to replicate
- Replication stimulates **both humoral and cell-mediated immunity**
- B cells produce **specific antibodies** (IgM initially, then IgG)
- **Memory B and T cells** are generated and persist long-term
- On future exposure to virulent virus, a rapid **anamnestic (secondary) immune response** is mounted
- Antibodies neutralise virus before it causes disease
- Produces **long-lasting immunity**, often lifelong, mimicking natural infection

### 2. OPV vs IPV
**Question:** OPV and IPV are attenuated and inactivated vaccines respectively. Describe how they were produced and explain why some countries prefer IPV over OPV
**Production:**
- **OPV (Oral Polio Vaccine / Sabin)** — poliovirus passaged repeatedly through non-human cell cultures at low temperatures until virulence is attenuated; virus is alive but weakened
- **IPV (Inactivated Polio Vaccine / Salk)** — wild poliovirus grown in culture then inactivated with **formalin**; virus is killed and cannot replicate
**Why some countries prefer IPV:**
- OPV can revert to virulent form — causes **Vaccine-Associated Paralytic Poliomyelitis (VAPP)**
- IPV cannot revert — **no risk of VAPP**
- IPV preferred in countries where wild polio has been eliminated
- OPV still preferred where polio is endemic — cheaper, oral administration, induces gut mucosal immunity

### 3. Basic Principle of Vaccination
**Question:** What is the basic principle of vaccination? How do vaccines prevent microbial infections?
**Basic principle:**
- Based on **immunological memory**
- Expose the immune system to an antigen without causing disease
- Primes the immune system to respond rapidly and powerfully upon real infection
**How vaccines prevent infection:**
- Vaccine antigen is processed by **antigen-presenting cells (APCs)**
- APCs activate **T helper cells (CD4+)**
- T helper cells stimulate **B cells** to differentiate and produce specific antibodies
- **Cytotoxic T cells (CD8+)** are also activated — important for intracellular pathogens
- **Memory B and T cells** persist long-term in lymphoid tissue
- On natural exposure: memory cells mount a fast, strong secondary immune response
- Virus is neutralised before clinical disease can develop

### 4. Active vs Passive Immunization
**Question:** Explain the mechanism of active immunization and how it differs from passive immunization
**Active immunization:**
- Host's **own immune system** is stimulated by an antigen
- Takes days to weeks to develop full immunity
- Produces **long-lasting** memory cells and antibodies
- Includes live attenuated, killed/inactivated, subunit, and toxoid vaccines
**Passive immunization:**
- **Preformed antibodies** are transferred directly to the host
- No immune stimulation or memory cell generation
- Provides **immediate** but **short-lived** protection — antibodies degraded over weeks to months
- Examples: immunoglobulin injections, maternal antibodies passed to neonate, anti-rabies immunoglobulin post-exposure

### 5. Interferon Mechanism
**Question:** Explain how interferon helps virus-infected cells fight back the infection
- Virus-infected cell produces **Type I interferons** (IFN-alpha and IFN-beta) in response to viral dsRNA
- Interferons are secreted and bind receptors on **neighbouring uninfected cells**
- Triggers the **JAK-STAT signalling pathway**
- Upregulates antiviral proteins: **Protein kinase R (PKR)**, **RNase L**, **Mx proteins**
- PKR phosphorylates eIF-2alpha — **halts protein synthesis**, preventing viral replication
- RNase L degrades viral RNA
- Interferons activate **NK cells** and **macrophages**
- Upregulate **MHC class I** expression — enhances recognition and destruction of infected cells by cytotoxic T cells
- Net result: viral spread is slowed while adaptive immunity develops

### 6. Herpes Simplex Virus
**Question:** Name the portal of entry of HSV1 and HSV2. Mention 3 diseases caused by herpes simplex viruses
**Portal of entry:**
- **HSV-1** — oral mucosa, skin (especially face), respiratory tract
- **HSV-2** — genital mucosa and skin; transmitted at birth (neonatal herpes)
**Diseases caused:**
- Oral herpes / cold sores (herpes labialis) — HSV-1
- Genital herpes — HSV-2 (occasionally HSV-1)
- Herpes encephalitis — HSV-1; most common cause of sporadic viral encephalitis
- Neonatal herpes — HSV-2; acquired during delivery
- Herpetic keratoconjunctivitis — HSV-1
- Herpetic whitlow — infection of the finger

### 7. Viral Receptors and Spikes
**Question:** Discuss in detail the clinical importance of viral receptors/spikes. You may give specific examples to support your answer
- Viral surface proteins (spikes) mediate **attachment to specific host cell receptors** — the essential first step of infection
- Determine **host range and tissue tropism** — explain why certain viruses infect specific organs
- Key examples:
  - **HIV gp120** binds CD4 and CCR5/CXCR4 co-receptors — tropism for T helper cells and macrophages
  - **Influenza haemagglutinin (HA)** binds sialic acid on respiratory epithelium
  - **SARS-CoV-2 spike protein** binds ACE2 receptors on lung and gut epithelium
  - **Rabies virus** binds nicotinic acetylcholine receptors at neuromuscular junctions — explains neurotropism
  - **Rhinovirus** binds ICAM-1 on nasal epithelium
- Receptors are prime targets for **vaccine development** e.g. spike protein vaccines for COVID-19
- Receptors are targets for **antiviral drugs** e.g. fusion inhibitors, receptor blockers
- Receptor binding triggers **conformational change** enabling membrane fusion and viral entry
- Mutations in host receptor genes can confer **natural resistance** e.g. CCR5-delta32 mutation — resistance to HIV
- Understanding receptors explains **species barriers** and **zoonotic spillover** events

### 8. Reverse Transcriptase and Integrase in HIV
**Question:** Discuss the importance of reverse transcriptase and integrase in the pathogenesis of HIV infection. Explain how antiretroviral drugs targeting these enzymes are selectively toxic
**Reverse Transcriptase (RT):**
- HIV carries its genome as **single-stranded RNA**
- RT converts viral RNA → **complementary DNA** (reverse transcription)
- Produces RNA-DNA hybrid; RT's **RNase H activity** then degrades the RNA strand
- RT synthesises second DNA strand → **double-stranded DNA (dsDNA)**
- Without RT, HIV cannot replicate or integrate into host genome
- RT has **high error rate with no proofreading** — generates mutations leading to drug resistance and immune evasion
**Integrase:**
- Viral dsDNA is transported to the nucleus
- Integrase catalyses **integration** of viral DNA into the host chromosome → forms **provirus**
- Provirus is **permanent** — persists for the life of the cell
- Allows **latent infection** and viral reservoir formation in resting CD4+ T cells
- Upon cell activation, host cell transcribes proviral DNA producing new virions
**Selective toxicity:**
- Human cells have **no reverse transcriptase** — RT is entirely virus-specific
- **NRTIs** (e.g. zidovudine, tenofovir) — nucleoside analogues that terminate DNA chain elongation; lack 3'-OH group
- **NNRTIs** (e.g. efavirenz, nevirapine) — bind allosteric site on RT and block its activity
- **INSTIs** (e.g. raltegravir, dolutegravir) — block the integration step
- Human cells use **different DNA polymerases** — drugs do not significantly inhibit them
- Result: drugs are highly toxic to virus but minimally toxic to host cells = **selective toxicity**

### 9. Cytomegalovirus Pathogenesis
**Question:** Describe pathogenesis of cytomegalovirus infection
- Primary infection usually **asymptomatic** in immunocompetent individuals
- Virus enters via mucous membranes — oral, genital, respiratory — or via blood transfusion, organ transplant, breast milk
- Replicates in **salivary glands, kidneys, and lymphocytes**
- Disseminates via infected **monocytes and lymphocytes** — cell-associated viraemia
- Establishes **latency** in myeloid progenitor cells and monocytes
- In immunocompromised: reactivation leads to end-organ disease — retinitis, pneumonitis, colitis, encephalitis
- Causes characteristic **"owl-eye" intranuclear inclusion bodies** in infected cells on histology
- CMV evades immunity by downregulating MHC class I expression on infected cells

### 10. CMV in Congenital Infections
**Question:** Discuss the various manifestations caused by CMV in congenital infections. Describe cytomegalic preventive measures and diagnosis
**Clinical manifestations:**
- Most common congenital viral infection worldwide
- Majority (90%) of infected neonates are **asymptomatic at birth**
- Symptomatic congenital CMV — cytomegalic inclusion disease:
  - **Hepatosplenomegaly**
  - **Jaundice** — conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia
  - **Petechiae and thrombocytopaenia**
  - **Microcephaly**
  - **Periventricular calcifications** on brain imaging
  - **Chorioretinitis** — vision impairment
  - **Sensorineural hearing loss** — most common long-term sequela, even in initially asymptomatic neonates
  - Intrauterine growth restriction
  - Intellectual disability and developmental delay
**Prevention:**
- No licensed vaccine currently available
- Hygiene measures — handwashing, avoid contact with urine and saliva of young children
- Antiviral prophylaxis — ganciclovir/valganciclovir in high-risk transplant recipients
**Diagnosis:**
- PCR — most sensitive and widely used
- Shell vial culture — traditional gold standard
- CMV pp65 antigenaemia test
- Serology — IgM/IgG (less useful in neonates)
- Histology — owl-eye intranuclear inclusions

### 11. Adenovirus
**Question:** Describe the modes of transmission, associated diseases, and prevention strategies for adenoviruses
**Modes of transmission:**
- **Respiratory droplets** — most common; causes pharyngitis and pneumonia
- **Faecal-oral route** — causes gastroenteritis
- **Direct contact and fomites** — causes conjunctivitis
- **Waterborne** — swimming pools; epidemic keratoconjunctivitis
- **Blood or organ transplant** — in immunocompromised patients
- **Autoinoculation** — eye infection from contaminated fingers
**Associated diseases:**
- Acute febrile pharyngitis
- Pharyngoconjunctival fever
- Epidemic keratoconjunctivitis
- Acute respiratory disease — classic in military recruits
- Pneumonia
- Gastroenteritis — especially in young children
- Haemorrhagic cystitis
- Meningitis and encephalitis — rare
**Prevention:**
- Hand hygiene and respiratory etiquette
- Chlorination of swimming pools
- Avoid sharing towels and eye drops
- Live oral adenovirus vaccine (types 4 and 7) — used in military recruits
- Strict infection control in healthcare settings

### 12. Rabies
**Question:** Describe the pathogenesis of rabies infection. What medication does a person need after exposure to rabies virus?
**Pathogenesis:**
- Entry via bite wound — infected saliva contacts mucosa or broken skin
- Virus replicates locally at bite site in **muscle cells**
- Binds **nicotinic acetylcholine receptors** and enters peripheral nerve endings
- Travels via **retrograde axonal transport** to CNS — spinal cord then brain
- Causes encephalitis — predominant involvement of **limbic system**, hippocampus, and brainstem
- **Negri bodies** — eosinophilic intracytoplasmic inclusions in Purkinje cells and hippocampal neurons — pathognomonic
- Spreads centrifugally to salivary glands, cornea, and skin
- Causes hydrophobia, agitation, hypersalivation, paralysis, coma, then death
**Post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP):**
- **Wound washing** — soap and water immediately, then iodine or 70% alcohol
- **Rabies immunoglobulin (RIG)** — injected into and around wound; provides immediate passive protection
- **Rabies vaccine** — 4-dose series on days 0, 3, 7, and 14; active immunisation for long-term immunity
- PEP is nearly 100% effective if given promptly before symptoms develop

### 13. Viral Encephalitis
**Question:** What is viral encephalitis? Name three viruses which can cause encephalitis. Describe the pathogenesis
**Definition:**
- Inflammation of brain parenchyma caused by viral infection
- Characterised by fever, headache, altered consciousness, seizures, and focal neurological deficits
**Viruses that cause encephalitis:**
- Herpes simplex virus (HSV-1) — most common sporadic cause
- Rabies virus
- Japanese encephalitis virus
- West Nile virus
- Enteroviruses e.g. Enterovirus 71
- CMV, EBV, VZV in immunocompromised individuals
**Pathogenesis:**
- Virus reaches brain via **haematogenous spread** (viraemia) or **retrograde neuronal transport** (rabies, HSV)
- Infects neurons and glial cells directly
- Triggers inflammatory response — perivascular lymphocytic infiltration
- Neuronal death from direct viral cytopathic effect and immune-mediated damage
- Cerebral oedema and haemorrhage in severe cases
- HSV-1 has predilection for **temporal lobes** — causes temporal lobe encephalitis

### 14. Influenza Antigenic Changes
**Question:** Explain the phenomena of minor and major antigenic changes in influenza virus. Describe the clinical significance of these antigenic changes
**Antigenic Drift — minor change:**
- Gradual accumulation of **point mutations** in haemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA) genes
- Due to lack of proofreading by viral RNA polymerase
- Results in slightly altered surface antigens
- Existing antibodies have reduced effectiveness against new strains
- Causes **seasonal influenza epidemics** annually
- Requires **annual update** of influenza vaccines
**Antigenic Shift — major change:**
- Sudden, dramatic change in HA and/or NA
- Occurs by **reassortment** of gene segments when two different influenza strains infect the same cell simultaneously
- Produces a **novel subtype** with entirely new HA and/or NA combination
- Population has **little or no pre-existing immunity**
- Can cause **global pandemics** e.g. 1918 Spanish flu, 2009 swine flu pandemic
- Only occurs with **Influenza A** — multiple animal reservoirs (birds, pigs) enable reassortment

### 15. Haemorrhagic Fever Viruses
**Question:** Describe how haemorrhagic fever viruses can be transmitted to humans. Give four names of viruses which can cause haemorrhagic fever
**Transmission routes:**
- **Direct contact with infected animal blood or fluids** — Ebola virus; bats and primates as reservoir
- **Rodent excreta** — Lassa fever; humans inhale infected urine or faeces of Mastomys rats
- **Arthropod vectors** — ticks (Crimean-Congo HF), mosquitoes (yellow fever, dengue)
- **Nosocomial/healthcare transmission** — Ebola, Lassa; contact with infected blood, needlestick injury
- **Bushmeat consumption** — handling carcasses of infected animals
**Viruses causing haemorrhagic fever:**
- Ebola virus
- Marburg virus
- Lassa virus
- Crimean-Congo haemorrhagic fever virus
- Yellow fever virus
- Dengue virus — severe dengue / dengue haemorrhagic fever

### 16. Prions
**Question:** Give the main characteristics that differentiate prions from other pathogens and describe the diseases caused in humans
**Characteristics:**
- Composed solely of **misfolded protein (PrPsc)** — contain **no nucleic acid** (no DNA or RNA)
- Abnormal isoform of normal host protein **PrPc** (cellular prion protein)
- Extremely resistant to heat, radiation, UV light, formaldehyde, and standard disinfection
- Cannot be visualised by conventional electron microscopy
- Extremely long **incubation period** — years to decades
- Transmissible — PrPsc induces conformational change in normal PrPc (template-directed misfolding)
- Cause **spongiform encephalopathies** — characteristic vacuolation of brain tissue with no inflammatory response
**Human prion diseases:**
- **Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (CJD)** — sporadic, familial, iatrogenic, and variant (vCJD from BSE-infected beef)
- **Kuru** — transmitted among Fore people of Papua New Guinea via ritualistic cannibalism
- **Gerstmann-Straussler-Scheinker syndrome (GSS)** — inherited prion disease
- **Fatal Familial Insomnia (FFI)** — inherited; progressive insomnia, autonomic dysfunction, dementia

### 17. Normal Cell vs Tumour Cell
**Question:** Compare and contrast the features of a normal and tumour/cancer cell
| Feature | Normal Cell | Tumour/Cancer Cell |
|---|---|---|
| Growth regulation | Controlled by growth factors | Uncontrolled, autonomous growth |
| Contact inhibition | Stops dividing on contact with other cells | Loses contact inhibition |
| Cell cycle checkpoints | Intact — p53 and Rb function normally | Checkpoint genes mutated or inactivated |
| Apoptosis | Normal programmed cell death | Resistance to apoptosis |
| Telomeres | Shorten with each division | Telomerase reactivated — cells become immortal |
| Differentiation | Well differentiated | Poorly differentiated or anaplastic |
| Metastasis | No invasion beyond tissue boundary | Invades locally and metastasises distantly |
| Angiogenesis | Normal vasculature | Induces new blood vessel formation via VEGF |
| Oncogenes/TSGs | Proto-oncogenes normal; TSGs intact | Oncogenes activated; tumour suppressor genes lost |

### 18. COVID-19 Contact Tracing
**Question:** Describe what is involved in contact tracing with regard to prevention of spread of COVID-19
- **Identification of index case** — confirmed positive COVID-19 case
- **Interview the index case** — determine timeline of symptoms, movements, and close contacts during the infectious period (2 days before symptom onset to point of isolation)
- **Definition of close contact** — person within 1–2 metres for 15 or more minutes, or direct exposure to respiratory secretions
- **Contact listing** — compile all contacts: household, workplace, healthcare settings, social gatherings
- **Notification of contacts** — inform identified contacts of potential exposure
- **Quarantine** — contacts advised to self-isolate for 10–14 days from last exposure
- **Testing** — PCR or rapid antigen testing of all identified contacts
- **Monitoring** — daily symptom checking during quarantine period
- **Ring containment** — process repeated for any new positive contacts identified
- **Data recording** — all contacts logged in public health surveillance database
- Goal: **break the chain of transmission** before contacts unknowingly spread infection to others
