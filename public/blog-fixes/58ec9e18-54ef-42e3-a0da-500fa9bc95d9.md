## Year 3 Pathology — Cardiovascular, Respiratory & GI/Hepatobiliary
## SECTION A: CARDIOVASCULAR PATHOLOGY
### A1. Atherosclerosis
- Stages: fatty streak → fibrous plaque → complicated plaque (calcification, ulceration, haemorrhage, thrombosis)
- Complicated plaque causes clinical events: MI, stroke, peripheral vascular disease, aneurysm
- Risk factors: hyperlipidaemia, hypertension, smoking, diabetes, age, male sex, family history
- Pathogenesis (response-to-injury hypothesis): endothelial injury → lipid insudation → macrophage foam cell formation → smooth muscle proliferation → fibrous cap formation
### A2. Myocardial Infarction
| Time after MI | Gross/microscopic change |
|---|---|
| <24 hours | Coagulative necrosis (earliest change) — may look normal grossly |
| 1–3 days | Neutrophil infiltration, yellow pallor |
| 3–7 days | Macrophages clear debris, yellow-tan, soft (weakest point, highest rupture risk) |
| 1–3 weeks | Granulation tissue formation, red-grey border |
| Weeks–months | Collagen scar formation, grey-white, firm |
**Complications by timing:** arrhythmia (first hours, #1 cause of death) → cardiogenic shock → papillary muscle rupture (day 3–5) → free wall rupture (day 4–7, coincides with softest phase above) → ventricular aneurysm (weeks–months, in the scarred phase)
### A3. Dilated Cardiomyopathy
- Cardiomegaly, all four chambers dilated; dilation predominates over hypertrophy
- Mural thrombi common in dilated chambers (embolism risk)
- Microscopic: non-specific myocyte hypertrophy, interstitial fibrosis
- Functional consequence: systolic (contractile) dysfunction → congestive heart failure
- Causes: idiopathic (most common), viral myocarditis (esp. Coxsackie B), alcohol, peripartum, genetic, doxorubicin toxicity
**Compare with:**
- *Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy* — diastolic dysfunction, asymmetric septal hypertrophy, myocyte disarray, cause of sudden death in young athletes
- *Restrictive cardiomyopathy* — diastolic dysfunction from myocardial infiltration (amyloid, sarcoidosis)
### A4. Vasculitis — Classified by Vessel Size
| Vessel size | Examples |
|---|---|
| Large | Takayasu arteritis (young Asian women, aortic arch); Giant cell/temporal arteritis (elderly, headache, jaw claudication, blindness risk) |
| Medium | Polyarteritis nodosa (necrotising, Hep B-associated); Kawasaki disease (children, coronary artery aneurysms) |
| Small | Granulomatosis with polyangiitis/Wegener's (c-ANCA, upper/lower respiratory + renal); Microscopic polyangiitis (p-ANC
A) |
### A5. Rheumatic Heart Disease
**Pathogenesis:** follows untreated Group A streptococcal pharyngitis; molecular mimicry — antibodies against streptococcal M protein cross-react with cardiac tissue; acute rheumatic fever → chronic RHD with repeated episodes
**Morphology:**
- Aschoff bodies — pathognomonic granulomatous lesions containing Anitschkow cells, found in myocardium
- Mitral valve most commonly affected → mitral stenosis (fish-mouth/buttonhole deformity)
- Valve changes: leaflet thickening, commissural fusion, chordae tendineae shortening
**Diagnosis (Jones criteria):** Major — carditis, polyarthritis, chorea, erythema marginatum, subcutaneous nodules. Minor — fever, arthralgia, raised ESR/CRP
### A6. Infective Endocarditis — Acute vs Sub-acute
| Feature | Acute IE | Sub-acute IE |
|---|---|---|
| Organism | Staphylococcus aureus (virulent) | Streptococcus viridans (less virulent) |
| Valve affected | Often previously normal | Usually previously damaged |
| Onset | Rapid, days | Slow, weeks–months |
| Vegetations | Large, destructive | Smaller |
| Course | Severe, rapidly fatal if untreated | Indolent |
**Related non-infective forms:** Non-bacterial thrombotic (marantic) endocarditis — sterile vegetations, associated with malignancy/hypercoagulable states. Libman-Sacks endocarditis — associated with SL
E.
### A7. Pulmonary Thromboembolism
**Pathogenesis:** most emboli arise from DVT of the lower limbs (femoral, iliac veins); Virchow's triad predisposes — venous stasis, hypercoagulability, endothelial injury; thrombus dislodges → right heart → pulmonary arteries
**Consequences by size:**
- Small — often asymptomatic/silent
- Large — sudden death via saddle embolus occluding the pulmonary artery bifurcation
- Medium — pulmonary infarction (wedge-shaped, haemorrhagic) if collateral circulation inadequate
- Chronic recurrent — pulmonary hypertension, cor pulmonale

## SECTION B: RESPIRATORY PATHOLOGY
### B1. Emphysema vs Chronic Bronchitis
| Feature | Emphysema (pink puffer) | Chronic bronchitis (blue bloater) |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Permanent airspace enlargement distal to terminal bronchiole, wall destruction | Chronic productive cough ≥3 months, 2 consecutive years |
| Main mechanism | Protease-antiprotease imbalance (↓alpha-1 antitrypsin), elastin destruction | Chronic irritation (smoking) → mucous gland hyperplasia (↑Reid index) |
| Appearance | Thin, breathless, pink complexion | Overweight, cyanotic, oedematous |
| Hypoxia/CO2 retention | Mild, compensates by hyperventilating | Marked hypoxia and CO2 retention |
| Cor pulmonale | Late | Early and more severe |
**Pathogenesis of emphysema:** cigarette smoke recruits neutrophils/macrophages → release proteases (elastase); normally alpha-1 antitrypsin inhibits elastase, but smoking also inactivates it; net effect — unopposed elastase destroys alveolar walls → permanent airspace enlargement. Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency (genetic) causes early-onset panacinar emphysema, especially lower lobes.
**Types by distribution:** centriacinar/centrilobular (smoking-related, upper lobes) vs panacinar (α1-AT deficiency, lower lobes) vs paraseptal (adjacent to pleura, risk of spontaneous pneumothorax in young adults)
### B2. Asthma — Bronchiole Changes (5 features)
- Bronchial smooth muscle hypertrophy
- Mucous gland hyperplasia with mucus plugging
- Basement membrane thickening
- Eosinophilic infiltration of the airway wall
- Curschmann spirals (mucus casts) and Charcot-Leyden crystals (eosinophil breakdown products) in sputum
**Pathogenesis (Type I hypersensitivity):** allergen exposure → IgE sensitisation of mast cells → re-exposure → mast cell degranulation → early phase (bronchospasm, minutes) and late phase (inflammatory cell recruitment, hours later — drives the chronic airway remodelling above)
### B3. Diffuse Alveolar Damage / ARDS (5 histomorphological features)
- Hyaline membrane formation lining alveolar walls (protein-rich exudate)
- Alveolar septal oedema and congestion
- Type II pneumocyte hyperplasia (regenerative response)
- Interstitial and intra-alveolar inflammatory infiltrate
- Progression to interstitial fibrosis in the organising phase
**Causes:** sepsis, aspiration, severe trauma, pneumonia, pancreatitis
**Phases:** exudative (hyaline membranes, oedema; days 1–7) → proliferative (type II pneumocyte hyperplasia, organising; week 1–3) → fibrotic (if unresolved; weeks+)
### B4. Pneumoconioses — Classification
- Coal worker's pneumoconiosis — carbon dust, upper lobes, "black lung"
- Silicosis — silica dust (mining, quarrying), upper lobes, egg-shell calcification of hilar nodes, increased TB risk
- Asbestosis — asbestos fibres, **lower lobes**, ferruginous bodies, risk of mesothelioma and bronchogenic carcinoma
- Berylliosis — beryllium exposure, non-caseating granulomas (mimics sarcoidosis)
*Note: asbestos is the odd one out — lower lobes, and the only one strongly linked to mesothelioma (a pleural tumour, not a lung tumour). Common exam trap.*
### B5. Nasal Polyps (5 histomorphological features)
- Oedematous stroma
- Eosinophilic inflammatory infiltrate
- Thickened basement membrane
- Covered by respiratory (ciliated columnar) epithelium
- Absence of malignant features (benign lesion)
Associated with allergic rhinitis, chronic sinusitis, asthma, aspirin sensitivity — **Samter's triad**: asthma + nasal polyps + aspirin sensitivity
### B6. Lung Carcinoma
| Type | Location | Key features |
|---|---|---|
| Squamous cell carcinoma | Central | Strongly smoking-associated, keratin pearls, may cavitate |
| Adenocarcinoma | Peripheral | Most common overall (incl. non-smokers), glandular pattern |
| Small cell carcinoma | Central | Neuroendocrine, worst prognosis, strongly paraneoplastic |
| Large cell carcinoma | Peripheral | Undifferentiated, poor prognosis |
**5 paraneoplastic syndromes:**
1. SIADH (small cell) — hyponatraemia
2. Ectopic ACTH secretion (small cell) — Cushing's syndrome
3. Hypercalcaemia via PTHrP (squamous cell)
4. Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome (small cell) — autoantibodies against presynaptic calcium channels
5. Clubbing / hypertrophic pulmonary osteoarthropathy (adenocarcinoma)
### B7. Tuberculosis
**Pathogenesis:** inhalation of M. tuberculosis droplets → alveolar macrophages phagocytose but fail to kill initially → cell-mediated (Type IV) hypersensitivity develops over 2–4 weeks → granuloma formation walls off the organism — Ghon focus (primary lesion, subpleural, mid/lower zone); Ghon focus + hilar lymph node = Ghon complex → outcome: contained (latent T
B) or, if immunocompromised, progresses to active/miliary TB
**Morphology:** caseating granulomas — central caseous necrosis surrounded by epithelioid macrophages, Langhans giant cells, lymphocytes. Secondary/reactivation TB typically apical, due to higher oxygen tension favouring the organism.
**Clinical presentation:** chronic cough >2 weeks, haemoptysis, night sweats, weight loss, low-grade fever
**Diagnosis:** Ziehl-Neelsen stain (gold standard for acid-fast bacilli), sputum culture, GeneXpert

## SECTION C: GI / HEPATOBILIARY PATHOLOGY
### C1. Alcoholic Liver Disease — Progressive Spectrum
- Hepatic steatosis (fatty liver) — reversible, macrovesicular fat accumulation
- Alcoholic hepatitis — hepatocyte swelling/ballooning, Mallory-Denk bodies (eosinophilic cytoplasmic inclusions), neutrophilic infiltrate, perivenular fibrosis
- Cirrhosis — irreversible, diffuse fibrosis with regenerative nodules, typically micronodular
### C2. Pancreatitis — Aetiology
Gallstones (commonest, obstruct ampulla of Vater); alcohol (second commonest); hypertriglyceridaemia; hypercalcaemia; drugs (thiazides, azathioprine); trauma/post-ERCP; idiopathic
**Mnemonic — I GET SMASHED:** Idiopathic, Gallstones, Ethanol, Trauma, Steroids, Mumps, Autoimmune, Scorpion sting, Hyperlipidaemia/Hypercalcaemia, ERCP, Drugs
**Key complication:** hypocalcaemia = poor prognostic sign (fat saponification consumes calcium)
### C3. Barrett's Oesophagus — Pathogenesis
- Chronic GERD exposes lower oesophageal squamous epithelium to acid
- Chronic injury triggers metaplasia — squamous epithelium replaced by columnar epithelium with goblet cells (intestinal-type metaplasia)
- Protective adaptation to acid, but premalignant
- Progresses through dysplasia (low-grade → high-grade) → risk of oesophageal adenocarcinoma
### C4. Gallstones — Types Compared
| Type | Composition | Risk factors |
|---|---|---|
| Cholesterol (most common, developed countries) | Cholesterol | "5 F's": Female, Fat, Forty, Fertile, Family history |
| Pigment (black) | Unconjugated bilirubin | Haemolytic conditions (sickle cell, spherocytosis) |
| Pigment (brown) | Calcium bilirubinate | Biliary infection/infestation (e.g. Clonorchis) |
| Mixed | Cholesterol + pigment | Most common overall |
### C5. Colorectal Carcinoma
**Pathogenesis — adenoma-carcinoma (Vogelstein) sequence:**
Normal epithelium → APC gene mutation → adenoma formation → KRAS mutation → adenoma growth/progression → p53 mutation → invasive carcinoma
**Alternative pathway:** Lynch syndrome/HNPCC follows the microsatellite instability pathway (mismatch repair gene mutations — MLH1, MSH2), distinct from the APC/chromosomal instability pathway above
**Morphology:** most arise in the rectosigmoid region; annular "napkin-ring" lesions in the left colon (obstructive); polypoid fungating masses in the right colon (bleeding, anaemia)
**Clinical presentation:**
- Left-sided — change in bowel habit, obstruction, blood-streaked stool
- Right-sided — iron deficiency anaemia, occult bleeding, vague abdominal pain (presents later, wider lumen)
**Risk factors:** familial adenomatous polyposis (APC gene), Lynch syndrome, inflammatory bowel disease, low-fibre/high-fat diet, age
### C6. H. pylori Gastritis — Pathogenesis
- Colonises the gastric antrum, produces urease (neutralises acid locally) and cytotoxins (CagA, Vac
A)
- Triggers chronic inflammation — initially neutrophilic (active), then chronic lymphocytic/plasma cell infiltrate
- Damages mucosal barrier → predisposes to peptic ulcer disease
- Chronic infection → intestinal metaplasia → risk of gastric adenocarcinoma and MALT lymphoma
### C7. Crohn's Disease vs Ulcerative Colitis
| Feature | Crohn's disease | Ulcerative colitis |
|---|---|---|
| Location | Anywhere mouth to anus (commonly terminal ileum) | Colon only, starts at rectum |
| Distribution | Skip lesions | Continuous |
| Depth | Transmural | Mucosal only |
| Gross appearance | Cobblestone mucosa, strictures | Continuous ulceration, pseudopolyps |
| Granulomas | Present (non-caseating) | Absent |
| Complications | Fistulas, strictures, malabsorption | Toxic megacolon, ↑colorectal cancer risk (higher than Crohn's) |
### C8. Bilirubin Metabolism Disorders
- Crigler-Najjar type II — reduced UGT activity
- Dubin-Johnson syndrome — conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia, black liver pigmentation on gross examination
- Gilbert syndrome — mild UGT deficiency, unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia, benign, very common
- Rotor syndrome — conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia, **no** black liver pigmentation (key differentiator from Dubin-Johnson)
