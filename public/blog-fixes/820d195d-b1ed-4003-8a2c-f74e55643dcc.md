## VECTOR TERMINOLOGY
- Zoophilic — prefers animal blood
- Anthropophilic — prefers human blood
- Endophagic — feeds indoors
- Exophagic — feeds outdoors
- Endophilic — rests indoors
- Exophilic — rests outdoors
- IRS/bed nets only work on endophagic + endophilic vectors — vectors that feed/rest outdoors avoid both
- Anautogenous — needs a blood meal before laying eggs
- Autogenous — lays eggs without a blood meal
- Monophagous — feeds on one host species only; classic example = louse (humans only)
- Parthenogenesis — asexual reproduction, offspring from unfertilized eggs
- Fomite — non-living object that transmits a pathogen
- Refractory vector — biologically incapable of transmitting a given pathogen even after biting an infected host

## TICKS — ARGASID (soft ticks)
- No scutum
- Multiple hosts across life cycle
- Multiple nymphal stages
- Feed briefly, repeatedly
- Example: *Ornithodoros* — vector of relapsing fever
## TICKS — IXODID (hard ticks)
- Has a scutum
- Classic 3-host life cycle
- Only ONE nymphal stage
- *Boophilus* = exception (one-host tick)
- Examples: *Rhipicephalus, Amblyomma, Hyalomma*
## Life cycle of a 3-host tick (15–20 marks — draw and label)
- Egg hatches in environment → Larva (6 legs)
- Larva attaches to Host 1, feeds, drops off
- Larva molts to Nymph (8 legs) in the environment — only nymphal stage in the whole cycle
- Nymph attaches to Host 2, feeds, drops off, molts to Adult in environment
- Adult attaches to Host 3, feeds, mates
- Engorged female drops off, lays eggs in environment — cycle repeats
## Medical importance of Ixodid ticks (20 marks)
- Blood loss/anemia from heavy infestation
- Tick paralysis — neurotoxin in saliva, ascending flaccid paralysis, resolves on tick removal
- Local dermatitis/skin irritation at bite site
- Secondary bacterial infection of bite wound
- Allergic reactions to saliva, occasionally severe
- Granuloma formation if mouthparts left in skin
- Vector of Lyme disease
- Vector of Rocky Mountain spotted fever and other rickettsial diseases
- Vector of Babesiosis (malaria-like protozoal disease)
- Vector of Anaplasmosis/Ehrlichiosis
- Vector of tick-borne encephalitis (viral, some regions)
- Vector of East Coast Fever (*Theileria parva*, via *Rhipicephalus appendiculatus*) — Kenya-relevant
- Long feeding duration (days) → higher transmission risk than soft ticks
- Transstadial transmission — pathogen persists through larva→nymph→adult molt
- Transovarial transmission — infected female passes pathogen into her eggs
- Wide host range across life stages → cross-species pathogen transfer
- Major livestock pest — reduces productivity independent of disease
- Drives need for acaricides, pasture management, protective clothing, tick checks
## Acaricides
- Kill ticks/mites
- Among malaria/leishmaniasis/amoebiasis/Q-fever/sleeping sickness options — Q-fever is best controlled by acaricides

## TRYPANOSOMIASIS — African (Old World) sleeping sickness
- Vector: *Glossina* (tsetse fly)
- *T. b. gambiense* → West African sleeping sickness → **Palpalis group**
- *T. b. rhodesiense* → East African sleeping sickness → **Morsitans group**
- **Fusca group** is NOT significant for *T. b. rhodesiense* transmission
- Arabuko Sokoke (forested area) = specific risk zone for sleeping sickness
- Treatment: melarsoprol, pentamidine, eflornithine, suramin
## TRYPANOSOMIASIS — American (New World) Chagas disease
- Caused by *Trypanosoma cruzi*
- Vector: triatomine bugs = kissing bugs = reduviid bugs (same insect)
- Found in Central/South America — NOT Glossina (Africa-only)
- Transmission: bug defecates near bite site while feeding; scratching rubs contaminated faeces into wound — it's the faeces, not the bite, that transmits the parasite
- Triatomine bugs in a home → predisposes household specifically to Chagas disease
- Treatment: nifurtimox

## LEISHMANIASIS
- Vector: sandflies — *Phlebotomus* (Old World) / *Lutzomyia* (New World)
- Sandflies are larviparous
- Stage injected into human by vector: **promastigote**
- Converts to **amastigote** once inside human macrophages — this is the multiplying form
- Treatment: sodium stibogluconate (pentavalent antimonial)
- Pathologies transmitted by Phlebotominae (12 marks):
 - Cutaneous leishmaniasis — skin ulcers/nodules at the bite site, parasite stays localized in skin macrophages
 - Mucocutaneous leishmaniasis — spreads from skin to mucosal tissue (nose, mouth), causing tissue destruction
 - Visceral leishmaniasis (kala-azar) — spreads to liver, spleen, bone marrow; fever, weight loss, organ enlargement; most severe form
 - Sandfly fever — separate viral disease carried by *Phlebotomus*, acute febrile illness
 - Bartonellosis (Oroya fever/Carrion's disease) — bacterial disease via *Lutzomyia* in South America, fever and hemolytic anemia in acute phase

## FILARIASIS
- *Wuchereria bancrofti* → vector Culex → elephantiasis/lymphatic filariasis
- *Onchocerca volvulus* → vector Simulium → river blindness (onchocerciasis)
- *Loa loa* → vector Chrysops → Calabar swellings, eye migration, "pink eye disease"
- Larval stage in thoracic/chest muscles of vector = L3 (infective stage)
- Microfilariae = the circulating blood stage seen on a blood smear (distinct from L3 — a likely distinguishing question)

## SIMULIUM & OTHER FLY-BORNE DISEASE
- *Simulium* breeds in fast-flowing rivers → river blindness
- "Black water disease" associated with *Simulium* presence
- *Chrysops* presence predisposes to pink eye disease/Loiasis
- Latin America vectors = triatomine/reduviid bugs — NOT Glossina (Africa-only)

## LICE, MITES, FLEAS
- *Pediculus humanus* (body louse) — nits in clothing
- *Pediculus capitis* (head louse) — nits on hair/scalp
- *Pthirus pubis* (crab louse) — pubic region; treatment: petroleum jelly
- *Sarcoptes scabiei* — causes scabies; pruritis is diagnostic; treatment: benzyl benzoate
- Mite infection diagnostic reagent: liquid crystal violet
- *Leptotrombidium* — vector of scrub typhus (mite)
- *Xenopsylla*/*Pulex* (flea) — vector of plague (*Yersinia pestis*); linked to endemic (murine) typhus
- Pediculosis treatment: fine tooth-comb + insecticides — NOT antibiotics
- Louse = classic monophagous arthropod

## SCRUB TYPHUS
- Pathogen: *Orientia tsutsugamushi*
- Vector: *Leptotrombidium* (mite)
- Transovarial transmission — vector passes pathogen to its own offspring via eggs
- Reject as answer: *Entamoeba histolytica, Onchocerca volvulus, Loa loa, Wuchereria bancrofti*

## MOSQUITO & INSECT BIOLOGY
- Holometabolous = complete metamorphosis = 4 life stages (egg, larva, pupa, adult)
- Mosquito life cycle has 4 larval instars
- *Aedes albopictus* mainly found in Asia
- Order Diptera = true flies (mosquitoes, tsetse, houseflies)
- Order Blattaria = cockroaches
- Order Siphonaptera = fleas
- Order Anoplura/Phthiraptera = lice
- Arboviruses mainly transmitted by Aedes
- Rift Valley fever mainly transmitted by Aedes
- Host-seeking mosquitoes attracted by CO2 and host odors — white color does NOT attract
- Typical larval habitat: clogged drainage ditches, lakes/reservoirs — fast-flowing stream is NOT typical (contrast *Simulium*)
- Pupal stage occurs only in complete metamorphosis

## INSECTICIDES & REPELLENTS
- DDT/Endosulfan (organochlorines) — target: GABA-gated chloride channels
- Bendiocarb (carbamate) — target: acetylcholinesterase
- Malathion (organophosphate) — target: acetylcholinesterase
- Permethrin (pyrethroid) — target: voltage-gated sodium channels
- DEET — a repellent, not an insecticide
- Insecticide resistance develops from high or prolonged doses (NOT low doses)

## VECTOR CONTROL — full breakdown
- Chemical: organochlorines, carbamates, organophosphates, pyrethroids, repellents (DEET), molluscicides (e.g. Methiocarb — kills snails, intermediate hosts of schistosomiasis)
- Biological: fish (*Gambusia*/Tilapia) eat larvae; Bti (*Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis*) bacterial larvicide; predatory tadpoles/copepods; Sterile Insect Technique (SIT) — sterile males mate once in a lifetime with wild females → no viable offspring; transgenic vectors — reduced vector competence
- Genetic control: chemosterilants; irradiation; must release males of same/closely related species — cross-species release fails; transgene vectors
- Environmental/mechanical: draining stagnant water, clearing resting sites/vegetation, proper faeces/waste disposal, bed nets (best against endophagic vectors), colored traps (black+blue attract both tsetse flies and Aedes)
- Integrated Vector Management (IVM): combines chemical+biological+environmental+community participation; method matched to vector behavior; reduces resistance risk; the answer to "best mode of malaria control" when offered
- IRS bottlenecks: insecticide resistance, high cost, community refusal, only works on endophilic/endophagic vectors, residual effect wanes over time
- Impediments in vector-borne disease control (distinct essay): insecticide resistance, behavioral adaptation (endophagic→exophagic shift to avoid IRS/nets), cost of sustained campaigns, poor community compliance, climate change expanding vector ranges, genetic control limited to same/closely related species

## MALARIA — full rebuild
- Causative agent: *Plasmodium falciparum, P. vivax, P. ovale, P. malariae* (and *P. knowlesi* in some regions) — *P. falciparum* causes the most severe disease
- Vector: *Anopheles* mosquito only (not *Culex/Aedes* — common distractor)
- Life cycle (if asked as its own essay, 15–20 marks):
 - Infected female *Anopheles* bites human, injects **sporozoites** from her salivary glands
 - Sporozoites travel to the liver, invade hepatocytes — this is the exo-erythrocytic (liver) stage
 - In the liver they multiply into **merozoites** — this stage is asymptomatic, no fever yet
 - Merozoites rupture out of liver cells into the bloodstream, invade red blood cells — begins the erythrocytic stage
 - Inside RBCs: ring stage → trophozoite → schizont
 - Schizont ruptures the RBC, releasing more merozoites, which invade fresh RBCs — cycle repeats; **this stage causes the clinical symptoms**
 - Some merozoites instead develop into **gametocytes** (male/female sexual forms) — asymptomatic but essential for transmission to the next mosquito
 - A new mosquito bites, ingests gametocytes with the blood meal
 - In the mosquito gut, gametocytes mature and fuse into a zygote → motile **ookinete** → penetrates gut wall → forms an **oocyst**
 - Oocyst ruptures, releasing sporozoites, which migrate to the salivary glands — ready to infect the next person, cycle complete
 - *P. vivax* and *P. ovale* can form **hypnozoites** — a dormant liver stage causing relapses months/years later
- Best mode of control (MCQ): Integrated Vector Management
- Fever cycle mechanism (MCQ): merozoites synchronously rupture from RBCs → triggers a strong immune reaction → fever spike → merozoites invade new RBCs → cycle repeats. Reject: surface protein change (that's antigenic variation, explains chronicity not the cycle); sporozoites causing it (they act only once, at initial infection)

## MYIASIS — full rebuild (20 marks)
- **Aetiology (3 marks):** infestation of living human tissue by the larvae (maggots) of certain flies; the fly lays eggs on or near a wound, sore, exposed mucosa, or even intact skin in some species; eggs hatch and larvae invade the tissue
- **Pathogenesis (5 marks):**
 - Larvae hatch and burrow into living or necrotic (dead) tissue
 - They feed on host tissue as they grow, using enzymes to digest it
 - In wounds, larvae can extend deeper into surrounding structures if untreated
 - **Ocular myiasis** specifically — larvae invade eye tissue (conjunctiva or deeper orbital tissue), causing local tissue destruction and inflammation; can extend into deeper orbital structures if not treated early
 - Secondary bacterial infection is common because the wound is already open and colonized by larvae
- **Diagnosis (4 marks):**
 - Clinical inspection of the wound/sore — larvae are often directly visible moving in the tissue
 - History of exposure to fly-infested environment or neglected wound
 - X-ray if deep tissue or bone involvement is suspected (larvae not visible on the surface)
 - Larvae can be collected and identified by species if needed for confirmation
- **Treatment (4 marks):**
 - Aseptic manual removal of larvae if they are superficial/accessible
 - Irrigation of the wound to flush out larvae
 - Surgical extraction if larvae have migrated deep into tissue
 - Wound debridement and antibiotic cover if secondary bacterial infection is present
- **Control (6 marks):**
 - Cover open wounds and sores to prevent flies from laying eggs (top-weighted answer)
 - Proper disposal of faeces and organic waste (reduces fly breeding sites)
 - Use of insecticides to reduce fly populations in the environment
 - Avoid leaving wounds exposed, especially in livestock and indigent/homeless populations
 - Good wound hygiene and prompt medical attention for any wound, especially in vulnerable populations

## MECHANICAL TRANSMISSION
- Mechanical vector = passive transporter; pathogen does NOT develop/multiply inside it
- Houseflies (*Musca*) transmit typhoid, cholera, dysentery via contaminated body parts
- *Musca sorbens* specifically transmits *Chlamydia trachomatis* (trachoma)
- Typhoid vector = true flies/*Musca* — reject *Haematogogus, Sabethes* (yellow fever vectors, not typhoid)
- Contamination via faeces, food, or wound contact — no biological cycle in vector

## COCKROACHES
- Order Blattaria
- German, American, Smokey brown roaches can all coexist in one region → "all of the above"
- Non-pesticide control: sealed food containers, nightly counter cleaning, sealing cracks/seams → "all of the above"
- Boric acid/borax = control agent for *Periplaneta* species specifically
- Mechanical vector — grouped with *Musca, Fannia*; *Bulinus* (a snail, intermediate host) is the odd one out in this grouping
- Can also mechanically carry helminth eggs and enteric viruses in unsanitary settings

## MILLIPEDES/CENTIPEDES
- Cause skin irritation via spraying defensive secretions — not stinging, not virus transmission, not egg-laying under skin

## CERATOPOGONIDAE (Culicoides)
- Larval habitats: freshwater marshes, salt marshes, wet muddy areas near cattle tanks → "all of the above"
- Vector of Oropouche virus

## SNAILS AS INTERMEDIATE HOSTS
- *Echinostoma ilocaanum* — snail is BOTH first AND second intermediate host (unusual, specifically tested)
- *Fasciolopsis buski*/*Faciola hepatica* — snail = first intermediate host; fish/water plant = second
- "Fish are second intermediate hosts of?" → pick the fluke (e.g. *Clonorchis sinensis*), not *Hymenolepis nana*

## THELAZIA (Eyeworm)
- Vector: fruit flies feeding on eye secretions
- Not faecal-oral — odd one out vs *Vibrio cholerae, Entamoeba histolytica, Rotavirus* in transmission-mode questions

## FIVE MEDICAL IMPORTANCES OF ARTHROPODS (10 marks)
- Vectors of disease (e.g. *Anopheles*→malaria, *Glossina*→sleeping sickness)
- Direct tissue invasion (myiasis, scabies)
- Allergic reactions/toxic secretions (bee stings, millipede secretions)
- Mechanical transmission (houseflies→cholera/typhoid)
- Intermediate hosts (snails→schistosomiasis, fleas→dog tapeworm)
