# High-Yield Paediatric Topics: Neonatal, GI, Infectious and Renal

**Year 4 · Paediatrics · General Paediatrics and Neonatology**

> **In one line:** a collection of commonly examined paediatric topics not covered fully in lectures: haemolytic uraemic syndrome, sudden infant death, the problems of the preterm baby (RDS, NEC, IVH, ROP, BPD), surgical neonatal and infant GI conditions, common childhood infections and rashes, hypernatraemic dehydration, and neonatal emergencies (TTN, hypoglycaemia, HIE).

## What you must know

- **HUS** = acute kidney injury + microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia + thrombocytopenia, usually after **E. coli O157:H7** bloody diarrhoea; **clotting is normal** (unlike DIC).
- **SIDS** is the commonest cause of death from 1 month to 1 year; peak **2 to 4 months**; back sleeping reduced it dramatically.
- **Antenatal corticosteroids** (completed course at least 24 h, within 1 to 7 days before birth) reduce RDS (RR 0.66), IVH (RR 0.54) and neonatal death (RR 0.69).
- **Pyloric stenosis:** boy aged 2 to 8 weeks, projectile non-bilious vomiting, olive mass, **hypochloraemic hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis**; correct the biochemistry before pyloromyotomy.
- **Bilious vomiting in a neonate = malrotation with volvulus until proven otherwise**: urgent upper GI contrast and surgery.
- **Intussusception:** 3 months to 2 years, colicky pain, pallor, redcurrant jelly stool; air enema reduction unless peritonitis.
- **Hypernatraemic dehydration:** lower sodium slowly (under 0.5 mmol/L per hour, over at least 48 h) to avoid cerebral oedema.
- **HIE:** therapeutic hypothermia (33 to 34°C for 72 h) for babies of 36 weeks and over with moderate or severe HIE, started within 6 hours.

## 1. Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS)

### Definition and types

- **Triad:** acute renal failure, **microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia**, **thrombocytopenia**.
- **Typical (diarrhoea-associated) HUS:** follows gastrointestinal infection with **verocytotoxin (Shiga toxin)-producing E. coli O157:H7** (contact with farm animals or uncooked beef) or, less often, *Shigella*. A prodrome of **bloody diarrhoea** precedes it.
- **Atypical HUS:** no diarrhoeal prodrome, may be genetic (complement dysregulation), relapses frequently.

### Pathophysiology

- Toxin enters the gut mucosa and localises to **renal endothelial cells**, causing intravascular thrombosis. The coagulation cascade is activated but **clotting is normal** (unlike DIC).
- **Platelets are consumed**; red cells are sheared as they pass through the occluded microcirculation (**low Hb, high LDH, fragmented red cells on film**).
- Brain, pancreas and heart can also be involved.

### Management and prognosis

- Early **supportive therapy including dialysis**: careful fluids, treat hypertension, transfuse if needed. Antibiotics and antimotility drugs are avoided in STEC infection.
- Typical HUS has a **good prognosis** with supportive care, but needs long-term follow-up for **persistent proteinuria, hypertension and progressive CKD**.
- **Eculizumab** (monoclonal anti-terminal complement complex antibody) has greatly improved atypical HUS, which previously had high rates of hypertension, CKD and death. It is **very expensive** and the duration of treatment is unknown.

> **Exam tip:** HUS has a normal PT and APTT (unlike DIC) and a negative Coombs test.

## 2. Sudden unexpected death in infancy (SUDI) and SIDS

- **SUDI:** sudden unexpected death of an infant. Some have an undiagnosed cause at autopsy (congenital heart disease, inborn error of metabolism). When **no cause is found after a detailed autopsy**, it is **SIDS**.
- UK: 200 deaths in 2018 (**0.3 per 1000** live births). SIDS is the **commonest cause of death at 1 month to 1 year**.
- **Peak age 2 to 4 months**; 55% boys; **fivefold risk** with low birth weight and with mothers under 20 years.
- Most are natural, unexplained deaths, even when recurrent in a family. Rarely, suffocation (especially **overlying while bed-sharing**) or non-accidental injury.
- Incidence fell dramatically after the **"Back to Sleep"** campaign (supine sleeping instead of prone).
- Advice to reduce risk: back to sleep, feet to foot of cot, firm flat mattress, no smoking in pregnancy or around the baby, avoid overheating, no bed-sharing in high-risk situations (alcohol, sedatives, smoking, prematurity), breastfeeding, room-sharing without bed-sharing.
- After a death: sympathetic support, a full investigation and autopsy, and follow-up of the family.

## 3. The preterm baby

### Antenatal corticosteroids

- Glucocorticoids before preterm delivery **accelerate lung maturity and surfactant production**. Over 15 randomised trials show reduced:
  - **RDS** (relative risk 0.66)
  - **Intraventricular haemorrhage** (RR 0.54)
  - **Neonatal mortality** (RR 0.69)
- A **completed course at least 24 hours before delivery** and **1 to 7 days before birth** is optimal. They also reduce bronchopulmonary dysplasia.

### Resuscitation and thermoregulation

- Preterm babies get cold easily (high surface area, little fat). Keep them warm during resuscitation.
- Babies **under 32 weeks**: place in a **plastic bag or wrap (without drying), except the face**, so plastic clings and prevents evaporative heat loss; use a radiant heater or thermal mattress and warmed humidified gases.
- **Avoid excess oxygen**: oxygen free radicals damage brain, lung and eyes.

### Respiratory distress syndrome (hyaline membrane disease)

- **Surfactant deficiency** raises surface tension, causing widespread **alveolar collapse** and poor gas exchange. The more preterm, the more RDS. Very common under **28 weeks**; worse in boys.
- Rare at term, but seen in **infants of diabetic mothers** and very rarely with surfactant gene mutations.
- Histology: proteinaceous exudate forming a **hyaline membrane**.
- **Clinical features (at delivery or within 4 hours):** tachypnoea >60/min, chest recession (sternal and subcostal), nasal flaring, **expiratory grunting** (creates positive end-expiratory pressure to maintain FRC), cyanosis if severe.
- **Chest X-ray:** ground-glass (reticulogranular) lungs with air bronchograms.
- **Management:** oxygen and **non-invasive support (CPAP or high-flow nasal cannula)**; **surfactant** via tracheal tube or a fine catheter between the cords; mechanical ventilation only if response is inadequate (non-invasive preferred).

### Fluids, nutrition and infection

- **Fluids:** about 60 to 90 mL/kg on day 1, increasing by 20 to 30 mL/kg/day to **150 to 180 mL/kg/day by about day 5**, adjusted to clinical state, electrolytes, urine output and weight.
- **Growth:** a 28-week infant doubles birth weight in 6 weeks and trebles in 12 weeks; a term baby doubles in 5 months and trebles in a year. Babies of **35 to 36 weeks** can suck and swallow; less mature babies need nasogastric or orogastric feeding.
- **Infection risk:** IgG crosses the placenta mainly in the last trimester and **no IgA or IgM** crosses. Cervical or ascending infection often causes preterm labour. Nosocomial infection with indwelling lines and ventilation is common.

### Necrotising enterocolitis (NEC)

- Serious; **incidence inversely related to gestational age**; usually in the **first few weeks**.
- Cause poorly understood: **ischaemic injury, bacterial invasion of the bowel wall and an altered gut microbiome**. Breast milk, and possibly prebiotics and probiotics, help; formula feeds, rapid feed increases and antibiotics harm.
- Risk factors: **IUGR** (especially with reversed end-diastolic flow on antenatal Doppler) and **perinatal asphyxia**.
- **Early signs:** feed intolerance, vomiting (may be bile-stained), abdominal distension, fresh blood in stool; may become shocked and need ventilation.
- **X-ray:** distended loops, bowel wall thickening, **intramural gas (pneumatosis intestinalis)**, gas in the **portal venous tract**, and free air if perforated.
- **Treatment:** stop oral feeds, broad-spectrum antibiotics (aerobic and anaerobic cover), parenteral nutrition, ventilation and circulatory support. **Surgery** for perforation, difficult ventilation or failed medical treatment. Mortality about **20%**.

### Brain lesions

| Lesion | Key facts |
|---|---|
| **Intraventricular haemorrhage (IVH)** | In about 25% of very low birth weight infants; arises in the **germinal matrix** (fragile vessels above the caudate nucleus). Most within the **first 72 hours**. More frequent with perinatal asphyxia, severe RDS and **pneumothorax**. Reduced by antenatal steroids. Diagnosed on **cranial ultrasound**. |
| **Grades of severity** | Small bleeds stay in the germinal matrix; larger bleeds extend into ventricles; the worst is **unilateral haemorrhagic parenchymal infarction**, usually causing **hemiplegia**. |
| **Post-haemorrhagic hydrocephalus** | Large IVH impairs CSF absorption; ventricular dilatation may resolve or progress, causing sutural separation and rapid head growth |
| **Periventricular leukomalacia (PVL)** | White matter injury from ischaemia and inflammation, with cystic lesions on ultrasound. Bilateral multiple cysts carry an **80 to 90% risk of spastic diplegia**, often with cognitive impairment. |

### Retinopathy of prematurity (ROP)

- Abnormal vessel proliferation at the junction of vascularised and non-vascularised retina, which may progress to **retinal detachment, fibrosis and blindness**.
- Originally linked to uncontrolled high-concentration oxygen; even with careful monitoring it occurs in about **35% of very low birth weight infants**, with **5%** needing treatment.
- **Screen** all infants of **birth weight ≤1500 g or under 32 weeks** by an ophthalmologist.
- Treatment: **laser therapy** reduces visual impairment; intravitreal **anti-VEGF** is under investigation. Severe bilateral visual impairment occurs in about 1% of very low birth weight infants.

### Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD, chronic lung disease)

- **Oxygen requirement at 36 weeks postmenstrual age.**
- Lung damage is now thought to be mainly from **delayed lung maturation**, plus barotrauma/volutrauma from ventilation, oxygen toxicity and infection.
- X-ray: widespread opacification, sometimes cystic.
- Weaning from ventilation to CPAP or high-flow nasal cannula to oxygen, sometimes over months. Some go home on oxygen; a few with severe disease die from infection or pulmonary problems.
- **Corticosteroids** help earlier weaning and cut oxygen need short term, but fear of **abnormal neurodevelopment including cerebral palsy** limits use to the highest-risk infants, in short, low-dose courses.

## 4. Congenital diaphragmatic hernia

- About **1 in 4000** births; many diagnosed antenatally. Presents with **failure to respond to resuscitation** or severe respiratory distress.
- Usually **left-sided posterolateral (Bochdalek)** herniation of abdominal contents. Signs: apex beat and heart sounds shifted to the right, poor air entry on the left, scaphoid abdomen.
- **Vigorous bag-mask ventilation** inflates the bowel and can cause pneumothorax in the good lung.
- Confirm with **chest and abdominal X-ray**. Pass a **large-bore nasogastric tube on suction** to prevent bowel distension.
- Stabilise ventilation first, then repair surgically.
- Main problem is **pulmonary hypoplasia** (from compression throughout pregnancy) often with **pulmonary hypertension**; mortality is high if the lungs are hypoplastic.

## 5. Gastrointestinal conditions

### Small bowel obstruction in the newborn

- May be seen antenatally on ultrasound. Presents with **persistent vomiting, bile-stained unless the obstruction is above the ampulla of Vater**; delayed or absent meconium after initial passage; **distension increases with more distal lesions**.
- High lesions present soon after birth; low lesions may take days.

| Cause | Key facts |
|---|---|
| **Duodenal atresia or stenosis** | **One third have Down syndrome**; associated malformations; "double bubble" on X-ray |
| **Jejunal or ileal atresia or stenosis** | May have multiple atretic segments |
| **Malrotation with volvulus** | Dangerous; can infarct the entire midgut |
| **Meconium ileus** | Putty-like inspissated meconium in the lower ileum; **almost all have cystic fibrosis** |

- Diagnosis: clinical features and **abdominal X-ray** showing obstruction.
- Treatment: correct fluid and electrolyte depletion, then **surgery** for atresia, stenosis and malrotation. Meconium ileus may be dislodged by **Gastrografin** enema, otherwise surgery.

### Malrotation and volvulus

- Failure of the gut to rotate and fix its mesentery in fetal life; small bowel mainly on the right, caecum in the right upper quadrant. Cause unknown.
- Usually presents at **1 to 3 days** with obstruction from **Ladd bands** across the duodenum or volvulus; can present at any age with ischaemic bowel.
- Features: **bilious vomiting**, abdominal pain and tenderness (peritonitis or ischaemia).
- **Urgent upper GI contrast study** for any bilious vomiting; **urgent surgery**.

> **Remember:** bilious vomiting in a baby is surgical until proven otherwise. Do not delay for a "gastroenteritis" label.

### Intussusception

- Proximal bowel invaginates into distal bowel; commonest is **ileum into caecum through the ileocaecal valve**.
- Commonest cause of **intestinal obstruction in infants after the neonatal period**; peak **3 months to 2 years**.
- Features: **paroxysmal colicky pain with pallor** (draws up legs, then quiet), vomiting, **sausage-shaped abdominal mass**, **redcurrant jelly stool**. **Shock** is an important complication.
- Diagnose by ultrasound (target or doughnut sign).
- **Treatment:** resuscitate; **reduction by rectal air insufflation** unless peritonitis is present. **Surgery** if air reduction fails or there is peritonitis.

### Pyloric stenosis

- Hypertrophy of pyloric muscle causes **gastric outlet obstruction**. Presents at **2 to 8 weeks** regardless of gestation. **Boys 4:1**, particularly first-born; family history (especially maternal).
- Features: **non-bilious vomiting** that becomes **projectile**; hungry and feeds well after vomiting until dehydration causes loss of interest; weight loss if late.
- Biochemistry: **hypochloraemic hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis** from loss of gastric acid; hyponatraemia may occur.
- Diagnosis: a **test feed** to palpate an **olive-shaped mass** in the right upper quadrant (the classic method); **ultrasound** of the hypertrophied pylorus has replaced this.
- **Management:** surgical **pyloromyotomy (Ramstedt)**, only after correcting the acid-base and electrolyte imbalance with IV fluids (often over 24 hours).

### Oral rehydration therapy (ORT)

- Key treatment in gastroenteritis. Contains **sodium and glucose**: glucose-coupled sodium absorption (active) draws water passively. Works **even with gut inflammation**.
- ORS does not stop diarrhoea; absorption exceeds secretion and keeps the child hydrated until the infection clears.
- Give **small amounts, frequently**, by nasogastric tube if needed.
- (In Kenya, add **zinc** for 10 to 14 days and continue feeding; use IV fluids for severe dehydration or shock.)

### Chronic non-specific diarrhoea (toddler diarrhoea)

- Commonest cause of **persistent loose stools in preschool children**.
- Stools vary from formed to explosive; **undigested vegetable matter** is common; the child is **well and thriving**.
- Some cases are due to undiagnosed **coeliac disease**, excess **fruit juice (apple juice)**, or temporary **cow's milk protein intolerance** after gastroenteritis (a trial of a cow's milk protein-free diet may help).
- Almost always improves with age.

## 6. Infectious diseases

### Scarlet fever

- **Exotoxin** from group A streptococcal pharyngitis.
- **Diffuse erythematous, sandpaper-textured rash** shortly after the pharyngitis; spreads rapidly to face, trunk and limbs, denser in the neck, axillae and groin (**Pastia lines**).
- **Circumoral pallor**; tongue white then desquamates to a red **strawberry tongue**; peeling of fingertips and toes.
- Throat swab confirms group A *Streptococcus*.
- Penicillin V or erythromycin hastens recovery by only about 16 hours, but a **10-day course** is needed to eradicate the organism and prevent **rheumatic fever and glomerulonephritis**. It is indicated where rheumatic fever risk is significant (as in Kenya), when the child returns to a closed institution such as boarding school, or when at increased risk of infection.

### Impetigo

- Localised, highly contagious **staphylococcal or streptococcal** skin infection of infants and young children; commoner with atopic eczema.
- Face, neck, hands: erythematous macules → vesicles/pustules or bullae → rupture → **confluent honey-coloured crusts**.
- Spreads by autoinoculation.
- Mild: topical **mupirocin**. Severe: narrow-spectrum systemic **flucloxacillin** (co-amoxiclav or cephalexin are broader but better tolerated, so adherence is better).
- Exclude from nursery or school until lesions are dry.

### Human herpesviruses

- Eight: **HSV-1, HSV-2, VZV, CMV, EBV, HHV-6, HHV-7, HHV-8**. HHV-8 is linked to **Kaposi sarcoma** in HIV.
- After primary infection they establish **latency** and can **reactivate**.
- **HSV** enters through mucous membranes or skin with intense local damage. HSV-1 is usually orolabial/skin, HSV-2 usually genital, but both can cause either.

### Chickenpox and shingles

- **Oral aciclovir** has variable absorption and limited benefit; it is not recommended in the UK for healthy children.
- **Immunocompromised children:** initial **IV aciclovir**; oral valaciclovir later if no organ dissemination.
- **Varicella zoster immunoglobulin (VZIG)** is recommended for high-risk immunocompromised people with deficient T-cell function after contact. Protection is not absolute and depends on how soon after contact it is given.
- **Shingles:** uncommon in children; reactivation of latent VZV with a **vesicular eruption in a dermatome** of sensory nerves.

### Parvovirus B19 (slapped-cheek syndrome, fifth disease)

- Outbreaks mostly in spring. Spread by respiratory secretions, vertical transmission and infected blood products.
- Infects **erythroid precursors** in the bone marrow.

| Syndrome | Features |
|---|---|
| **Asymptomatic** | Common; antibodies in 5 to 10% of preschool children and 65% of adults |
| **Erythema infectiosum** | Fever, malaise, headache, myalgia, then a week later a **slapped-cheek rash**, then a lace-like maculopapular rash on trunk and limbs |
| **Aplastic crisis** | Most serious; in chronic haemolytic anaemias (**sickle cell disease, thalassaemia**) and immunocompromised children |
| **Fetal disease** | Fetal anaemia, **hydrops** and death; most infected fetuses recover |

### Mumps

- Incubation **15 to 24 days**. Fever, malaise, **parotitis** (one side first, bilateral later); up to **30% subclinical**.
- Earache and pain on eating or drinking; red swollen parotid duct opening. Fever settles in 3 to 4 days.
- **Raised amylase** (parotid, or pancreatitis if abdominal pain).
- Infective for up to 7 days after parotid swelling. Usually mild and self-limiting. Hearing loss is rare, usually unilateral and transient. (Orchitis, meningitis and pancreatitis are other complications.)

### Measles

- Treatment is **supportive**; isolate inpatients. **Ribavirin** may be used in the immunocompromised. **Vitamin A** should be given in low-income countries (including Kenya).

### Rubella (German measles)

- **Maculopapular rash** begins on the face and spreads centrifugally, fading in 3 to 5 days.
- Prominent **suboccipital and postauricular lymphadenopathy**.
- Rare complications: arthritis, encephalitis, thrombocytopenia, myocarditis.
- Clinical diagnosis is unreliable; **confirm serologically** whenever a non-immune pregnant woman may have been exposed.
- No antiviral treatment; prevention is by **immunisation** (MR/MMR) to prevent congenital rubella syndrome.

### Lyme disease

- Tick-borne *Borrelia burgdorferi* infection: **erythema migrans** (expanding annular rash at the bite site), then arthritis, neurological (facial palsy, meningitis) and cardiac involvement. Treatment is doxycycline (age 8 and over) or amoxicillin. Not endemic in Kenya.

## 7. Nephrology: hypernatraemic dehydration

- If IV fluids are needed, a **rapid fall** in plasma sodium and osmolality shifts water into brain cells and causes **seizures and cerebral oedema**.
- Lower sodium **slowly, over at least 48 hours**, using 0.9% saline or 0.9% saline with 5% glucose, tailored to response. Measure plasma sodium regularly. Aim for **less than 0.5 mmol/L per hour**.
- Clinical clue: doughy skin, irritability, a relatively preserved circulation despite significant fluid loss.

## 8. Neonatal problems

### Transient tachypnoea of the newborn (TTN)

- **Commonest cause of respiratory distress in term infants.**
- **Delayed resorption of lung liquid**; commoner after **caesarean section**.
- CXR: **fluid in the horizontal fissure**. Give supplemental oxygen; feed by nasogastric tube or IV fluids if needed.
- Usually settles within a day, occasionally several days. **A diagnosis of exclusion** (consider infection and other causes).

### Neonatal hypoglycaemia

- Likely in the first 24 hours in babies with **IUGR, prematurity, diabetic mothers, large-for-dates, hypothermia, polycythaemia, or any illness**.
- Growth-restricted and preterm babies have **poor glycogen stores**; infants of diabetic mothers have **enough glycogen but hyperinsulinism** (islet hyperplasia from maternal hyperglycaemia).
- Symptoms: jitteriness, irritability, apnoea, lethargy, drowsiness, seizures.
- Glucose above **2.6 mmol/L** is desirable for the best neurodevelopmental outcome. Prolonged symptomatic hypoglycaemia causes permanent brain injury.
- Prevention: **early, frequent milk feeds**; monitor pre-feed glucose in at-risk babies.
- **Management by glucose level:**
  - **2 to 2.6 mmol/L:** recheck until satisfactory (at least three pre-feed values >2 mmol/L).
  - **Under 2 mmol/L:** **dextrose gel** to the mouth, recheck after 30 minutes.
  - **Under 1.0 mmol/L, or under 2.0 with symptoms, or no response to two doses of gel:** **IV dextrose infusion** immediately.

### Hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy (HIE)

- **Causes:**
  - Failed placental gas exchange: excessive or prolonged contractions, placental abruption, uterine rupture.
  - Interrupted umbilical flow: cord compression, shoulder dystocia, cord prolapse.
  - Inadequate maternal placental perfusion: maternal hypotension or hypertension.
  - Compromised fetus: IUGR, anaemia.
  - Failed cardiorespiratory adaptation at birth (failure to breathe).
- Features begin at once or up to **48 hours** later.

| Grade | Features |
|---|---|
| **Mild (1)** | Irritable, exaggerated response to stimulation, staring eyes, hyperventilation |
| **Moderate (2)** | Marked abnormality of movement, hypotonia, cannot suck, brief apnoeas, seizures |
| **Severe (3)** | No spontaneous movement or response to pain, hypotonia, prolonged refractory seizures, multi-organ failure |

- **Therapeutic hypothermia:** cool to a rectal temperature of **33 to 34°C for 72 hours** (cooling jacket) for babies of **36 weeks or more with moderate or severe HIE**, **started within 6 hours of birth**. It reduces brain damage.

## 9. Physical abuse (red flags)

- Suspect when the history does not fit the injury, presentation is delayed, injuries are in different stages of healing, or the child is non-ambulant ("those who don't cruise rarely bruise").
- Suspicious patterns: bruises on the ears, neck, trunk or buttocks, patterned marks, fractures of ribs (posterior), metaphyseal corner fractures, skull fractures with inconsistent history, retinal haemorrhages with subdural bleeding (abusive head trauma), burns with clear margins.
- Action: full examination and documentation, skeletal survey in young children, safeguarding referral, admission for safety.

## Key points to revise

1. HUS: AKI + MAHA + low platelets after E. coli O157:H7; normal clotting; eculizumab for atypical HUS.
2. SIDS peaks at 2 to 4 months; supine sleeping, no smoking, no overheating; bed-sharing risk.
3. Antenatal steroids reduce RDS, IVH and death; babies under 32 weeks go in a plastic bag; avoid hyperoxia.
4. RDS: surfactant deficiency, grunting, recession; treat with CPAP and surfactant. NEC: feed intolerance, pneumatosis, stop feeds and give antibiotics.
5. IVH occurs in the first 72 hours; PVL gives spastic diplegia; screen ROP in babies of 1500 g or under 32 weeks; BPD is oxygen at 36 weeks.
6. Bilious vomiting needs urgent contrast study for malrotation; duodenal atresia links to Down syndrome; meconium ileus links to cystic fibrosis.
7. Pyloric stenosis: hypochloraemic hypokalaemic alkalosis, correct before pyloromyotomy; intussusception: redcurrant jelly stool, air enema.
8. Neonatal glucose: below 2 mmol/L give dextrose gel; below 1 or symptomatic give IV dextrose. HIE: cool 33 to 34°C for 72 hours within 6 hours.
