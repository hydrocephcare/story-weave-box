# Pleural Effusion

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Respiratory Medicine**

> **In one line:** pleural effusion is an abnormal collection of fluid in the pleural space, caused by raised hydrostatic pressure, increased permeability, reduced oncotic pressure or impaired lymphatic drainage. The key step is to decide whether it is a **transudate** (heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome) or an **exudate** (pneumonia, TB, malignancy, pulmonary embolism) using Light's criteria, then sample, treat the cause and drain when needed.

## What you must know

- Normal pleural space contains **3.5 to 7.0 mL** of clear, low-protein fluid with a few mononuclear cells.
- Fluid forms at about **0.02 mL/kg/hour** and can be cleared at about **0.2 mL/kg/hour** (a tenfold reserve), so effusion means formation exceeds clearance.
- **Dullness to percussion ("stony dull"), reduced breath sounds and absent tactile fremitus** are the signs. Key symptom: breathlessness.
- Upright chest X-ray needs about **300 mL** of fluid to show; lateral decubitus film detects about **50 mL**.
- **Light's criteria** (exudate if any one): pleural:serum protein above 0.5; pleural:serum LDH above 0.6; pleural LDH above two-thirds the upper limit of normal serum LDH.
- Pleural protein under 25 g/L suggests transudate; over 35 g/L suggests exudate.
- **Pleural pH below 7.2** in parapneumonic effusion means it must be drained.
- **80% of bilateral effusions are due to heart failure.**
- Malignant effusion: lung then breast cancer most common; manage with drainage and **pleurodesis**.

## 1. Physiology

- The pleural space lies between the **parietal** and **visceral** pleura. Fluid enters from intercostal (systemic) microvessels and drains by **lymphatics (stomata) to mediastinal nodes**.
- **Starling's law:** fluid movement = L·A·[(P_cap − P_pl) − (π_cap − π_pl)], where L is the filtration coefficient, A the surface area, P the hydrostatic pressures and π the oncotic pressures of the capillary and the pleural space.
- **Definition:** presence of a large amount of fluid in the pleural space irrespective of cause.

## 2. Causes and mechanisms

| Mechanism | Example |
|---|---|
| Raised pulmonary capillary pressure | Congestive heart failure |
| Increased capillary permeability | Pneumonia |
| Lowered intrapleural pressure | Atelectasis |
| Reduced plasma oncotic pressure | Hypoalbuminaemia (cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome) |
| Increased pleural membrane permeability | Malignancy |
| Lymphatic obstruction | Malignancy |
| Diaphragmatic defect | **Hepatic hydrothorax** (ascitic fluid passes through small diaphragmatic holes) |
| Thoracic duct rupture | **Chylothorax** |

**Other causes:** nephrotic syndrome, TB, collagen vascular disease, urinothorax, SVC syndrome, **Meigs syndrome**, rheumatoid arthritis, pancreatitis, yellow-nail syndrome, drugs.

> **Remember:** **pulmonary embolism** causes an effusion because obstruction raises pleural permeability, giving a transudate or exudate.

## 3. Clinical features

### Symptoms

- **Breathlessness** (key): fluid stops the lung expanding fully, so the patient breathes faster.
- Mild, sharp, stabbing **pleuritic pain** if the parietal pleura is irritated.
- Dry cough.
- Sometimes **no symptoms**, especially when fluid accumulates gradually.

### Signs

- **Stony dullness** to percussion, **decreased or absent breath sounds**, absent tactile fremitus.
- Large effusions push the mediastinum (trachea) **away** from the effusion.

### History to take

Pleuritic chest pain, cough, fever, haemoptysis, weight loss, trauma, history of cancer, cardiac surgery.

## 4. Types of effusion

1. **Transudate**
2. **Exudate**
3. **Empyema** (pus)
4. **Haemorrhagic effusion / haemothorax**
5. **Chylous or chyliform effusion**
6. **Urinothorax** (rare)

## 5. Imaging

### Chest X-ray (PA)

- Fluid collects in the lowest part of the chest according to posture. Upright: obscures the **costophrenic angle and hemidiaphragm**.
- A uniformly white lower zone with a concave upper edge (**meniscus sign**); heart border and diaphragm obscured.
- At least **300 mL** is needed for blunting of the costophrenic angle on an upright film.
- **Lateral decubitus film** is more sensitive and detects as little as **50 mL**.
- A **supine** film is poor: the fluid layers posteriorly and is hard to see.
- Large effusions cause **mediastinal shift away** from the effusion.
- **80% of bilateral effusions are caused by heart failure.**

## 6. Thoracentesis and fluid analysis

### Indications

- Effusion **larger than 1 cm** in height on decubitus film or ultrasound, of unknown origin.
- Not needed when the cause is obvious (e.g. bilateral effusions in heart failure). But tap in heart failure if: **fever or pleuritic pain, unilateral effusion, no cardiomegaly, or no response to diuretics.**

### Gross appearance

| Finding | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Haematocrit under 1% | Not significant |
| Haematocrit 1 to 20% | Cancer, pulmonary embolism or trauma |
| Haematocrit over 50% (of peripheral blood) | **Haemothorax** |
| Cloudy, triglycerides over 110 mg/dL | **Chylothorax** |
| Putrid odour | Infection (anaerobes); stain and culture |

### Transudate or exudate?

| Feature | Transudate | Exudate |
|---|---|---|
| Process | Passive (pressure related) | Active (inflammation) |
| Vascular permeability | Normal | Increased |
| Plasma protein leak | Absent | Present |
| Vessels | Normal | Dilated |
| Pleural protein | Under 25 g/L | Over 35 g/L |

- For protein 25 to 35 g/L use **Light's criteria**. Fluid is an **exudate** if **one or more**:
  1. Pleural protein : serum protein ratio **above 0.5**
  2. Pleural LDH : serum LDH ratio **above 0.6**
  3. Pleural LDH **more than two-thirds** of the upper limit of normal serum LDH

| Transudate | Exudate |
|---|---|
| **Heart failure (bilateral)**, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome | Pneumonia, **TB**, malignancy, pulmonary embolism |

### Further tests on exudates

| Test | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| **Cell count** | Neutrophils: acute process (pneumonia, PE). Lymphocytes: chronic (cancer, TB, post-CABG) |
| **Gram stain and culture** | Infected fluid |
| **Glucose below 60 mg/dL** | Pneumonia (empyema), cancer, rheumatoid; the lower the glucose, the more bacteria consuming it |
| **Cytology** | Malignancy; if negative and suspicion remains, thoracoscopy |
| **pH** | Low pH reflects anaerobic glycolysis from infection or tumour. **Below 7.2** in a parapneumonic effusion = empyema, **must be drained**. Very low pH in malignant effusion = large tumour burden, poor prognosis |
| TB suspected | PCR or acid-fast bacilli |
| Rheumatoid arthritis | Pleural rheumatoid factor |
| SLE | Serum complement, pleural ANA, LE cells (pleuritis is common in SLE) |
| Pancreatitis | Pleural **amylase** |
| Ruptured oesophagus | Low pH (gastric contents), food in the fluid |

> **Exam tip:** pleural pH below 7.2 means the effusion needs a chest drain.

## 7. Malignant effusion

- The pleura may look abnormal or a lung mass is seen. **Lung and breast** cancers commonly cause effusions. Order of frequency: **lung > breast > lymphoma / leukaemia**.
- Clues: no fever, blood-tinged fluid, CT suggesting malignancy.
- **Positive cytology** gives the diagnosis in about **70%** (e.g. metastatic adenocarcinoma).
- **Treatment:** sample the fluid and treat the primary tumour. Drain the fluid because it tends to re-accumulate and worsen symptoms, then prevent recurrence with **pleurodesis**: induce inflammation, fibrosis and adhesion of the pleural layers by instilling **tetracycline, talc powder, or sometimes blood**.
- **VATS** (video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery) can clear the pleural cavity under direct vision.

## 8. Treatment by type

| Condition | Management |
|---|---|
| Uncomplicated pneumonia effusion | Antibiotics |
| Haemothorax / empyema | Drain (otherwise risk of inflammation, fibrosis and trapped lung) |
| Malignant effusion | Drain, then pleurodesis to prevent re-accumulation |
| Transudate | Treat the cause (diuretics in heart failure) |

**Indications for a chest tube**

1. Empyema
2. Complicated parapneumonic effusion with pH below 7.2
3. Haemothorax
4. Malignant effusion, with or without pleurodesis

## 9. The undiagnosed effusion

- Makes up **15 to 20%** of effusions. Review history, examination, medicines, smoking and other risks. Consider an occult abdominal process and **pulmonary embolism**.
- Suspect **TB or cancer** if any of: weight loss over 4.5 kg (10 lb), fever over 38°C, a large effusion, more than 95% lymphocytes in the fluid.
- **Pleural biopsy** helps in TB but has limited value in malignancy; **thoracoscopy** is best for malignancy.

> **Check:** in Kenya, TB is a leading cause of lymphocytic exudative effusion, particularly in HIV-positive people; pleural adenosine deaminase (ADA) is a useful supportive test where available.

## Key points to revise

1. Effusion = formation exceeds drainage; causes are pressure, permeability, oncotic or lymphatic.
2. Signs: stony dull, reduced breath sounds, absent fremitus; mediastinal shift away in large effusion.
3. Upright CXR needs about 300 mL; lateral decubitus detects 50 mL.
4. Light's criteria: protein ratio above 0.5, LDH ratio above 0.6, or LDH above two-thirds ULN means exudate.
5. Transudates: heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic. Exudates: pneumonia, TB, cancer, PE.
6. pH below 7.2, pus or haemothorax means chest drain.
7. Malignant effusion: lung then breast; drain and pleurodesis (talc, tetracycline).
8. Undiagnosed effusion: think TB, cancer, PE; thoracoscopy for suspected cancer.
