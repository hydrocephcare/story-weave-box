## MBPA 3413 & MBPA 3412 — Exam Crash Course Notes
*General Pathology II & III | Point-form, comprehensive*
## Section One: Genetic Disorders (MBPA 3413)
*Mutations, Mendelian Disorders, Molecular Diagnosis, Chromosomal Disorders, Complex Multigenic Disorders*
### 1.1 Key Definitions
- **Genetics** — study of individual genes and heredity
- **Genomics** — study of the entire genome (all genes + interactions)
- **Genetic disorder** — disease caused by abnormality in an individual's DNA
- **DNA (Deoxyribonucleic acid)** — double-stranded molecule carrying genetic instructions
- **Exons** — coding sequences of DNA that are expressed into protein
- **Introns** — non-coding sequences within a gene; spliced out before translation
- **Intergenic regions** — DNA between genes; mostly non-coding
- **SNP (Single Nucleotide Polymorphism)** — variation at a single base pair; occurs ~every 1,000 bp; used as genetic markers
- **Epigenetics** — heritable changes in gene expression without altering DNA sequence
- **Proteomics** — large-scale study of all proteins expressed by a genome
- **Bioinformatics** — use of computational tools to analyse biological data (e.g., genome sequences)
- **Genotype** — the genetic makeup of an individual
- **Phenotype** — observable characteristics resulting from genotype + environment
- **Gene therapy** — treatment of disease by correcting/replacing defective genes
- **Hereditary** — passed from parent to child through the germline
- **Familial** — runs in families but not necessarily purely genetic
- **Congenital** — present at birth (may or may not be genetic)
- **Mutation** — permanent change in DNA sequence
- **DNA microarray analysis** — "gene chip" technology; interrogates thousands of genes simultaneously on a single platform
- **Recombinant DNA technology** — lab technique of joining DNA from different sources to produce new genetic combinations
### 1.2 Mutations
**Definition:** permanent change in the DNA; can be inherited (germline) or acquired (somatic)
**Categories of mutations**
- **Genome mutations** — loss/gain of whole chromosomes → monosomies / trisomies
- **Chromosome mutations** — visible structural rearrangements of chromosomes
- **Gene mutations** — partial/complete deletion of a gene or single base pair change
**Types of gene mutations**
- **Point mutations (substitutions)**
  - *Missense* — changes one amino acid to another (conservative or non-conservative, e.g. sickle cell anaemia)
  - *Nonsense* — changes an amino acid codon to a stop codon → truncated, non-functional protein
- **Frameshift mutations** — insertion/deletion of 1 or 2 base pairs → shifts the entire reading frame → abnormal protein
- **Deletions / insertions**
  - < 3 base pairs → frameshift
  - Multiples of 3 → missing amino acid(s) but frame intact → abnormal protein
- **Trinucleotide repeat mutations** — dynamic amplification of a 3-nucleotide sequence (e.g. Huntington's disease)
- **Promoter/enhancer mutations** — disrupt transcription factor binding → reduced or absent gene expression (e.g. some hereditary haemolytic anaemias)
**How mutations cause genetic disease** — through:
- Enzyme defects → failure of metabolic pathways
- Enzyme inhibitor defects
- Defective receptors → impaired cell signalling
- Defective transport systems
- Abnormal structural proteins (e.g. collagen disorders)
- Defects in haemostasis
- Defects in growth regulation
- Adverse drug reactions (pharmacogenomics)
### 1.3 Mendelian Disorders & Transmission Patterns
**Key facts**
- Every person carries 5–8 deleterious genes (mostly recessive)
- 80% of mutations are familial; the remainder are de novo
- **Pleiotropism** — one mutant gene produces many effects
- **Genetic heterogeneity** — mutations at different loci produce the same phenotype
**Autosomal Dominant (AD)**
- At least one parent usually affected (de novo possible in older fathers)
- Affects males and females equally
- Both sexes can transmit the disorder
- 50% risk per pregnancy
- Features: reduced penetrance, variable expressivity, often delayed onset
- Mechanisms: reduced gene product production, or production of an inactive/abnormal protein
**Autosomal Recessive (AR)**
- Largest category of Mendelian disorders
- Parents usually unaffected (carriers); siblings may be affected
- 25% recurrence risk per birth
- More uniform expression than AD; complete penetrance common
- Onset frequently early in life
- Includes nearly all inborn errors of metabolism
- Consanguinity increases risk
**X-Linked Disorders**
- Almost all X-linked disorders are recessive
- Affected males do not transmit to sons — daughters become carriers
- Carrier females may show mild features
- X-linked dominant is rare (e.g. vitamin D-resistant rickets)
### 1.4 Molecular Diagnosis of Mendelian & Complex Disorders
**Background**
- Field emerged in the latter 20th century via karyotyping and Southern blotting
- Four advances drove expansion: ① human genome sequencing ② PCR kits ③ microarrays ④ next-generation sequencing
- Mutations tested can be germline (in every cell, e.g. CFTR in cystic fibrosis) or somatic (tissue-specific, e.g. NMYC in neuroblastoma)
**Diagnosis of copy number abnormalities**
| Method | What it detects |
|---|---|
| G-banding karyotype | Whole chromosome changes; low resolution |
| FISH | Specific chromosomal regions > 100 kb; no dividing cells needed |
| Array-CGH | Genome-wide; detects amplifications/deletions down to 10 kb resolution |
| SNP microarray | > 1 million SNPs; highest resolution; detects copy number + zygosity |
**FISH (Fluorescence In Situ Hybridization)**
- Uses fluorescent probes that bind to specific chromosomal regions
- Can be used on: amniocentesis cells, chorionic villus biopsy, peripheral blood, archival tissue
- Detects: aneuploidy, microdeletions, complex translocations, gene amplification
- Limitation: requires prior knowledge of the region being tested
**Array-Based CGH (Comparative Genomic Hybridization)**
- Test DNA + reference DNA labelled with different fluorescent dyes (Cy3 & Cy5)
- Hybridised to an array of genomic segments on a glass slide
- Gains → increased signal; losses → decreased signal vs. reference
- No prior knowledge of the mutation needed
**PCR (Polymerase Chain Reaction)**
- Exponentially amplifies specific DNA sequences
- Requires knowledge of the normal gene sequence
- If RNA is the starting material → reverse transcribed to cDNA first (RT-PCR)
- Mutations then identified by sequencing or hybridisation
**Sanger Sequencing**
- Reads nucleotide order in DNA; compares to wild-type sequence to identify mutations
- Fluorescent dye-based; largely automated
- Also done via gene chips (microarrays with tiled oligonucleotides)
**Next-Generation (NextGen) Sequencing**
- "Massively parallel sequencing" — millions of fragments sequenced simultaneously
- Generates > 1 gigabase of data per day at low cost
- Allows whole genome sequencing in days
- Used for: somatic tumour mutation profiling, rare Mendelian disorder identification
- Works by: fluorescently labelled nucleotides incorporated into immobilised template strands → signal read → mapped to genome using bioinformatics
**Linkage Analysis & GWAS**
- Used when the causative gene is unknown or the disease is polygenic
- Uses marker loci (especially SNPs) in linkage with disease genes
- HapMap project — divided the genome into haplotype blocks; allows efficient SNP-based gene hunting
- GWAS (Genome-Wide Association Studies) — screens millions of SNPs to find disease-associated variants in large populations
**Indications for genetic testing**
*Prenatal:*
- Advanced maternal age > 34 years (↑ trisomy risk)
- Carrier of balanced translocation/inversion
- Previous child with chromosomal abnormality
- Partner is a carrier of an X-linked disorder
*Postnatal (on peripheral blood lymphocytes):*
- Multiple congenital anomalies
- Unexplained intellectual disability / developmental delay
- Features of Down syndrome, Prader-Willi, Turner syndrome, fragile X
- Infertility (rule out sex chromosome anomaly)
- Recurrent spontaneous abortions
### 1.5 Chromosomal Disorders
**Numerical abnormalities**
- Caused by nondisjunction during meiosis → gametes with n+1 or n−1 chromosomes
- **Trisomy** (2n+1) — e.g. Down syndrome (trisomy 21)
- **Monosomy** (2n−1) — autosomal monosomy incompatible with life; sex chromosome monosomy viable (e.g. Turner syndrome, 45,X)
- **Mosaicism** — two or more cell populations with different chromosomal complements in the same individual; results from postzygotic mitotic nondisjunction
**Structural abnormalities** (after chromosomal breakage)
- **Translocation** — transfer of a chromosomal segment to another chromosome
  - *Balanced reciprocal* — exchange with no net loss/gain
  - *Robertsonian* — between two acrocentric chromosomes; one large + one tiny (lost) chromosome formed; predisposes to unbalanced gametes
- **Isochromosome** — centromere divides horizontally → chromosome with two identical arms (e.g. i(Xq), most common in live births)
- **Deletion** — loss of a chromosomal segment; an isolated fragment without a centromere is usually lost
- **Inversion** — segment re-inserted in reverse orientation after two breaks
- **Ring chromosome** — segments lost from both ends; arms join to form a ring
Notation: p = short arm (petit); q = long arm — e.g. 2q34 = chromosome 2, long arm, region 3, band 4
**General rules of chromosomal disorders**
- Loss of material is more severe than gain
- Sex chromosome imbalance is better tolerated than autosomal imbalance
- Sex chromosome disorders are often subtle; infertility may be the first sign (detected at adolescence)
- Most are de novo (parents normal, low recurrence risk) — exception: translocation Down syndrome
### 1.6 Complex Multigenic (Multifactorial) Disorders
- Also called polygenic or multifactorial disorders
- Caused by interactions between variant genes and environmental factors
- A **polymorphism** = a genetic variant with ≥ 2 alleles occurring in ≥ 1% of the population
- **Common disease–common variant hypothesis** — many polymorphisms, each with modest effect and low penetrance, are co-inherited to produce disease
**Key points**
- Different polymorphisms vary in significance — e.g. in type 1 diabetes, 6–7 of 20–30 implicated genes are most important; a few HLA alleles contribute > 50% of risk
- Some polymorphisms are shared across similar diseases; others are disease-specific
- **Quantitative trait loci (QTLs)** — traits like height, skin colour, and intelligence follow multigenic inheritance with continuous variation
- Environment significantly modifies expression — e.g. obesity unmasks the type 2 diabetes genetic trait
- Diagnosis of multifactorial inheritance requires familial clustering + exclusion of Mendelian and chromosomal modes of transmission
- A variable severity range is suggestive — but variable expressivity of single-gene disorders must be excluded

## Section Two: Neoplasia & Oncology (MBPA 3412)
*Introduction to Neoplasia, Tumour Nomenclature, TNM Staging, Carcinogenic Agents, Viral Oncogenesis*
### 2.1 Key Definitions
- **Neoplasia** — formation of new, abnormal tissue growth
- **Neoplasm / Tumour** — abnormal mass of tissue; growth exceeds and is uncoordinated with normal tissue; persists after the initiating stimulus ceases
- **Mesenchymal tissue** — connective tissue origin (fat, muscle, bone, cartilage)
- **Epithelial tissue** — tissue lining surfaces and glands
- **Cancer** — common term for all malignant neoplasms
- **Benign** — localised, non-invasive tumour; does not metastasise
- **Malignant** — invasive tumour capable of metastasis; life-threatening
- **Clonal proliferation** — tumour arising from a single transformed precursor cell
- **Differentiation** — degree to which tumour cells resemble their normal cell of origin
- **Anaplasia** — lack of differentiation; a hallmark of malignancy
### 2.2 Tumour Nomenclature & Classification
**Benign tumours**
- Named by cell/tissue of origin + suffix "-oma"
- Mesenchymal origin → fibroma, chondroma, lipoma, leiomyoma
- Glandular epithelial → adenoma (solid mass) or cystadenoma (cyst-forming)
- Finger-like projections → papilloma
**Malignant tumours**
- Mesenchymal origin → sarcomas (e.g. liposarcoma, fibrosarcoma, osteosarcoma)
- Epithelial origin → carcinomas (e.g. squamous cell carcinoma)
- Glandular epithelial → adenocarcinoma
- Oddly named exceptions: melanoma, seminoma, mesothelioma (don't follow the standard rules)
### 2.3 TNM Staging System
Published by the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) — in use for over 70 years.
- **T** — Primary Tumour: size and local extent
- **N** — Regional lymph Nodes: involvement and extent
- **M** — Distant Metastases: present (1) or absent (0)
**Importance of TNM**
- Guides treatment planning
- Indicates prognosis
- Evaluates treatment outcomes
- Facilitates information exchange between treatment centres
- Supports cancer registries and national cancer control planning
- Internationally standardised "common language" for oncology professionals
### 2.4 Carcinogenic Agents
Core principle: genetic damage is the foundation of carcinogenesis. Three classes of agents cause this.
**Chemical carcinogens**
*Direct-acting agents* — require no metabolic conversion to be carcinogenic
- Generally weak carcinogens
- Examples: alkylating agents (cyclophosphamide, chlorambucil), acylating agents
- Note: anticancer drugs (e.g. in Hodgkin lymphoma treatment) can themselves cause secondary leukaemia
*Indirect-acting agents (procarcinogens)* — require metabolic activation
- Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (e.g. benzo[a]pyrene in cigarette smoke, charbroiled meat)
- Aromatic amines & azo dyes (e.g. β-naphthylamine → 50× increased bladder cancer in dye workers)
- Natural products: aflatoxin B1 (Aspergillus mould → hepatocellular carcinoma in Africa/Asia)
- Others: vinyl chloride, arsenic, nickel, chromium, nitrosamines
*Mechanism of chemical carcinogenesis*
- Most are mutagens → form covalent adducts with DNA
- Common targets: RAS oncogene, TP53 tumour suppressor
- Aflatoxin B1 produces a "signature mutation" in TP53
- **Initiation** → mutation of an oncogene (e.g. RAS activation)
- **Promotion** → subsequent exposure to promoters (e.g. phorbol esters, hormones) → clonal expansion of initiated cells → accumulation of further mutations → malignancy
- Cytochrome P-450 enzyme polymorphisms affect individual susceptibility to chemical carcinogens
**Radiation carcinogenesis**
- UV rays, X-rays, and nuclear radiation are all established carcinogens
- Ionising radiation (X-rays, gamma) → chromosome breaks, translocations, point mutations; double-stranded DNA breaks are the most important
- Hiroshima/Nagasaki survivors → markedly increased leukaemia (especially myelogenous) after ~7-year latency; also thyroid, breast, colon, and lung cancers
- UV radiation → forms pyrimidine dimers in DNA, normally repaired by nucleotide excision repair
- Excessive UV → overwhelms repair → skin cancers (melanoma, squamous cell carcinoma, basal cell carcinoma)
- Xeroderma pigmentosum — inherited defect in nucleotide excision repair → greatly increased skin cancer risk
**Viral & microbial oncogenesis**
*Oncogenic RNA virus*
- **HTLV-1** (Human T-cell Lymphotropic Virus-1) → T-cell leukaemia/lymphoma
  - Endemic in Japan and the Caribbean; transmitted sexually, via blood products, or breastfeeding
  - Only 3–5% of infected persons develop leukaemia; 20–50 year latency
  - Mechanism: viral TAX protein transactivates cytokine genes (IL-2, IL-15) → autocrine T-cell proliferation; TAX also inactivates tumour suppressors (CDKN2A/p16, TP53)
  - TAX is necessary and sufficient for cellular transformation
*Oncogenic DNA viruses*
- **HPV** (Human Papillomavirus)
  - Low-risk types (6, 11) → genital warts; low malignant potential
  - High-risk types (16, 18) → cervical carcinoma, anogenital cancers
- **EBV** (Epstein-Barr Virus) → Burkitt lymphoma, nasopharyngeal carcinoma, some B-cell lymphomas
- **HBV & HCV** → hepatocellular carcinoma
- **HHV-8 / KSHV** → Kaposi sarcoma
- **H. pylori** (bacterium) → gastric carcinoma, MALT lymphoma
### 2.5 Cancer Burden (Statistics to Know)
- Global, 2000: 10 million new cases; 6 million deaths
- Global, 2020: 18.1 million new cases; 10 million deaths (1 in every 6 deaths globally)
- Kenya, 2018: ~47,887 new cases; ~32,987 deaths → 3rd leading cause of death

## Section Three: Specific Genetic Disorders, Cancer Biology & Advanced Oncology
*Specific Mendelian Disorders, Hallmarks of Cancer, Oncogenes & Tumour Suppressors, Metastasis, Host Response, Paraneoplastic Syndromes, Gene Therapy, Mitochondrial & Non-Mendelian Disorders*
### 3.1 Specific Mendelian Disorders — Autosomal Recessive
**Cystic Fibrosis (CF)**
- Most common lethal AR disorder in white populations
- Gene: CFTR (Cystic Fibrosis Transmembrane Conductance Regulator) on chromosome 7
- Most common mutation: ΔF508 (deletion of phenylalanine at position 508)
- CFTR normally functions as a chloride channel regulated by cAMP
- Defective CFTR → abnormal chloride transport → thick, viscid secretions in lungs, pancreas, liver, gut, reproductive tract
- Features:
  - Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease → recurrent infections (Pseudomonas, Staph aureus)
  - Pancreatic insufficiency → malabsorption, steatorrhoea
  - Meconium ileus in neonates
  - Infertility in males (absent vas deferens)
  - Elevated sweat chloride → diagnostic (sweat chloride > 60 mEq/L)
**Phenylketonuria (PKU)**
- Deficiency of phenylalanine hydroxylase → cannot convert phenylalanine to tyrosine
- Accumulation of phenylalanine is toxic to the developing brain
- Features:
  - Intellectual disability (if untreated)
  - Seizures, tremors, behavioural disturbances
  - Fair skin and hair (reduced melanin — tyrosine is needed for melanin synthesis)
  - Musty/mousy body odour
- Screened at birth (Guthrie test); treated with a low-phenylalanine diet
**Sickle Cell Anaemia (SCA)**
- Point mutation (non-conservative missense): glutamic acid → valine at position 6 of the β-globin chain
- Results in haemoglobin S (HbS)
- Under low oxygen → HbS polymerises → sickling of RBCs
- Features:
  - Chronic haemolytic anaemia
  - Vaso-occlusive crises → pain, organ infarction
  - Splenic sequestration → functional asplenia → increased infection risk (encapsulated organisms)
  - Stroke, acute chest syndrome, avascular necrosis
  - Heterozygotes (HbAS) — sickle cell trait; relatively protected against malaria
**Tay-Sachs Disease**
- Deficiency of hexosaminidase A → accumulation of GM2 ganglioside in neurons
- Progressive neuronal destruction
- Features: motor weakness, blindness, cherry-red spot on the macula, seizures → death by age 2–3
- Common in Ashkenazi Jewish populations
### 3.2 Specific Mendelian Disorders — Autosomal Dominant
**Huntington's Disease**
- Trinucleotide repeat mutation — CAG repeat expansion in the HTT gene on chromosome 4
- Normal: < 36 repeats; disease: > 36 repeats
- **Anticipation** — disease worsens / earlier onset in successive generations
- Features:
  - Progressive involuntary choreiform movements
  - Dementia
  - Psychiatric disturbances (depression, personality change)
  - Onset typically 35–45 years
  - No cure; uniformly fatal
**Marfan Syndrome**
- Mutation in the FBN1 gene → defective fibrillin-1 (structural glycoprotein in elastic tissue)
- Features:
  - Tall stature, long limbs, arachnodactyly (spider fingers)
  - Lens dislocation (ectopia lentis) — upward
  - Aortic aneurysm / dissection — most common cause of death
  - Mitral valve prolapse
  - High arched palate
**Neurofibromatosis Type 1 (NF1)**
- Mutation in the NF1 gene → loss of neurofibromin (tumour suppressor / RAS-GAP)
- Features:
  - Multiple neurofibromas (benign peripheral nerve sheath tumours)
  - Café-au-lait spots (≥ 6, > 1.5 cm diameter)
  - Lisch nodules (iris hamartomas)
  - Increased risk of malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumours
### 3.3 Chromosomal Disorders — Specific Conditions
**Down Syndrome (Trisomy 21)**
- Most common chromosomal disorder compatible with life
- 95% → nondisjunction (extra free chromosome 21); 4% → Robertsonian translocation; 1% → mosaic
- Maternal age is strongly associated with the nondisjunction form
- Features:
  - Intellectual disability (variable severity)
  - Flat facial profile, epicanthic folds, upward slanting palpebral fissures
  - Single palmar crease (simian crease)
  - Congenital heart disease (especially ASD, VSD) — most common cause of death
  - Duodenal atresia
  - Increased risk of ALL and AML
  - Early-onset Alzheimer's disease (chromosome 21 carries the APP gene)
**Turner Syndrome (45,X)**
- Most common sex chromosome disorder in females
- 57% → complete monosomy X; the rest → mosaics or structural X abnormalities
- Features:
  - Short stature
  - Primary amenorrhoea / ovarian failure (streak gonads)
  - Webbed neck, low posterior hairline
  - Broad shield chest, widely spaced nipples
  - Bicuspid aortic valve / coarctation of the aorta
  - Normal intelligence (usually)
**Klinefelter Syndrome (47,XXY)**
- Most common cause of male hypogonadism
- Extra X chromosome in males
- Features:
  - Tall stature with long legs
  - Small, firm testes → infertility (azoospermia)
  - Gynaecomastia
  - Reduced body/facial hair
  - Mild intellectual impairment possible
### 3.4 Hallmarks of Cancer
Fundamental biological capabilities acquired during tumour development:
- Self-sufficiency in growth signals → oncogene activation (e.g. RAS, MYC)
- Insensitivity to growth-inhibitory signals → tumour suppressor loss (e.g. RB, TP53)
- Evasion of apoptosis → BCL-2 overexpression; TP53 loss
- Limitless replicative potential → telomerase reactivation
- Sustained angiogenesis → VEGF upregulation → new vessel formation feeding the tumour
- Invasion and metastasis → loss of E-cadherin; matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) activation
- Reprogramming of energy metabolism → Warburg effect (aerobic glycolysis)
- Evading immune destruction → downregulation of MHC class I; PD-L1 expression
### 3.5 Oncogenes & Tumour Suppressor Genes
**Oncogenes**
- Mutated/overactivated versions of normal proto-oncogenes
- Act in a dominant fashion — one mutant allele is sufficient
- Drive uncontrolled cell proliferation
| Oncogene | Function | Associated cancer |
|---|---|---|
| RAS | GTPase signal transduction | Lung, colon, pancreas |
| MYC | Transcription factor | Burkitt lymphoma, many others |
| HER2/NEU | Growth factor receptor | Breast, ovarian cancer |
| BCL-2 | Anti-apoptotic protein | Follicular lymphoma |
| NMYC | Transcription factor | Neuroblastoma |
**Tumour suppressor genes**
- Normal function: inhibit cell proliferation, promote apoptosis
- Act in a recessive fashion — both alleles must be lost (Knudson's two-hit hypothesis)
| Gene | Function | Associated cancer |
|---|---|---|
| TP53 | "Guardian of the genome" — apoptosis, DNA repair | Most human cancers |
| RB | Cell cycle checkpoint (G1/S) | Retinoblastoma, osteosarcoma |
| APC | Wnt signalling inhibitor | Colorectal cancer |
| BRCA1/2 | DNA repair | Breast, ovarian cancer |
| CDKN2A/p16 | CDK inhibitor → cell cycle arrest | Melanoma, many cancers |
**DNA repair genes**
- When mutated → accumulation of mutations genome-wide → "mutator phenotype"
- Examples: MLH1, MSH2 → microsatellite instability → hereditary non-polyposis colorectal cancer (HNPCC/Lynch syndrome)
### 3.6 Tumour Invasion & Metastasis
Metastasis = spread of a tumour to a site discontinuous with the primary tumour; the defining feature of malignancy.
**Steps of metastasis (the metastatic cascade)**
1. **Local invasion** — tumour cells detach from the primary mass; degrade the basement membrane using MMPs; invade surrounding stroma
2. **Intravasation** — entry into blood vessels or lymphatics
3. **Survival in circulation** — tumour cells evade immune attack; often travel as clumps with platelets
4. **Arrest at a distant site** — tumour cells adhere to endothelium at distant capillary beds
5. **Extravasation** — exit the vessel into distant tissue
6. **Colonisation** — proliferate and establish a metastatic deposit; requires a suitable microenvironment ("seed and soil" hypothesis — Paget)
**Molecular changes enabling invasion**
- Loss of E-cadherin → loss of cell-cell adhesion → cells detach
- Epithelial-Mesenchymal Transition (EMT) → tumour cells acquire migratory, invasive properties
- Upregulation of MMPs (matrix metalloproteinases) → ECM degradation
- Altered integrins → changed adhesion to ECM components
**Common metastatic patterns**
- Carcinomas → lymphatics first, then blood
- Sarcomas → blood first
- Colorectal → liver (portal drainage)
- Lung, breast → bone, brain, liver, adrenals
- Prostate → bone (osteoblastic)
### 3.7 Host Response to Tumours & Paraneoplastic Syndromes
**Immune response to tumours**
- Tumours express tumour-associated antigens (TAAs) → recognised by the immune system
- CD8+ cytotoxic T cells — primary anti-tumour effectors
- NK cells — kill tumour cells with low/absent MHC class I expression
- Tumours evade immunity by: downregulating MHC I, secreting immunosuppressive cytokines (TGF-β, IL-10), expressing PD-L1 (checkpoint inhibition)
**Paraneoplastic syndromes**
- Symptoms not caused by the direct tumour mass or metastasis
- Caused by tumour secretion of hormones/cytokines, or immune cross-reactivity
- Important to recognise — may be the first sign of cancer
| Syndrome | Mechanism | Associated cancer |
|---|---|---|
| Hypercalcaemia | PTHrP secretion | Squamous cell lung, renal, breast |
| SIADH | Ectopic ADH | Small cell lung cancer |
| Cushing's syndrome | Ectopic ACTH | Small cell lung cancer |
| Polycythaemia | Ectopic EPO | Renal cell carcinoma, hepatoma |
| Trousseau's sign | Hypercoagulability | Pancreatic, lung cancer |
| Acanthosis nigricans | TGF-α secretion | Gastric, lung cancer |
| Eaton-Lambert syndrome | Anti-VGCC antibodies | Small cell lung cancer |
### 3.8 Gene Therapy
**Definition:** treatment of disease by introducing, altering, or replacing genetic material within a person's cells.
**Approaches**
- Gene replacement — introduce a functional copy of a defective gene
- Gene silencing — use RNA interference (RNAi) to suppress an overactive/mutant gene
- Gene editing — CRISPR-Cas9 to precisely correct mutations at the DNA level
**Vectors used for gene delivery**
- Viral vectors (retroviruses, adenoviruses, AAV — adeno-associated virus) — most efficient
- Non-viral vectors (liposomes, naked DNA) — safer but less efficient
**Germline vs. somatic gene therapy**
- Somatic — changes only the treated individual; not heritable → ethically accepted
- Germline — changes passed to offspring → highly controversial; not currently permitted
**Clinical applications**
- SCID (Severe Combined Immunodeficiency) — ADA deficiency → one of the first successes
- Cystic fibrosis — trials ongoing
- Haemophilia A & B — promising results with AAV vectors
- Cancer — CAR-T cell therapy (chimeric antigen receptor T cells)
### 3.9 Mitochondrial Disorders
- Mitochondria have their own DNA (mtDNA) — circular, 16,569 base pairs; encodes 37 genes
- Maternal inheritance — mitochondria come exclusively from the oocyte (egg); fathers do not transmit
- **Heteroplasmy** — coexistence of normal and mutant mtDNA in the same cell; the degree determines severity
- All cells rely on mitochondria → mutations affect high-energy-demand tissues most: brain, muscle, heart, retina
**Features of mitochondrial diseases**
- Maternal inheritance pattern
- Variable severity within a family (heteroplasmy)
- Organs affected: CNS, skeletal muscle, cardiac muscle, eye
- Lactic acidosis (failure of oxidative phosphorylation → anaerobic metabolism)
**Examples**
- MELAS — Mitochondrial Encephalomyopathy, Lactic Acidosis, Stroke-like episodes
- MERRF — Myoclonic Epilepsy with Ragged-Red Fibres
- Leber's Hereditary Optic Neuropathy (LHON) → sudden blindness in young adults
### 3.10 Non-Mendelian Inheritance Patterns
**Genomic imprinting**
- Certain genes are expressed from only one parental allele (the other is silenced epigenetically)
- The same deletion causes a different disease depending on which parent it came from
- **Prader-Willi syndrome** — deletion of chromosome 15q11-q13 from the father → hypotonia, obesity, intellectual disability, hypogonadism
- **Angelman syndrome** — deletion of the same region from the mother → severe intellectual disability, seizures, happy affect, absent speech
**Uniparental disomy (UPD)**
- Both copies of a chromosome (or region) inherited from the same parent
- Can cause imprinting disorders without a visible deletion
**Trinucleotide repeat disorders**
- Unstable DNA sequences that expand across generations
- Anticipation — earlier onset and greater severity in successive generations
- Examples: Huntington's (CAG), Fragile X (CGG), Myotonic Dystrophy (CTG), Friedreich's Ataxia (GAA)
**Fragile X Syndrome**
- Most common inherited cause of intellectual disability
- X-linked; CGG repeat expansion in the FMR1 gene
- Normal: < 55 repeats; premutation: 55–200; full mutation: > 200 repeats
- Full mutation → methylation and silencing of FMR1 → loss of FMRP protein
- Features: intellectual disability, macroorchidism (large testes), large ears, prominent jaw, autism-like behaviour
