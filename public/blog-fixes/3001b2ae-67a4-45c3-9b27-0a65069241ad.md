# Seizures and Epilepsy in Children

**Year 4 · Paediatrics · Paediatric neurology**

> **In one line:** a **seizure is a symptom** of abnormal, hypersynchronous electrical activity in cortical neurons, not a disease; **epilepsy** is a tendency to recurrent unprovoked seizures. Diagnosis is **clinical** (history and observation), investigations support it, and treatment is matched to the seizure type with the child managed as a whole. Febrile seizures and the mimics of epilepsy are the commonest exam topics.

## What you must know

- Seizure = symptom of cerebral pathology; **epilepsy needs at least two unprovoked seizures**.
- Seizures occur in **3-5%** of children; febrile seizures in **2-4%**; epilepsy in about **1%**.
- **Focal** seizures: consciousness preserved (simple) or impaired (complex). **Generalised** seizures: loss of consciousness.
- Epilepsy is a **clinical diagnosis**; an abnormal EEG does not prove, and a normal EEG does not exclude, epilepsy.
- Generalised seizures: think **valproate**; focal seizures: think **carbamazepine**.
- Febrile seizure is **not** an indication for antiepileptic drugs; give diazepam only for seizures lasting over 5 minutes.
- **Infantile spasms**: hypsarrhythmia on EEG; **childhood absence**: 3 Hz spike-and-wave, ethosuximide first-line.

## 1. Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Seizure** | Clinical manifestation of aberrant, abnormal electrical activity in cortical neurons; a symptom of cerebral pathology |
| **Epilepsy** | Chronic disorder with a tendency to spontaneous, recurrent seizures; requires at least **two unprovoked seizures** |
| **Epileptic syndrome** | Complex of symptoms and signs that define a unique epilepsy condition (for example Lennox-Gastaut syndrome) |
| **Epileptic disorder** | Chronic neurological condition characterised by recurrent epileptic seizures |
| **Non-epileptic seizures (NES)** | Paroxysmal events that can be mistaken for epilepsy but are not due to an epileptic disorder |

## 2. Epidemiology

- Seizures are the commonest neurological problem in children, in **3-5%**.
- Febrile seizures occur in **2-4%** of the paediatric population.
- Epilepsy occurs in about **1%**.

## 3. History

Ask about the event in three phases:

| Pre-ictal | Ictal | Post-ictal |
|---|---|---|
| Warning or aura? Abdominal discomfort, fear, unpleasant sensations? | Which body movements? | How did the child feel? |
| What was the child doing; asleep or awake? | Perioral cyanosis? | Confused and tired? |
| Sleep deprived? | Loss of consciousness? | Time to return to baseline? |
| Triggers? | How long did it last? | Headache? |
| Well beforehand, or fever or illness? | How many episodes; how often? | Weakness noticed (Todd's paresis)? |

**Also ask**: previous seizures, history of febrile seizures, past medical history, pregnancy and birth history, **developmental history**, current medications and family history of seizures.

## 4. Examination

A complete paediatric examination, with attention to:
- Vitals including **temperature**.
- Height, weight and **head circumference** plotted on a growth chart.
- Developmental stage (gross motor, fine motor, language, social).
- Signs of **trauma** and of **raised intracranial pressure**.
- **Skin lesions** (neurocutaneous disease, for example tuberous sclerosis, neurofibromatosis).
- **Fundoscopy**.
- Neurological examination: **focal deficits indicate a symptomatic seizure**.

## 5. Categorising the seizure

Two questions determine treatment and prognosis:
1. Is it **focal or generalised**?
2. Is it **simple or complex** (is consciousness impaired)?

### 5.1 Classic classification of epileptic seizures

| Group | Types |
|---|---|
| **Focal (partial)**: no loss of consciousness (simple) | **Simple partial** (motor, sensory, hallucinations, autonomic, psychic); **complex partial** (impaired consciousness); partial with **secondary generalisation** |
| **Generalised**: complete loss of consciousness | **Absence** (typical and atypical), **myoclonic**, **clonic**, **tonic**, **tonic-clonic**, **atonic** |
| Unclassified | |

> **Remember:** an **aura** occurs with temporal lobe epilepsy; frontal lobe epilepsy does not have an aura.

### 5.2 International classification of epilepsies and syndromes

- **Localisation-related** (focal, local, partial).
- **Generalised** epilepsies and syndromes.
- **Undetermined** epilepsies: with both generalised and focal seizures (neonatal seizure, severe myoclonic epilepsy of infancy, epilepsy with continuous slow-wave during sleep, Landau-Kleffner syndrome) or without unequivocal features.
- **Special syndromes**: conditions with seizures that do not require a diagnosis of epilepsy, such as **febrile convulsion**, isolated seizure or status epilepticus, and seizures only with an acute or toxic event.

The classification framework stresses that you must **take the child as a whole** (seizure type, epilepsy type, syndrome, aetiology, comorbidities).

## 6. Causes of seizures

Think by age and mechanism: fever and CNS infection (meningitis, encephalitis, cerebral malaria, which is important in Kenya); metabolic (hypoglycaemia, hypocalcaemia, hyponatraemia, hypomagnesaemia); hypoxic-ischaemic injury; structural lesions, trauma and tumour; genetic and neurocutaneous syndromes; toxins and drugs; and idiopathic (genetic) epilepsy.

## 7. Differential diagnosis

Syncope, breath-holding spells, GERD, panic attack, daydreaming, conversion or non-epileptic seizure, benign sleep myoclonus, benign paroxysmal vertigo, complicated migraine and motor tics (see section 12).

## 8. Investigations

**Epilepsy is a clinical diagnosis.**

- **Blood tests** to rule out acute symptomatic causes: FBC and differential, **glucose**, electrolytes (**sodium, calcium, magnesium, phosphorus**).
- **Additional tests**: INR and PTT (haemorrhagic basis); drug levels (toxic basis); karyotype and specific genetic tests; metabolic tests (ammonia, lactate, pyruvate, amino acids, urine organic acids).
- **Lumbar puncture** (AAP recommendation) if there is fever:
  - **Under 12 months**: strongly considered (meningeal signs are very subtle).
  - **Under 18 months**: considered.
  - **Any child with meningeal signs.**
- **Neuro-imaging**: **CT** if head trauma is present or suspected; **MRI** for focal seizure, focal neurological deficit or signs of raised ICP.
- **EEG**: recommended as part of the evaluation of an apparent first unprovoked seizure. An abnormal EEG alone is **not sufficient** to diagnose epilepsy, and a normal EEG does not rule it out.

## 9. Treatment

- Choose the antiepileptic drug (AED) by seizure type.
- **Start with a small dose and build up slowly.**
- Explain possible side effects to the parents.
- Teach parents how to manage an attack.

| Seizure type | First thought |
|---|---|
| **Generalised** | **Valproate** |
| **Focal** | **Carbamazepine** |

### AEDs that can worsen specific seizures

| Drug | Syndrome or seizure type made worse |
|---|---|
| **Carbamazepine, vigabatrin, tiagabine, phenytoin** | Childhood absence epilepsy, juvenile absence epilepsy, juvenile myoclonic epilepsy |
| **Vigabatrin** | Absence and absence status |
| **Clonazepam** | Generalised tonic status in Lennox-Gastaut syndrome |
| **Lamotrigine** | Dravet syndrome, juvenile myoclonic epilepsy |

> **Exam tip:** carbamazepine and phenytoin can aggravate absence and myoclonic seizures; do not use them in generalised epilepsy of these types.

**Other modalities**: epilepsy surgery, vagal nerve stimulation, ketogenic diet.

### When to stop AEDs (AAN practice parameter, 1996)
- Seizure-free for **2-5 years** on AEDs.
- Single type of seizure.
- Normal neurological examination and normal IQ.
- EEG normalised with treatment.

## 10. Febrile seizures

### 10.1 Simple febrile seizure
- Affects about **4%** of children; age **5 months to 6 years**.
- Usually generalised convulsion, duration **under 15 minutes**.
- Fever **above 38.4 degrees C**.
- No previous neonatal seizures or unprovoked seizures.

### 10.2 Complex febrile seizure
- Prolonged (**over 15 minutes**) or partial onset.
- **Multiple recurrences within 24 hours.**
- **Febrile status epilepticus**: a seizure lasting over 30 minutes, or a series without full recovery in between.

### 10.3 AAP guidance on fever with seizure
- Routine serum electrolytes, calcium, phosphorus, magnesium, FBC or glucose have **limited value** in the absence of a suspicious history or abnormal examination in infants over 6 months.
- CT/MRI: not helpful; consider in prolonged focal seizure with no clear cause.
- EEG: limited value.
- Remember meningitis in the young child (lumbar puncture rules above).

### 10.4 Risk factors
| Recurrence | Later epilepsy |
|---|---|
| Family history of febrile seizure | Pre-existing neurodevelopmental abnormality |
| First febrile seizure **before 18 months** | Complex febrile seizures |
| Low-grade fever | **Family history of epilepsy** (strongest factor) |
| Brief interval (**under 1 hour**) between onset of fever and seizure | Shorter duration of fever before the first seizure; lower temperature at the first seizure |

### 10.5 Treatment
- Usually brief and self-limited; **reassure and educate** parents.
- Antipyretics may reduce fever but **do not affect recurrence or duration** of seizures.
- **Febrile seizure is not an indication for AEDs**; for a seizure **longer than 5 minutes give rectal diazepam**.
- Intermittent diazepam (oral or rectal) for prolonged or repetitive seizures; note that sedation can mask signs of meningitis.
- Abortive rectal diazepam is the preferred modality for prolonged repetitive seizures.

## 11. Common epilepsies in childhood

| Syndrome | Age and features | EEG | Treatment and outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Infantile spasms (West)** | Under 1 year, peak **4-7 months**; flexor, extensor or mixed spasms in **clusters** followed by irritability; developmental arrest or regression. Cause found in 75% (**tuberous sclerosis** 20%; brain malformation and hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy commonest) | **Hypsarrhythmia** | **ACTH or prednisone** in idiopathic; **vigabatrin** in tuberous sclerosis; others: carbamazepine, valproate, pyridoxine. **Idiopathic has the best prognosis** |
| **Childhood absence epilepsy** | 5-10 years, girls more than boys; 30% develop generalised tonic-clonic seizures | **3 Hz generalised spike and wave** | First choice **ethosuximide** or valproate; second line lamotrigine, topiramate. Remission in **80%** after age 10 |
| **Benign childhood epilepsy with centrotemporal spikes (Rolandic)** | 5-9 years; mostly **nocturnal**, sensory-motor symptoms of face and oropharynx | Central and temporal spikes | Benign, remits by **16 years**; often no drug (seizures once or twice a year). Treat if it affects school performance, causes family anxiety, EEG is abnormal or frequency increases |

> **Check:** in infantile spasms, carbamazepine is generally avoided; first-line treatments are vigabatrin or hormonal therapy (ACTH, prednisolone).

## 12. Seizure mimics (paroxysmal non-epileptic events)

| With altered consciousness | Without altered consciousness |
|---|---|
| Breath-holding spells, syncope, hyperventilation | Stereotypy, gratification disorder |
| Sleep-related phenomena, apnoea | Movement disorders, GERD |
| Somatoform disorders, Munchausen syndrome by proxy | Complicated headaches, hyperekplexia |
| Episodic behavioural syndromes | Alternating hemiplegia of childhood, diaphragmatic flutter |
| | Paroxysmal extra-ocular gaze deviations, familial rectal pain syndrome |

### Key mimics
- **Jitteriness** (neonates): excessive response to stimulation (touch, noise); baby awake, **no autonomic change**; **stops when the limb is held or the stimulus removed**. Causes: mild (healthy newborns), drug withdrawal, hypocalcaemia, hypoglycaemia, hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy.
- **Apnoea**: pause in breathing over **15 seconds**. Non-epileptic apnoea is associated with **bradycardia** (no rise in heart rate, BP or temperature). Suspect epileptic apnoea if eyes open or close, eye deviation, mouth movements, high BP or tachycardia.
- **Benign neonatal sleep myoclonus**: repetitive bilateral symmetric jerks during **non-REM sleep** in the first weeks; stop on arousal; resolve by 2-3 months; development and examination normal.
- **Breath-holding spells** (6 months to 6 years; resolve spontaneously; iron deficiency is more common):

| Cyanotic | Pallid |
|---|---|
| Child angry or upset, brief crying, then breath-holding in expiration with apnoea and cyanosis, then limpness and loss of consciousness; a few have generalised motor seizures | Less common; after a **minor head or upper body trauma** the child loses consciousness, stops breathing, becomes pale, diaphoretic and limp |

- **Gratification disorder** (benign idiopathic infantile dyskinesia): 3 months to 3 years; pressure on the perineum with characteristic posturing of the legs, grunting, facial flushing and sweating; **stops with distraction**; associated with boredom, tension, excitement, genital infection.
- **Tics**: brief, sudden, rapid, intermittent movements or sounds, usually with an urge; may be repetitive.
- **Sandifer syndrome (GERD)**: intermittent opisthotonic posturing within about 30 minutes of feeds, a pain response to acid reflux.
- **Stereotypies**: patterned, repetitive, purposeless movements (head nodding, hand flapping, finger wiggling, grimacing) with excitement or boredom; common in autism but occur in normal children.
- **Psychogenic non-epileptic seizures**: dramatic events in a conscious child, prolonged, atypical generalised movements with prompt return of consciousness; eyes tightly closed and resist opening; comorbid epilepsy is common.

## Key points to revise

1. A seizure is a symptom; epilepsy needs at least two unprovoked seizures.
2. Seizures in 3-5% of children, febrile seizures 2-4%, epilepsy about 1%.
3. Focal seizures: consciousness preserved or impaired; generalised: loss of consciousness.
4. Epilepsy is a clinical diagnosis; EEG supports but does not prove or exclude it.
5. Generalised: valproate; focal: carbamazepine; carbamazepine and phenytoin can worsen absence and myoclonic seizures.
6. Simple febrile seizure: 5 months to 6 years, under 15 minutes, no AED; rectal diazepam for over 5 minutes.
7. Infantile spasms: hypsarrhythmia, treat with ACTH/prednisone or vigabatrin (tuberous sclerosis).
8. Absence: 3 Hz spike-wave, ethosuximide; Rolandic epilepsy remits by 16 years.
