## SECTION 1: BLOOD GROUPS AND COMPATIBILITY TESTING
### The ABO Blood Group System
The ABO system is the most important blood group system in transfusion medicine. ABO incompatibility causes the most severe and potentially fatal transfusion reactions. ABO antibodies are naturally occurring — they are present in the serum without prior exposure to foreign red cells.
| Blood Group | Antigens on RBC | Antibodies in Serum | Can Donate RBCs To | Can Receive RBCs From |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | A antigen | Anti-B | A, AB | A, O |
| B | B antigen | Anti-A | B, AB | B, O |
| AB | A and B | None | AB only | All groups — Universal Recipient |
| O | None | Anti-A and Anti-B | All groups — Universal Donor | O only |
- Universal RBC donor = O negative (no ABO antigens, no Rh D antigen)
- Universal plasma donor = AB positive (no anti-A or anti-B antibodies in plasma)
- Universal recipient = AB positive
### The Rh Blood Group System
- The Rh system is the second most important blood group system after ABO
- The most clinically significant antigen is the **D antigen**
- Rh positive = D antigen present on RBC surface
- Rh negative = D antigen absent from RBC surface
- Rh-negative individuals do NOT naturally carry anti-D antibodies — they develop them only after exposure to Rh-positive blood, a process called sensitisation
- Once sensitised, subsequent exposure to Rh-positive blood triggers a haemolytic reaction
**Haemolytic Disease of the Newborn (HDN):**
- An Rh-negative mother carries an Rh-positive fetus in the first pregnancy — usually uneventful
- At delivery (or any sensitising event), fetal RBCs enter the maternal circulation
- The mother's immune system recognises the D antigen as foreign and forms anti-D IgG antibodies — she is now sensitised
- In a subsequent Rh-positive pregnancy, maternal anti-D IgG crosses the placenta
- These antibodies attack fetal RBCs, causing haemolysis
- Clinical consequences range from mild jaundice to severe anaemia, hydrops fetalis, and intrauterine death
- **Prevention:** Anti-D immunoglobulin (Rhogam) is given to Rh-negative mothers within 72 hours of delivery, miscarriage, abortion, amniocentesis, or any other sensitising event, and also prophylactically at 28 weeks gestation. It neutralises any fetal RBCs before the mother can mount an immune response.
### Other Clinically Important Blood Group Systems
After ABO and Rh, the most clinically relevant systems in order of importance are:
| Rank | System | Clinical Significance |
|---|---|---|
| 3rd | Kell | Strong immunogen; causes HDN and haemolytic transfusion reactions |
| 4th | Duffy | Duffy-null individuals are resistant to Plasmodium vivax malaria |
| 5th | Kidd | Notorious for causing delayed haemolytic transfusion reactions |
| 6th | MNS | Variable clinical significance |

## SECTION 2: PRE-TRANSFUSION COMPATIBILITY TESTING
Before any non-emergency transfusion, compatibility testing is performed to reduce the risk of transfusion reactions. Testing proceeds in three steps:
- **Step 1 — ABO and Rh Typing:** The patient's blood group and Rh status are determined
- **Step 2 — Antibody Screen:** The patient's serum is tested against a panel of reagent RBCs to detect unexpected or irregular antibodies that could react against donor blood
- **Step 3 — Crossmatch:** The patient's serum is mixed directly with the chosen donor's RBCs. If no reaction occurs, the unit is compatible and safe to transfuse. If agglutination or haemolysis occurs, the unit is incompatible and must not be used.
The crossmatch primarily detects irregular antibodies in the patient's serum that react against specific donor RBC antigens.
### Direct and Indirect Antiglobulin Tests (Coombs Tests)
The antiglobulin test uses anti-human globulin (Coombs reagent) to detect antibodies either bound to RBCs in the body or free in the serum.
| Test | What It Detects | When Used |
|---|---|---|
| Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT) | Antibodies already bound to the patient's own RBCs in vivo | Haemolytic transfusion reactions; autoimmune haemolytic anaemia; HDN |
| Indirect Antiglobulin Test (IAT) | Free antibodies in the patient's serum in vitro | Pre-transfusion antibody screening; crossmatching |
- A positive DAT means antibodies are actively coating the patient's own RBCs inside the body
- The crossmatch is essentially a form of indirect antiglobulin testing
### Metabisulfite Test (Sickle Solubility Test)
- Sodium metabisulfite is added to the patient's blood, creating a deoxygenating environment
- Haemoglobin S becomes insoluble and causes RBCs to sickle
- A positive result confirms the presence of HbS — either sickle cell trait (HbAS) or sickle cell disease (HbSS)
- This test does NOT distinguish trait from disease — haemoglobin electrophoresis is needed for that

## SECTION 3: BLOOD PRODUCTS AND THEIR CLINICAL USE
Whole blood is separated by centrifugation into its components: packed red blood cells, platelet concentrate, and plasma. Plasma is further processed into fresh frozen plasma and cryoprecipitate.
### 1. Packed Red Blood Cells (pRBCs)
- Contains red cells with minimal residual plasma
- Storage: 1–6°C for 35–42 days
- Indications: symptomatic anaemia, acute haemorrhage, preoperative optimisation
- In an emergency with unknown blood type: give **O negative pRBCs**
### 2. Fresh Frozen Plasma (FFP)
- Contains all coagulation factors, fibrinogen, albumin, and immunoglobulins
- Does NOT contain platelets
- Storage: −18°C or below for up to 12 months
- Indications: coagulation factor deficiency when specific concentrates are unavailable, liver disease with coagulopathy, urgent warfarin reversal, DIC, massive transfusion protocol, therapeutic plasma exchange in TTP
- Universal plasma donor: **AB positive FFP**
### 3. Platelet Concentrate
- Contains platelets suspended in a small volume of plasma
- Storage: 20–24°C (room temperature) with continuous gentle agitation for **5 days**
- Has the shortest shelf life of all blood products
- Has the highest risk of bacterial contamination because it is stored at room temperature
- Indications: thrombocytopenia with active bleeding, prophylactic transfusion before procedures in severe thrombocytopenia
### 4. Cryoprecipitate
Cryoprecipitate is prepared by slowly thawing FFP at 1–6°C and collecting the cold-insoluble precipitate that forms.
**It contains high concentrations of:**
- Fibrinogen (Factor I) — the most abundant component
- Factor VIII
- Factor XIII
- Von Willebrand factor (vWF)
- Fibronectin
**It does NOT contain** significant amounts of Factor IX, Factor VII, or other coagulation factors.
Indications: Haemophilia A when Factor VIII concentrate is unavailable, Von Willebrand disease, hypofibrinogenaemia, DIC (to replace fibrinogen), uraemic bleeding.
### 5. Albumin
- Prepared from pooled plasma; does not transmit viruses (heat-treated)
- Indications: hypoalbuminaemia, liver cirrhosis, burns, nephrotic syndrome, spontaneous bacterial peritonitis
### Storage Summary
| Blood Product | Storage Temperature | Shelf Life |
|---|---|---|
| Packed RBCs | 1–6°C | 35–42 days |
| Fresh Frozen Plasma | −18°C or below | 12 months |
| Platelet Concentrate | 20–24°C with agitation | 5 days |
| Cryoprecipitate | −18°C or below | 12 months |
| Granulocytes | 20–24°C | 24 hours |

## SECTION 4: TRANSFUSION REACTIONS
Transfusion reactions are adverse responses to blood transfusion. They are classified by timing into immediate reactions (during or within 24 hours) and delayed reactions (days to weeks after transfusion).
### Immediate Transfusion Reactions
**1. Acute Haemolytic Transfusion Reaction (AHR)**
- The most serious immediate transfusion reaction
- Cause: ABO incompatibility — the most common underlying cause is a clerical error (wrong blood given to wrong patient)
- Mechanism: recipient's preformed IgM antibodies bind to donor ABO antigens on transfused RBCs, activating the complement system, causing rapid intravascular haemolysis
- Clinical features: fever, rigors, back and flank pain, haemoglobinuria (red or brown urine), haemoglobinaemia, hypotension, tachycardia, DIC, and acute renal failure
- Management: stop the transfusion immediately, maintain IV access with normal saline, check patient identity against blood product label, send blood and urine for repeat crossmatch and DAT, monitor urine output, treat DIC and renal failure as they develop, notify the blood bank
**2. Febrile Non-Haemolytic Transfusion Reaction (FNHTR)**
- The most common transfusion reaction overall
- Cause: recipient antibodies react against donor leukocyte HLA antigens, or cytokines accumulate in stored blood products
- Features: fever (temperature rise of 1°C or more), chills, malaise — no haemolysis
- Management: slow or stop the transfusion; administer paracetamol
- Prevention: **leukoreduction** — removal of white cells from the blood product by filtration before storage
**3. Allergic and Urticarial Reaction**
- Cause: recipient IgE antibodies react against donor plasma proteins
- Features: urticaria (hives), pruritus, flushing — mild; no fever, no haemolysis
- Management: slow the transfusion; give antihistamines (chlorpheniramine)
- If anaphylaxis develops: stop transfusion immediately; give adrenaline, corticosteroids, and IV fluids
**Anaphylactic Reaction:**
- Most commonly occurs in IgA-deficient patients who have formed anti-IgA antibodies
- Even trace amounts of IgA in donor plasma trigger severe anaphylaxis
- Management: stop transfusion immediately; adrenaline; use washed RBCs or IgA-deficient products for all future transfusions
**4. Transfusion-Associated Circulatory Overload (TACO)**
- Cause: volume overload from transfusion, especially in patients with cardiac or renal compromise, the elderly, or the very young
- Features: dyspnoea, hypertension, tachycardia, raised JVP, bilateral pulmonary oedema — cardiogenic in nature
- Management: slow or stop transfusion; sit patient upright; give oxygen; administer furosemide
- Prevention: transfuse slowly; give diuretics between units in at-risk patients
**5. Transfusion-Related Acute Lung Injury (TRALI)**
- The most serious pulmonary complication of transfusion
- Cause: donor antibodies (anti-HLA or anti-neutrophil antibodies) react against recipient leukocytes in the pulmonary capillaries, causing neutrophil activation and non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema
- Features: acute respiratory distress within 6 hours of transfusion, hypoxia, bilateral pulmonary infiltrates on chest X-ray, fever — blood pressure is normal or low (not elevated as in TACO)
- Management: supportive — high-flow oxygen, mechanical ventilation if needed. Does NOT respond to diuretics
- Prevention: use plasma from male donors or screened female donors to reduce the risk of HLA antibodies in donated blood
**6. Bacterial Contamination**
- Most commonly affects platelet concentrates because they are stored at room temperature, allowing bacterial growth
- Common organisms: Staphylococcus epidermidis, Staphylococcus aureus, gram-negative bacteria
- Features: high fever, rigors, severe hypotension, and septic shock — occurring during or immediately after transfusion
- Management: stop transfusion immediately; take blood cultures from patient and the blood bag; give broad-spectrum antibiotics; resuscitate
### TACO versus TRALI — Key Distinction
| Feature | TACO | TRALI |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | Volume overload | Donor antibodies against recipient leukocytes |
| Blood Pressure | Elevated | Normal or low |
| JVP | Raised | Normal |
| Response to Diuretics | Yes — improves | No — does not respond |
| Chest X-ray | Bilateral infiltrates with cardiomegaly | Bilateral infiltrates, normal heart size |
| Onset | During or just after transfusion | Within 6 hours |
### Delayed Transfusion Reactions
**1. Delayed Haemolytic Transfusion Reaction**
- Timing: 3–10 days after transfusion
- Cause: the patient has low levels of alloantibodies from a previous transfusion or pregnancy that were not detected on the pre-transfusion antibody screen. After transfusion, an anamnestic (memory) immune response occurs — antibody levels rise rapidly and attack the transfused RBCs
- Features: unexpected fall in haemoglobin, mild jaundice, and a positive DAT
- Kidd system antibodies are notorious for causing this reaction
- Management: usually mild and self-limiting; monitor renal function
**2. Post-Transfusion Purpura (PTP)**
- Timing: 5–10 days after transfusion
- Mechanism: the patient develops antibodies against a platelet-specific antigen (most commonly HPA-1a) present on donor platelets. These antibodies also cross-react with and destroy the patient's own platelets
- Features: sudden, severe thrombocytopenia leading to widespread purpura and bleeding
- Predominantly affects women sensitised through previous pregnancies
- Management: high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG)
**3. Transfusion-Associated Graft versus Host Disease (TA-GvHD)**
- Timing: 1–6 weeks after transfusion
- Mechanism: viable donor T-lymphocytes engraft in an immunocompromised host and mount an immune attack against host tissues — skin, liver, gastrointestinal tract, and bone marrow
- Features: rash, diarrhoea, liver dysfunction, and pancytopenia
- Mortality exceeds 90% — there is no effective treatment once established
- Prevention: **irradiation** of blood products with gamma radiation (25–50 Gy) inactivates donor T-lymphocytes before transfusion
**Who requires irradiated blood products:**
- Immunocompromised patients (congenital or acquired immunodeficiency)
- Recipients of intrauterine transfusions
- Patients receiving directed donations from blood relatives
- Haematopoietic stem cell transplant recipients
- Patients with Hodgkin lymphoma

## SECTION 5: SPECIAL BLOOD PRODUCTS AND MODIFICATIONS
### Leukoreduced Blood
- White cells are removed from the blood product by filtration before storage or at the bedside
- Prevents: febrile non-haemolytic transfusion reactions, CMV transmission, HLA alloimmunisation, and platelet refractoriness
- Standard practice for most transfusions in many countries
### Irradiated Blood
- Blood products are exposed to gamma radiation to inactivate donor T-lymphocytes
- Prevents TA-GvHD
- Required for all immunocompromised patients, intrauterine transfusions, directed donations from blood relatives, and haematopoietic stem cell transplant recipients
### Washed Red Blood Cells
- RBCs are washed with normal saline to remove all residual plasma proteins, IgA, and cytokines
- Used for IgA-deficient patients with anti-IgA antibodies to prevent anaphylaxis
- Also used for patients with severe or recurrent allergic transfusion reactions
### CMV-Negative Blood
- Blood from donors who test negative for CMV antibodies
- Used for CMV-seronegative immunocompromised patients, pregnant women, and premature neonates
- Leukoreduced blood is considered an acceptable alternative because CMV is carried inside white cells

## SECTION 6: MASSIVE TRANSFUSION
### Definition
Massive transfusion is defined as replacement of more than 100% of the patient's blood volume within 24 hours, or transfusion of more than 10 units of pRBCs within 24 hours, or transfusion of more than 4 units of pRBCs within 1 hour.
### Complications of Massive Transfusion
| Complication | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Hypothermia | Large volumes of cold stored blood lower core body temperature; worsens coagulopathy |
| Hypocalcaemia | Citrate anticoagulant in stored blood chelates (binds) circulating calcium |
| Hyperkalaemia | Potassium leaks from stored RBCs during prolonged storage |
| Dilutional Coagulopathy | Clotting factors and platelets are diluted by large volumes of pRBCs and crystalloids |
| Metabolic Acidosis | Stored blood has low pH; large volumes worsen acidosis |
### Massive Transfusion Protocol (MTP)
The standard approach uses a balanced 1:1:1 ratio of blood products to mimic whole blood and prevent dilutional coagulopathy:
- **FFP : Platelets : pRBCs = 1 : 1 : 1**
Additional management includes:
- Tranexamic acid given early — inhibits fibrinolysis and reduces blood loss in trauma
- Calcium supplementation — counteracts citrate chelation
- Warming of all blood products — prevents hypothermia
- Correction of acidosis

## SECTION 7: CLINICAL SCENARIOS FOR ESSAY QUESTIONS
### Scenario 1 — Unknown Blood Group in an Emergency
Give O negative packed RBCs immediately — O negative is the universal RBC donor with no ABO or Rh antigens. If plasma is also needed, give AB positive FFP — the universal plasma donor. Once the patient's blood group is identified, switch to type-specific blood as soon as possible to conserve O negative supplies.
### Scenario 2 — Suspected Acute Haemolytic Reaction Mid-Transfusion
- Stop the transfusion immediately — this is the single most important first step
- Keep the IV line open with normal saline
- Check patient identity against the blood product label — is this the right blood for the right patient?
- Send urgent samples: repeat crossmatch, DAT, full blood count, renal function, urine for haemoglobin
- Monitor urine output closely — acute renal failure is the major cause of death
- Give aggressive IV fluid resuscitation to maintain urine flow
- Monitor for and manage DIC
- Notify the blood bank and complete an incident report
### Scenario 3 — Distinguishing TACO from TRALI
Both present with respiratory distress during or shortly after transfusion. The key distinction is:
- TACO is caused by fluid overload → raised blood pressure, raised JVP, responds to diuretics, cardiogenic pulmonary oedema
- TRALI is immune-mediated lung injury → normal or low blood pressure, no fluid overload, bilateral infiltrates on CXR with normal heart size, does NOT respond to diuretics
### Scenario 4 — Rh-Negative Pregnant Woman
An Rh-negative woman pregnant with an Rh-positive fetus is at risk of sensitisation and HDN in future pregnancies. Give anti-D immunoglobulin (Rhogam) prophylactically at 28 weeks gestation and within 72 hours after delivery. Also give it after any sensitising event during pregnancy including miscarriage, ectopic pregnancy, amniocentesis, chorionic villus sampling, or antepartum haemorrhage. This prevents the formation of anti-D antibodies and protects all future pregnancies.
### Scenario 5 — Patient Requiring Repeated Transfusions (Sickle Cell Disease, Thalassaemia)
These patients face specific long-term complications:
- **Iron overload** — each unit of pRBCs delivers approximately 250 mg of iron; the body has no mechanism to excrete excess iron. Treatment: iron chelation therapy with desferrioxamine or deferasirox
- **Alloimmunisation** — repeated exposure to foreign RBC antigens leads to development of multiple alloantibodies, making future crossmatching increasingly difficult
- **Delayed haemolytic reactions** — from previously formed alloantibodies
- **CMV infection** — use leukoreduced or CMV-negative blood
- **Hypersplenism** — from chronic haemolysis and repeated transfusions
