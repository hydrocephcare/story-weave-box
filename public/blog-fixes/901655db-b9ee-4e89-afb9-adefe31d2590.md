## Summary
This comprehensive document covers the pharmacotherapeutic management of infectious diseases, spanning antibiotic principles, antimicrobial resistance, drug classes targeting bacterial cell walls and ribosomes, antimycobacterials, antifungals, antivirals, antiparasitic agents, HIV management, and the pharmacotherapy of acne.

## Key Points
* **Selective toxicity** is the foundational principle of chemotherapy — drugs must harm the pathogen while sparing the host, exploiting differences between microbial and human biochemistry.
* **Antimicrobial resistance (AMR)** is driven by enzymatic inactivation, target site mutation, efflux pumps, and reduced permeability, and spreads most importantly via **conjugation** (plasmid transfer).
* **Beta-lactams** inhibit penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs) to block peptidoglycan cross-linking; resistance via beta-lactamases is countered by beta-lactamase inhibitor combinations.
* **Vancomycin** is the drug of choice for MRSA but carries risks of red man syndrome, nephrotoxicity, and ototoxicity; therapeutic drug monitoring is essential.
* **Aminoglycosides** are concentration-dependent bactericidal agents with irreversible ototoxicity and nephrotoxicity as dose-limiting toxicities.
* **Fluoroquinolones** inhibit DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV and are contraindicated in children, pregnant women, and patients with myasthenia gravis due to tendinopathy and cartilage toxicity.
* **First-line TB therapy (RIPE)** targets multiple stages of mycobacterial metabolism; rifampicin is a potent CYP450 inducer with clinically critical drug interactions.
* **HIV management** relies on combination ART targeting multiple steps of the viral replication cycle; preferred first-line regimen is TDF + 3TC + DTG with a treatment goal of viral load <50 copies/mL.
* **Isotretinoin** is the most effective acne treatment but is highly teratogenic (Category X), mandating a strict pregnancy prevention programme.

## Detailed Notes

### SECTION 1 — INTRODUCTION TO CHEMOTHERAPY & THE IMMUNE SYSTEM

### Principles of Chemotherapy

* **Chemotherapy** = use of chemical agents to treat or control disease — includes all antimicrobials, not just oncology agents
* **Paul Ehrlich** — father of chemotherapy; coined the concept of the **"magic bullet"** — a drug that selectively destroys a pathogen without harming the host
* **Selective toxicity** = the ability of a drug to harm a microorganism while sparing host cells; this is possible because microorganisms possess unique biochemical targets absent in human cells (e.g. peptidoglycan cell walls, 70S ribosomes, ergosterol membranes)

**Key terminology:**

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Bactericidal** | Kills bacteria directly |
| **Bacteriostatic** | Inhibits bacterial growth; relies on host immunity to clear infection |
| **MIC** | Minimum Inhibitory Concentration — lowest drug concentration that inhibits visible growth |
| **MBC** | Minimum Bactericidal Concentration — lowest concentration killing 99.9% of bacteria |
| **Broad spectrum** | Active against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative organisms |
| **Narrow spectrum** | Active against a limited range of organisms |
| **Post-antibiotic effect** | Continued bacterial suppression after drug concentration falls below MIC |

### The Immune System

**Innate immunity (non-specific, first line):**
* **Physical barriers** — skin, mucous membranes, cilia, gastric acid, normal flora
* **Cellular components** — neutrophils (first responders), macrophages, NK cells, dendritic cells
* **Soluble factors** — complement system, interferons, acute phase proteins (CRP, fibrinogen, ferritin)
* **Pattern recognition** — Toll-like receptors (TLRs) on innate cells recognise **PAMPs** (pathogen-associated molecular patterns — LPS, flagellin, peptidoglycan)
* Response is **rapid (minutes to hours)** with NO immunological memory

**Adaptive immunity (specific, second line):**
* **Humoral immunity** — B cells → plasma cells → **antibodies** (IgG, IgM, IgA, IgE, IgD); IgM = first responder; IgG = most abundant; IgA = mucosal secretions
* **Cell-mediated immunity** — T lymphocytes:
  * **CD4+ T helper cells** — coordinate immune response; activate B cells and macrophages; subtypes: Th1, Th2, Th17, T-reg
  * **CD8+ cytotoxic T cells** — directly kill virus-infected cells and tumour cells via perforin/granzyme
* Response is **slower (days to weeks)** but generates **immunological memory** (basis of vaccination)

**Key immunological concepts:**

| Concept | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Antigen** | Foreign molecule triggering an immune response |
| **Opsonisation** | Antibody/complement coating of pathogens → enhanced phagocytosis |
| **MHC class I** | Presents endogenous antigens to CD8+ T cells; expressed on all nucleated cells |
| **MHC class II** | Presents exogenous antigens to CD4+ T cells; expressed only on APCs |
| **Complement MAC** | Membrane attack complex — lysis of Gram-negative bacteria |

### SECTION 2 — PRINCIPLES OF ANTIBIOTIC THERAPY

### Targets of Antimicrobial Action

| Target | Drug Classes |
|---|---|
| **Cell wall synthesis** | Beta-lactams, glycopeptides, fosfomycin |
| **Cell membrane** | Polymyxins, daptomycin |
| **30S ribosome** | Aminoglycosides, tetracyclines |
| **50S ribosome** | Macrolides, chloramphenicol, clindamycin, linezolid |
| **DNA gyrase/topoisomerase** | Fluoroquinolones |
| **RNA polymerase** | Rifampicin |
| **Folic acid synthesis** | Sulfonamides, trimethoprim |
| **Mycolic acid synthesis** | Isoniazid, ethambutol |

### Pharmacokinetic/Pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) Principles

* **Absorption** — oral bioavailability varies greatly; fluoroquinolones and linezolid have near-complete oral absorption
* **Distribution** — volume of distribution (Vd) determines tissue penetration; CNS, bone, and abscess penetration vary critically between agents
* **Metabolism** — hepatic CYP450 system; rifampicin = potent inducer; azoles/macrolides = inhibitors
* **Excretion** — renal excretion requires dose adjustment in renal failure; doxycycline and moxifloxacin are safe in renal failure

**PK/PD killing patterns:**

| Pattern | Drugs | Clinical implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Time-dependent killing** | Beta-lactams, vancomycin | Keep drug concentration above MIC for as long as possible — continuous infusion or frequent dosing |
| **Concentration-dependent killing** | Aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones | Maximise peak concentration (Cmax) — once-daily dosing preferred |
| **AUC-dependent** | Vancomycin (AUC/MIC), fluoroquinolones | Total drug exposure drives efficacy |

### Principles of Appropriate Antibiotic Use

* Confirm infection before prescribing; avoid empirical use for viral illness
* Obtain **cultures before starting antibiotics** whenever possible
* **Empirical therapy** = start broad based on clinical syndrome; **de-escalate** when culture results available
* Choose drug based on likely organism, site of infection, local resistance patterns, and patient factors (allergy, renal/hepatic function, pregnancy)
* Use the **shortest effective course** — reduces resistance and adverse effects
* **IV-to-oral switch** — transition to oral agents as soon as patient is clinically stable
* **Combination therapy** indications: polymicrobial infections, prevent resistance emergence (TB), synergy required (enterococcal endocarditis), empirical severe sepsis

**Drug interaction types:**

| Type | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Synergy** | Combined effect > sum of individual effects | Beta-lactam + aminoglycoside |
| **Additive** | Combined effect = sum of parts | — |
| **Antagonism** | Combined effect < individual effects | Bacteriostatic + bactericidal (tetracycline + penicillin) |

### SECTION 3 — ANTIMICROBIAL RESISTANCE (AMR)

### Definition and Global Significance

* **AMR** = the ability of a microorganism to withstand effects of an antimicrobial to which it was previously susceptible
* **WHO priority global health threat** — drives increased morbidity, mortality, healthcare costs, and treatment failure
* Driven by: antibiotic overuse/misuse, incomplete courses, agricultural use, poor infection prevention and control

### Mechanisms of Resistance

| Mechanism | How it works | Key examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Enzymatic inactivation** | Enzymes produced by bacteria destroy the drug | Beta-lactamases, ESBLs, carbapenemases (KPC, NDM) |
| **Altered target site** | Mutation changes drug-binding site | MRSA (altered PBP2a — mecA gene); fluoroquinolone resistance (mutated DNA gyrase) |
| **Reduced permeability** | Loss of porin channels reduces drug entry | Gram-negative resistance to beta-lactams |
| **Efflux pumps** | Active drug expulsion from the cell | Tetracycline, fluoroquinolone, macrolide resistance |
| **Bypass pathway** | Acquire alternative metabolic route | Sulfonamide resistance — acquire exogenous folate |

### Spread of Resistance — Horizontal Gene Transfer

| Mechanism | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Transformation** | Uptake of naked DNA fragments from the environment |
| **Transduction** | Transfer of resistance genes via bacteriophage |
| **Conjugation** | **Most important mechanism** — direct cell-to-cell transfer of plasmids carrying resistance genes via sex pili |
| **Transposons** | Mobile genetic elements ("jumping genes") transferring resistance between chromosomes and plasmids |

### Clinically Critical Resistant Organisms

| Organism | Resistance | Treatment of choice |
|---|---|---|
| **MRSA** | mecA gene → altered PBP2a; methicillin/all beta-lactam resistant | **Vancomycin**; linezolid; daptomycin |
| **VRE** | vanA/vanB genes → altered D-Ala-D-Lac; vancomycin resistant | Linezolid; daptomycin |
| **ESBL producers** | Extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (E. coli, Klebsiella) | **Carbapenems** |
| **CRE** | Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae; KPC, NDM enzymes | Colistin; tigecycline; ceftazidime-avibactam |
| **MDR-TB** | Resistant to isoniazid + rifampicin | Second-line drugs ≥18–24 months |

### Antimicrobial Stewardship

* **Antimicrobial Stewardship Programs (ASP)** — promote rational antibiotic prescribing
* De-escalation once culture and sensitivity results available
* Infection prevention and control (IPC) — hand hygiene, PPE, isolation of resistant organisms
* Avoid unnecessary prophylaxis; educate patients against self-medication
* New drug development and vaccine programmes

### SECTION 5 — CELL WALL INHIBITORS

### Beta-Lactams — Shared Mechanism

* All share a **beta-lactam ring** — essential for antimicrobial activity; its integrity is required for drug function
* **Mechanism:** inhibit **Penicillin Binding Proteins (PBPs)** → block **transpeptidation** (cross-linking of peptidoglycan strands) → cell wall weakens → osmotic lysis → **bactericidal**
* Gram-negative bacteria possess an **outer membrane** — drug must penetrate via **porin channels**
* **Beta-lactamases** produced by resistant bacteria cleave the beta-lactam ring → inactivation

### Penicillins

| Group | Examples | Spectrum | Key Clinical Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Natural penicillins** | Benzylpenicillin (G) IV; Phenoxymethylpenicillin (V) oral | Narrow — Gram +ve cocci, streptococci, spirochaetes | Strep throat, syphilis, meningococcal meningitis |
| **Antistaphylococcal** | Flucloxacillin, cloxacillin | Staph aureus — beta-lactamase stable | MSSA skin/soft tissue infections, osteomyelitis |
| **Aminopenicillins** | Amoxicillin, ampicillin | Broader — + E. coli, H. influenzae, Listeria | UTI, otitis media, community pneumonia, Listeria |
| **Extended spectrum** | Piperacillin, ticarcillin | + Pseudomonas aeruginosa | Severe hospital-acquired infections |
| **+ Beta-lactamase inhibitors** | Co-amoxiclav, piperacillin-tazobactam | Broad; overcomes many beta-lactamase-producing strains | CAP, intra-abdominal infections, healthcare-associated infections |

**Side effects of penicillins:**
* **Hypersensitivity reactions** — most important; range from maculopapular rash → urticaria → anaphylaxis; cross-reactivity with cephalosporins is low (~1–2%) but must be considered
* **Ampicillin rash** — non-allergic maculopapular rash occurring in patients with EBV infectious mononucleosis; NOT a true penicillin allergy
* **Diarrhoea** — especially amoxicillin; risk of *C. difficile* colitis
* **Neurotoxicity** — seizures at very high IV doses (especially in renal failure)

### Cephalosporins

* Share beta-lactam ring with penicillins; increasing generation = increasing Gram-negative coverage + beta-lactamase stability

| Generation | Examples | Key Features |
|---|---|---|
| **1st generation** | Cephalexin, cefazolin | Gram +ve; MSSA; surgical prophylaxis |
| **2nd generation** | Cefuroxime, cefaclor | + H. influenzae; respiratory infections |
| **3rd generation** | Ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, ceftazidime | Broad; excellent CNS penetration; meningitis; ceftazidime covers Pseudomonas |
| **4th generation** | Cefepime | Broad; Pseudomonas; febrile neutropenia |
| **5th generation** | Ceftaroline | **Active against MRSA** |

* **Side effects:** Hypersensitivity · diarrhoea · *C. difficile* · disulfiram-like reaction with alcohol (some cephalosporins)

### Carbapenems

* **Broadest spectrum beta-lactams**; stable to most beta-lactamases except carbapenemases
* **Imipenem** requires co-administration with **cilastatin** (inhibits renal dehydropeptidase that would otherwise degrade imipenem)
* **Ertapenem** does NOT cover Pseudomonas aeruginosa or Acinetobacter — important clinical distinction

| Drug | Key Feature |
|---|---|
| Meropenem | Broad; CNS penetration; Pseudomonas |
| Imipenem-cilastatin | Broad; highest seizure risk |
| Ertapenem | Once daily; no Pseudomonas/Acinetobacter cover |
| Doripenem | Broad; used in HAP/VAP |

* **Side effects:** Seizures (especially imipenem) · nausea/vomiting · hypersensitivity · *C. difficile*

### Monobactams

* **Aztreonam** — sole monobactam; active only against **aerobic Gram-negative bacteria** including Pseudomonas
* **Safe in penicillin allergy** (no cross-reactivity except with ceftazidime)

### Glycopeptides

* **Mechanism:** bind the **D-Ala-D-Ala** terminus of peptidoglycan precursor → block both transglycosylation and transpeptidation → **bactericidal**
* **Only active against Gram-positive organisms** — unable to penetrate the Gram-negative outer membrane
* **Vancomycin** — drug of choice for MRSA; also oral vancomycin for *C. difficile* (stays in gut)
* **Vancomycin monitoring:** target AUC/MIC ratio; trough levels 15–20 mg/L for serious infections

**Vancomycin side effects:**

| Side Effect | Details |
|---|---|
| **Red man syndrome** | Flushing, pruritus, rash, hypotension from rapid infusion → histamine release; **NOT a true allergic reaction**; prevented by slowing infusion rate |
| **Nephrotoxicity** | Especially when combined with aminoglycosides; monitor renal function |
| **Ototoxicity** | Tinnitus, sensorineural hearing loss; especially with high levels |
| **Thrombophlebitis** | At IV infusion site |

### Polymyxins

* **Polymyxin B and Colistin (Polymyxin E)**
* **Mechanism:** bind **lipopolysaccharide (LPS)** in the Gram-negative outer membrane → disrupt membrane integrity → cell death → **bactericidal**
* **Gram-negative only** — last-resort agents for MDR/XDR Gram-negative infections (CRE, MDR Pseudomonas, Acinetobacter)
* **Side effects:** **Nephrotoxicity** (dose-limiting) · neurotoxicity (paraesthesia, dizziness) · should only be used when no safer alternatives exist

### SECTION 6 — PROTEIN SYNTHESIS INHIBITORS

### 30S Inhibitors — Aminoglycosides

* **Mechanism:** bind **30S ribosomal subunit** → cause misreading of mRNA → insertion of wrong amino acids → non-functional proteins + membrane disruption → **bactericidal**
* **Concentration-dependent killing** — once-daily dosing maximises peak concentration (Cmax/MIC) and exploits the post-antibiotic effect
* Active against aerobic **Gram-negative bacteria**; require oxygen for drug uptake → ineffective against anaerobes
* **Synergistic** with beta-lactams for Gram-positive organisms (enterococci, streptococci)

| Drug | Key Clinical Use |
|---|---|
| **Gentamicin** | Gram-negative sepsis; endocarditis (synergistic dosing) |
| **Amikacin** | Gentamicin-resistant Gram-negatives; TB (second-line) |
| **Streptomycin** | TB (first injectable second-line); brucellosis; plague |
| **Neomycin** | Topical only — too nephrotoxic/ototoxic for systemic use; bowel prep |
| **Tobramycin** | Pseudomonas; inhaled formulation for CF patients |

**Side effects:**

| Toxicity | Details |
|---|---|
| **Nephrotoxicity** | Proximal tubular damage; usually reversible; monitor creatinine + drug levels |
| **Ototoxicity** | Cochlear (irreversible sensorineural hearing loss) + vestibular (balance disturbance); **irreversible** — most feared |
| **Neuromuscular blockade** | Blocks ACh release at NMJ; **avoid in myasthenia gravis** |

* **Contraindicated in pregnancy** — cochlear damage to fetus
* Monitor **trough levels** (nephrotoxicity) and **peak levels** (efficacy)

### 30S Inhibitors — Tetracyclines

* **Mechanism:** bind **30S ribosomal subunit** → block binding of aminoacyl-tRNA to ribosome A site → **bacteriostatic**
* Broad spectrum: Gram +ve, Gram -ve, **atypicals** (Chlamydia, Mycoplasma, Rickettsia, Coxiella), spirochaetes, protozoa

| Drug | Key Clinical Use |
|---|---|
| **Doxycycline** | Chlamydia, Mycoplasma pneumonia, Lyme disease, Brucella, Rickettsia, malaria prophylaxis, acne, PID |
| **Minocycline** | Acne; some MRSA activity; leprosy (alternative) |
| **Tetracycline** | H. pylori eradication; acne; avoid in renal failure |
| **Tigecycline** | MDR organisms; MRSA; ESBL producers; intra-abdominal + skin/soft tissue infections |

**Side effects:**

| Side Effect | Details |
|---|---|
| **GI upset** | Nausea, vomiting — most common; take with food |
| **Photosensitivity** | Avoid prolonged sun exposure; use sunscreen |
| **Chelation** | Binds Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Fe²⁺, Al³⁺ → reduced absorption; avoid dairy, antacids, iron supplements |
| **Tooth discolouration + bone growth inhibition** | Deposits in developing teeth and bones → **contraindicated in children <8 years and pregnancy** |
| **Oesophageal ulceration** | Take with large amount of water in upright position |
| **Anti-anabolic effect** | Tetracycline (not doxycycline) → avoid in renal failure; **doxycycline is safe in renal failure** |

### 50S Inhibitors — Macrolides

* **Mechanism:** bind **50S ribosomal subunit** (23S rRNA) → block **translocation** → **bacteriostatic** (bactericidal at high concentrations)
* Excellent tissue penetration; high intracellular concentrations — ideal for atypical intracellular pathogens

| Drug | Key Clinical Use |
|---|---|
| **Azithromycin** | CAP; STIs (chlamydia, gonorrhoea); MAC prophylaxis in HIV; pertussis; traveller's diarrhoea |
| **Clarithromycin** | H. pylori eradication (triple therapy); MAC treatment; CAP |
| **Erythromycin** | Penicillin allergy alternative; gastroparesis (prokinetic via motilin receptor); whooping cough |

**Side effects:**

| Side Effect | Details |
|---|---|
| **GI upset** | Most common — especially erythromycin (prokinetic effect on motilin receptors → cramping, diarrhoea) |
| **QT prolongation** | Risk of torsades de pointes; avoid combination with other QT-prolonging drugs |
| **Hepatotoxicity** | Cholestatic jaundice — especially erythromycin estolate |
| **Drug interactions** | Inhibit **CYP3A4** → ↑ levels of statins, warfarin, cyclosporin, tacrolimus (clarithromycin >> erythromycin >> azithromycin) |
| **Hearing loss** | At high doses |

### 50S Inhibitors — Chloramphenicol

* **Mechanism:** bind **50S ribosomal subunit** → inhibit **peptidyl transferase** → block peptide bond formation → **bacteriostatic**
* Broad spectrum; excellent CNS and intracellular penetration
* **Rarely used systemically** due to serious toxicity

**Side effects:**

| Side Effect | Details |
|---|---|
| **Grey baby syndrome** | Neonates lack UDP-glucuronyl transferase → drug accumulates → cardiovascular collapse, abdominal distension, grey skin discolouration, death |
| **Aplastic anaemia** | Idiosyncratic, dose-independent, **irreversible** — rare but potentially fatal; limits systemic use |
| **Reversible BM suppression** | Dose-dependent; anaemia, leucopenia, thrombocytopenia |

* Current uses: **topical eye drops** (conjunctivitis); meningitis/typhoid in resource-limited settings

### 50S Inhibitors — Clindamycin

* **Mechanism:** bind **50S ribosomal subunit** → block peptide bond formation → **bacteriostatic**
* Active against: **Gram-positive cocci** (MSSA, streptococci) + **anaerobes** (especially below diaphragm — Bacteroides fragilis)
* Excellent bone and joint penetration

| Key Use | Details |
|---|---|
| Skin/soft tissue infections | MSSA, streptococcal |
| Dental infections | Penicillin-allergy alternative |
| Osteomyelitis | Excellent bone penetration |
| Toxin-mediated infections | Inhibits toxin production in TSS (Toxic Shock Syndrome) and necrotising fasciitis |
| Anaerobic infections | Intra-abdominal, pelvic |
| Malaria | In combination (clindamycin + quinine) |

* **Side effects:** **C. difficile colitis** — highest risk of all antibiotics; GI upset; diarrhoea; rash

### 50S Inhibitors — Oxazolidinones (Linezolid)

* **Mechanism:** bind **50S ribosomal subunit** at a **unique site** (23S rRNA) → prevent formation of the **70S initiation complex** → block the very start of translation → **bacteriostatic**
* Active against **MRSA, VRE, drug-resistant streptococci**; used in XDR-TB

**Side effects:**

| Side Effect | Details |
|---|---|
| **Myelosuppression** | Thrombocytopenia most common; also anaemia; monitor FBC weekly during treatment |
| **Serotonin syndrome** | Linezolid is a weak MAO inhibitor → avoid SSRIs, SNRIs, TCAs, pethidine |
| **Peripheral neuropathy** | Prolonged use (>4 weeks) |
| **Optic neuropathy** | Prolonged use → visual disturbance; monitor vision |

* **Advantage:** no dose adjustment required in renal or hepatic failure

### SECTION 7 — QUINOLONES & FOLIC ACID ANTAGONISTS

### Fluoroquinolones

* **Mechanism:** inhibit **DNA gyrase (topoisomerase II)** and **topoisomerase IV** → prevent DNA supercoiling/relaxation → DNA strand breaks → inability to replicate/repair DNA → **bactericidal**
* **Concentration-dependent killing** — maximise Cmax; once or twice daily dosing
* Excellent oral bioavailability (near-complete absorption — oral = IV for most indications)

| Drug | Spectrum | Key Clinical Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Ciprofloxacin** | Best Gram-negative (including Pseudomonas); limited Gram +ve | UTI, GI infections (Salmonella, Shigella, Campylobacter), anthrax, Pseudomonas infections |
| **Levofloxacin** | Broad; good Gram +ve + atypicals | CAP, UTI, TB (second-line), healthcare-associated infections |
| **Moxifloxacin** | Broadest; excellent Gram +ve, atypicals + anaerobes; NO Pseudomonas | CAP, TB (second-line), intra-abdominal infections |
| **Ofloxacin** | Broad | UTI, STIs, TB (second-line) |
| **Nalidixic acid** | Gram-negative only | Uncomplicated UTI (largely obsolete now) |

**Side effects:**

| Side Effect | Details |
|---|---|
| **Tendinopathy/tendon rupture** | Especially Achilles tendon; risk ↑ with corticosteroids + elderly + renal failure |
| **QT prolongation** | Risk of torsades de pointes; avoid with other QT-prolonging agents |
| **CNS effects** | Headache, dizziness, confusion, hallucinations, seizures; ↑ risk if combined with NSAIDs |
| **Phototoxicity** | Avoid prolonged sun exposure |
| **Cartilage damage** | Growing joint cartilage — **contraindicated in children and pregnancy** |
| **C. difficile** | Significant risk with broad-spectrum fluoroquinolones |
| **Drug interactions** | Inhibit CYP1A2 → ↑ theophylline levels; chelation with antacids/iron/calcium reduces absorption |

* **Contraindicated in:** children · pregnancy · myasthenia gravis (worsen neuromuscular blockade)

### Folic Acid Antagonists — Sulfonamides

* **Mechanism:** structural analogue of **para-aminobenzoic acid (PABA)** → competitive inhibition of **dihydropteroate synthase** → ↓ dihydrofolic acid synthesis → ↓ purines and pyrimidines → **bacteriostatic**
* Humans obtain folate exogenously (diet) — bacteria must synthesise it; this is the basis of selective toxicity

| Drug | Key Use |
|---|---|
| **Sulfamethoxazole** (+ trimethoprim = co-trimoxazole) | UTI, PCP prophylaxis/treatment, Toxoplasma, Nocardia |
| **Sulfadiazine** | Toxoplasmosis (with pyrimethamine) |
| **Silver sulfadiazine** | **Topical** — burns wound management |
| **Sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine** | Malaria (IPTp in pregnancy) |

**Side effects:**

| Side Effect | Details |
|---|---|
| **Hypersensitivity** | Rash, Stevens-Johnson syndrome, toxic epidermal necrolysis |
| **Crystalluria** | Maintain adequate fluid intake to prevent renal stone formation |
| **Haemolytic anaemia** | In **G6PD deficiency** — check before prescribing |
| **Kernicterus** | Sulfonamides displace bilirubin from albumin in neonates → **avoid in pregnancy (3rd trimester) and neonates** |
| **Bone marrow suppression** | Megaloblastic anaemia, pancytopenia |

### Folic Acid Antagonists — Trimethoprim

* **Mechanism:** inhibit **dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR)** → ↓ tetrahydrofolate → ↓ purines and DNA synthesis → **bacteriostatic**
* **Co-trimoxazole (TMP-SMX)** = trimethoprim + sulfamethoxazole — **sequential blockade** of the folate pathway at two steps → synergistic; often **bactericidal** in combination

**Key uses of co-trimoxazole:**

| Indication | Details |
|---|---|
| **Uncomplicated UTI** | First-line in many settings |
| **PCP treatment + prophylaxis** | **Pneumocystis jirovecii** pneumonia — high-dose IV treatment; prophylaxis when CD4 <200 in HIV |
| **Toxoplasmosis prophylaxis** | When CD4 <100 in HIV |
| **Nocardia** | Drug of choice |
| **Stenotrophomonas** | One of few active agents |
| **Listeria** | Alternative to ampicillin |

**Additional side effects of TMP component:**
* **↑ Serum creatinine** — trimethoprim competes with creatinine for tubular secretion → apparent rise in creatinine is NOT true renal failure
* **Hyperkalaemia** — trimethoprim blocks ENaC-mediated potassium secretion in collecting duct (similar mechanism to amiloride)
* Avoid in **folate deficiency** and **first trimester of pregnancy** (teratogenic)
* **Leucovorin (folinic acid)** can rescue human bone marrow toxicity without rescuing bacteria (bacteria cannot use preformed folate)

### SECTION 8 — ANTIMYCOBACTERIALS

### Tuberculosis — Overview

* Caused by **Mycobacterium tuberculosis** (MTB) — slow-growing obligate aerobe
* **Acid-fast bacilli (AFB)** — visualised by **Ziehl-Neelsen (ZN) stain** (resist decolourisation by acid-alcohol due to mycolic acid-rich waxy cell wall)
* Slow growth rate necessitates **long treatment duration** to kill both actively replicating and dormant bacilli
* Multiple drugs required to: prevent resistance emergence, target bacteria in different metabolic states and anatomical compartments

### First-Line TB Drugs — RIPE Mnemonic

| Drug | Mechanism | Key Side Effects |
|---|---|---|
| **R — Rifampicin** | Inhibits **bacterial RNA polymerase** (β subunit) → blocks mRNA synthesis → bactericidal against all populations | **Red/orange discolouration** of urine, tears, saliva, sweat (harmless — warn patients) · **hepatotoxicity** · **potent CYP450 inducer** (reduces levels of OCPs, warfarin, ARVs, methadone, ciclosporin) · flu-like syndrome · thrombocytopenia |
| **I — Isoniazid (INH)** | Inhibits **InhA (enoyl-ACP reductase)** → blocks **mycolic acid synthesis** → bactericidal (only actively replicating MTB) | **Peripheral neuropathy** (pyridoxine/B6 depletion) — **give B6 prophylactically** · hepatotoxicity · lupus-like syndrome · CNS effects (seizures, psychosis) · haemolytic anaemia in G6PD deficiency |
| **P — Pyrazinamide (PZA)** | Converted to pyrazinoic acid → disrupts membrane potential + inhibits fatty acid synthase → bactericidal at **acidic pH** (kills intracellular/dormant MTB in macrophage lysosomes) | **Hepatotoxicity** (most hepatotoxic first-line drug) · **hyperuricaemia/gout** (inhibits uric acid excretion) · arthralgia · rash · photosensitivity |
| **E — Ethambutol** | Inhibits **arabinosyl transferase** → blocks **arabinogalactan** synthesis (cell wall component) → bacteriostatic | **Optic neuritis** — reduced visual acuity, impaired red-green colour discrimination; **dose-dependent and reversible if stopped early** → baseline visual acuity + colour vision testing before and during treatment |

### Standard TB Treatment Regimen

* **Intensive phase (2 months):** RIPE — Rifampicin + Isoniazid + Pyrazinamide + Ethambutol
* **Continuation phase (4 months):** RI — Rifampicin + Isoniazid
* **Total duration: 6 months**
* **TB meningitis:** 9–12 months total; add **dexamethasone** (reduces mortality)
* **Latent TB infection (LTBI):** Isoniazid alone × 6 months (or 3 months RIPE in some guidelines)

### Drug-Resistant TB

| Classification | Definition | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **MDR-TB** | Resistant to **isoniazid + rifampicin** | Second-line drugs ≥18–24 months |
| **Pre-XDR-TB** | MDR + resistant to any fluoroquinolone | — |
| **XDR-TB** | MDR + fluoroquinolone resistant + resistant to at least one injectable | Bedaquiline, linezolid, delamanid |

* **Second-line drugs:** Levofloxacin, moxifloxacin · Injectable aminoglycosides (amikacin, kanamycin) · Ethionamide · Cycloserine · Para-aminosalicylic acid (PAS) · **Bedaquiline** (inhibits mycobacterial ATP synthase) · **Linezolid** · **Delamanid** (inhibits mycolic acid synthesis)

### Other Mycobacterial Infections

| Organism | Disease | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **M. leprae** | Leprosy (Hansen's disease) — paucibacillary | **Rifampicin + Dapsone** × 6 months |
| **M. leprae** | Leprosy (Hansen's disease) — multibacillary | **Rifampicin + Dapsone + Clofazimine** × 12 months |
| **MAC (M. avium complex)** | Disseminated MAC in AIDS (CD4 <50) | **Clarithromycin + Ethambutol ± Rifabutin** |
| **M. bovis** | Bovine TB | Standard regimen — **NOT pyrazinamide** (intrinsically resistant) |

* **Dapsone side effects:** Haemolytic anaemia (especially G6PD deficiency) · methaemoglobinaemia · agranulocytosis · peripheral neuropathy · hepatotoxicity
* **Clofazimine side effects:** Skin discolouration (red-brown) · GI effects

### SECTION 9 — ANTIFUNGALS

### Fungal Drug Targets

| Target | Drug Class |
|---|---|
| **Ergosterol** (cell membrane — direct binding) | Polyenes |
| **Ergosterol synthesis** (lanosterol 14α-demethylase inhibition) | Azoles |
| **Glucan synthesis** (cell wall) | Echinocandins |
| **DNA/RNA synthesis** | Flucytosine |
| **Microtubule polymerisation** | Griseofulvin |
| **Squalene epoxidase** | Terbinafine |

### Polyenes

* **Amphotericin B and Nystatin**
* **Mechanism:** bind **ergosterol** in fungal cell membrane → form transmembrane pores → cell contents leak → **fungicidal**

**Amphotericin B:**
* Broadest spectrum antifungal — active against Candida, Cryptococcus, Aspergillus, Mucor, Histoplasma, Blastomyces
* **Formulations:**
  * Conventional (deoxycholate) — cheaper; more nephrotoxic
  * **Liposomal amphotericin B (L-AmB)** — preferred; targets fungal-infected tissue; significantly less nephrotoxic

**Amphotericin B side effects:**

| Side Effect | Details |
|---|---|
| **Nephrotoxicity** | Most important dose-limiting toxicity; tubular damage → hypokalaemia + hypomagnesaemia (wasting); monitor renal function + electrolytes daily |
| **Infusion reactions** | Fever, rigors, chills, hypotension during infusion; premedicate with paracetamol + antihistamine ± hydrocortisone; slow infusion rate |
| **Anaemia** | ↓ erythropoietin (EPO) production by damaged renal tubular cells |
| **Phlebitis** | At IV site; use central line where possible |

**Nystatin:**
* Too toxic for systemic use → **topical/oral formulations only**
* Uses: oral candidiasis ("swish and swallow"), vaginal candidiasis, skin candidiasis (nappy rash)

### Azoles

* **Mechanism:** inhibit fungal cytochrome P450 enzyme **lanosterol 14α-demethylase** → ↓ ergosterol synthesis → accumulation of toxic methylated sterol intermediates → **fungistatic** (fungicidal at high concentrations for some agents)
* Also inhibit **human CYP450** to varying degrees → clinically significant drug interactions

| Drug | Spectrum | Key Clinical Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Fluconazole** | Candida species (NOT C. krusei or C. glabrata); Cryptococcus | Oral/oesophageal/vaginal/systemic candidiasis; Cryptococcal meningitis consolidation + maintenance; prophylaxis in immunocompromised |
| **Itraconazole** | Broad + dermatophytes + Aspergillus | Onychomycosis; aspergillosis; endemic mycoses (histoplasmosis, blastomycosis, sporotrichosis) |
| **Voriconazole** | Broadest systemic azole; Aspergillus + Candida + Fusarium + Scedosporium | **Invasive aspergillosis — first-line treatment**; refractory Candida infections |
| **Posaconazole** | Broadest including Mucor/Zygomycetes | **Prophylaxis** in haematological malignancy; **Mucormycosis** salvage; refractory aspergillosis |
| **Clotrimazole/Miconazole** | Topical azoles | Dermatophyte infections (tinea); vulvovaginal candidiasis; oral thrush |

**Azole class side effects:**
* **Hepatotoxicity** — monitor LFTs; avoid in significant liver disease
* **QT prolongation** — especially voriconazole; avoid concurrent QT-prolonging drugs
* **Visual disturbances** — voriconazole-specific: photopsia (flashes of light/altered colour perception); occurs in ~30%; transient and reversible
* **Drug interactions** — inhibit CYP3A4 → ↑ levels of warfarin, ciclosporin, tacrolimus, statins, sirolimus (clinically critical in transplant patients)
* **Hormonal effects** — gynecomastia; inhibit steroidogenesis with prolonged use (itraconazole)

### Echinocandins

* **Caspofungin, Micafungin, Anidulafungin**
* **Mechanism:** non-competitively inhibit **β-1,3-glucan synthase** → ↓ glucan synthesis → disruption of fungal cell wall → **fungicidal** against Candida; **fungistatic** against Aspergillus
* **NOT active against Cryptococcus neoformans** — lacks significant glucan in its cell wall
* IV administration only; generally well tolerated; minimal renal toxicity
* **Side effects:** Hepatotoxicity (mild); histamine-mediated infusion reactions (caspofungin); minimal clinically significant drug interactions

### Flucytosine (5-Fluorocytosine, 5-FC)

* **Mechanism:** taken up by fungal cytosine permease → converted intracellularly to **5-fluorouracil (5-FU)** → inhibits thymidylate synthase → ↓ DNA synthesis; also inhibits RNA synthesis
* **Always used in combination** with amphotericin B — rapid resistance development if used alone
* **Primary use: Cryptococcal meningitis induction** (amphotericin B + flucytosine × 2 weeks)
* **Side effects:** **Myelosuppression** (bone marrow suppression — thrombocytopenia, anaemia, leucopenia) · GI toxicity · hepatotoxicity · dose adjustment required in renal failure

### Cryptococcal Meningitis Treatment Protocol

* **Induction (2 weeks):** Amphotericin B + Flucytosine
* **Consolidation (8 weeks):** Fluconazole 400 mg/day
* **Maintenance (lifelong or until CD4 >200 on ART):** Fluconazole 200 mg/day

### Other Antifungals

| Drug | Mechanism | Use | Side Effects |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Griseofulvin** | Disrupts **microtubule polymerisation** → inhibits mitosis in dermatophytes | Dermatophytosis (tinea capitis — oral; tinea unguium) | Headache · GI upset · photosensitivity · hepatotoxicity · **teratogenic** · induces CYP (reduces OCP efficacy) |
| **Terbinafine** | Inhibits **squalene epoxidase** → squalene accumulates → toxic | Dermatophyte onychomycosis (toenails — most effective oral agent) | Hepatotoxicity · GI · taste disturbance (dysgeusia) · depression |

### Antiviral Drug Targets

| Target | Drug Class |
|---|---|
| **Viral DNA polymerase** | Nucleoside/nucleotide analogues (aciclovir, ganciclovir) |
| **Reverse transcriptase** | NRTIs, NNRTIs (HIV) |
| **Viral protease** | Protease inhibitors (HIV, HCV) |
| **Neuraminidase** | Oseltamivir, zanamivir (influenza) |
| **Integrase** | Integrase strand transfer inhibitors (HIV) |
| **Viral fusion** | Fusion inhibitors, CCR5 antagonists (HIV) |
| **NS5A replication complex** | NS5A inhibitors (HCV) |

### Herpesvirus Antivirals — Aciclovir

* **Mechanism of selective toxicity (three steps):**
  1. **Viral thymidine kinase (TK)** phosphorylates aciclovir → aciclovir monophosphate (ONLY in virus-infected cells — basis of selectivity)
  2. Cellular kinases → aciclovir triphosphate (active form)
  3. Aciclovir-TP → **competitive inhibitor of viral DNA polymerase** + causes **obligate chain termination** (lacks 3'-OH group — no further nucleotide addition)
* **Virostatic** — inhibits viral replication; does not eradicate latent virus

| Indication | Dose/Route |
|---|---|
| Genital HSV (treatment) | Oral aciclovir/valaciclovir |
| **Herpes encephalitis** | **High-dose IV aciclovir** — medical emergency; start empirically |
| Varicella-zoster (chickenpox/shingles) | Oral or IV aciclovir |
| HSV prophylaxis in immunocompromised | Oral aciclovir |
| Neonatal HSV | **IV aciclovir** — high dose |

* **Side effects:** Generally well tolerated · **nephrotoxicity** (crystalluria — maintain good hydration) · CNS effects at high IV doses (tremor, confusion) · phlebitis at IV site

**Related antiherpetic drugs:**

| Drug | Key Difference from Aciclovir |
|---|---|
| **Valaciclovir** | Prodrug of aciclovir; **much better oral bioavailability** (3–5× higher AUC) |
| **Famciclovir** | Prodrug of penciclovir; **longer intracellular half-life** |
| **Ganciclovir** | Active against **CMV** (aciclovir has no clinically useful CMV activity); significantly more toxic |
| **Valganciclovir** | Oral prodrug of ganciclovir; CMV retinitis treatment + transplant prophylaxis |
| **Foscarnet** | Directly inhibits viral DNA polymerase; **does NOT require TK activation** → use for **TK-deficient aciclovir-resistant HSV/CMV**; nephrotoxic |
| **Cidofovir** | Broad-spectrum nucleotide analogue; CMV; aciclovir-resistant HSV; highly nephrotoxic |

### Influenza Antivirals

| Drug | Mechanism | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Oseltamivir (Tamiflu)** | Inhibits **neuraminidase** → prevents viral release from infected cells → limits spread | Influenza A + B treatment (start within **48 hours** of symptoms) + prophylaxis |
| **Zanamivir (Relenza)** | Neuraminidase inhibitor | Inhaled; influenza A + B; use when oral not possible |
| **Baloxavir** | Inhibits **cap-dependent endonuclease** (PA subunit of RNA polymerase) | Single-dose oral treatment influenza A + B |
| **Amantadine/Rimantadine** | Block **M2 ion channel** → prevent viral uncoating | Influenza A only; **high resistance rates**; largely obsolete |

### Hepatitis B Antivirals

| Drug | Class | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Tenofovir (TDF/TAF)** | Nucleotide RT inhibitor | First-line; also treats HIV; TAF less nephrotoxic |
| **Entecavir** | Nucleoside analogue | First-line; very **low resistance rate** |
| **Lamivudine** | Nucleoside analogue | High resistance rates → less preferred |
| **Pegylated Interferon-α** | Immune modulator + antiviral | Finite course (48–52 weeks); contraindicated in cirrhosis/autoimmune disease |
| **Adefovir** | Nucleotide analogue | Second-line; nephrotoxic at high doses |

### Hepatitis C — Direct-Acting Antivirals (DAAs)

* **Cure rates >95%** across all genotypes with pangenotypic DAA regimens
* Treatment duration typically **8–12 weeks**

| Drug Class | Examples | Target |
|---|---|---|
| **NS3/4A protease inhibitors** | Glecaprevir, grazoprevir, paritaprevir | Viral protease — essential for polyprotein cleavage |
| **NS5A inhibitors** | Pibrentasvir, ledipasvir, velpatasvir, ombitasvir | NS5A replication complex protein |
| **NS5B polymerase inhibitors** | **Sofosbuvir** (nucleotide) | Viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase |

**Common pangenotypic regimens:**
* **Sofosbuvir/Velpatasvir** (Epclusa) — all genotypes; 12 weeks
* **Glecaprevir/Pibrentasvir** (Mavyret) — all genotypes; 8 weeks (treatment-naive without cirrhosis)
* **Ledipasvir/Sofosbuvir** (Harvoni) — genotype 1; 12 weeks

### Antihelminthic Drugs

| Drug | Mechanism | Indications | Key Side Effects |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Albendazole** | Inhibits **β-tubulin polymerisation** → impairs microtubule formation → worm immobility and death | Ascariasis, hookworm, strongyloidiasis, hydatid disease (E. granulosus), neurocysticercosis, lymphatic filariasis, trichuriasis | GI effects · hepatotoxicity · bone marrow suppression (prolonged use) · **teratogenic — avoid in pregnancy** |

**Malaria drug targets by life cycle stage:**

| Stage | Drug Type |
|---|---|
| **Liver schizonticide** (kills liver stages) | Primaquine (also kills hypnozoites — radical cure) |
| **Blood schizonticide** (kills asexual blood stages) | Chloroquine, quinine, artemisinins, mefloquine, atovaquone-proguanil |
| **Gametocytocide** (prevents transmission) | Primaquine (P. falciparum); artemisinins |

|---|---|---|---|
| **Chloroquine** | Accumulates in parasite food vacuole → inhibits **haem polymerase** → toxic free haem accumulates → parasite death | Chloroquine-sensitive P. vivax, P. ovale, P. malariae; also rheumatoid arthritis/SLE | **Retinopathy** (prolonged use — irreversible) · GI upset · pruritus (especially in Africans) · QT prolongation |
| **Artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs)** | Artemisinin activated by haem → generates **free radicals** → oxidative damage to parasite proteins and membranes | **First-line treatment of uncomplicated P. falciparum** (artemether-lumefantrine; artesunate-amodiaquine; artesunate-mefloquine) | Generally well tolerated · neurotoxicity (high doses — avoid in first trimester) |

| **Primaquine** | Generates reactive oxygen species → damages parasite mitochondria | **Radical cure of P. vivax and P. ovale** (eradicates dormant liver hypnozoites to prevent relapse) · P. falciparum gametocytocide | **Haemolytic anaemia in G6PD deficiency** — **screen for G6PD before use** · methaemoglobinaemia · **contraindicated in pregnancy** |
| **Mefloquine** | Blood schizonticide (mechanism similar to quinine) | Treatment + prophylaxis of chloroquine-resistant P. falciparum | **Neuropsychiatric effects** (nightmares, anxiety, depression, psychosis — can be severe) · QT prolongation |
| **Atovaquone-proguanil (Malarone)** | Atovaquone inhibits **mitochondrial electron transport** (cytochrome bc1); proguanil inhibits DHFR | Prophylaxis + treatment of uncomplicated P. falciparum | GI effects · headache · mouth ulcers; take with food/milk |
| **Doxycycline** | Protein synthesis inhibitor (see antibiotics section) | **Malaria prophylaxis** (especially Southeast Asia); combination treatment with quinine | Photosensitivity · GI · **contraindicated in children <8 years + pregnancy** |

### SECTION 12 — HIV: TREATMENT & MANAGEMENT

### HIV Biology

* **HIV** = Human Immunodeficiency Virus — **Lentivirus** (subtype of retrovirus); positive-sense single-stranded RNA virus
* **HIV-1** — global pandemic strain; more pathogenic; multiple subtypes (A, B, C, D — subtype C most prevalent worldwide)
* **HIV-2** — predominantly West Africa; less pathogenic; slower progression; some drugs less effective
* **Target cells:** CD4+ T lymphocytes · macrophages · dendritic cells — cells expressing **CD4 receptor + CCR5 or CXCR4 co-receptors**

### HIV Replication Cycle — Targets for Therapy

| Step | Molecular Event | Drug Target |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Attachment** | gp120 binds CD4 receptor | CCR5 antagonists (maraviroc) |
| **2. Fusion** | gp41 mediates membrane fusion | Fusion inhibitors (enfuvirtide) |
| **3. Reverse transcription** | Viral RNA → DNA via reverse transcriptase | NRTIs, NNRTIs |
| **4. Integration** | Viral DNA integrates into host chromosome via integrase | Integrase inhibitors (INSTIs) |
| **5. Transcription + translation** | Host machinery produces viral proteins | — |
| **6. Protease cleavage** | Viral polyprotein precursors cleaved into functional proteins | Protease inhibitors (PIs) |
| **7. Budding + maturation** | New virions released | Capsid inhibitors (lenacapavir) |

### WHO Clinical Staging of HIV

| Stage | Features |
|---|---|
| **Stage 1** | Asymptomatic · persistent generalised lymphadenopathy (PGL) |
| **Stage 2** | Mild — seborrhoeic dermatitis · herpes zoster · recurrent URTI · oral ulcers · angular cheilitis |
| **Stage 3** | Advanced — oral candidiasis · unexplained chronic diarrhoea >1 month · pulmonary TB · bacterial pneumonia · unexplained anaemia/thrombocytopenia |
| **Stage 4 (AIDS)** | Severe — PCP · Cryptococcal meningitis · CMV retinitis · oesophageal candidiasis · Kaposi sarcoma · HIV wasting syndrome · HIV encephalopathy · disseminated MAC · CNS toxoplasmosis · progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML) |

### CD4 Count Clinical Thresholds

| CD4 Count | Clinical Action |
|---|---|
| Any CD4 | **Start ART** (treat all policy) |
| <200 cells/µL | **PCP prophylaxis** — co-trimoxazole |
| <100 cells/µL | **Toxoplasma prophylaxis** — co-trimoxazole (same as PCP) |
| <50 cells/µL | **MAC prophylaxis** — azithromycin weekly; CMV retinitis risk |

### Antiretroviral Drug Classes

### NRTIs — Nucleoside/Nucleotide Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors

* **Mechanism:** Structurally mimic natural nucleosides/nucleotides → incorporated into growing viral DNA chain → **obligate chain termination** (lack 3'-OH group); require intracellular phosphorylation to active triphosphate form
* Form the **backbone of all ART regimens** — always used as a pair (2 NRTIs + 1 third agent)
* **Class toxicity:** Mitochondrial toxicity (inhibit mitochondrial DNA polymerase gamma) → **lactic acidosis + hepatic steatosis** — mainly older NRTIs (stavudine, didanosine, zidovudine)

| Drug | Key Features | Key Side Effects |
|---|---|---|
| **Tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF)** | Most widely used; also active vs HBV | **Nephrotoxicity** (proximal tubular dysfunction, Fanconi syndrome) · ↓ bone mineral density |
| **Tenofovir alafenamide (TAF)** | Newer prodrug of TDF; lower plasma concentrations | Less nephrotoxic and less bone toxic than TDF; slight weight gain |
| **Emtricitabine (FTC)** | Closely related to 3TC; once daily | Very well tolerated; skin hyperpigmentation; also active vs HBV |
| **Lamivudine (3TC)** | Widely used; also active vs HBV — **flare on discontinuation in HBV** | Well tolerated; low resistance barrier — but widely used in resource-limited settings |
| **Abacavir (ABC)** | Used when TDF contraindicated | **Hypersensitivity reaction (HSR)** — fever, rash, GI, respiratory; **linked to HLA-B*5701** — **screen before prescribing**; can be **fatal on rechallenge** |
| **Zidovudine (AZT/ZDV)** | First antiretroviral; used in PMTCT | **Anaemia + neutropenia** (BM suppression) · myopathy · headache · lactic acidosis; being phased out of first-line |
| **Stavudine (d4T)** | Being phased out | Peripheral neuropathy · lipodystrophy · lactic acidosis — significant long-term toxicity |

### NNRTIs — Non-Nucleoside Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors

* **Mechanism:** Bind allosteric site on reverse transcriptase (NOT the active nucleotide-binding site) → conformational change → block RT function → **non-competitive inhibition**
* **Do NOT require intracellular phosphorylation**
* **Low genetic barrier to resistance** — single point mutation (K103N for efavirenz/nevirapine) confers high-level resistance and class cross-resistance

| Drug | Key Features | Key Side Effects |
|---|---|---|
| **Efavirenz (EFV)** | Once daily; long half-life; previously most widely used NNRTI | **CNS effects** (vivid dreams, dizziness, impaired concentration, depression, psychosis — especially first 2–4 weeks) · rash · hepatotoxicity · **teratogenic** (neural tube defects — avoid in first trimester/periconception) · CYP450 inducer |
| **Nevirapine (NVP)** | Twice daily; used in PMTCT syrup | **Hepatotoxicity** (severe — highest risk in women with CD4 >250 + men with CD4 >400 at initiation → **avoid in these groups**) · **Stevens-Johnson syndrome** · rash · CYP450 inducer |
| **Rilpivirine (RPV)** | Once daily; better CNS tolerability | Must be taken **with a meal** (requires gastric acid — avoid PPIs) · depression · QT prolongation · **only use if VL <100,000 + CD4 >200** at start |
| **Doravirine (DOR)** | Once daily; newer; fewer CNS/metabolic effects | Generally excellent tolerability |
| **Etravirine (ETR)** | Active against some K103N-resistant strains | For treatment-experienced patients; rash |

### Protease Inhibitors (PIs)

* **Mechanism:** Inhibit **HIV aspartyl protease** → viral polyprotein precursor Gag-Pol not cleaved → immature, non-infectious virions produced
* **High genetic barrier to resistance** — require multiple accumulating mutations
* **All clinically used PIs are boosted** with **ritonavir (low dose)** or **cobicistat** — both inhibit CYP3A4 → increase PI plasma levels (pharmacokinetic boosting; ritonavir at low dose = pure booster, not therapeutic PI at this dose)

| Drug | Key Features |
|---|---|
| **Darunavir/ritonavir (DRV/r)** | Preferred boosted PI; high barrier to resistance; well tolerated |
| **Atazanavir/ritonavir (ATV/r)** | Once daily; **indirect hyperbilirubinaemia** (benign jaundice — UGT1A1 inhibition, NOT hepatotoxicity) · nephrolithiasis |
| **Lopinavir/ritonavir (LPV/r)** | Co-formulated; used in paediatrics + PMTCT; significant GI side effects |
| **Ritonavir** | Now used exclusively as a **low-dose pharmacokinetic booster** — too toxic at full therapeutic dose |

**PI class side effects:**

| Side Effect | Details |
|---|---|
| **GI disturbance** | Diarrhoea, nausea, vomiting — most common class effect |
| **Lipodystrophy** | Fat redistribution — central obesity (visceral fat), buffalo hump, lipoatrophy (limbs, face) |
| **Dyslipidaemia** | ↑ triglycerides, ↑ LDL, ↓ HDL — monitor lipids regularly |
| **Insulin resistance/diabetes** | ↑ blood glucose; monitor HbA1c |
| **Drug interactions** | Major CYP3A4 inhibitors → multiple interactions; critical with rifampicin, anticonvulsants, warfarin |
| **Nephrolithiasis** | Especially atazanavir and indinavir |

### Integrase Strand Transfer Inhibitors (INSTIs)

* **Mechanism:** Inhibit **HIV integrase** enzyme → prevent strand transfer step of viral DNA integration into host chromosome → viral DNA cannot integrate → no persistent infection established
* **Preferred third agents** in most international guidelines — excellent tolerability, rapid viral load decline, high barrier to resistance (bictegravir, dolutegravir)

| Drug | Key Features |
|---|---|
| **Dolutegravir (DTG)** | Once daily; **highest barrier to resistance** of all INSTIs; **preferred** in WHO + Kenyan guidelines; inhibits tubular creatinine secretion → ↑ creatinine (NOT renal failure) |
| **Bictegravir (BIC)** | Only available as BIC/TAF/FTC (Biktarvy); high barrier to resistance; excellent tolerability |
| **Raltegravir (RAL)** | First INSTI; twice daily (or high-dose once daily); lower barrier to resistance |
| **Elvitegravir (EVG)** | Requires cobicistat boosting; now largely replaced by newer INSTIs |
| **Cabotegravir (CAB)** | Long-acting injectable (monthly × 2 then every 2 months); also approved for PrEP |

* **DTG neural tube defect risk** — periconception exposure; counsel all women of childbearing potential; risk appears low but requires informed consent
* **INSTI class side effects:** Weight gain (significant — especially DTG + women + Sub-Saharan African populations) · insomnia · headache · ↑ CPK

### Entry/Fusion Inhibitors

| Drug | Target | Key Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Enfuvirtide (T-20)** | **gp41** — prevents membrane fusion | Only SC injectable; injection site reactions (almost universal); expensive; salvage use only |
| **Maraviroc** | **CCR5 co-receptor** — blocks R5-tropic HIV | **Tropism testing mandatory before use** — NOT effective for X4-tropic or dual/mixed-tropic virus; hepatotoxicity |

### Capsid Inhibitor

* **Lenacapavir** — long-acting SC injection every 6 months; inhibits multiple functions of HIV capsid protein; used for salvage therapy in MDR HIV; also being investigated for PrEP

### Recommended First-Line ART Regimens

**WHO/Kenyan national guidelines — preferred:**
* **TDF + 3TC + DTG** — adults and adolescents (most widely recommended)
* **TAF + FTC + BIC** (Biktarvy) — alternative; less nephrotoxic
* **ABC + 3TC + DTG** — if TDF/TAF contraindicated; **check HLA-B*5701 before ABC**

**Components: 2 NRTIs (backbone) + 1 INSTI (preferred) or NNRTI or boosted PI**

### When to Start ART

* **Treat ALL HIV-positive individuals** regardless of CD4 count — WHO 2015 "Treat All" policy
* **Start as soon as possible** — same day or within 7 days of diagnosis ideally
* **Defer ART when:**
  * **TB meningitis** — defer 4–8 weeks (high IRIS risk with severe neurological consequences)
  * **Cryptococcal meningitis** — defer 4–6 weeks after antifungal induction (IRIS risk)
  * **Active TB with CD4 >50** — start ART within 8 weeks of TB treatment
  * **Active TB with CD4 <50** — start ART within 2 weeks of TB treatment

### HIV Treatment Monitoring

| Parameter | Timing |
|---|---|
| **Viral load (VL)** | Baseline → 6 months → 12 months → annually if suppressed |
| **CD4 count** | Baseline; less critical once VL suppressed; guides OI prophylaxis decisions |
| **Renal function (eGFR, creatinine, urine protein)** | Baseline + 3–6 monthly (TDF) |
| **Liver function tests** | Baseline + as clinically indicated |
| **FBC** | Baseline; regularly if on ZDV |
| **Fasting lipids + glucose/HbA1c** | Baseline + annually (PIs, INSTIs) |
| **Pregnancy test** | Women of reproductive age at baseline |

* **Treatment success:** Viral load **<50 copies/mL** (or <20 copies/mL in some assays) at 6–12 months
* **Virological failure:** VL **>1000 copies/mL** on two consecutive measurements after enhanced adherence counselling

### IRIS — Immune Reconstitution Inflammatory Syndrome

* Paradoxical worsening or unmasking of an OI after starting ART due to recovery of immune function
* **Paradoxical IRIS** — previously diagnosed and treated OI worsens after ART start
* **Unmasking IRIS** — previously subclinical OI becomes clinically apparent after immune recovery
* Most common: **TB-IRIS** (most frequent) · Cryptococcal IRIS · CMV IRIS · MAC IRIS
* Management: **Continue ART**; treat underlying OI; NSAIDs or corticosteroids for severe IRIS (e.g. prednisone 1.25 mg/kg/day for TB-IRIS)
* **Cryptococcal IRIS can be fatal** — carefully controlled CSF pressure management critical

### HIV in Pregnancy — PMTCT

* **Transmission routes:** In utero (transplacental) · intrapartum (during delivery) · postpartum (breastfeeding)
* **Without ART:** MTCT risk 25–45%
* **With comprehensive PMTCT interventions + ART:** <2% risk of transmission
* **Preferred regimen:** TDF + 3TC + DTG (or EFV as alternative)
* **Avoid:** Efavirenz in first trimester · Stavudine · Didanosine · Indinavir
* **Infant prophylaxis:** NVP syrup daily from birth × 6 weeks (extended if high risk)
* **Breastfeeding:** Recommended and safe if mother has suppressed viral load on effective ART

### HIV OI Prophylaxis Summary

| OI | Threshold | Drug |
|---|---|---|
| **TB (LTBI)** | All HIV-positive (rule out active TB first) | **Isoniazid Preventive Therapy (IPT)** × 6 months + pyridoxine |
| **PCP** | CD4 <200 | **Co-trimoxazole** 960 mg daily |
| **Toxoplasmosis** | CD4 <100 | Co-trimoxazole (same dose as PCP prophylaxis) |
| **MAC** | CD4 <50 | **Azithromycin** 1200 mg weekly |
| **Cryptococcus** | CD4 <100 (with high prevalence) | Fluconazole 100–200 mg daily (in some guidelines) |
| **Malaria** | All HIV-positive in endemic areas | Co-trimoxazole provides collateral antimalarial benefit |

### PrEP and PEP

**PrEP (Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis):**
* For HIV-negative individuals at high ongoing risk of HIV acquisition
* **Oral PrEP:** TDF + FTC (Truvada) daily — >99% effective when taken correctly
* **Injectable PrEP:** Cabotegravir LA — monthly × 2 loading doses then every 2 months
* Requires: confirmed HIV-negative status · eGFR >60 · 3-monthly monitoring (HIV test, STI screen, renal function)

**PEP (Post-Exposure Prophylaxis):**
* **Start within 72 hours** of exposure (sooner = better; ideal <4 hours)
* **Duration: 28 days** — do NOT stop early
* **Regimen:** TDF + FTC + DTG (preferred); or TDF + FTC + lopinavir/r
* Indications: occupational (needlestick) · sexual assault · unprotected sex with HIV+ partner

### SECTION 13 — PHARMACOTHERAPY OF ACNE

### Pathophysiology of Acne Vulgaris

**Four key interacting pathogenic factors:**
1. **↑ Sebum production** — androgen-stimulated sebaceous gland activity (particularly testosterone → DHT via 5α-reductase); explains androgen-driven acne
2. **Follicular hyperkeratinisation** — abnormal desquamation of follicular keratinocytes → blocked pilosebaceous unit → **microcomedo** (precursor lesion to all acne lesions)
3. **Cutibacterium acnes (C. acnes) proliferation** — colonises the anaerobic, lipid-rich environment of the plugged follicle; was previously called Propionibacterium acnes
4. **Inflammation** — C. acnes activates innate immunity via TLR2 → cytokine release (IL-1, TNF, IL-8) → inflammatory papules, pustules, nodules, and cysts

**Acne lesion types:**

| Lesion | Type | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Open comedone | Non-inflammatory | "Blackhead" — dilated follicle filled with melanin-oxidised keratin |
| Closed comedone | Non-inflammatory | "Whitehead" — plugged follicle with narrow opening |
| Papule | Inflammatory | Small raised, red, tender lesion <5 mm |
| Pustule | Inflammatory | Papule containing pus |
| Nodule | Inflammatory | Large (>5 mm), deep, painful lesion; risk of scarring |
| Cyst/nodulocyst | Inflammatory | Fluctuant, deeply inflamed lesion; highest scarring risk |

### Topical Acne Treatments

| Drug | Mechanism | Key Use | Side Effects |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Benzoyl peroxide (BPO)** | Releases free oxygen radicals → **bactericidal** against C. acnes · keratolytic · anti-inflammatory · NO resistance develops | **First-line for inflammatory acne**; always combine with topical antibiotics to prevent resistance | Dryness · bleaching of clothing and hair · contact dermatitis |
| **Retinoids (Tretinoin, Adapalene, Tazarotene)** | Bind retinoic acid receptors (RAR) → **normalise follicular keratinisation** → prevent microcomedone formation · anti-inflammatory | **First-line for comedonal acne**; best for non-inflammatory; foundation of maintenance therapy | **Retinoid dermatitis** (dryness, peeling, erythema — especially first 4–8 weeks; improves with continued use) · photosensitivity · **teratogenic — contraindicated in pregnancy** |
| **Topical clindamycin** | Inhibits **50S ribosome** → ↓ C. acnes protein synthesis + anti-inflammatory (↓ neutrophil chemotaxis) | Inflammatory acne (papulopustular); **always combine with BPO** to prevent resistance | Dryness · mild irritation · C. difficile (rare with topical) |
| **Topical erythromycin** | Same as clindamycin | Inflammatory acne; **high C. acnes resistance rates** → less preferred now; use with BPO | Similar to clindamycin topically |
| **Azelaic acid** | Anti-inflammatory · inhibits C. acnes · ↓ follicular hyperkeratinisation · inhibits tyrosinase → ↓ post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation | Mild–moderate inflammatory + comedonal acne; **safe in pregnancy**; useful in skin of colour | Tingling · transient erythema · mild dryness |
| **Dapsone gel (5%)** | Anti-inflammatory + antimicrobial | Inflammatory acne; particularly in women | Local dryness; **orange discolouration if used with BPO** |
| **Salicylic acid** | **Keratolytic** — dissolves intercellular cement between follicular keratinocytes → unblocks pores | Mild comedonal acne; OTC skincare products | Mild dryness and irritation |

### Systemic Antibiotics for Acne

* Use for **maximum 3–6 months** — prolonged use drives C. acnes resistance
* **Always combine with topical BPO** to reduce resistance selection
* Do not use as monotherapy — always combine with a topical retinoid

| Drug | Key Notes |
|---|---|
| **Doxycycline** | **First-line oral antibiotic for acne**; once or twice daily; anti-inflammatory at sub-antimicrobial doses; **avoid sun exposure** (photosensitivity) |
| **Minocycline** | Effective; risk of **blue-black pigmentation** of skin, teeth, mucosae + dizziness + lupus-like syndrome — now less preferred than doxycycline |
| **Erythromycin** | Alternative if tetracyclines contraindicated (pregnancy); **high resistance rates** — limit use |
| **Trimethoprim** | Alternative when tetracyclines contraindicated; reasonable efficacy |
| **Lymecycline** | Tetracycline class; once daily; better tolerated GI |

### Hormonal Therapy for Acne (Women Only)

| Drug | Mechanism | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Combined oral contraceptive pill (COCP)** | ↑ sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) → ↓ free androgens → ↓ sebaceous gland stimulation | Licensed for acne: ethinyl estradiol + norgestimate/drospirenone/cyproterone |
| **Spironolactone** | Androgen receptor blocker + ↓ adrenal androgen production → ↓ sebum | 50–200 mg/day; widely used off-label for hormonal/adult female acne; **contraindicated in pregnancy** (feminisation of male fetus) · monitor potassium |
| **Cyproterone acetate** | Antiandrogen | Combined with ethinyl estradiol (co-cyprindiol/Dianette) specifically licensed for acne; increased VTE risk vs standard OCP |

### Isotretinoin — Systemic Retinoid

* **Most effective acne treatment** — only treatment that can achieve long-term or permanent remission
* **Mechanism:** ↓ sebaceous gland size and sebum production (>90% reduction) · normalises follicular keratinisation · inhibits C. acnes colonisation indirectly · anti-inflammatory

**Indications:**
* Severe nodulocystic/conglobate acne
* Moderate acne unresponsive to adequate trials of topical + systemic antibiotics
* Acne causing significant scarring or psychological distress

**Dose:** 0.5–1.0 mg/kg/day; target **cumulative dose 120–150 mg/kg** (higher cumulative dose = more durable remission)

**Side effects:**

| Side Effect | Details |
|---|---|
| **Teratogenicity (Category X)** | Most serious concern — **highly teratogenic causing severe craniofacial, cardiac, CNS, and skeletal malformations**; **mandatory pregnancy prevention programme** (iPLEDGE in USA; similar programmes globally): 2 negative pregnancy tests before starting, effective contraception 1 month before, throughout, and 1 month after course |
| **Mucocutaneous dryness** | **Most common** — cheilitis (chapped lips — virtually universal), xerosis, dry eyes (caution in contact lens wearers), epistaxis, dry nasal passages |
| **Hyperlipidaemia** | ↑ triglycerides (dose-dependent), ↑ LDL, ↓ HDL — monitor fasting lipids; dose reduction or stop if severe |
| **Hepatotoxicity** | ↑ LFTs — monitor; usually mild and reversible |
| **Depression/suicidality** | Association debated — monitor mental health; pre-existing psychiatric disease is relative contraindication |
| **Photosensitivity** | Use high-SPF sunscreen; avoid prolonged sun exposure |
| **Night blindness** | Impaired dark adaptation; avoid night driving |
| **Musculoskeletal** | Arthralgia, myalgia; avoid intense physical training |
| **Premature epiphyseal closure** | Caution in adolescents still growing |
| **Wax hair removal** | Contraindicated during and for 6 months after — increased skin fragility |

**Monitoring during isotretinoin course:**
* **Before starting:** Pregnancy test × 2 · fasting lipids · LFTs · FBC · glucose
* **Monthly:** Pregnancy test · fasting lipids · LFTs

### Acne Severity — Treatment Algorithm

| Severity | Recommended Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Mild comedonal** | Topical retinoid alone |
| **Mild inflammatory** | Topical BPO ± topical antibiotic + topical retinoid |
| **Moderate papulopustular** | Topical retinoid + BPO + oral doxycycline (3–6 months max) |
| **Severe/nodulocystic** | **Oral isotretinoin** — refer to dermatologist |
| **Women with hormonal acne** | Add COCP or spironolactone to topical regimen |
| **Maintenance (post-antibiotic)** | Topical retinoid ± BPO (indefinitely) |

## Practice Questions

1. **Question:** A patient develops nausea, rash, and fever 10 days after starting abacavir. What should be done, and what pharmacogenomic test should have been performed? → **Answer:** This is an **abacavir hypersensitivity reaction (HSR)** — **stop abacavir immediately** and NEVER rechallenge (risk of fatal anaphylaxis). **HLA-B*5701** genetic screening should have been performed before prescribing, as this allele strongly predicts HSR risk.
2. **Question:** A patient on rifampicin for TB reports that her combined oral contraceptive pill seems less effective. Why, and how should this be managed? → **Answer:** Rifampicin is a **potent CYP450 inducer** → accelerates metabolism of ethinyl estradiol and progestins → **reduces OCP efficacy** → risk of unintended pregnancy. Use an alternative contraceptive method (e.g. barrier method or injectable/IUD) for the duration of TB treatment.
3. **Question:** A patient with MDR-TB complains of visual blurring and difficulty distinguishing red from green. What drug is responsible, and what action is required? → **Answer:** This is **ethambutol-induced optic neuritis** — presenting as reduced visual acuity and red-green colour blindness. **Stop ethambutol immediately** and arrange urgent ophthalmology review. Baseline and regular visual monitoring should have been performed before and during treatment.
4. **Question:** An HIV-positive patient with CD4 count of 45 presents with worsening confusion 3 weeks after starting ART. MRI shows enhancing cerebral lesions. What complication has occurred? → **Answer:** This represents **CNS Toxoplasma IRIS** (or could represent PML IRIS/Cryptococcal IRIS) — immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome following ART initiation in a severely immunocompromised patient with pre-existing CNS opportunistic infection. Continue ART; treat the underlying OI; consider corticosteroids for severe IRIS.
5. **Question:** A 28-year-old woman on oral isotretinoin for severe nodular acne presents with a 6-week amenorrhoea. What is the immediate concern and management? → **Answer:** The immediate concern is **pregnancy on isotretinoin** — highly teratogenic (Category X) causing severe fetal malformations. Perform an urgent pregnancy test. If confirmed pregnant, the patient needs urgent counselling regarding teratogenic risks and referral; the drug should be stopped immediately.
6. **Question:** A child is found to have severe G6PD deficiency during screening. He has been diagnosed with P. vivax malaria and cleared the blood-stage infection with chloroquine. Why must careful consideration be given to the next treatment step? → **Answer:** Radical cure with **primaquine** is required to eradicate dormant **liver hypnozoites** of P. vivax to prevent relapse. However, primaquine is **absolutely contraindicated in severe G6PD deficiency** — it causes severe haemolytic anaemia. Alternative strategies (e.g. tafenoquine with appropriate G6PD level, or frequent low-dose primaquine under supervision) must be considered with specialist input.
7. **Question:** Explain why clindamycin is not combined with penicillin for the treatment of streptococcal infections, yet is recommended in necrotising fasciitis alongside beta-lactams. → **Answer:** In the treatment of **toxin-mediated infections** such as necrotising fasciitis and streptococcal toxic shock syndrome, clindamycin is specifically added for its ability to **inhibit bacterial protein and toxin synthesis** (at the 50S ribosome), reducing the production of virulence factors even in non-replicating bacteria. The theoretical antagonism (bacteriostatic + bactericidal) is clinically irrelevant here because the **aim is toxin suppression**, not just bacterial killing — a role the beta-lactam alone cannot fulfil.
8. **Question:** Why does co-trimoxazole cause a rise in serum creatinine even when kidney function is normal, and why is this clinically important in HIV patients? → **Answer:** **Trimethoprim** competitively inhibits tubular secretion of creatinine via organic cation transporters, **raising serum creatinine without reducing actual GFR** — this is not true renal failure. This is clinically important in HIV patients starting ART containing **tenofovir** + **dolutegravir** (DTG also inhibits tubular creatinine secretion), as the combination may produce an apparent creatinine rise that could be misinterpreted as nephrotoxicity, leading to inappropriate ART modification.
