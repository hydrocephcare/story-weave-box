## Part 1: Vaccines and Antivirals

## 1. Introduction

Vaccines and antiviral drugs are the two primary strategies for controlling viral infections. Vaccines work by preparing the immune system before infection occurs — they are preventive. Antiviral drugs work by interfering with specific steps in the viral replication cycle — they are therapeutic. Both strategies depend on understanding viral structure, replication, and the host immune response. These topics are among the most consistently examined at MKU, appearing in essays across virtually every past paper, particularly questions on OPV vs IPV, active vs passive immunization, and the mechanism of selective toxicity of antivirals.

## 2. Vaccines

### What is a Vaccine

A vaccine is a biological preparation that induces active immunity against a specific pathogen by stimulating the immune system to produce antibodies and memory cells without causing the disease itself.

### Mechanism of Protection

1. Vaccine antigen is administered
2. Antigen-presenting cells (dendritic cells, macrophages) process the antigen
3. Presented to CD4+ T-helper cells via MHC class II
4. T-helper cells activate B cells → differentiate into plasma cells
5. Plasma cells produce specific antibodies against the viral antigen
6. CD8+ cytotoxic T cells also activated — important for live vaccines
7. Long-lived memory B and T cells formed
8. On exposure to real virus — rapid, amplified secondary immune response
9. Virus neutralized before disease can develop

### Types of Vaccines

**1. Live Attenuated Vaccines**
- Contain weakened but living virus — reduced virulence, retained antigenicity
- Produced by: repeated passage of virus in non-human cells until it loses ability to cause disease in humans
- Advantages:
  - Strongest and most durable immunity — mimics natural infection
  - Both humoral and cell-mediated immunity induced
  - Single dose often sufficient
  - Mucosal immunity produced (especially important for gut/respiratory viruses)
- Disadvantages:
  - Risk of reversion to virulence — attenuated virus can mutate back
  - Cannot be given to immunocompromised patients — can cause disease
  - Requires cold chain — heat sensitive
  - Cannot be given in pregnancy
- Examples: OPV (oral polio), MMR (measles, mumps, rubella), Yellow fever vaccine, Varicella vaccine, LAIV (live attenuated influenza)

**2. Inactivated (Killed) Vaccines**
- Contain killed virus — treated with heat, formalin, or beta-propiolactone
- Virus cannot replicate but antigens are preserved
- Advantages:
  - Cannot cause disease — safer
  - Can be given to immunocompromised patients
  - Stable — does not revert to virulence
- Disadvantages:
  - Weaker immunity — mainly humoral, less cell-mediated
  - Multiple doses required (boosters needed)
  - Adjuvant often needed to boost response
  - No mucosal immunity
- Examples: IPV (injected polio), inactivated influenza vaccine, Hepatitis A vaccine, Rabies vaccine

**3. Subunit Vaccines**
- Contain only specific viral proteins — not whole virus
- Examples: Hepatitis B vaccine (HBsAg), HPV vaccine (L1 capsid protein)
- Very safe — no viral genetic material
- Requires adjuvant and boosters

**4. Toxoid Vaccines**
- Not strictly viral — used for bacterial toxins
- Inactivated toxin — cannot cause disease but induces antitoxin antibodies

**5. mRNA Vaccines**
- Deliver mRNA encoding viral antigen into host cells
- Host cells produce the antigen → immune response
- Examples: COVID-19 vaccines (Pfizer-BioNTech, Moderna)
- Advantages: rapid production, no live virus needed

### OPV vs IPV — The Most Examined Vaccine Comparison

This comparison has appeared in essay form multiple times at MKU.

| Feature | OPV (Oral Polio Vaccine) | IPV (Inactivated Polio Vaccine) |
|---|---|---|
| Type | Live attenuated | Killed/inactivated |
| Route | Oral (swallowed) | Injected (intramuscular) |
| Developed by | Albert Sabin | Jonas Salk |
| Immunity type | Humoral + cell-mediated + mucosal (gut IgA) | Humoral only |
| Herd immunity | Yes — vaccine virus shed in stool, spreads to contacts | No — does not spread |
| Dose | Single dose effective | Multiple doses required |
| Cost | Cheaper | More expensive |
| Risk | Vaccine-associated paralytic poliomyelitis (VAPP) — rare but real | No risk of VAPP |
| Use in immunocompromised | Contraindicated — can cause disease | Safe |
| Cold chain | Required | Required |
| Why some countries prefer IPV | Eliminate risk of VAPP, especially in countries with low polio burden | Safer for immunocompromised populations |

**Why OPV produces better gut immunity:**
- OPV is swallowed → replicates in gut epithelium → stimulates local mucosal IgA production
- Gut IgA blocks poliovirus entry at the primary site of infection
- IPV injected → only systemic IgG produced → no gut mucosal immunity
- This is why OPV is better at preventing transmission — it blocks infection at the gut

**Why some countries prefer IPV:**
- Risk of VAPP — approximately 1 in 2.4 million doses causes vaccine-associated paralysis
- Countries that have already eliminated wild poliovirus use IPV to avoid the risk of introducing vaccine-derived poliovirus into a susceptible population

### Active vs Passive Immunization — High Yield

| Feature | Active Immunization | Passive Immunization |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Stimulating the host immune system to produce its own antibodies | Administering pre-formed antibodies directly |
| Onset | Slow — days to weeks | Immediate — within hours |
| Duration | Long-lasting — months to years (memory cells) | Short-lived — weeks (antibodies degraded) |
| Source | Vaccine or natural infection | Human or animal serum, immunoglobulins |
| Memory | Yes — immunological memory formed | No memory cells produced |
| Use | Prevention before exposure | Treatment after exposure, or in immunocompromised |
| Examples | All standard vaccines | Rabies immunoglobulin, tetanus immunoglobulin, HNIG, anti-D |

**How an attenuated vaccine protects a child:**
1. Attenuated virus administered — cannot cause disease in immunocompetent child
2. Virus replicates minimally — enough to stimulate immune response
3. B cells produce antibodies against viral antigens
4. Memory B and T cells formed
5. On exposure to wild virus — memory cells activated rapidly
6. Large amounts of antibody produced within days — neutralize virus before disease develops
7. Child is protected

## 3. Antiviral Drugs

### Introduction

Antiviral drugs work by targeting specific steps in the viral replication cycle. Unlike antibiotics, antivirals must be selectively toxic — they must inhibit viral replication without significantly harming host cells. This selectivity is achieved by targeting viral enzymes or processes that are unique to the virus or significantly different from host cell equivalents.

### The Viral Replication Cycle and Drug Target Sites

Antiviral drugs can target any of the following stages:

**Stage 1: Attachment/Adsorption**
- Target: viral spike — host receptor interaction
- Drugs: Maraviroc (blocks CCR5 co-receptor — HIV), Monoclonal antibodies against viral spikes

**Stage 2: Penetration and Fusion**
- Target: membrane fusion
- Drugs: Enfuvirtide/Fuzeon (blocks HIV gp41 fusion), Amantadine (blocks influenza M2 ion channel — prevents uncoating)

**Stage 3: Uncoating**
- Target: release of viral genome inside the cell
- Drugs: Amantadine, Rimantadine (influenza M2 channel blockers)

**Stage 4: Replication of Viral Genome**
- Target: viral polymerases — most important drug target stage
- Drugs:
  - NRTIs — Zidovudine, Tenofovir, Lamivudine (HIV reverse transcriptase)
  - NNRTIs — Nevirapine, Efavirenz (HIV reverse transcriptase)
  - Acyclovir triphosphate (HSV DNA polymerase)
  - Ganciclovir (CMV DNA polymerase)
  - Sofosbuvir (HCV RNA polymerase)
  - Oseltamivir — technically targets release but also reduces replication efficiency

**Stage 5: Transcription and Translation**
- Less commonly targeted
- Drugs: Fomivirsen (antisense oligonucleotide — CMV)

**Stage 6: Assembly**
- Target: viral protease — processing of polyproteins into functional proteins
- Drugs: Protease inhibitors — Lopinavir, Ritonavir, Atazanavir (HIV)

**Stage 7: Release/Budding**
- Target: neuraminidase — release of influenza virions
- Drugs: Oseltamivir (Tamiflu), Zanamivir — neuraminidase inhibitors

### Selective Toxicity — How Antivirals Spare Host Cells

Selective toxicity means a drug is toxic to the virus or virus-infected cells but not to normal host cells. This is achieved through:

**1. Targeting virus-specific enzymes not present in host cells:**
- HIV reverse transcriptase — host cells do not reverse transcribe RNA into DNA
- HIV integrase — host cells do not integrate foreign DNA via viral integrase
- Influenza neuraminidase — host cells do not express neuraminidase

**2. Targeting viral enzymes that differ sufficiently from host equivalents:**
- Viral DNA polymerases (HSV, CMV) differ from human DNA polymerase in structure
- Acyclovir triphosphate has much higher affinity for HSV DNA polymerase than human DNA polymerase

**3. Requiring virus-specific activation:**
- Acyclovir requires viral thymidine kinase for first phosphorylation
- Only HSV-infected cells contain viral TK → only infected cells activate acyclovir
- Uninfected cells cannot activate acyclovir → spared

### Key Antiviral Drugs Summary

| Drug | Virus Target | Mechanism | Stage Blocked |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acyclovir | HSV, VZV | Inhibits viral DNA polymerase after activation by viral TK | Replication |
| Ganciclovir | CMV | Inhibits CMV DNA polymerase | Replication |
| Zidovudine (AZT) | HIV | NRTI — inhibits reverse transcriptase | Replication |
| Nevirapine | HIV | NNRTI — inhibits reverse transcriptase | Replication |
| Raltegravir | HIV | Integrase inhibitor | Integration |
| Lopinavir/Ritonavir | HIV | Protease inhibitor | Assembly |
| Enfuvirtide (Fuzeon) | HIV | Fusion inhibitor — blocks gp41 | Penetration/Fusion |
| Maraviroc | HIV | CCR5 antagonist | Attachment |
| Oseltamivir (Tamiflu) | Influenza A and B | Neuraminidase inhibitor | Release |
| Amantadine | Influenza A only | M2 channel blocker | Uncoating |
| Sofosbuvir | HCV | NS5B RNA polymerase inhibitor | Replication |
| Ribavirin | Broad spectrum | Multiple mechanisms | Replication |

## Part 2: Rabies Virus

## 1. Introduction

Rabies is an acute, progressive, invariably fatal viral encephalitis caused by the Rabies virus. It is one of the oldest known infectious diseases and remains a significant public health problem in Africa and Asia. Despite being almost universally fatal once clinical symptoms appear, rabies is completely preventable through vaccination and post-exposure prophylaxis. This stark contrast — preventable yet fatal — makes it one of the most clinically important viruses to understand. At MKU, rabies has appeared in essay questions asking students to describe its pathogenesis and the management of an exposed patient.

## 2. Classification and Structure

- **Family:** Rhabdoviridae
- **Genus:** Lyssavirus
- **Genome:** Single-stranded, negative-sense RNA
- **Enveloped virus**
- **Shape:** Bullet-shaped — distinctive, unique among human viruses
- **Key surface protein:** Glycoprotein G — mediates attachment to host receptors; target of neutralizing antibodies; basis of rabies vaccine

**Internal proteins:**
- **N protein (nucleoprotein)** — encapsidates RNA genome; used in diagnosis
- **L protein** — RNA-dependent RNA polymerase
- **P protein** — polymerase cofactor
- **M protein** — matrix protein; links envelope to nucleocapsid

## 3. Transmission

- **Animal bite** — primary route; infected saliva introduced into wound
- **Scratch** — if contaminated with infected saliva
- **Licking of broken skin or mucous membranes** — by infected animal
- **Aerosol transmission** — very rare; reported in bat caves with high bat density
- **Transplantation** — extremely rare; infected cornea or organ transplant

**Reservoir animals:**
- Dogs — most common source of human rabies globally, especially in Africa and Asia
- Bats — main reservoir in Americas
- Foxes, raccoons, skunks — in specific geographic regions
- Wild animals — jackals, mongooses, wolves

**Important:** Virus is present in saliva of infected animals. Animals are infectious before showing symptoms — in the prodromal phase.

## 4. Pathogenesis — High Yield

The pathogenesis of rabies is one of the most distinctive in virology and explains all the clinical features.

### Step 1: Inoculation
- Infected animal bites → virus deposited in wound
- Virus remains at the wound site, replicating in local muscle cells
- **Incubation at wound site:** days to weeks — this is the window for post-exposure prophylaxis

### Step 2: Peripheral Nerve Entry
- Virus binds to **nicotinic acetylcholine receptors** at neuromuscular junctions
- Also binds NCAM (neural cell adhesion molecule) and p75 neurotrophin receptor
- Virus enters peripheral nerve endings at the wound site

### Step 3: Retrograde Axonal Transport

- Virus travels **retrograde** (from nerve ending toward cell body) along peripheral sensory and motor nerves
- Travels inside axons — protected from immune system
- Rate of travel: approximately 50–100 mm per day
- This is why wounds closer to the CNS (face, neck) cause faster disease — shorter journey
- Wounds on feet or hands cause slower disease — longer journey to CNS

### Step 4: CNS Entry and Spread
- Virus reaches the spinal cord and brain
- Spreads rapidly throughout CNS — infects neurons extensively
- Primarily infects the **limbic system, brainstem, and cerebellum**
- No inflammatory destruction of neurons — virus causes functional disruption rather than cell death
- This explains why there is little structural damage on pathology despite severe clinical disease

### Step 5: Centrifugal Spread

- Virus spreads **centrifugally** (outward from CNS) along peripheral nerves to many organs
- Reaches salivary glands → virus present in saliva — this is how transmission occurs
- Also reaches cornea, skin, heart, adrenal glands, kidneys

### Step 6: Death
- Death from respiratory failure, cardiovascular collapse, or brainstem dysfunction
- Almost 100% fatal once clinical symptoms appear

## 5. Clinical Features

**Incubation period:**
- Average: 1–3 months
- Range: 4 days to several years (rare)
- Shorter with: head/neck bites, severe wounds, large inoculum

### Phase 1: Prodromal Phase (2–10 days)
- Non-specific symptoms — fever, malaise, headache, fatigue
- Paraesthesia or pain at the wound site — highly specific early sign
- Anxiety, agitation, irritability

### Phase 2: Acute Neurological Phase (2–7 days)

**Furious (Encephalitic) Rabies — 80% of cases:**
- Hydrophobia — fear of water; attempts to swallow trigger violent pharyngeal spasms — pathognomonic of rabies
- Aerophobia — fear of air/drafts; similar spasms triggered by air on skin
- Hypersalivation — virus in salivary glands; swallowing painful
- Hyperexcitability, agitation, aggression
- Hallucinations, confusion
- Autonomic dysfunction — tachycardia, hypertension, hyperthermia
- Periods of calm between episodes of spasm

**Paralytic (Dumb) Rabies — 20% of cases:**
- Ascending flaccid paralysis — resembles Guillain-Barré syndrome
- Less dramatic — often misdiagnosed
- Eventually progresses to coma

### Phase 3: Coma and Death (days)
- Progressive coma
- Respiratory failure
- Death — virtually 100% once symptomatic

## 6. Diagnosis

- **Clinical diagnosis** — in endemic areas, classic presentation is sufficient
- **Direct fluorescent antibody (DFA) test** — gold standard; detects rabies antigen in brain tissue (post-mortem) or skin biopsy (corneal epithelium)
- **Negri bodies** — eosinophilic intracytoplasmic inclusion bodies in neurons (especially Purkinje cells of cerebellum and hippocampal neurons); pathognomonic on histology
- **RT-PCR** — detects viral RNA in saliva, CSF, skin
- **Serology** — antibody detection; useful in vaccinated individuals

## 7. Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP) — High Yield

PEP is effective because of the long incubation period before virus reaches the CNS. Once the virus enters peripheral nerves and travels to the brain, PEP is no longer effective.

### Wound Management — First and Most Critical Step

- Immediate and thorough washing of wound with soap and water for at least 15 minutes
- Apply antiseptic — povidone iodine or alcohol
- Do not suture wound primarily if possible — suturing can push virus deeper
- This simple step significantly reduces viral inoculum

### Passive Immunization — Rabies Immunoglobulin (RIG)
- Administered once — at the time of first presentation
- Infiltrated directly into and around the wound — neutralizes virus locally
- Remaining dose given intramuscularly at a distant site
- Human rabies immunoglobulin (HRIG) — preferred; 20 IU/kg
- Equine rabies immunoglobulin (ERIG) — cheaper alternative; test for allergy first
- Purpose — provides immediate protection while active immunity develops

### Active Immunization — Rabies Vaccine
- Modern cell-culture rabies vaccines — HDCV (Human Diploid Cell Vaccine), PCECV (Purified Chick Embryo Cell Vaccine)
- Schedule for unvaccinated person: days 0, 3, 7, 14 (and sometimes day 28)
- Schedule for previously vaccinated person: days 0 and 3 only — no RIG needed
- Vaccine induces active immunity — antibodies against glycoprotein G
- Takes 7–14 days to produce protective antibody levels

### Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis (PrEP)
- For high-risk groups — veterinarians, wildlife workers, laboratory workers, travellers to endemic areas
- Three doses — days 0, 7, 21 or 28
- Simplifies PEP if exposed — only 2 booster doses needed, no RIG

## Part 3: CMV and EBV

## 1. Introduction

Cytomegalovirus (CMV) and Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV) are both members of the Herpesviridae family and share the fundamental herpesvirus property of establishing lifelong latent infection. However, they differ significantly in their target cells, clinical manifestations, and oncogenic potential. CMV is the most important cause of congenital viral infection worldwide and is a major pathogen in immunocompromised patients. EBV is the cause of infectious mononucleosis and is strongly associated with several malignancies including Burkitt's lymphoma and nasopharyngeal carcinoma. Both are high-yield topics at MKU, appearing in MCQs and essays.

## 2. Cytomegalovirus (CMV)

### Classification
- **Family:** Herpesviridae
- **Subfamily:** Betaherpesvirinae
- **Genome:** Double-stranded DNA — largest herpesvirus genome
- **Enveloped virus**
- Characteristic **owl-eye inclusion bodies** in infected cells — large intranuclear inclusion surrounded by a clear halo

### Epidemiology
- Ubiquitous — infects 50–90% of adults worldwide
- Transmitted through: saliva, urine, breast milk, sexual contact, blood transfusion, organ transplantation, transplacental
- Primary infection usually asymptomatic in immunocompetent individuals
- Establishes latency in monocytes, macrophages, and endothelial cells

### Target Cells
- Virtually all nucleated cells — explains why CMV causes disseminated disease
- Latency in: monocytes and their precursors in bone marrow
- Reactivation when cell-mediated immunity falls

### CMV in Immunocompetent Patients
- Usually asymptomatic
- Can cause a mononucleosis-like syndrome — fever, fatigue, lymphadenopathy, hepatitis
- Distinguished from EBV mononucleosis by negative monospot test (heterophile antibody negative)

### Congenital CMV — Highest Yield

Congenital CMV is the most common congenital viral infection worldwide.

**Transmission:**
- Transplacental — primary maternal infection during pregnancy most dangerous
- Risk of transmission: 30–40% if primary infection in pregnancy
- Secondary infection (reactivation) can also transmit but causes less severe disease

**Clinical features — the congenitally infected neonate:**
- 90% asymptomatic at birth — but 10–15% develop late sequelae
- 10% symptomatic at birth:
  - **Microcephaly** — small head; most serious sequela
  - **Periventricular calcifications** — on CT scan; pathognomonic
  - Hepatosplenomegaly
  - Jaundice
  - Petechiae and purpura — blueberry muffin appearance
  - Chorioretinitis — can cause blindness
  - Sensorineural hearing loss — most common long-term sequel
  - Mental retardation
  - Seizures

**Diagnosis of congenital CMV:**
- **Urine culture or PCR** — within first 3 weeks of life; virus shed in urine
- Blood PCR — detects CMV DNA
- Serology — IgM in neonate (IgM does not cross placenta — presence confirms active neonatal infection)

**Treatment:**
- Ganciclovir (IV) or Valganciclovir (oral prodrug)
- Given to symptomatic neonates — reduces hearing loss progression
- Mechanism: Ganciclovir inhibits CMV DNA polymerase

### CMV in Immunocompromised Patients

In transplant recipients and HIV/AIDS patients, CMV causes severe disease:
- **CMV retinitis** — most common in AIDS; causes progressive blindness; treated with ganciclovir
- **CMV pneumonitis** — severe, life-threatening pneumonia in bone marrow transplant patients
- **CMV colitis** — bloody diarrhea, abdominal pain
- **CMV encephalitis** — in advanced AIDS
- **CMV hepatitis**

**Treatment of CMV disease:**
- **Ganciclovir** (IV) — first-line
- **Valganciclovir** (oral) — prodrug; used for maintenance
- **Foscarnet** — used when resistance to ganciclovir
- **Cidofovir** — second-line

### Diagnosis
- **Owl-eye inclusions** — histology of infected tissue
- **PCR** — most sensitive; detects CMV DNA in blood, urine, CSF
- **Shell vial culture** — rapid culture method
- **pp65 antigenemia** — detects CMV antigen in leukocytes; used to monitor transplant patients

## 3. Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV)

### Classification
- **Family:** Herpesviridae
- **Subfamily:** Gammaherpesvirinae
- **Genome:** Double-stranded DNA
- **Enveloped virus**

### Transmission
- Saliva — primary route; kissing disease
- Blood transfusion — rare
- Organ transplantation

### Target Cells
- **B lymphocytes** — primary target; EBV binds CD21 (complement receptor 2)
- Epithelial cells of oropharynx and nasopharynx
- Latency in B lymphocytes — lifelong

### Infectious Mononucleosis (Glandular Fever)

**Pathogenesis:**
- EBV infects oropharyngeal epithelial cells → spreads to B lymphocytes in tonsillar tissue
- B cells become immortalized and proliferate massively
- CD8+ cytotoxic T cells respond aggressively to destroy infected B cells
- The reactive T cells are the atypical lymphocytes (Downey cells) seen on blood film
- Clinical disease is largely immune-mediated — the T cell response causes symptoms

**Clinical features:**
- Affects adolescents and young adults predominantly
- **Triad:** fever, pharyngitis (exudative tonsillitis), lymphadenopathy (especially posterior cervical)
- Splenomegaly — in 50%; risk of splenic rupture — avoid contact sports
- Hepatomegaly and mild hepatitis
- Periorbital oedema
- Maculopapular rash — especially if ampicillin/amoxicillin given — characteristic drug reaction
- Fatigue — can persist for weeks to months

**Diagnosis:**
- **Monospot test (Paul-Bunnell test)** — detects heterophile antibodies; positive in 85–90% of cases
- **Blood film** — atypical lymphocytes (large, activated CD8+ T cells)
- **EBV-specific serology** — VCA IgM (acute infection), VCA IgG (past infection), EA antibodies (active replication), EBNA antibodies (appear late, confirm past infection)
- **PCR** — detects EBV DNA; used in immunocompromised
- **FBC** — lymphocytosis with atypical lymphocytes

**Treatment:**
- Supportive — rest, analgesia, adequate hydration
- Avoid ampicillin/amoxicillin — causes rash in EBV infection
- Avoid contact sports until splenomegaly resolves — risk of splenic rupture
- Steroids — for severe airway obstruction from tonsillar enlargement
- No specific antiviral — acyclovir has limited benefit

### EBV and Oncogenesis — High Yield

EBV is one of the most important oncogenic viruses. It causes malignancy through:
- B cell immortalization via latent gene expression
- Integration of viral DNA near oncogenes
- Immunosuppression in host allows uncontrolled B cell proliferation

**EBV-associated malignancies:**

| Malignancy | Key Facts |
|---|---|
| Burkitt's lymphoma | B cell lymphoma; jaw mass in African children; t(8;14) translocation — c-myc oncogene activation; endemic in malaria belt of Africa |
| Nasopharyngeal carcinoma | Common in Southern China and Southeast Asia; EBV in virtually all cases |
| Hodgkin's lymphoma | EBV in 40–50% of cases; Reed-Sternberg cells contain EBV |
| Post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder (PTLD) | Uncontrolled EBV-driven B cell proliferation in immunosuppressed transplant recipients |
| Primary CNS lymphoma | In AIDS patients with very low CD4 counts |

## Part 4: Adenovirus and Enteroviruses

## 1. Introduction

Adenoviruses and enteroviruses are both non-enveloped viruses that cause a wide range of clinical diseases. Adenoviruses are DNA viruses causing respiratory, ocular, and gastrointestinal infections. Enteroviruses are RNA viruses that enter through the gut but cause systemic disease including meningitis, encephalitis, myocarditis, and paralysis. Both groups have appeared in MKU essay questions — adenovirus as a full essay on transmission, diseases, and prevention, and enteroviruses as essay questions on portal of entry, diseases caused, and viral encephalitis.

## 2. Adenovirus

### Classification and Structure
- **Family:** Adenoviridae
- **Genome:** Double-stranded DNA
- **Non-enveloped** — this is clinically important
- **Shape:** Icosahedral capsid with characteristic **fiber proteins** projecting from each vertex — these are the attachment proteins (bind host cell receptors)
- **Stable in environment** — non-enveloped viruses resist drying, heat, detergents, and disinfectants — this explains their ability to spread via fomites and water

### Transmission — High Yield

Adenoviruses are transmitted by multiple routes:

- **Respiratory droplets** — sneezing, coughing; causes respiratory infections
- **Fecal-oral route** — contaminated food and water; causes gastroenteritis
- **Direct contact** — hand-to-eye; causes conjunctivitis
- **Swimming pools (waterborne)** — epidemic keratoconjunctivitis from contaminated pool water
- **Fomites** — virus survives on surfaces for extended periods due to non-enveloped structure
- **Blood transfusion and organ transplantation** — in immunocompromised patients

### Diseases Caused — High Yield

| Disease | Notes |
|---|---|
| Acute febrile pharyngitis | Most common; fever, sore throat, cervical lymphadenopathy; types 1–5, 7 |
| Pharyngoconjunctival fever | Fever, pharyngitis, follicular conjunctivitis; associated with swimming pools; types 3, 7 |
| Epidemic keratoconjunctivitis | Severe eye infection; corneal infiltrates; highly contagious; types 8, 19, 37 |
| Acute respiratory disease (ARD) | Pneumonia, bronchitis; types 4, 7; especially in military recruits |
| Gastroenteritis | Diarrhea in children; types 40, 41 (enteric adenoviruses) |
| Haemorrhagic cystitis | Bloody urine; types 11, 21; especially in immunocompromised |
| Disseminated infection | Hepatitis, pneumonia, encephalitis; in immunocompromised and neonates |

### Prevention

- **Handwashing** — most important general measure
- **Proper chlorination of swimming pools** — prevents waterborne spread
- **Avoid sharing towels, eye drops** — prevents conjunctivitis spread
- **Live oral adenovirus vaccine** — types 4 and 7; used in military recruits in some countries; not widely available
- **Isolation of infected patients** — in healthcare settings
- **Adequate water treatment and sanitation** — prevents fecal-oral transmission

### Diagnosis
- **PCR** — most sensitive; detects adenovirus DNA in respiratory secretions, stool, blood, urine
- **Viral culture** — from respiratory secretions, conjunctival swab, stool
- **Antigen detection** — rapid tests for stool adenovirus (types 40/41)
- **Serology** — four-fold rise in antibody titre

### Treatment

- No specific antiviral approved for adenovirus
- Supportive treatment
- **Cidofovir** — used in severe adenovirus disease in immunocompromised patients

## 3. Enteroviruses

### Classification and Structure
- **Family:** Picornaviridae
- **Genus:** Enterovirus
- **Genome:** Single-stranded, positive-sense RNA
- **Non-enveloped** — stable in acidic environment of stomach; survives GIT passage
- Small icosahedral viruses

### Members of the Enterovirus Genus — High Yield

| Virus | Serotypes | Key Disease |
|---|---|---|
| Poliovirus | Types 1, 2, 3 | Poliomyelitis — flaccid paralysis |
| Coxsackievirus A | 23 serotypes | Herpangina, hand-foot-and-mouth disease, aseptic meningitis |
| Coxsackievirus B | 6 serotypes | Myocarditis, pericarditis, Bornholm disease (pleurodynia), aseptic meningitis |
| Echovirus | 28 serotypes | Aseptic meningitis, rash, respiratory illness |
| Enterovirus 71 (EV71) | — | Hand-foot-and-mouth disease, encephalitis |
| Enterovirus D68 (EV-D68) | — | Acute flaccid myelitis, respiratory illness |

### Portal of Entry — High Yield

The portal of entry for all enteroviruses is the **gastrointestinal tract** — specifically the oropharynx and small intestine.

**Route:**
1. Virus ingested in contaminated food or water (fecal-oral transmission)
2. Survives stomach acid — non-enveloped capsid is acid-resistant
3. Infects epithelial cells and lymphoid tissue (Peyer's patches) of small intestine
4. Primary replication in gut
5. Spreads to regional lymph nodes
6. Viremia → target organs (CNS, heart, skin, muscle)

### Transmission
- **Fecal-oral route** — primary route; contaminated water and food
- **Respiratory droplets** — for some serotypes causing pharyngitis
- **Direct contact** — hand-to-mouth

### Diseases Caused — High Yield

**Poliovirus:**
- **Subclinical infection** — 95% of infections
- **Abortive poliomyelitis** — 4–8%; non-specific febrile illness; no neurological involvement
- **Non-paralytic poliomyelitis (aseptic meningitis)** — 1–2%; meningeal irritation without paralysis
- **Paralytic poliomyelitis** — less than 1%; destruction of anterior horn motor neurons → flaccid paralysis
  - Bulbar poliomyelitis — involves brainstem; respiratory paralysis; most fatal form

**Coxsackievirus A:**
- **Herpangina** — vesicular lesions on soft palate and uvula; high fever in children
- **Hand-foot-and-mouth disease** — vesicular lesions on hands, feet, and oral mucosa; caused by CA16 and EV71
- Aseptic meningitis

**Coxsackievirus B:**
- **Myocarditis** — inflammation of heart muscle; can cause dilated cardiomyopathy
- **Pericarditis** — inflammation of pericardium
- **Bornholm disease (epidemic pleurodynia)** — severe pleuritic chest pain and myalgia; devil's grip
- Aseptic meningitis, neonatal sepsis

**Echoviruses:**
- Aseptic meningitis — most common cause of viral meningitis
- Febrile rash illness
- Respiratory illness

### Viral Encephalitis — High Yield

**Definition:** Inflammation of the brain parenchyma caused by viral infection, as distinct from meningitis (inflammation of meninges only).

**Enteroviruses that cause encephalitis:**
- Enterovirus 71 (EV71) — most important; causes brainstem encephalitis in children
- Coxsackievirus A and B
- Echoviruses
- Poliovirus — encephalitis rare; paralysis more common

**Other important causes of viral encephalitis (non-enterovirus):**
- **HSV-1** — most common cause of fatal sporadic encephalitis
- **Rabies virus**
- **Japanese encephalitis virus** (arbovirus)
- **West Nile virus** (arbovirus)
- **CMV** — in immunocompromised
- **Measles** — post-infectious encephalitis
- **VZV** — in elderly and immunocompromised

**Clinical features of viral encephalitis:**
- Fever
- Headache
- Altered consciousness — confusion, disorientation
- Seizures
- Focal neurological deficits
- Behavioural changes

**Diagnosis:**
- **MRI brain** — gold standard imaging; shows temporal lobe involvement in HSV
- **CSF analysis** (lumbar puncture):
  - Lymphocytic pleocytosis
  - Normal or mildly elevated protein
  - Normal glucose
- **CSF PCR** — gold standard for diagnosis; identifies specific virus
- **EEG** — temporal lobe changes in HSV encephalitis

**Treatment:**
- **IV Acyclovir** — started empirically for all encephalitis until HSV excluded
- Supportive — anticonvulsants, management of raised ICP, ICU care
- No specific treatment for most enterovirus encephalitis — supportive only

### Diagnosis of Enteroviruses
- **PCR** — gold standard; detects enterovirus RNA in CSF, stool, throat swab, blood
- **Viral culture** — from stool, throat, CSF
- **Serology** — less useful due to many serotypes

### Treatment
- No specific antiviral for most enteroviruses
- Supportive treatment
- **Pleconaril** — an experimental capsid inhibitor; has been used for severe enterovirus disease in some settings
- **IVIG** — intravenous immunoglobulin for severe neonatal enterovirus sepsis and enterovirus in immunocompromised

### Prevention
- **Handwashing** — most important
- **Clean water and sanitation** — prevents fecal-oral spread
- **Poliovirus vaccines** — OPV and IPV (as detailed in Part 1)
- No vaccine available for other enteroviruses except EV71 vaccine (licensed in China)
