## Focal Segmental Glomerulosclerosis & HIV-Associated Nephropathy

### Focal Segmental Glomerulosclerosis (FSGS)
**Definition:** Sclerosis affecting some but not all glomeruli (focal) and within affected glomeruli, only a portion of the capillary tuft is involved (segmental).
**Epidemiology:**
- Most common cause of nephrotic syndrome in adults overall
- Most common cause of nephrotic syndrome in Black adults
- Can occur at any age
**Classification — Settings in Which FSGS Occurs:**
| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| Primary (idiopathic) | Most common; cause unknown |
| Secondary — associated conditions | HIV, heroin addiction, sickle cell disease, morbid obesity |
| Secondary — scarring of prior lesions | IgA nephropathy, focal GN |
| Adaptive response to nephron loss | Unilateral renal agenesis, reflux nephropathy, hypertensive nephropathy |
| Inherited forms | Mutations in podocin (NPHS2), α-actinin 4, TRPC6 |
**Pathogenesis:**
- Considered a **podocytopathy** — primary injury directed at podocytes
- Mechanisms include:
  - Inherited mutations in structural podocyte genes (podocin, α-actinin 4, TRPC6) → disruption of glomerular filtration barrier
  - Circulating permeability factors (cytokines) — autoantibodies found in serum include antinephrin, antiannexin A2, anti-UCHL1
  - Mechanical stress — hypertension and obesity cause physical stress on podocytes
  - Viral injury (HIV, parvovirus B19) — direct podocyte infection
  - Drug-induced (pamidronate)
- These mechanisms → progressive podocyte loss → podocytopenia → increased unselective filtration through bare GBM → collapse and occlusion of capillary loops → adhesion to Bowman's capsule → segmental sclerosis
- Link with Minimal Change Disease: MCD and primary FSGS may represent a spectrum of the same podocyte injury — same circulating autoantibodies found in both
**Morphology:**
Light microscopy:
- Focal and segmental lesions — may involve only a minority of glomeruli; biopsy must contain sufficient glomeruli or lesions are missed
- Sclerotic segments show: collapse of capillary loops, increased matrix, segmental hyalinosis (plasma protein deposition along capillary wall)
- Lipid droplets and foam cells often present
- Non-sclerotic glomeruli may show increased mesangial matrix
- With progression: more glomeruli involved → global sclerosis → tubular atrophy and interstitial fibrosis
Immunofluorescence:
- IgM and C3 in sclerotic areas and/or mesangium
Electron microscopy:
- Diffuse effacement of foot processes in both sclerotic and non-sclerotic areas
- Focal detachment of epithelial cells and denudation of underlying GBM
**Collapsing Glomerulopathy — Morphologic Variant:**
- Retraction and/or collapse of the entire glomerular tuft
- Proliferation and hypertrophy of glomerular visceral epithelial cells — characteristic feature
- Associated with: HIV-associated nephropathy, pamidronate toxicity, idiopathic
- Accompanied by prominent tubular injury — microcyst formation, interstitial inflammation
- Poor prognosis
**Clinical Features:**
- Nephrotic syndrome or non-nephrotic proteinuria (acute or subacute onset)
- Hypertension, microscopic haematuria, azotemia commonly present at diagnosis
- Little tendency for spontaneous remission in idiopathic FSGS
- Variable response to corticosteroids
- Children have better prognosis than adults
**Prognosis:**
- About 20% follow a rapid course — intractable proteinuria → renal failure within 2 years
- Factors associated with rapid progression: degree of proteinuria, renal insufficiency at diagnosis, collapsing histologic variant
- Tip variant has relatively good prognosis
- Recurrence after transplantation: 25–50%

### HIV-Associated Nephropathy
**Definition:** A severe form of the collapsing variant of FSGS occurring in HIV-infected individuals.
**Epidemiology:**
- Reported in 5–10% of HIV-infected individuals in older series
- More frequent in Black patients than White patients — linked to APOL1 risk alleles
- Incidence significantly reduced with antiretroviral therapy (ART)
**Pathogenesis:**
- Not fully understood
- Primarily linked to G1/G2 risk alleles of **APOL1** gene — explains racial disparity
- HIV can directly infect tubular epithelial cells and podocytes
- Direct viral injury → podocyte damage → collapsing FSGS pattern
**Morphology — Three Distinguishing Features:**
1. High frequency of collapsing variant of FSGS
2. Striking focal cystic dilation of tubule segments filled with proteinaceous material + interstitial inflammation and fibrosis
3. Large numbers of **tubuloreticular inclusions** within endothelial cells on electron microscopy
   - Also present in SLE
   - Represent modifications of endoplasmic reticulum induced by circulating interferon-α
   - Not present in idiopathic FSGS — therefore have diagnostic value on biopsy
**Clinical Features:**
- Heavy proteinuria, often nephrotic range
- Rapid progression to renal failure
- Kidneys may be enlarged on imaging (unlike most chronic kidney diseases)
- Blood pressure often normal despite advanced disease
**Management:**
- Antiretroviral therapy is the cornerstone — can stabilize or improve renal function
- ACE inhibitors/ARBs for proteinuria reduction

## Section 6: MPGN, Dense Deposit Disease, Fibrillary GN, IgA Nephropathy, Alport Syndrome & Thin Basement Membrane Nephropathy

### Membranoproliferative Glomerulonephritis (MPGN)
**Definition:** A pattern of immune-mediated glomerular injury — not a specific disease — characterized by both mesangial proliferation and basement membrane thickening/duplication.
**Classification:**
| Type | Mechanism | Complement | Immunofluorescence |
|---|---|---|---|
| MPGN Type I | Immune complex deposition (IgG + complement) | Low C3, low C1q, low C4 | IgG, C3, C1q, C4 granular subendothelial |
| Dense Deposit Disease (formerly Type II) | Alternative complement pathway dysregulation | Low C3, normal C1 and C4 | C3 only, no IgG |
**MPGN Type I:**
- Caused by deposition of circulating immune complexes in subendothelial locations
- Associated with: hepatitis C (most common in adults), hepatitis B, SLE, cryoglobulinemia, chronic infections
- Complement: both classical and alternative pathways activated — C3, C1q, C4 all low
Light microscopy:
- Mesangial and endocapillary proliferation
- GBM thickening and splitting — "tram-track" or "double contour" appearance on silver stain
- Due to mesangial cell interposition between endothelium and GBM
Electron microscopy:
- Subendothelial electron-dense deposits
**Clinical Presentation (both types):**
- Nephritic/nephrotic syndrome — overlap of both
- Haematuria, proteinuria, hypertension, azotemia
- Low serum complement levels

### Dense Deposit Disease (MPGN Type II / C3 Glomerulopathy)
**Pathogenesis:**
- Acquired or genetic dysregulation of the **alternative complement pathway**
- Most cases: **C3 nephritic factor (C3NeF)** — autoantibody that stabilizes alternative pathway C3 convertase → uncontrolled C3 consumption
- Result: markedly low serum C3; normal C1 and C4
- Decreased Factor B and properdin
- Glomerular deposits contain C3 and properdin but not IgG
**Morphology:**
Light microscopy:
- Mesangial proliferative or membranoproliferative pattern
- GBM thickening and splitting
Immunofluorescence:
- C3 only — no IgG, no C1q, no C4 (distinguishes from MPGN type I)
Electron microscopy:
- **Dense, ribbon-like, osmiophilic deposits within the GBM lamina densa** — highly characteristic and diagnostic
- Also in mesangium and tubular basement membranes
**Clinical Features:**
- Haematuria
- Chronic renal failure
- Associated with **partial lipodystrophy** — loss of subcutaneous fat from upper body (C3NeF activates complement in adipocytes)
- Associated with **drusen** — retinal deposits causing visual disturbance
**Prognosis:**
- Poor — most progress to ESRD within 10 years
- High recurrence rate after transplantation (due to circulating C3NeF)

### Fibrillary Glomerulonephritis
**Definition:** A rare glomerular disease characterized by fibrillary deposits in the mesangium and glomerular capillary walls that resemble amyloid but are distinct.
**Key distinguishing features from amyloid:**
- Do not stain with **Congo red** (amyloid does)
- Larger fibrils than amyloid on electron microscopy (~20 nm vs ~10 nm for amyloid)
- Random fibrillar arrangement similar to amyloid but ultrastructurally different
**Pathogenesis:**
- Unknown
- **DNAJB9** (a co-chaperone for heat shock protein 70) — recently identified as a highly sensitive and specific marker for fibrillary GN; found in the deposits
**Morphology:**
Light microscopy:
- Mesangioproliferative or membranoproliferative pattern
Immunofluorescence:
- Selective deposition of polyclonal IgG (predominantly IgG4), complement C3, Igκ and Igλ light chains
Electron microscopy:
- Randomly arranged fibrils (~20 nm diameter) in mesangium and capillary walls
**Clinical Features:**
- Nephrotic syndrome
- Haematuria
- Progressive renal insufficiency
- Recurs in kidney transplants

### IgA Nephropathy (Berger Disease)
**Definition:** Glomerulonephritis characterized by mesangial deposits of IgA-containing immune complexes.
**Epidemiology:**
- Most common cause of glomerulonephritis worldwide
- Common cause of recurrent haematuria in young adults
- More common in Asia and Southern Europe
**Pathogenesis:**
- Elevated circulating levels of **galactose-deficient IgA1** (Gd-IgA1)
- Gd-IgA1 recognized as abnormal → autoantibodies (IgG or IgA) form against it → immune complexes
- Immune complexes deposit in the mesangium → complement activation → mesangial cell proliferation → injury
- Often triggered or exacerbated by mucosal infections (upper respiratory or GI) — explains synpharyngitic haematuria (haematuria occurring simultaneously with infection, not weeks later as in postinfectious GN)
**Morphology:**
Light microscopy:
- Focal mesangial proliferative glomerulonephritis
- Mesangial widening
Immunofluorescence:
- IgA (dominant) ± IgG, IgM, and C3 in the mesangium — pathognomonic
Electron microscopy:
- Mesangial and paramesangial electron-dense deposits
**Clinical Features:**
- Recurrent episodes of macroscopic haematuria coinciding with upper respiratory infections (synpharyngitic)
- Persistent microscopic haematuria between episodes
- Variable proteinuria — can reach nephrotic range
- Hypertension in some patients
- Can present with nephritic syndrome
**Prognosis:**
- Variable — slowly progressive in many patients
- 20–30% reach ESRD over 20 years
- Poor prognostic factors: heavy proteinuria, hypertension, renal insufficiency at presentation, crescents on biopsy
- Recurs after transplantation
**Relationship to Henoch-Schönlein Purpura:**
- IgA nephropathy and HSP are considered manifestations of the same disease — both have mesangial IgA deposits; HSP additionally has systemic vasculitis

### Alport Syndrome
**Definition:** A hereditary nephritis caused by mutations in genes encoding **type IV collagen** of the GBM.
**Genetics:**
- X-linked (most common, ~80%) — mutations in **COL4A5** gene
- Autosomal recessive — mutations in **COL4A3** or **COL4A4**
- Autosomal dominant — mutations in COL4A3 or COL4A4 (milder disease)
**Pathogenesis:**
- Type IV collagen is a major structural component of basement membranes in the kidney, cochlea, and eye
- Mutations → structurally abnormal GBM → progressive glomerular damage
**Clinical Features:**
- Haematuria — persistent microscopic haematuria; episodic macroscopic haematuria
- Slowly progressive proteinuria
- Declining renal function → ESRD (typically by 3rd–4th decade in X-linked males)
- **Sensorineural deafness** — due to cochlear basement membrane defect
- **Ocular abnormalities** — anterior lenticonus (lens protrudes into anterior chamber), macular flecks
**Morphology:**
Light microscopy:
- Normal until late in the disease course
- Eventually: focal and segmental glomerulosclerosis, tubular atrophy, interstitial fibrosis
- Foam cells in interstitium
Electron microscopy:
- **Irregular thickening, thinning, and splitting (lamellation) of the GBM lamina densa** — "basket-weave" appearance — diagnostic
Immunofluorescence:
- Absence of α3, α4, α5 chains of type IV collagen staining on biopsy — useful diagnostic tool
**Management:**
- No cure — ACE inhibitors slow progression
- Kidney transplantation: risk of developing anti-GBM nephritis post-transplant (host immune system recognizes normal donor GBM type IV collagen as foreign)

### Thin Basement Membrane Nephropathy (Benign Familial Haematuria)
**Definition:** A benign hereditary condition characterized by persistent microscopic haematuria due to thinning of the GBM.
**Genetics:**
- Mutations in **COL4A3** or **COL4A4** — heterozygous mutations (homozygous mutations cause Alport syndrome)
- Part of the same disease spectrum as Alport syndrome
**Morphology:**
Light microscopy: Normal
Immunofluorescence: Normal
Electron microscopy:
- **Diffuse, uniform thinning of the GBM** (normal GBM thickness: 300–400 nm; in TBMN: less than 150–200 nm) — diagnostic
**Clinical Features:**
- Persistent microscopic haematuria — often discovered incidentally
- No significant proteinuria
- Normal renal function
- Benign course — no progression to renal failure in most cases
- Family history of haematuria (autosomal dominant inheritance)
**Distinguishing from IgA Nephropathy and Alport Syndrome:**
| Feature | Thin BM Nephropathy | IgA Nephropathy | Alport Syndrome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Haematuria | Microscopic, persistent | Synpharyngitic, recurrent macro | Microscopic + episodic macro |
| Proteinuria | Absent/minimal | Variable | Progressive |
| GBM on EM | Uniformly thin | Normal or mild changes | Thick, irregular, basket-weave |
| IF | Normal | IgA mesangial deposits | Absent α3/α4/α5 collagen |
| Prognosis | Benign | Variable | Progressive to ESRD |
| Deafness/eye | Absent | Absent | Present |

## Section 7: Glomerular Lesions in Systemic Diseases

### Lupus Nephritis
**Definition:** Renal involvement in SLE — one of the most serious complications of the disease, occurring in up to 50–60% of SLE patients.
**Pathogenesis:**
- Immune complex deposition — DNA-anti-DNA complexes deposited in the glomerulus
- Both in situ formation and circulating complex trapping
- Complement activation → inflammatory injury
- "Full house" immunofluorescence pattern — IgG, IgA, IgM, C3, C1q all positive — highly characteristic of lupus nephritis
**ISN/RPS Classification (International Society of Nephrology):**
| Class | Description | Clinical Significance |
|---|---|---|
| I | Minimal mesangial lupus nephritis | Normal light microscopy; mesangial deposits on IF/EM |
| II | Mesangial proliferative | Mild haematuria/proteinuria; good prognosis |
| III | Focal lupus nephritis | Less than 50% glomeruli; active or chronic lesions |
| IV | Diffuse lupus nephritis | More than 50% glomeruli; most severe; RPGN possible |
| V | Membranous lupus nephritis | Nephrotic syndrome; similar to idiopathic membranous nephropathy |
| VI | Advanced sclerosing lupus nephritis | More than 90% global sclerosis; represents end-stage disease |
**Class IV** is the most severe and most common clinically significant form.
**Wire loop lesions:** Thickened, rigid-appearing capillary walls due to massive subendothelial immune complex deposits — characteristic of Class IV lupus nephritis on light microscopy.
**Clinical Features:**
- Recurrent microscopic or gross haematuria
- Nephritic syndrome
- RPGN
- Nephrotic syndrome
- Acute and chronic renal failure
- Hypertension
- Low complement (C3, C4, CH50) — correlates with disease activity
- Elevated anti-dsDNA antibodies — correlates with disease activity
**Management:**
- Class I–II: no specific renal treatment
- Class III–IV: mycophenolate mofetil or cyclophosphamide + corticosteroids
- Class V: as per membranous nephropathy
- Hydroxychloroquine for all lupus patients — renoprotective

### Henoch-Schönlein Purpura (IgA Vasculitis)
**Definition:** A systemic small vessel vasculitis mediated by IgA immune complexes, presenting as a tetrad of purpura, arthralgia, abdominal pain, and renal disease.
**Epidemiology:**
- Most common systemic vasculitis in children
- Peak age: 3–8 years
- Also occurs in adults — renal manifestations more severe in adults
- Often follows upper respiratory infection
- Strong background of atopy in about one-third of patients
**Pathogenesis:**
- Same as IgA nephropathy — elevated galactose-deficient IgA1 → immune complexes → IgA deposition in mesangium and small vessel walls
- IgA and C3 deposits in glomeruli and skin vessels
- Immune complex–mediated small vessel vasculitis
**Clinical Features — The Tetrad:**
- **Purpura** — palpable, non-thrombocytopenic; characteristically on extensor surfaces of arms, legs, and buttocks
- **Arthralgias** — joint pain, usually large joints; non-destructive
- **Abdominal pain** — colicky; may have intestinal bleeding, vomiting; intussusception is a complication in children
- **Renal disease** — occurs in one-third of patients:
  - Microscopic or gross haematuria
  - Proteinuria
  - Nephritic or nephrotic syndrome
  - A small number (mostly adults) develop RPGN with crescents
**Not all components need to be present for diagnosis.**
**Morphology:**
Kidney:
- Histologic lesions vary: mild focal mesangial proliferation → diffuse mesangial/endocapillary proliferation → crescentic GN
- Immunofluorescence: IgA (dominant) ± IgG and C3 in the mesangium, sometimes extending to capillary loops — identical to IgA nephropathy
Skin:
- Subepidermal haemorrhages
- Necrotizing vasculitis of small dermal vessels
- IgA, IgG, and C3 deposits in vessel walls
**Prognosis:**
- Most children: excellent — full recovery
- Haematuria may recur for years
- Patients with diffuse lesions, crescents, or nephrotic syndrome: poorer prognosis
- Adults generally have more severe and persistent renal disease

### Diabetic Nephropathy
**Definition:** Glomerular and tubulointerstitial damage resulting from diabetes mellitus — the leading cause of chronic kidney disease and ESRD in the developed world.
**Epidemiology:**
- Occurs in up to 40% of both Type 1 and Type 2 diabetics
- Risk increases with duration of diabetes and poor glycaemic control
**Pathogenesis — Multiple Mechanisms:**
- **Hyperglycaemia** → non-enzymatic glycation of GBM proteins → structural GBM changes
- **Advanced glycation end products (AGEs)** → cross-link collagen → thickened, less permeable GBM
- **Haemodynamic changes:** hyperglycaemia → glomerular hyperfiltration and hypertension → glomerular hypertrophy → eventually glomerulosclerosis
- **Activation of RAAS** — angiotensin II promotes mesangial matrix expansion and fibrosis
- **PKC (protein kinase C) activation** → increased VEGF → increased vascular permeability → proteinuria
- Growth factors (TGF-β) → increased matrix production → sclerosis
**Morphology:**
Light microscopy:
- **GBM thickening** — earliest finding
- **Diffuse glomerulosclerosis** — diffuse increase in mesangial matrix; most common lesion
- **Nodular glomerulosclerosis (Kimmelstiel-Wilson lesion)** — ovoid/spherical deposits of laminated matrix in the periphery of the glomerulus, often with peripheral capillaries compressed — **pathognomonic of diabetic nephropathy**
- **Exudative lesions** — hyaline caps (accumulation of plasma proteins in capillary lumens) and capsular drops
- Hyalinosis of afferent AND efferent arterioles — hyalinosis of efferent arteriole is virtually pathognomonic of diabetes
- Tubular basement membrane thickening
- Interstitial fibrosis in advanced disease
Immunofluorescence:
- Linear IgG and albumin along GBM — nonspecific; due to trapping, not immune complex deposition
Electron microscopy:
- GBM thickening
- Mesangial matrix expansion
- No immune deposits
**Clinical Staging (Mogensen Stages for Type 1 DM):**
| Stage | Feature |
|---|---|
| I | Hyperfiltration — increased GFR |
| II | Silent — normal albumin excretion |
| III | Microalbuminuria (30–300 mg/day) — incipient nephropathy |
| IV | Macroproteinuria (>300 mg/day) — clinical nephropathy; GFR declining |
| V | ESRD |
**Microalbuminuria** is the earliest clinical marker of diabetic nephropathy — not detectable on routine dipstick.
**Clinical Features:**
- Proteinuria — initially microalbuminuria, progressing to nephrotic range
- Hypertension
- Progressive decline in GFR
- Retinopathy almost always present when nephropathy is present in Type 1 DM — absence of retinopathy in a diabetic with heavy proteinuria should prompt biopsy to exclude other diagnoses
- Anaemia (due to reduced erythropoietin even before ESRD)
**Management:**
- Strict glycaemic control — slows progression
- ACE inhibitors/ARBs — reduce proteinuria and slow progression (first-line regardless of blood pressure)
- SGLT2 inhibitors — renoprotective independent of glycaemic effects
- Blood pressure control
- Dialysis or transplantation for ESRD

### Goodpasture Syndrome
**Definition:** An autoimmune disease characterized by the triad of:
- Rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis
- Pulmonary haemorrhage
- Anti-GBM antibodies
**Pathogenesis:**
- Autoantibodies directed against the **NC1 domain of the α3 chain of type IV collagen** — present in GBM and alveolar basement membrane
- Triggers include: pulmonary infection, smoking, hydrocarbon solvent exposure — these may expose cryptic GBM antigens
- Antibodies bind GBM → linear IgG pattern on IF → complement activation + neutrophil recruitment → necrotizing GN + pulmonary haemorrhage
**Clinical Features:**
- Haemoptysis (can be massive and life-threatening)
- Haematuria, red cell casts
- Rapidly progressive renal failure
- Anti-GBM antibodies detectable in serum — diagnostic
- c-ANCA may also be positive in some cases (double-positive disease — worse prognosis)
**Morphology:**
- Necrotizing crescentic GN
- Linear IgG and C3 along GBM on immunofluorescence — diagnostic
- Pulmonary: alveolar haemorrhage with haemosiderin-laden macrophages
**Treatment:**
- Plasmapheresis — to remove circulating anti-GBM antibodies (urgent)
- High-dose corticosteroids + cyclophosphamide
- Early treatment essential — dialysis-dependent patients at presentation rarely recover renal function

### Microscopic Polyangiitis & Granulomatosis with Polyangiitis
**Shared Features:**
- Both are ANCA-associated vasculitides causing pauci-immune crescentic GN (Type III RPGN)
- Glomerular lesions: focal and segmental necrotizing GN → crescentic GN in severe cases
- Immunofluorescence: negative or minimal (pauci-immune)
- No significant immune complex deposition
| Feature | Microscopic Polyangiitis | Granulomatosis with Polyangiitis |
|---|---|---|
| ANCA type | p-ANCA (anti-MPO) | c-ANCA (anti-PR3) |
| Granulomas | Absent | Present (lungs, upper airways) |
| Upper airway involvement | Absent | Sinusitis, epistaxis, saddle-nose deformity |
| Pulmonary involvement | Pulmonary haemorrhage | Pulmonary nodules/cavities |
| Renal involvement | Crescentic GN | Crescentic GN |

### Essential Mixed Cryoglobulinemia
**Definition:** Systemic deposition of cryoglobulins (IgG-IgM complexes that precipitate in cold) causing vasculitis and glomerulonephritis.
**Association:**
- Strongly associated with **hepatitis C virus** infection — most cases
- Hepatitis C → chronic antigenaemia → IgM rheumatoid factor production → IgG-IgM cryoglobulin complexes
**Clinical Features:**
- Purpura (skin)
- Arthralgias
- Peripheral neuropathy
- Proliferative glomerulonephritis — typically MPGN type I pattern
**Morphology:**
- MPGN type I pattern on light microscopy
- Intraluminal "thrombi" — precipitated cryoglobulins within capillary lumens
- IgG, IgM, C3 deposits on immunofluorescence
**Management:**
- Treat underlying hepatitis C — direct-acting antivirals
- Rituximab for severe cases
