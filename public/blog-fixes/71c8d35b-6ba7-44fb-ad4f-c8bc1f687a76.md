# Rheumatic Fever and Rheumatic Heart Disease

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Cardiology**

> **In one line:** acute rheumatic fever (ARF) is an autoimmune reaction, 2 to 4 weeks after group A streptococcal pharyngitis, in which antibodies against streptococcal antigens cross-react with heart, joint, brain and skin tissue (molecular mimicry). Repeated attacks scar the valves, producing chronic rheumatic heart disease (RHD), mainly mitral stenosis. Diagnose ARF with the Jones criteria, treat with salicylates and penicillin, and prevent recurrence with long-term penicillin prophylaxis.

## What you must know

- RHD is the **commonest cardiovascular disease in the young** worldwide, a disease of poverty and crowding, and the leading cause of cardiovascular death in the first five decades in poor countries, **including Kenya and the region**.
- ARF follows **group A streptococcal pharyngitis** (not skin infection in classical teaching) by **2 to 4 weeks**; the cardiac damage appears years later (20 to 30 years).
- **Jones criteria:** 2 major, or 1 major and 2 minor, **plus evidence of recent streptococcal infection**.
- **Major:** carditis, arthritis (migratory polyarthritis), Sydenham chorea, subcutaneous nodules, erythema marginatum. **Echocardiography** is now used to detect subclinical carditis.
- Carditis is the **only manifestation that leaves permanent damage**; **mitral valve** is affected in over 90% of RHD.
- Treatment: bed rest, **aspirin**, **penicillin**, steroids in severe carditis, heart failure treatment. **Secondary prophylaxis** with benzathine penicillin is the key preventive measure.
- Long-term prophylaxis prevents ARF recurrence but **does not prevent infective endocarditis**.

## 1. Epidemiology

- Globally RHD is the most common cardiovascular disease in young people (under 25 years).
- ARF incidence is **5 to 51 per 100,000** (mean 19 per 100,000). In children aged 5 to 14 years it is **0.8 to 5.7 per 1000** (median 1.3 per 1000).
- Incidence and prevalence have **declined sharply in Europe, North America and other developed nations** (better hygiene, less household crowding, better medical care).
- The burden is now in **low- and middle-income countries** (India, Middle East, Africa) and in some indigenous populations of developed countries (Australia, New Zealand).
- It is a **disease of poverty**: patients often do not seek care for pharyngitis.

### Global burden

| Measure | Figure |
|---|---|
| People living with RHD | About 20 million |
| With heart failure | About 3 million |
| Needing valve surgery | About 1 million |
| New cases of ARF each year | About 0.5 million, nearly half develop carditis |
| Deaths from RHD | About 230,000 per year |

## 2. ARF versus RHD

- **Acute rheumatic fever:** an inflammatory, multisystem disorder after a group A streptococcal throat infection. It affects connective tissue, causing temporary, painful arthritis and other features (Jones criteria).
- **Rheumatic heart disease:** the **long-term complication** and late manifestation of ARF, with permanent valve damage.

# Part A. Acute rheumatic fever

## 3. Epidemiology and pathogenesis

- Usually affects children, most commonly **5 to 15 years** (also young adults); the commonest cause of acquired heart disease in childhood and adolescence.
- Immune-mediated, **delayed (2 to 4 weeks)** reaction to specific strains of group A streptococcus. Their antigens (M protein) **cross-react with cardiac myosin and sarcolemmal membrane proteins**. The resulting antibodies and T cells inflame the endocardium, myocardium and pericardium, joints, skin and brain.

### Pathology

- **Fibrinoid degeneration** of collagen in connective tissue.
- **Aschoff nodules** are pathognomonic and occur only in the heart. They are granulomas of multinucleated giant cells surrounded by macrophages and T lymphocytes, seen in the subacute or chronic phases of carditis. There are **no bacteria** in the lesion.
- **Anitschkow cells** are enlarged macrophages ("caterpillar" nuclei) within the Aschoff bodies. Larger cells coalesce to form multinucleated Aschoff giant cells.

## 4. Clinical presentation and diagnosis

- Fever, anorexia, lethargy and joint pain, **2 to 3 weeks after streptococcal pharyngitis** (there may be no history of sore throat). **Arthritis occurs in about 75%.** Other features: rash, carditis, neurological changes.
- **No single test** diagnoses ARF; the picture overlaps many inflammatory and infectious diseases. **Overdiagnosis** leads to unnecessary treatment; **underdiagnosis** leads to further attacks, valve surgery and early death.
- Diagnosis is clinical, using the **Jones criteria**.
- Only about **25%** have a positive throat culture for group A streptococcus at diagnosis (latent interval). A **raised ASO titre** is helpful.
- A presumptive diagnosis can be made **without evidence of preceding streptococcal infection** for **isolated chorea** or **pancarditis**, if other causes are excluded.
- In established RHD or past ARF, a **recurrence** can be diagnosed on **multiple minor criteria** plus evidence of preceding streptococcal infection (2 major, or 1 major and 2 minor, or 3 minor).

### Jones criteria

**Original (1992 modified):** two or more major, or one major and two or more minor, **plus evidence of preceding streptococcal infection**.

**2015 revision:**

1. Populations are split by risk. **Low risk:** ARF incidence under 2 per 100,000 school-age children, or all-age RHD prevalence under 1 per 1000 per year. All others (including Kenya) are **moderate or high risk**.
2. **Echocardiography** (with Doppler) is advised in all confirmed or suspected ARF; **subclinical carditis (valvulitis) counts as a major criterion**.
3. **Aseptic monoarthritis** counts as a major criterion in moderate or high-risk populations.
4. **Polyarthralgia** counts as a major manifestation in moderate or high-risk populations.
5. Fever and inflammatory marker thresholds as minor criteria:
   - Low risk: fever above 38.5 degrees C, ESR above 60 mm/hour and/or CRP above 3 mg/dL.
   - Moderate or high risk: fever above 38 degrees C, ESR above 30 mm/hour and/or CRP above 3 mg/dL.

A firm diagnosis needs **2 major, or 1 major and 2 minor, manifestations AND evidence of recent streptococcal infection**.

**Evidence of preceding GAS infection:** raised or rising **ASO** or **anti-DNase B** titre; positive throat culture (or rapid antigen test).

| Major criteria | Minor criteria |
|---|---|
| Carditis (including subclinical on echo) | Fever |
| Arthritis (migratory polyarthritis; monoarthritis or polyarthralgia in high-risk groups) | Arthralgia (when arthritis is not the major) |
| Sydenham chorea | Raised ESR or CRP |
| Subcutaneous nodules | Prolonged PR interval on ECG |
| Erythema marginatum | |

> **Exam tip:** do not count arthritis as both major and minor, and do not count carditis and a prolonged PR interval together as major and minor.

## 5. The major manifestations

### Carditis (50 to 70%)

- A **pancarditis**: endocardium, myocardium and pericardium involved to varying degrees. Incidence falls with age, from about 90% at 3 years to around 30% in adolescence.
- The **only manifestation that leaves serious permanent damage**. May be subclinical. Cardiomegaly and heart failure can occur, from myocardial dysfunction or valvular regurgitation.
- Symptoms: breathlessness (failure or effusion), palpitations, chest pain (pericarditis).
- **Pericarditis:** chest pain, friction rub, precordial tenderness.
- Other signs: tachycardia (out of proportion to fever), cardiac enlargement, new or changed murmurs.
  - **Soft apical pansystolic murmur of mitral regurgitation** is very common.
  - **Carey Coombs murmur:** soft mid-diastolic murmur from mitral valvulitis (nodules on the leaflets).
  - **Aortic regurgitation** occurs in about 50%; the tricuspid and pulmonary valves are rarely involved.
  - **Mitral or aortic regurgitation** in the acute stage; **mitral stenosis** in late stages.
- ECG: ST and T wave changes; conduction defects (first-degree AV block, sometimes causing syncope).

### Arthritis (35 to 66%)

- The **earliest and commonest** major manifestation, appearing when streptococcal antibody titres are high.
- **Acute, painful, asymmetric, migratory (fleeting) polyarthritis of large joints** (knees, ankles, shoulders, elbows). Joints are red, swollen and tender, involved in quick succession.
- Short duration (under 1 week per joint). **Responds rapidly to salicylates**; if it does not, the diagnosis is in doubt.
- Does not progress to chronic disease and does not cause permanent damage (though it may rarely leave deformity, Jaccoud arthropathy).
- Carditis and arthritis are the commonest manifestations.

### Sydenham chorea (10 to 30%)

- Also called **St Vitus's dance**; female predominance. A **late neurological (extrapyramidal) manifestation** appearing **at least 3 months** after the infection, when other signs may have disappeared.
- Emotional lability may come first, then purposeless, involuntary choreiform movements of the hands, face, neck, trunk and limbs. Speech may be explosive and halting; clumsiness, deteriorating handwriting, grimacing.
- Spontaneous recovery within a few months; about **one quarter** later develop chronic rheumatic valve disease.
- **Exclude** Huntington disease, Wilson disease and encephalopathies (and SLE).

### Subcutaneous nodules (5 to 7%)

- Small (0.5 to 2.0 cm), firm, **non-tender and painless**; over extensor surfaces of joints, bony prominences, tendons and the spine.
- Appear more than 3 weeks after other features, so they **confirm rather than make** the diagnosis. Short-lived (days). **Associated with severe carditis.**

### Erythema marginatum (under 5%)

- Red macules that fade in the centre (pale centre), remaining red at the edges; round or irregular serpiginous borders; **non-itchy, transient**. The rings may coalesce.
- On the **trunk and proximal limbs, not the face**. Uncommon but highly specific; associated with carditis.

## 6. Investigations

- FBC (leucocytosis), **ESR**, **CRP**.
- Blood cultures if febrile (to exclude endocarditis and sepsis).
- **ECG** (if PR is prolonged or other rhythm abnormality: repeat in 2 weeks and again in 2 months if still abnormal).
- **Chest X-ray** if clinical or echo evidence of carditis.
- **Echocardiogram** (consider repeating after 1 month if negative). Always do echo in suspected or confirmed ARF to confirm or refute carditis.
- **Throat swab** (before antibiotics) for group A streptococcus.
- **Streptococcal serology:** both ASO and anti-DNase B, if available; repeat after 10 to 14 days if the first test is not confirmatory.

## 7. Management of ARF

There is **no specific treatment** for ARF itself.

1. **Bed rest:** lessens joint pain and cardiac workload; guided by symptoms, temperature, white cell count and ESR, and continued until they settle.
2. **Salicylates (aspirin):** relieve arthritis rapidly; a response within 24 hours supports the diagnosis. **75 to 100 mg/kg/day in 4 divided doses for 6 to 8 weeks**, aiming for a blood level of 20 to 30 mg/dL. Watch for salicylate toxicity (tinnitus, vomiting, hyperventilation).
3. **Penicillin:** to eradicate streptococci. As in the slides, procaine penicillin 4 million units/day for 10 days; erythromycin or a cephalosporin if allergic.
   > **Check:** current guidance is a single IM dose of benzathine penicillin G or **10 days of oral phenoxymethylpenicillin**. Follow local protocols.
4. **Steroids (prednisolone):** more rapid symptom relief than aspirin; indicated in **severe carditis** or severe arthritis. **2 mg/kg/day tapered over 6 weeks.** There is no evidence that long-term steroids help.
5. **Heart failure treatment:** diuretics and ACE inhibitors. If failure does not respond to medical treatment, **valve replacement** may be needed, and is often followed by a dramatic fall in rheumatic activity. AV block seldom progresses and a pacemaker is rarely needed.
6. Chorea: calm environment, and valproate or carbamazepine (or haloperidol) in severe cases.

### Secondary prevention (preventing recurrence)

- Patients with ARF are susceptible to further attacks after each streptococcal infection.
- **Benzathine penicillin 1.2 million units IM monthly** (the better choice if compliance is doubtful; every 3 to 4 weeks), or **oral phenoxymethylpenicillin 250 mg twice daily**.
- **Sulfadiazine or erythromycin** if allergic to penicillin. Sulphonamides prevent infection but do **not eradicate** group A streptococci.
- **Duration:** further attacks are unusual after age **21**, when treatment may be stopped. Extend it if an attack occurred in the **last 5 years**, if the patient lives in a high-prevalence area, or has a high-exposure occupation (for example teaching). With **residual heart disease**, continue until **10 years after the last episode or age 40, whichever is later**.
- Prophylaxis prevents ARF recurrence but **does not protect against infective endocarditis**.

> **Remember:** primary prevention is early diagnosis and treatment of streptococcal pharyngitis; secondary prevention is regular penicillin. In Kenya, active case-finding and echo screening of school children is being promoted.

# Part B. Chronic rheumatic heart disease

## 8. Epidemiology and pathology

- Chronic valvular disease develops in **at least half** of those with rheumatic carditis. **Two thirds of cases are in women.** Some attacks go unnoticed; only about half of patients with chronic RHD recall rheumatic fever or chorea.
- **Valves involved:** **mitral over 90%**, aortic 40%, tricuspid 10%, pulmonary 2%.
- Valve disease may be symptomatic in fulminant ARF but is often silent for many years.
- **Mitral stenosis is more common in women (3:1)**; men have a higher incidence of **aortic regurgitation**.
- Pathology: **progressive fibrosis**. Valves are mainly affected, but pericardial and myocardial involvement can add to heart failure and conduction disorders. **Fusion of the mitral commissures** and **shortening of the chordae** cause mitral stenosis with or without regurgitation. Similar changes in the aortic and tricuspid valves give distortion and rigidity of cusps (stenosis and regurgitation). Once damaged, abnormal haemodynamic stress **perpetuates the damage** even without continuing rheumatic activity.

## 9. Mitral stenosis (the commonest lesion in RHD)

- Flow from left atrium (LA) to left ventricle (LV) is restricted; **LA pressure rises**, causing pulmonary venous congestion and breathlessness. The LA dilates and hypertrophies, and LV filling depends more on atrial contraction. The rise in LA pressure also raises pulmonary capillary, venous and arterial pressure.
- **Normal mitral valve area 4 to 6 cm²; severe stenosis is below 1.5 cm².**

### Clinical features

| Symptoms | Signs |
|---|---|
| Dyspnoea, fatigue, palpitations | **Mitral facies** (malar flush) |
| Haemoptysis (10%) | Irregularly irregular pulse if AF |
| **Hoarseness (Ortner's syndrome)**: recurrent laryngeal nerve compressed by enlarged LA/pulmonary artery | Parasternal heave; **diastolic thrill** |
| Dysphagia (enlarged LA compressing the oesophagus) | **Tapping apex beat** (palpable loud S1) |
| Stroke or peripheral embolism (AF, LA thrombus) | **Loud S1**, loud P2 if pulmonary hypertension |
| | **Opening snap** (the closer to S2, the worse the stenosis) |
| | **Mid-diastolic rumble** with presystolic accentuation in sinus rhythm |

### Investigations

- **Chest X-ray:** straightening of the left heart border, **double density** (enlarged LA behind the right heart border), **Kerley B lines**, calcification of the mitral valve.
- **ECG:** left atrial enlargement (**P mitrale**), right ventricular dominance, AF.
- **Echo-Doppler:** valve area, gradient, LA size, thrombus, pulmonary pressure.

### Management

- Minor symptoms: medical treatment.
  - **Beta blockers or rate-limiting calcium channel blockers** to lengthen diastole (more time for filling)
  - **Digoxin** for AF
  - **Warfarin** for AF or embolism (not a DOAC in rheumatic mitral stenosis)
  - Diuretics for congestion
- **Intervention** if symptoms persist despite treatment or pulmonary hypertension develops: **percutaneous balloon valvuloplasty**, mitral valvotomy, or **mitral valve replacement**.

## 10. Mitral regurgitation

- Also follows mitral valvotomy or valvuloplasty. Chronic MR causes gradual LA dilatation with little pressure rise, so few symptoms for a long time.
- Features: asymptomatic, then dyspnoea, orthopnoea and PND; **displaced apex beat** and thrill; **soft S1**; **pansystolic murmur** radiating to the axilla.
- Treatment is **surgical** (repair or replacement) when symptomatic or LV function declines.

## 11. Aortic regurgitation

- Caused by disease of the aortic cusps or dilatation of the root. The LV dilates and hypertrophies; the stroke volume may be doubled or trebled, so the large arteries are conspicuously pulsatile. As disease progresses, LV diastolic pressure rises and breathlessness develops.
- Signs of a wide pulse pressure (high systolic, low diastolic BP):
  - **Water-hammer (collapsing) pulse**
  - **Corrigan's sign** (visible carotid pulsations)
  - **De Musset's sign** (head nodding with each heartbeat)
  - **Muller's sign** (pulsating uvula)
  - **Quincke's sign** (capillary pulsation in the nail bed)
  - **Hill's sign** (leg systolic pressure higher than arm; normally 10 to 20 mmHg, in AR it can be 60 to 100 mmHg)
  - **Displaced apex beat** (down and to the left) and S3
- **Murmur:** high-pitched early diastolic decrescendo, best at the left sternal edge (Erb's point) with the patient sitting forward in held expiration. Severe AR gives a **shorter** murmur (aorta and LV pressures equalise quickly). An **Austin Flint murmur** (mid-diastolic rumble at the apex) may be heard.
- Treatment: **aortic valve replacement** (surgical or transcatheter).

## 12. Aortic stenosis

- Classic symptoms: **angina** (hypertrophied LV, inadequate blood), **syncope** (usually exertional), **dyspnoea**.
- Signs: **plateau pulse, small volume (parvus) and slow rising (tardus)**; **sustained, non-displaced apex beat**; systolic thrill; **S4**; **late-peaking ejection systolic murmur**; **single S2** (soft or absent A2) or **paradoxical splitting of S2** (heard in expiration, disappears in inspiration).
- Treatment: **aortic valve replacement** (surgical or transcatheter).

## 13. Revision questions in brief

- Apical low-pitched diastolic rumble, dyspnoea, palpitations and haemoptysis: **mitral stenosis, from rheumatic fever in youth**.
- Young patient with migratory arthritis, fever, new apical systolic murmur, subcutaneous nodule, marginate rash, PR prolongation and ESR 60: **ARF; the most critical test to document preceding infection is the ASO titre** (then echo).
- S2 splitting in aortic stenosis: **paradoxical**.
- The AR murmur in severe disease is **shorter** than in mild disease.
- The commonest cause of mitral stenosis is **rheumatic fever**.

## Key points to revise

1. ARF is a delayed (2 to 4 weeks) autoimmune response to group A streptococcal pharyngitis; molecular mimicry with cardiac myosin.
2. Jones criteria: 2 major or 1 major plus 2 minor, plus evidence of recent streptococcal infection (ASO, anti-DNase B, throat culture). Use echo for subclinical carditis.
3. Majors: carditis, migratory polyarthritis, Sydenham chorea, subcutaneous nodules, erythema marginatum.
4. Carditis is the only lesion that causes permanent damage; the mitral valve is affected in over 90%.
5. Treatment: bed rest, aspirin 75 to 100 mg/kg/day, penicillin, steroids for severe carditis, heart failure therapy.
6. Secondary prophylaxis: benzathine penicillin 1.2 MU IM monthly until 21, or 10 years after last attack or age 40 (whichever is later) in residual heart disease.
7. Mitral stenosis: loud S1, opening snap, mid-diastolic rumble, AF, Ortner's syndrome; treat with rate control, warfarin, balloon valvuloplasty or replacement.
8. Aortic regurgitation has a wide pulse pressure and eponymous signs; aortic stenosis has angina, syncope, dyspnoea and a slow-rising pulse.
