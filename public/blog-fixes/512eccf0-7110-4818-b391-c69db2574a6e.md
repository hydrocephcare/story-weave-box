# Hypothyroidism in Children (Congenital and Acquired)

**Year 4 · Paediatrics · Paediatric Endocrinology**

> **In one line:** hypothyroidism is too little circulating thyroid hormone, from a defect in the gland (primary) or low TSH drive (central). In neonates it is usually **thyroid dysgenesis** and must be found by newborn screening and treated with **levothyroxine within the first weeks** to prevent brain damage; in older children it is usually **Hashimoto (chronic lymphocytic) thyroiditis**, presenting with slowed growth and goitre.

## What you must know

- **Congenital hypothyroidism** occurs in about **1 in 2000** births. **Thyroid dysgenesis** causes **80 to 85%** of permanent cases (agenesis in 33%, hypoplastic or ectopic gland in 66%); dyshormonogenesis causes about 15%.
- Most newborns look normal (maternal T4 crosses the placenta), so **newborn screening** (TSH, or T4 then TSH) is how it is found.
- Treat with oral **levothyroxine 10 to 15 µg/kg/day**; normalise thyroid function **within 2 weeks** for the best IQ outcome. Brain development depends on thyroid hormone in the **first 1 to 2 years**.
- **Worldwide, iodine deficiency** is the commonest cause of congenital hypothyroidism.
- Commonest acquired cause: **chronic lymphocytic (Hashimoto) thyroiditis**; first sign is **slowing of growth**; diagnose with raised TSH, low free T4 and **anti-TPO / anti-thyroglobulin antibodies**.
- **Primary** hypothyroidism: TSH high. **Central**: TSH low or inappropriately normal with low free T4, so monitor with **free T4**.
- At-risk groups for autoimmune thyroiditis: Down syndrome, Turner syndrome, Klinefelter syndrome, coeliac disease, type 1 diabetes, autoimmune polyglandular syndromes.

## 1. Thyroid physiology (the basics behind the disease)

### Hormone synthesis

- The thyroid makes **T4 and T3**; iodine's only known physiological role is in these hormones.
- **Recommended iodine intake:** 110 to 130 µg/day in infants, 90 to 120 µg in children, 150 µg in adolescents and adults (**220 µg in pregnancy**).
- Steps:
  1. **Iodide trapping** from blood by the **sodium-iodide symporter (NIS)**.
  2. Transport across the apical membrane into colloid by **pendrin** (SLC26A4).
  3. **Organification:** iodide is oxidised (needs **thyroperoxidase (TPO)**, H2O2 from **DUOX2** and its maturation factor DUOXA2, and haematin) and attached to tyrosine residues of **thyroglobulin** (about 660 kDa, 138 tyrosines, about 14 iodinated) forming **MIT and DIT**.
  4. **Coupling:** DIT + DIT = **T4**; DIT + MIT = **T3**.
  5. Hormone is stored in colloid on thyroglobulin; follicular cells endocytose colloid and proteases release T4 and T3.
  6. **Iodotyrosine deiodinase (IYD)** recycles iodide from MIT and DIT.

### Hormones in the circulation

- Adult thyroid secretes about **85 µg T4 and 6 to 7 µg T3** daily. Only **20% of circulating T3** comes from the gland; the rest is made by **deiodination of T4** (types 1 and 2 deiodinases).
- **T3 is the active hormone** (10 to 15 times greater receptor affinity than T4).
- **Binding:** about **70% of T4** is bound to thyroxine-binding globulin (TBG), the rest to albumin and prealbumin; **free T4 is 0.03%**. T3 is 50% TBG, 50% albumin, **free T3 0.3%**. Alter TBG and total T4 changes, so interpret total levels with care.
- **Actions:** raise oxygen consumption, stimulate protein synthesis, affect growth, differentiation, and carbohydrate, lipid and vitamin metabolism. Intracellular T3 binds nuclear thyroid hormone receptors (isoforms alpha1, alpha2, beta1, beta2; zinc-finger DNA-binding domain), recruits co-activators and drives transcription.
- Tissue-specific modulation occurs through deiodination, membrane transport (for example MCT8) and receptor expression.

### Regulation

- **Hypothalamus (TRH) → anterior pituitary (TSH) → thyroid** (TSH acts via adenylate cyclase on every step from iodine uptake to hormone release).
- Low thyroid hormone raises TSH and TRH; high hormone suppresses them (negative feedback).
- In non-thyroidal illness, fasting, malnutrition and some drugs, **T3 falls** (an adaptive reduction in oxygen use and catabolism): do not label this as hypothyroidism.

### Thyroid function tests

- TSH is the **most sensitive test for primary thyroid disease**: high in primary hypothyroidism, suppressed in hyperthyroidism.
- **Central hypothyroidism:** TSH low or inappropriately normal (can be immunoreactive but less bioactive) with low free T4.
- Direct free T4 assays are reliable in healthy children but unreliable in acute illness or severe binding abnormalities; free T3 assays are poorly standardised. **Always use age-specific ranges**, especially in neonates.
- **Thyroglobulin** is raised by TSH stimulation, in neonates, Graves, autoimmune thyroid disease, endemic goitre and differentiated thyroid cancer; it is **very low in athyreosis**.

## 2. Classification

| Type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Primary** | Defect in the thyroid gland itself; TSH high |
| **Central (secondary / tertiary)** | Reduced TSH (pituitary) or TRH (hypothalamus) stimulation |
| **Congenital** | Present from birth |
| **Acquired** | Develops later in life |

## 3. Congenital hypothyroidism

### Epidemiology

- About **1 in 2000** worldwide by newborn screening.
- In US data lower in African Americans, higher in Asian Americans, Pacific Islanders, Hispanics and Native Americans.

### Causes

| Cause | Key facts |
|---|---|
| **Thyroid dysgenesis** | **80 to 85%** of permanent cases. Agenesis in 33%; hypoplasia or **ectopic gland** (lingual, sublingual, subhyoid) in 66%. **Twice as common in girls.** Mostly sporadic, cause unknown; familial cases occur. Developmental anomalies (thyroglossal cyst, hemiagenesis) in 8 to 10% of first-degree relatives. |
| **Dyshormonogenesis** | About **15%** (1 in 30 000 to 50 000 births). **Autosomal recessive**. Gland responds to high TSH, so a **goitre is almost always present**. Partial defects can present years later. |
| **Maternal TSHR-blocking antibodies (TRBAb)** | About 2% (1 in 50 000 to 100 000). Transient. |
| **Iodine excess or deficiency** | See below |
| **Central hypothyroidism** | See section 5 |
| **Radioiodine in pregnancy** | Fetal thyroid traps iodine from 70 to 75 days |

### Dyshormonogenesis in detail

| Defect | Features |
|---|---|
| **Iodide transport (NIS mutation)** | Very rare; failure to concentrate iodide |
| **Pendred syndrome (pendrin, SLC26A4)** | Autosomal recessive **sensorineural deafness + goitre**; impaired organification; **positive perchlorate discharge test**. Pendrin is expressed in thyroid and cochlea. Some patients with pendrin mutations have deafness but no thyroid disease. |
| **Organification defects (TPO, DUOX2, DUOXA2)** | **Commonest synthetic defect.** Perchlorate or thiocyanate given 2 h after a radioiodine test dose discharges **40 to 90%** of thyroid radioactivity (normal under 10%). Many TPO mutations. DUOX2 mutations (15 to 40% of apparent dyshormonogenesis; 50 to 60% in some Chinese and Korean series) cause permanent or **transient** disease. |
| **Thyroglobulin (TG) defects** | Hypothyroidism with goitre and **low or absent thyroglobulin**; over 40 mutations |
| **Deiodination defect (IYD)** | MIT and DIT are lost in urine, causing severe iodine deficiency, goitre and hypothyroidism, from birth or delayed |
| **Hormone transport defect (MCT8, SLC16A2, X-linked)** | **Allan-Herndon-Dudley syndrome:** profound developmental delay, low muscle mass, dysarthria, athetoid movements, hypotonia progressing to spastic paraplegia; **high T3, low T4, normal or mildly raised TSH** |

### Maternal TSHR-blocking antibodies

- Transplacental TRBAb block TSH binding in the baby.
- Suspect with maternal autoimmune thyroid disease (Hashimoto or Graves), maternal hypothyroidism, or transient congenital hypothyroidism in a sibling, but it can occur with no maternal history. Measure maternal TBII in pregnancy. Babies and mothers may also have stimulating and TPO antibodies; thyroglobulin is low.
- Needs levothyroxine at first, then **remits at about 3 to 6 months** as antibodies clear. Correct diagnosis avoids needless long-term treatment and warns of recurrence in later pregnancies. Prognosis is good, but developmental delay can occur if the mother's hypothyroidism was untreated in pregnancy.

### Iodine exposure and deficiency

- **Excess iodine (transient):** iodine antiseptics for caesarean section or cervical preparation; mothers taking large amounts (up to 12 mg/day) in supplements or seaweed while breastfeeding; iodine antiseptics in low birth weight neonates; in older children, asthma preparations and **amiodarone**. Goitre is usually present. Do not mistake for permanent disease.
- **Radioiodine:** do a **pregnancy test before any 131I** in a woman of childbearing age, whatever her contraception history; it is also contraindicated in lactation (excreted in milk).
- **Iodine deficiency (endemic goitre)** is the **commonest cause of congenital hypothyroidism worldwide**. Adult intake should be 150 µg daily, rising to 220 µg in pregnancy. Universal salt iodisation is not complete because of economic and practical obstacles. Preterm babies depend on maternal iodine and low-iodine formulas, so they are particularly at risk.

## 4. Thyroid function in preterm and low birth weight infants

- Qualitatively like term infants, but **quantitatively lower**: cord T4 falls with gestational age and weight; the postnatal TSH surge is reduced.
- Very preterm babies show a **fall in T4 in the first week** (term babies rise); T4 recovers by about 6 weeks. Free T4 is less affected and may be normal by equilibrium dialysis (gold standard).
- Causes: immature hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis, **loss of maternal T4 supply** in the third trimester, severe illness and drugs (dopamine, glucocorticoids).

## 5. Central (secondary) congenital hypothyroidism

- Occurs in **1 in 16 000 to 30 000** infants. **75%** have multiple pituitary hormone deficiencies.
- Suspect with **hypoglycaemia, persistent jaundice, micropenis or cryptorchidism, midline defects** (cleft lip or palate, midface hypoplasia).

| Gene | Deficiencies |
|---|---|
| **POU1F1 (PIT1)** | TSH, GH, prolactin |
| **PROP1** | TSH, GH, prolactin, LH, FSH, variable ACTH |
| **HESX1** | TSH, GH, prolactin, ACTH; **septo-optic dysplasia** (optic nerve hypoplasia) |
| **IGSF1** | Commonest genetic cause of isolated TSH deficiency: **X-linked central hypothyroidism with macro-orchidism**; prolactin deficiency, sometimes GH deficiency |
| **TSHB** | Central hypothyroidism with very low TSH (sometimes normal or raised immunoreactive TSH, raised alpha-subunit) |
| **TRHR** | Very rare; TSH and prolactin fail to respond to TRH |

## 6. Clinical features of congenital hypothyroidism

### At birth and early weeks

- Most are **asymptomatic at birth** because maternal T4 provides about 33% of normal fetal levels. Diagnosis depends on **screening**.
- Birth weight and length normal; head size slightly increased (cerebral myxoedema); **wide open anterior and posterior fontanelles** (a clue).
- **Prolonged indirect jaundice** (delayed glucuronide conjugation).
- Cry little, sleep much, poor feeding and choking, sluggish.
- Respiratory difficulty from **macroglossia**: apnoea, noisy breathing, nasal obstruction.
- **Constipation** unresponsive to treatment; large abdomen; **umbilical hernia**.
- **Hypothermia** (often <35°C), cold mottled skin; oedema of genitals and extremities.
- Slow pulse, murmurs, cardiomegaly, asymptomatic pericardial effusion; **macrocytic anaemia**.
- About **10%** have associated anomalies (cardiac most common; nervous system, eye); hearing loss may occur.

### Syndromic gene associations

| Gene | Features with congenital hypothyroidism |
|---|---|
| **NKX2-1** | Respiratory distress, ataxia or choreoathetosis |
| **FOXE1** | Spiky or curly hair, cleft palate |
| **PAX8** | Genitourinary anomalies, renal agenesis |

### If untreated (fully developed by 3 to 6 months: the "cretin")

- Stunted growth, short limbs, normal or large head, large anterior fontanelle, posterior fontanelle open.
- Widely spaced eyes, depressed broad nasal bridge, narrow palpebral fissures, swollen eyelids.
- Open mouth, thick protruding tongue, delayed dentition.
- Short thick neck with fat pads above the clavicles; broad hands, short fingers.
- Dry scaly skin, little sweating, myxoedema (eyelids, back of hands, genitals), sallow pallor, carotenaemia (sclerae stay white).
- Coarse, brittle, scanty hair; low hairline; wrinkled forehead.
- Lethargy, delayed motor milestones, hoarse voice, no speech, **severe intellectual disability**, delayed puberty.
- Hypotonia; rarely muscular pseudohypertrophy (**Kocher-Debre-Semelaigne syndrome**, athletic look with calf hypertrophy).
- **Breast milk does not protect** an affected baby and does not alter the screening result.
- Milder or partial deficiency gives a later, milder picture.

### Mild or missed cases

- Children with **ectopic thyroid** (lingual, sublingual, subhyoid) may be euthyroid for years and present with a **midline mass at the tongue base or at hyoid level**, sometimes with a thyroglossal duct cyst. **Removing ectopic tissue** usually causes hypothyroidism, so scan before surgery.

## 7. Diagnosis of congenital hypothyroidism

### Newborn screening

- Most screening programmes measure **TSH** (detects primary disease, including mild cases; may miss central disease and delayed TSH rise).
- Others measure **T4 first, then TSH** if low (also finds some central and delayed-TSH cases, and benign TBG deficiency).
- Interpret by age-specific ranges, especially in the first week.
- **Repeat screen at 2 to 6 weeks** for high-risk groups: **preterm and low birth weight infants** (delayed TSH rise), **Down syndrome**, **congenital heart disease**, and **monochorionic twins** (the normal twin compensates through shared circulation so the affected twin may be missed early).

### Confirmatory tests

- Low **T4 and free T4**; T3 is often normal and not useful.
- **TSH raised, often >100 mU/L** in primary disease.
- **Thyroglobulin:** low in agenesis, TSH receptor defects (including TRBAb) and thyroglobulin synthesis defects; raised in ectopy and other synthesis defects (wide overlap).
- **Imaging:** ultrasound or isotope scan to define gland position and size (do not delay treatment for it).
- **Bone age X-ray:** delayed osseous maturation in about **60%** at birth (absent distal femoral and proximal tibial epiphyses). Later features: epiphyseal dysgenesis (multiple ossification foci), **beaking of T12, L1 or L2**, wide sutures and large fontanelles, **wormian bones**, enlarged round sella (rarely eroded), delayed dentition, cardiomegaly or pericardial effusion.

### Differential diagnosis

Down syndrome, other causes of prolonged jaundice, constipation (Hirschsprung disease), hypotonia, failure to thrive and macroglossia (for example Beckwith-Wiedemann).

## 8. Treatment and prognosis of congenital hypothyroidism

- **Oral levothyroxine (L-T4).** 80% of circulating T3 comes from T4, so T4 alone restores both.
- **Starting dose 10 to 15 µg/kg/day** (about 37.5 to 50 µg/day for most term infants); use the **higher end** if T4 <5 µg/dL or imaging shows aplasia.
- **Normalise thyroid function within 2 weeks** for best neurodevelopment.
- **Monitoring:** T4 or free T4 and TSH **every 1 to 2 months in the first 6 months**, then every 2 to 4 months between 6 months and 3 years.
- **Targets:** TSH in the age reference range and free T4 or total T4 in the **upper half** of the range. Both undertreatment and overtreatment can lower IQ.
- About **35%** of infants with a normally located gland have **transient** disease. After age 3, a 3 to 4 week trial off L-T4 checks whether TSH rises (permanent disease). This is not needed in proven dysgenesis or those whose TSH rose after 6 to 12 months of therapy because of poor adherence or low dose.

### Prognosis

- Treatment started in the first week prevents irreversible brain damage; screened infants usually have verbal, psychomotor and IQ scores similar to siblings or classmates.
- The most severely affected (lowest T4, most delayed bone age) may still have lower IQ, incoordination, hypo- or hypertonia, attention and speech problems, difficulty with vocabulary, reading, arithmetic and memory.
- About **10%** have neurosensory hearing loss.
- Adult follow-up shows delayed social development, lower self-esteem and quality of life, mostly in those with lower cognitive outcome or associated malformations.
- Delay in treatment, slow correction of low T4, under-treatment or poor adherence in the first **2 to 3 years** causes variable brain damage; untreated severe cases have profound intellectual disability and growth failure. Onset **after age 2** has a much better neurodevelopmental outlook, showing how dependent the brain is on thyroid hormone in the first year.

> **Exam tip:** congenital hypothyroidism is a medical emergency in the sense that every day of delay costs IQ points. Start treatment on a positive screen without waiting for imaging.

## 9. Acquired hypothyroidism

### Epidemiology

- About **0.3% (1 in 333)** of school-age children. **Subclinical hypothyroidism** (raised TSH, normal T4 or free T4) occurs in about **2%** of adolescents.
- **Autoimmune thyroid disease** is the commonest cause: evidence in 6% of 12 to 19 year olds; **girls twice as often** as boys. Usually adolescence, but can occur in the first year.

### Causes

| Group | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Autoimmune** | **Chronic lymphocytic (Hashimoto) thyroiditis**, the commonest cause |
| **Associated conditions** | Trisomy 21, Turner, Klinefelter, coeliac disease, type 1 diabetes, **autoimmune polyglandular syndromes (APS)** |
| **Drugs** | Iodides (Wolff-Chaikoff effect: expectorants, supplements); **amiodarone** (37% iodine by weight; hypothyroidism in about 20% of treated children, so monitor serially); anticonvulsants (phenytoin, phenobarbital, valproate: usually subclinical, via hepatic CYP450 clearance of T4; **oxcarbazepine causes central hypothyroidism**); **antithyroid drugs** (methimazole, PTU) given for Graves; lithium, rifampicin, tyrosine kinase inhibitors, interferon-alpha, stavudine, thalidomide, aminoglutethimide |
| **Radiation or surgery** | Therapeutic irradiation (Hodgkin disease, head and neck cancers, before bone marrow transplant); **central** hypothyroidism in up to 10% after craniospinal irradiation; radioiodine or thyroidectomy for Graves or cancer; removal of ectopic thyroid tissue or a thyroglossal cyst that holds the only thyroid tissue (**scan before surgery**) |
| **Metabolic and infiltrative** | **Nephropathic cystinosis** (two thirds need L-T4 by age 13); Langerhans cell histiocytosis (thyroid infiltration, or pituitary involvement causing central disease) |
| **Infection** | Chronic hepatitis C (subclinical, non-autoimmune) |
| **Consumptive** | Large **liver haemangiomas** express type 3 deiodinase, which inactivates T4 and T3 to reverse T3 and T2 |
| **Mild congenital disease presenting late** | Dysgenesis or mild dyshormonogenesis that escaped screening |
| **Central** | Hypothalamic-pituitary tumours (**craniopharyngioma** commonest in children) or their treatment, cranial irradiation, head trauma, Langerhans cell histiocytosis |
| **Other** | Williams syndrome (subclinical, non-autoimmune, no antibodies) |

### Autoimmune polyglandular syndromes and associations

- **APS-1:** rare, **autosomal recessive, AIRE gene**. Triad of **mucocutaneous candidiasis, hypoparathyroidism, primary adrenal insufficiency**. Autoimmune thyroiditis in only about 10%; also type 1 diabetes, hypogonadism, pernicious anaemia, vitiligo, alopecia, nephritis, hepatitis, GI dysfunction.
- **APS-2:** far commoner, cause unclear (genetic and environmental). Any combination of autoimmune thyroiditis (about **70%**), type 1 diabetes, coeliac disease, adrenal insufficiency, hypogonadism, pernicious anaemia, vitiligo.
- About **20% of children with type 1 diabetes** develop thyroid autoantibodies and about **5%** become hypothyroid.
- **Down syndrome:** thyroid antibodies in about 30%; hypothyroidism (subclinical or overt) in 15 to 20%.
- **Turner syndrome:** antibodies in about 40%; hypothyroidism in 15 to 30%, rising with age.
- Other associations: IPEX and IPEX-like disorders, IgG4-related disease, Sjogren syndrome, multiple sclerosis.

## 10. Clinical features of acquired hypothyroidism

- **Slowing of growth is usually the first sign**, but is often missed.
- **Goitre** is a common presentation. In Hashimoto thyroiditis it is **non-tender, firm, rubbery with a pebbly (bosselated) surface**.
- Weight gain is mostly fluid (myxoedema), not true obesity.
- Insidious myxoedematous skin changes, **constipation, cold intolerance**, tiredness, extra sleep. School performance usually does not suffer.
- Bradycardia, muscle weakness and cramps, nerve entrapment, ataxia.
- **Delayed bone age**, proportional to the duration of disease.
- **Adolescents:** delayed puberty; older girls may have menometrorrhagia and **galactorrhoea** (TRH stimulates prolactin).
- **Pituitary enlargement** from thyrotroph hyperplasia in long-standing disease can cause headache and visual problems and may be mistaken for a pituitary tumour or prolactinoma.
- Rarely **pseudoprecocious puberty** in profound hypothyroidism (breast development, vaginal bleeding, testicular enlargement) because very high TSH stimulates the FSH receptor. Unlike true precocity, there is growth arrest and delayed bone age.

> **Remember:** hypothyroid children are short and growing slowly with delayed bone age; most causes of precocity cause tall children with advanced bone age.

## 11. Investigation of acquired hypothyroidism

- **TSH and free T4**, compared with age-specific ranges.
- **Thyroid peroxidase (TPO) or thyroglobulin antibodies** confirm autoimmune thyroiditis.
- Other abnormalities: **hyponatraemia, macrocytic anaemia, hypercholesterolaemia, raised creatine kinase**.
- **Ultrasound** (diffuse enlargement, heterogeneous echo texture) only if a nodule is suspected.
- **Bone age X-ray** shows duration and severity.
- Screen for associated autoimmunity in at-risk children (coeliac, type 1 diabetes).

## 12. Treatment of acquired hypothyroidism

- **Levothyroxine** with weight-based doses that fall with age:

| Age | Average daily L-T4 |
|---|---|
| 1 to 3 years | 4 to 6 µg/kg |
| 3 to 10 years | 3 to 5 µg/kg |
| 10 to 16 years | 2 to 4 µg/kg |

- **Monitor TSH every 4 to 6 months** and **4 to 6 weeks after any dose change**; keep TSH in the age range.
- **Under 3 years:** also check free T4 and keep it in the **upper half** of the range. Older children do not need routine free T4 unless adherence is in doubt.
- **Central hypothyroidism:** TSH is unreliable, so monitor **free T4 alone** (upper half of the range).
- In the first year of treatment, school deterioration, poor sleep, restlessness, short attention span and behaviour problems may appear; these are transient, so warn families. Starting at a lower dose and increasing slowly may help.
- **Persistent headache or visual change** after starting L-T4 in children aged 8 to 13 should prompt evaluation for **pseudotumour cerebri**.
- Growth rate after catch-up is a good index of adequacy of treatment. In long-standing hypothyroidism catch-up may be incomplete and adult height permanently reduced.

## Key points to revise

1. Congenital hypothyroidism is about 1 in 2000; dysgenesis causes 80 to 85%; dyshormonogenesis (autosomal recessive, goitre) about 15%.
2. Babies are normal at birth (maternal T4), so rely on newborn screening; repeat in preterm, Down syndrome, cardiac disease and monochorionic twins.
3. Treat congenital disease with L-T4 10 to 15 µg/kg/day, normalise within 2 weeks; monitor every 1 to 2 months in the first 6 months.
4. Late signs: large fontanelles, macroglossia, umbilical hernia, constipation, prolonged jaundice, hypothermia, hoarse cry, delayed bone age.
5. Pendred syndrome: deafness + goitre + positive perchlorate test; MCT8 mutation: high T3, severe neurology (Allan-Herndon-Dudley).
6. Hashimoto thyroiditis is the commonest acquired cause; first sign is slowed growth; firm rubbery goitre; anti-TPO positive.
7. Higher risk of thyroiditis in Down, Turner, Klinefelter, type 1 diabetes, coeliac disease and APS; watch amiodarone, lithium and anticonvulsants.
8. Central hypothyroidism: low or normal TSH with low free T4; monitor with free T4 alone.
