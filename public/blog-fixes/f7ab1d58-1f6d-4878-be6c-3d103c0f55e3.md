# Pneumonia

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Respiratory Medicine**

> **In one line:** pneumonia is infection of the lung parenchyma, with alveoli filling with inflammatory exudate that shows as new shadowing on the chest X-ray. The likely organism depends on where the patient caught it (community, hospital, aspiration, immunocompromised), and that decides the empirical antibiotic. Severity scoring (CURB-65) decides where the patient is treated.

## What you must know

- **Definition:** acute respiratory illness with **recent radiological shadowing**, segmental or involving more than one lobe.
- **Streptococcus pneumoniae** is the commonest cause of community-acquired pneumonia (about 30%).
- Pathology passes through **congestion, red hepatisation, grey hepatisation and resolution**.
- Severity: **CURB-65**; the cardinal markers are respiratory rate 30 or more, diastolic BP 60 or less, urea 7 mmol/L or more, and confusion.
- Treatment: oxygen for hypoxaemia, fluids, analgesia, and antibiotics started after specimens are taken (**benzyl penicillin** for pneumococcus).
- **Hospital-acquired pneumonia** begins 48 hours or more after admission, is usually Gram-negative or MRSA, and carries about 30% mortality.
- **Aspiration pneumonia and lung abscess** are polymicrobial and need prolonged treatment (4 to 6 weeks) with **amoxicillin plus metronidazole**.
- In the immunocompromised host, think of **Pseudomonas, fungi, TB, CMV and Pneumocystis**.

## 1. Definition

- Pneumonia is an **acute respiratory illness associated with recently developed radiological pulmonary shadowing** that is segmental or affects more than one lobe.
- It may be bacterial, viral, mycobacterial or fungal.

## 2. Pathology (stages of lobar pneumonia)

| Stage | Features |
|---|---|
| **Congestion** | Proteinaceous exudate in the alveoli with vascular engorgement |
| **Red hepatisation** | Red cells leak into the alveolar space; the lung is red, firm and liver-like |
| **Grey hepatisation** | **Neutrophils** predominate with abundant **fibrin**; red cells break down; the lung looks grey |
| **Resolution** | **Macrophages** predominate and clear the debris; the lung returns to normal |

## 3. Classification

### Anatomical

- **Lobar pneumonia:** a radiological and pathological term for homogeneous opacification (red hepatisation) of one or more lobes, often with pleural inflammation.
- **Bronchopneumonia:** patchy alveolar consolidation with bronchial and bronchiolar inflammation, usually affecting both lower lobes.
- **Interstitial pneumonia:** inflammation mainly of the interstitium (typical of viruses and atypical organisms).

### Clinical

1. **Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP):** typical, atypical or aspiration
2. **Nosocomial (hospital-acquired, HAP) and ventilator-associated (VAP)**
3. **Pneumonia in the immunocompromised host**
4. Pneumonia in the elderly

# Part A. Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP)

## 4. Definition, causes and risk factors

- Pneumonia acquired at home or in a non-hospital setting such as a nursing home (not hospitalised in the preceding 14 days). It is mainly spread by **inhalation**. It is the commonest type of pneumonia and a leading cause of illness and death worldwide.

### Organisms

| Organism | Share of CAP |
|---|---|
| *Streptococcus pneumoniae* | 30% |
| *Chlamydia pneumoniae* | 10% |
| *Mycoplasma pneumoniae* | 9% |
| *Legionella pneumophila* | 5% |
| *Haemophilus influenzae* | 3% |
| Others: *Moraxella catarrhalis*, *Streptococcus pyogenes* | Small |
| *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Chlamydia psittaci*, *Coxiella burnetii*, *Klebsiella pneumoniae*, *Actinomyces israelii* | Each under 1% |
| **Viruses:** influenza (7 to 8% of CAP), parainfluenza, measles, RSV (in infancy), varicella | |

> **Remember:** in Kenya, also think of **TB** and **HIV-related pneumonias** (including *Pneumocystis*) in any patient who is slow to respond.

### Risk factors

- Cigarette smoking, alcohol, corticosteroid therapy, old age
- Recent influenza infection, pre-existing lung disease
- Contact with sick birds (*C. psittaci*)
- Farm environments (*Coxiella burnetii*), pollution

## 5. Clinical features

### Symptoms

- Cough, typically productive of **rust-coloured sputum** (pneumococcus)
- Fever, malaise, **pleuritic chest pain**
- Headache, confusion, nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea (more with *Legionella*), fatigue, muscle aches

### Signs

- Fever, tachycardia, **tachypnoea**, hypoxaemia, hypotension, confusion, cyanosis
- **Consolidation:** dull percussion, bronchial breath sounds, increased vocal fremitus and resonance, aegophony and whispering pectoriloquy, crepitations
- Pleural rub
- **Pleural effusion:** stony dullness, reduced or absent breath sounds

## 6. Investigations

| Test | Use and findings |
|---|---|
| **Chest X-ray** | Homogeneous opacity localised to a lobe or segment. Shows pleural effusion, abscess formation or empyema. **Hilar lymphadenopathy** is seen in mycoplasma pneumonia |
| **Microbiology** | Sputum microscopy, culture and sensitivity; tracheal or bronchial aspirates during bronchoscopy; **blood cultures** |
| **Arterial blood gas** | Assesses severity (hypoxaemia, respiratory failure) |
| **Full blood count** | **Neutrophil leucocytosis** suggests bacterial pneumonia; **leucopenia** suggests viral cause (or severe sepsis) |
| **Urea, electrolytes, albumin, LFTs** | Severity scoring and complications; also consider HIV test and sputum for AFB or GeneXpert in Kenya |

## 7. Assessment of severity

### Clinical parameters

- Age 60 years or older
- Respiratory rate over 30/min
- Diastolic BP 60 mmHg or less
- Confusion
- More than one lobe involved on X-ray
- Underlying disease

### Laboratory parameters

- Hypoxaemia (**PaO₂ below 8 kPa**)
- Leucopenia (below 4 × 10⁹/L) or leucocytosis (above 20 × 10⁹/L)
- Raised serum urea 7 mmol/L or more
- Positive blood culture
- Hypoalbuminaemia

> **Check:** the slides write the white cell units as "x10⁹/L" in thousands (4000 and 20000). The standard thresholds are **below 4 × 10⁹/L** and **above 20 × 10⁹/L**.

### The four cardinal markers of severity

1. **Respiratory rate** 30/min or more
2. **Diastolic BP** 60 mmHg or less
3. **Urea (BUN)** 7 mmol/L or more
4. **Confusion**

Patients with **two or more** of these have a **36-fold higher risk of death** than patients with none.

### CURB-65

- Used to estimate prognosis and decide where to treat CAP. Derived and validated in 1068 patients in three prospective studies (UK, New Zealand, Netherlands). It is not yet validated in COVID-19.
- One point for each: **C**onfusion, **U**rea above 7 mmol/L, **R**espiratory rate 30 or more, **B**lood pressure low (systolic below 90 or diastolic 60 or less), age **65** or more.

| Score | Risk | Usual disposition |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 1 | Low | Home treatment |
| 2 | Moderate | Short hospital stay or supervised outpatient |
| 3 to 5 | High | Hospital; consider ICU for 4 to 5 |

> **Exam tip:** the slides list four "cardinal markers" (CURB without the age); CURB-65 adds age 65 or more. Do not mix the two.

## 8. Management

- Good response to antibiotics is expected. If recovery is delayed, suspect **complications, wrong diagnosis, proximal bronchial obstruction (for example tumour) or recurrent aspiration**.

### Supportive

- **Oxygen** for all hypoxaemic patients; use concentrations of **35% or more** in those without advanced COPD or hypercapnia
- IV fluids
- Chest physiotherapy
- Analgesia: **paracetamol**; if severe, **pethidine or morphine** (for pleuritic pain)

### Specific (antibiotics given after specimens are taken)

| Organism | Treatment |
|---|---|
| *Streptococcus pneumoniae* | **Benzyl penicillin** |
| *Klebsiella pneumoniae* | **Gentamicin and ceftazidime** |
| *Chlamydia pneumoniae* | Erythromycin or tetracycline |
| *Chlamydia psittaci* | Tetracycline |
| Actinomycosis | Benzyl penicillin |
| Varicella pneumonia | Oral acyclovir |

> **Remember:** for atypical pathogens (*Mycoplasma*, *Legionella*, *Chlamydia*), use a macrolide, doxycycline or a respiratory fluoroquinolone. Beta-lactams do not work because these organisms lack the target (*Mycoplasma* has no cell wall).

## 9. Complications

- Parapneumonic effusion
- **Empyema**
- Sputum retention (with lobar collapse)
- Pneumothorax
- Bacteraemia
- Shock and respiratory failure
- Lung abscess

## 10. Prevention

- **Influenza A/B vaccine** for all people over 50 years.
- **23-valent pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine and Hib vaccine** for susceptible people: elderly over 65, diabetes and other comorbidities, immunocompromised, sickle cell disease, asplenia.
- **Treat water sources** to prevent *Legionella pneumophila*.
- Stop smoking and limit alcohol.

# Part B. Hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP)

## 11. Definition and causes

- A **new episode of pneumonia at least 48 hours after hospital admission** (also called nosocomial pneumonia).
- Occurs in **2 to 5%** of admissions; mortality about **30%**.
- Organisms:
  - **Gram-negative bacteria:** *E. coli*, *Pseudomonas*, *Klebsiella*
  - ***Staphylococcus aureus*, including MRSA**
  - **Anaerobes**

### Risk factors

| Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Reduced host defences** | Diabetes, malignancy; reduced cough (post-operative); disordered mucociliary clearance; bulbar or vocal cord palsy |
| **Aspiration of nasopharyngeal or gastric secretions** | Immobility or reduced consciousness; vomiting, dysphagia, achalasia, severe reflux; nasogastric tubes |
| **Bacteria introduced into the lower airway** | Endotracheal intubation or tracheostomy; infected ventilators, nebulisers or bronchoscopes; dental or sinus infection |
| **Bacteraemia** | Abdominal sepsis, infected IV cannulae, infected emboli |

## 12. Clinical features and investigations

- Cough, fever, **purulent sputum**, breathlessness, central cyanosis, crepitations.
- **Chest X-ray:** mottled shadowing in both lower zones.
- FBC: neutrophil leucocytosis. Tracheal aspirates and blood cultures.

## 13. Management

- Supportive: physiotherapy, oxygen, fluids with monitoring.
- **IV antibiotics guided by cultures**. Cover Gram-negative organisms with:
  - a **third-generation cephalosporin plus an aminoglycoside**, or
  - **imipenem**, or
  - a monobactam (aztreonam and similar) **plus flucloxacillin** (to cover staphylococci).
- Add MRSA cover (for example vancomycin) when risk factors are present. Follow local antibiogram and the IDSA/ATS guidelines for CAP, HAP and VAP.

## 14. Prevention

- Good pulmonary toilet after surgery: frequent suctioning, incentive spirometry, non-cough-suppressant analgesia
- **Handwashing**
- Proper sterilisation and handling of respiratory equipment
- Correct placement of NG tubes; appropriate volume and rate of feeding
- **Head of bed raised to 30 to 45 degrees**

# Part C. Aspiration and suppurative pneumonia

## 15. Definition and mechanisms

- Pneumonia from the introduction of foreign material or substances into the lower respiratory tract.
- Organisms: Enterobacteriaceae, *Staph. aureus*, *Strep. pneumoniae*, *H. influenzae* (and oral anaerobes).
- Mechanisms:
  1. Primary bacterial pneumonia with suppuration (*Staph. aureus*, *Klebsiella*)
  2. Inhalation of septic material during operations on nose, mouth or throat under general anaesthesia
  3. Inhalation of vomit
  4. Infection of a pulmonary infarct or collapsed lobe

### Risk factors

- Gross oral sepsis, bulbar palsy, vocal cord palsy, achalasia, reflux disease
- Alcoholics
- **IV drug users:** lung abscesses from right-sided endocarditis (tricuspid and pulmonary valves)

## 16. Clinical features

- Productive cough with **large amounts of foul, bloodstained sputum**, pleuritic chest pain.
- **Sudden expectoration of copious foul sputum** if an abscess ruptures into a bronchus.
- High fever, weight loss, signs of consolidation, pleural rub.
- **Digital clubbing** may develop in as little as 10 to 14 days.

## 17. Investigations and management

- **Chest X-ray:** lobar or segmental opacity (consolidation or collapse). A frank lung abscess is a large dense opacity that later cavitates and shows an **air-fluid level**. An infected emphysematous bulla also shows a cavity with a fluid level.
- Sputum microscopy and culture.
- Treatment: physiotherapy and postural drainage of large cavities; **amoxicillin plus metronidazole**; adjust to microbiology; **treat for 4 to 6 weeks**; remove any obstructing endobronchial lesion (bronchoscopy); **surgery** for abscesses that do not resolve despite optimal medical therapy.
- Complications: **fibrosis, bronchiectasis** (also empyema).
- Prevention: good oral hygiene; nurse patients at risk of aspiration with the head of bed raised to 30 to 45 degrees.

# Part D. Pneumonia in the immunocompromised host

## 18. Definition and causes

- Pneumonia in patients on immunosuppressive drugs or with defects of cellular or humoral immunity. Organisms include Gram-negative bacteria (especially *P. aeruginosa*) and unusual organisms normally of low virulence.

| Immune defect | Cause | Likely organisms |
|---|---|---|
| **Neutropenia** | Cytotoxic drugs, agranulocytosis, acute leukaemia | *Staph. aureus*, Gram-negative bacteria, *Candida albicans*, *Aspergillus fumigatus* |
| **T-cell defects** | Lymphoma, CLL, immunosuppressants, bone marrow transplant, splenectomy | *Candida*, ***M. tuberculosis***, ***Pneumocystis* (formerly *carinii*, now *jirovecii*)**, CMV, Gram-negatives, *Staph. aureus*, *Strep. pneumoniae*, *H. influenzae* |
| **Antibody defects** | CLL, myeloma | *Strep. pneumoniae*, *H. influenzae* |

> **Remember:** **HIV** is the commonest cause of T-cell defect in Kenya, so *Pneumocystis* pneumonia and TB are leading considerations.

## 19. Features, investigations and management

- Fever, cough, breathlessness (often dry cough in PCP).
- Chest X-ray; sputum culture (induced with **hypertonic saline** if dry cough); **bronchoalveolar lavage** for culture; **lung biopsy** if needed.
- Treat on an established aetiology. Empirically use a **broad-spectrum regimen**: a third-generation cephalosporin or a quinolone, **plus an antipseudomonal penicillin plus an aminoglycoside**.
- **PCP:** high-dose cotrimoxazole (septrin), with steroids if hypoxaemic.
- Prevention in neutropenia: **granulocyte colony-stimulating factors** (filgrastim, sargramostim).

## Key points to revise

1. Pneumonia is acute illness with new radiological shadowing; pneumococcus causes about 30% of CAP.
2. Stages: congestion, red hepatisation, grey hepatisation, resolution.
3. Four cardinal markers: RR 30 or more, diastolic BP 60 or less, urea 7 or more, confusion. CURB-65 adds age 65 or more.
4. CAP treatment: oxygen, fluids, analgesia, benzyl penicillin for pneumococcus; macrolide or tetracycline for atypicals.
5. HAP is 48 hours or more after admission; Gram-negatives, MRSA and anaerobes; mortality about 30%.
6. Aspiration pneumonia and abscess: amoxicillin plus metronidazole for 4 to 6 weeks, clubbing can appear in 10 to 14 days.
7. Immunocompromised: match organism to the defect (neutropenia, T-cell, antibody) and treat PCP with high-dose cotrimoxazole.
8. Delayed recovery: think of complications, wrong diagnosis, obstruction or recurrent aspiration.
