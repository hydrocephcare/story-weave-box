GASTROINTESTINAL PATHOLOGY (Part 1)

## Summary
This document provides essential notes on **gastrointestinal pathology**, focusing on the **oral cavity**, **oesophagus**, and **stomach**. It covers common inflammatory lesions in the oral cavity, such as **aphthous ulcers**, **HSV infections**, and **candidiasis**, alongside proliferative and neoplastic lesions like **leukoplakia**, **erythroplakia**, and **oral squamous cell carcinoma**. Diseases of the salivary glands, including **xerostomia**, **mumps**, **mucoceles**, and **bacterial sialadenitis**, are also addressed. For the oesophagus, conditions discussed range from congenital anomalies and motility disorders (e.g., **achalasia**, **hiatal hernia**) to inflammatory oesophagitis (**infectious**, **reflux**, **eosinophilic**) and the premalignant **Barrett oesophagus**. The section on the stomach details **acute and chronic gastritis** (**H. pylori** and **autoimmune**), **peptic ulcer disease**, and various gastric tumours, including **adenocarcinomas**, **lymphomas**, **carcinoid tumours**, and **gastrointestinal stromal tumours (GISTs)**.

## Key Points

### Oral Inflammatory Lesions
**Aphthous ulcers** (canker sores) are common, painful, recurrent, and of unknown cause. **Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV)** infections (cold sores) are reactivations, showing **intranuclear inclusions** and **multinucleated giant cells**. **Oral Candidiasis** (thrush) is a fungal infection, typically appearing as grey-to-white membranes that **can be scraped off**, distinguishing it from leukoplakia.

### Oral Proliferative and Neoplastic Lesions
**Leukoplakia** is a white patch that **cannot be scraped off** and is considered precancerous (5-25% progress to SCC) . **Erythroplakia** is a red, velvety area with a **much greater risk of malignant transformation** (>50%). **Oral Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC)** accounts for 95% of oral cancers, is aggressive, and is linked to "**field cancerisation**." It has two main pathways: tobacco/alcohol (**TP53 mutations**) and **HPV-related** (**HPV-16**, **p16 overexpression**, better prognosis).

### Salivary Gland Diseases
**Xerostomia** (dry mouth) is common, often drug-induced or seen in **Sjögren syndrome**. **Mumps** is a viral sialadenitis (paramyxovirus), primarily affecting parotids, and can cause **pancreatitis** or **orchitis** in adults. A **mucocele** is the most common inflammatory salivary lesion, resulting from duct blockage/rupture, typically on the lower lip.

### Oesophageal Motility and Congenital Disorders
**Oesophageal atresia** is a congenital anomaly (blind pouch), often with a **tracheoesophageal fistula**, posing an aspiration risk. **Achalasia** involves incomplete LES relaxation, increased LES tone, and aperistalsis, characterized by a "**bird's beak**" appearance on barium swallow.

### Oesophageal Inflammation
**Infectious oesophagitis** affects immunocompromised patients: **HSV** causes "**punched-out**" ulcers with viral inclusions and giant cells; **CMV** causes shallower ulcers with nuclear and cytoplasmic inclusions; **Candida** causes adherent grey-white pseudomembranes. **Reflux Oesophagitis (GERD)** is the most frequent cause, leading to mucosal injury from gastric acid; histology shows **eosinophils**, **basal zone hyperplasia** (>20%), and elongated papillae. **Eosinophilic Oesophagitis** is characterized by large numbers of eosinophils in the oesophageal epithelium (especially superficially and away from the GEJ), often resistant to PPIs and associated with **atopy**.

### Barrett Oesophagus
A complication of chronic GERD, **Barrett oesophagus** is defined by **intestinal metaplasia** within the oesophageal squamous mucosa, significantly increasing the risk of **oesophageal adenocarcinoma**.

### Gastritis
**Acute gastritis** involves inflammation without mucosal injury (e.g., NSAIDs, alcohol, stress); severe cases can lead to **acute haemorrhagic erosive gastropathy**. **Chronic gastritis** is characterized by mucosal inflammation, atrophy, and metaplasia.

**H. pylori Gastritis:** This is the most common chronic gastritis, predominantly affects the **antrum**, is associated with increased acid secretion, and can lead to **PUD**, **MALT lymphoma**, and **adenocarcinoma**.

**Autoimmune Gastritis:** This affects the **body and fundus**, is associated with **anti-parietal cell** and **anti-intrinsic factor antibodies**, leading to **achlorhydria** (decreased acid) and **hypergastrinemia**. It is a major cause of **pernicious anaemia** and increases gastric cancer risk.

### Peptic Ulcer Disease (PUD) **Peptic Ulcer Disease (PUD)** is most often associated with **H. pylori infection** or **NSAID use**, commonly found in the gastric antrum and first portion of the duodenum (**duodenal ulcers are 4x more common**). It is characterized by "**punched-out**" defects, pain relieved by alkali or food, and complications including haemorrhage and perforation. **Zollinger-Ellison syndrome** causes multiple, severe ulcers due to excessive gastrin.

### Gastric Tumours
**Gastric Adenocarcinoma:** This has highly variable incidence worldwide; **H. pylori** is the most common etiologic agent. It is classified into **intestinal pattern** (bulky, ulcerated) and **diffuse pattern** (**signet ring cells**, **linitis plastica**).

**Primary Gastric Lymphoma (MALT):** Often induced by chronic **H. pylori** gastritis; **H. pylori eradication can lead to regression** of early MALT lymphoma.

**Carcinoid Tumour:** These arise from endocrine cells, are most common in the GI tract (small intestine most aggressive, appendix almost always benign). **Carcinoid syndrome** involves flushing, diarrhoea, and **right-sided cardiac valvular fibrosis**.

**Gastrointestinal Stromal Tumour (GIST):** This is the most common mesenchymal tumour of the abdomen, primarily in the stomach. It arises from **interstitial cells of Cajal** and often has activating mutations in **c-KIT** or **PDGFRA**, making them **responsive to kinase inhibitors like imatinib**.

## Detailed Notes

SECTION 1: ORAL CAVITY

## 1.1 Oral Inflammatory Lesions

### Aphthous Ulcers (Canker Sores)
**Aphthous ulcers** are the most common superficial mucosal ulcerations, affecting up to **40% of the population**. They are more common in the **first two decades of life**, are extremely painful, and recurrent. The cause is **unknown**, but they are associated with **coeliac disease**, **IBD**, and **Behçet disease**, and may be familial. Lesions can be solitary or multiple, appearing as shallow hyperaemic ulcerations covered by a thin exudate, rimmed by a narrow zone of erythema. They resolve spontaneously in **7–10 days** but recur. There is no specific proven treatment, only supportive care.

### Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV) Infections
Most orofacial infections are caused by **HSV-1**, though **HSV-2** is increasingly common with changing sexual practices. Primary infection typically occurs in children aged **2–4 years** and is usually **asymptomatic**. However, **10–20%** manifest as **acute herpetic gingivostomatitis**, characterized by the abrupt onset of vesicles and ulcerations. Most adults harbour latent HSV-1, leading to reactivation known as a "**cold sore**" or **herpes labialis**. Reactivation can be triggered by trauma, UV light, URTI, pregnancy, menstruation, immunosuppression, temperature extremes, or allergies. Common sites include the lips, nasal orifices, buccal mucosa, gingiva, and hard palate. These lesions resolve in **7–10 days** but can persist in immunocompromised individuals, requiring **systemic antiviral therapy**.

**Morphology:** Infected cells balloon, forming **large eosinophilic intranuclear inclusions**. Adjacent cells fuse to create **large multinucleated polykaryons** (giant cells). The lesions resemble those found in oesophageal and genital herpes.

### Oral Candidiasis (Thrush)
**Oral candidiasis** is the most common **fungal** infection of the oral cavity. **Candida albicans** is a normal oral flora, causing disease only under unusual circumstances. Predisposing factors include **immunosuppression**, specific strains of *C. albicans*, altered oral microbiota from broad-spectrum antibiotics, diabetes, and a debilitated state.

There are three clinical forms:
*   **Pseudomembranous (thrush)** – the most common form.
*   Erythematous.
*   Hyperplastic.

**Pseudomembranous thrush** presents as a superficial curd-like **grey-to-white** inflammatory membrane of matted organisms enmeshed in fibrinosuppurative exudate. This membrane can be **easily scraped off**, revealing an erythematous base. In cases of mild immunosuppression (e.g., diabetics), the infection is usually superficial; however, in severe immunosuppression, it can spread to deeper sites. A key distinction in examination is that thrush **scrapes off**, whereas leukoplakia does **NOT**.

## 1.2 Proliferative and Neoplastic Lesions

### Fibrous Proliferative Lesions

**Fibromas**
**Fibromas** are submucosal nodular fibrous tissue masses resulting from **chronic irritation**, leading to reactive connective tissue hyperplasia. They are most often found on the **buccal mucosa along the bite line**. Treatment involves complete surgical excision and removal of the irritant source.

**Pyogenic Granulomas**
**Pyogenic granulomas** are pedunculated masses typically found on the **gingiva** of children, young adults, and **pregnant women**. They are richly vascular and typically ulcerated, giving them a **red to purple colour**. Their rapid growth can raise concern for malignancy, but histology reveals dense immature vessel proliferation, resembling granulation tissue. These lesions can regress, mature into a dense fibrous mass, or develop into a **peripheral ossifying fibroma**. Treatment involves complete surgical excision.

### Leukoplakia and Erythroplakia

**Leukoplakia**
The WHO defines **leukoplakia** as a white patch or plaque that **cannot be scraped off** AND cannot be characterized clinically or pathologically as any other disease. The term is reserved for lesions with **no known aetiologic factor**. Approximately **3% of the world population** has leukoplakic lesions. Importantly, **5–25% are premalignant** and may progress to **SCC**, meaning **ALL leukoplakias must be considered precancerous** until proved otherwise by histologic evaluation.

**Erythroplakia**
**Erythroplakia** is a red, velvety, possibly eroded area that is flat or slightly depressed relative to the surrounding mucosa. It carries a **much greater risk of malignant transformation than leukoplakia**, with **>50%** of cases undergoing malignant transformation. This makes erythroplakia a higher risk lesion than leukoplakia, a critical point for examination.

**Both lesions:**
Both leukoplakia and erythroplakia typically affect adults **40–70 years** of age, with a **2:1 male preponderance**. The most common risk factor is **tobacco use** (cigarettes, pipes, cigars, chewing tobacco).
**Morphology:** The lesions exhibit a spectrum ranging from hyperkeratosis/acanthosis with orderly mucosa to marked dysplasia and carcinoma in situ. The most severe dysplastic changes are typically seen in erythroplakia. Increasing dysplasia is often accompanied by a subjacent **lymphocyte and macrophage infiltrate**.

### Squamous Cell Carcinoma (Oral SCC) **Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC)** accounts for **95% of oral cavity cancers**, with the remainder largely being salivary gland adenocarcinomas. It is the **6th most common neoplasm worldwide** and an aggressive malignancy. The overall long-term survival has remained **<50% for the past 50 years**, often due to diagnosis at an advanced stage. Multiple primary tumours are detected at a rate of **3–7% per year** after an initial diagnosis. Patients surviving 5 years have up to a **35% chance** of developing a new primary within that interval, and may die of a second primary tumour despite a small initial tumour. Therefore, surveillance and early detection of new premalignant lesions are **critical for long-term survival**. This phenomenon is known as "**field cancerisation**," where multiple independent primary tumours arise from years of chronic mucosal carcinogen exposure (alcohol, tobacco).

**Two Pathogenic Pathways:**

| Pathway           | Features                                                                         |
| :---------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Tobacco/alcohol** | Chronic users; mutations in **TP53**, **p63**, **NOTCH1**; molecular signature of tobacco carcinogens |
| **HPV-related**   | Tonsillar crypts/base of tongue; oncogenic **HPV-16**; fewer mutations; overexpress **p16**; **better prognosis**; not accessible to cytologic screening unlike cervix |

HPV-associated oropharyngeal SCC is predicted to **surpass cervical cancer incidence** in the next decade, with the HPV vaccine offering hope for prevention. In India and Southeast Asia, **betel quid and paan** (areca nut + slaked lime + tobacco in betel leaf) are important predisposing factors, following a similar pathway to tobacco-associated tumours.

**Morphology:** The most common locations for oral SCC are the **ventral surface of the tongue, floor of the mouth, lower lip, soft palate, and gingiva**. Early lesions appear as raised firm pearly plaques or irregular roughened/verrucous mucosal thickenings, which may be on a background of leukoplakia/erythroplakia. Late-stage lesions present as ulcerated protruding masses with **irregular indurated or rolled borders**. Histologically, they range from well-differentiated keratinizing tumours to anaplastic sarcomatoid tumours; the degree of differentiation does **NOT** correlate with biologic behaviour. The cancer spreads by infiltrating locally first, then to **cervical lymph nodes** (the most common regional metastasis), and finally to distant metastases in mediastinal lymph nodes.

## 1.3 Diseases of Salivary Glands

### Xerostomia
**Xerostomia**, defined as **dry mouth**, results from decreased saliva production. The most frequent cause is **medications**, including anticholinergic, antidepressant/antipsychotic, diuretic, antihypertensive, sedative, muscle relaxant, analgesic, and antihistaminic agents. It affects **>20% of individuals above age 70**. Xerostomia is a **major feature of Sjögren syndrome**, where it is usually accompanied by dry eyes (sicca syndrome). It is also a major complication of **radiation therapy** to the head and neck. Oral findings include dry mucosa, atrophy of tongue papillae, fissuring, and ulcerations. In Sjögren syndrome, concomitant inflammatory salivary gland enlargement may be present. Complications include increased dental caries, candidiasis, and difficulty swallowing and speaking.

### Sialadenitis

**Viral Sialadenitis — Mumps**
**Mumps** is the most common form of viral sialadenitis, caused by a **paramyxovirus** (related to influenza/parainfluenza). It predominantly involves the **parotid glands**, though all glands may enlarge. Histologically, it shows interstitial inflammation with a **mononuclear infiltrate**. In children, it is a self-limited benign condition. In adults, it can cause **pancreatitis** or **orchitis**, with orchitis sometimes leading to sterility.

**Mucocele**
A **mucocele** is the **most common inflammatory lesion of salivary glands**. It results from the **blockage or rupture of a salivary gland duct**, causing saliva to leak into the surrounding connective tissue stroma. Mucoceles are most common in toddlers, young adults, and the aged. They typically present as a **fluctuant swelling of the lower lip**, which changes size, especially around meals. Histology reveals a cyst-like space lined by inflammatory granulation tissue or fibrous connective tissue, filled with mucin and macrophages. Treatment involves complete excision of the cyst **and** the associated minor salivary gland lobule.

**Bacterial Sialadenitis**
**Bacterial sialadenitis** most often involves the **major salivary glands**, particularly the **submandibular glands**. The most frequent pathogens are **Staphylococcus aureus** and **Streptococcus viridans**. A common antecedent is **duct obstruction by stones (sialolithiasis)**, but it can also be caused by impacted food debris or injury-related oedema. Dehydration and decreased secretory function (e.g., in Sjögren syndrome) predispose individuals to bacterial sialadenitis, which is often seen in elderly, debilitated patients.

SECTION 2: OESOPHAGUS

## 2.1 Congenital and Motility Disorders

### Oesophageal Atresia and Tracheoesophageal Fistula
**Oesophageal atresia** is an abnormal developmental condition characterized by a **blind upper oesophageal pouch**, with the **distal oesophagus often arising from the trachea** (tracheoesophageal fistula being the most common variant). Infants present with regurgitation during feeding and aspiration. Surgery is curative.

### Achalasia
**Achalasia** is defined by incomplete LES relaxation, increased LES tone, and oesophageal aperistalsis. This leads to functional obstruction with proximal oesophageal dilatation.

**Primary achalasia** is the most common form, being idiopathic and involving the loss of inhibitory neurons (nitric oxide, VIP) in the oesophageal wall.

**Secondary achalasia** is less common and can be caused by:
*   **Chagas disease**, where *Trypanosoma cruzi* infection destroys enteric plexuses.
*   **Diabetic neuropathy, amyloidosis, polio, or autonomic neuropathy**.
*   **Malignancy**, such as infiltration of the myenteric plexus by SCC of the oesophagus, gastric carcinoma, or lymphoma.

**Clinical Features:**
*   **Dysphagia** to both solids and liquids.
*   Regurgitation of undigested food, which can cause aspiration.
*   Chest pain.
*   Weight loss.

**Diagnosis:** A barium swallow shows a "**bird's beak**" appearance of the narrowed LES. Manometry confirms aperistalsis and a non-relaxing LES.

**Complications:** These include oesophagitis, oesophageal ulceration, diverticula, and an increased risk of **oesophageal SCC** (5%).

Treatment options include Botox injection, pneumatic dilation, surgical myotomy, and calcium channel blockers (CCBs).

## 2.2 Oesophagitis

### Infectious Oesophagitis
**Infectious oesophagitis** most commonly affects **immunocompromised patients** (e.g., those with AIDS, transplant recipients, malignancy, or undergoing chemotherapy).

*   **HSV** causes "**punched-out ulcers**" with nuclear viral inclusions within a rim of degenerating epithelial cells at the ulcer edge, along with multinucleated giant cells.
*   **CMV** leads to **shallower ulcerations** with characteristic nuclear AND cytoplasmic inclusions within capillary endothelium and stromal cells.
*   **Candida** is the most common fungal pathogen, presenting in advanced form as adherent grey-white pseudomembranes of densely matted fungal hyphae and inflammatory cells over the oesophageal mucosa. Mucormycosis and aspergillosis are also possible.

Immunohistochemical staining for viral antigens serves as an ancillary diagnostic tool. Nonpathogenic oral bacteria are frequently found in ulcer beds, while pathogenic organisms account for approximately 10% of infectious oesophagitis cases.

### Reflux Oesophagitis (GERD) **Reflux oesophagitis (GERD)** is the **most frequent cause of oesophagitis** and the most common outpatient GI diagnosis. The stratified squamous epithelium of the oesophagus is resistant to abrasion but **sensitive to acid**. Normal protection is provided by mucin and bicarbonate from submucosal glands, and constant LES tone prevents reflux.

**Pathogenesis:** Reflux of gastric juices (and duodenal bile in severe cases) leads to mucosal injury. Factors that reduce LES tone or increase abdominal pressure include alcohol and tobacco use, obesity, CNS depressants, pregnancy, hiatal hernia, delayed gastric emptying, and increased gastric volume.

**Morphology (mild → severe):**
*   In mild GERD, the only alteration may be simple **hyperaemia** (redness on endoscopy), and mucosal histology is often unremarkable.
*   In more significant disease, **eosinophils are recruited** into the squamous mucosa, followed by **neutrophils** in cases of more severe injury.
*   Histological hallmarks include **basal zone hyperplasia** exceeding **20% of total epithelial thickness** and **elongation of lamina propria papillae** extending into the upper third of the epithelium.

**Clinical Features:** GERD is most common in **adults >40 years** but also occurs in infants and children. Symptoms include **heartburn, dysphagia**, and less often, regurgitation of sour-tasting gastric contents. Rarely, severe chest pain mimicking heart disease can occur. The severity of symptoms is **NOT closely related** to the degree of histologic damage. Treatment with **proton pump inhibitors** reduces gastric acidity and provides symptomatic relief. Complications include oesophageal ulceration, haematemesis, melena, stricture, and **Barrett oesophagus**.

### Hiatal Hernia
A **hiatal hernia** involves the separation of the diaphragmatic crura and protrusion of the stomach into the thorax through the resulting gap. It can be congenital (in infants/children) or acquired. It is **asymptomatic in >90% of adult cases**. When symptomatic, symptoms are similar to GERD and are associated with other causes of LES incompetence.

### Eosinophilic Oesophagitis
**Eosinophilic oesophagitis** presents in **adults** with food impaction and dysphagia, and in **children** with feeding intolerance or GERD-like symptoms. The **cardinal histologic feature** is epithelial infiltration by **large numbers of eosinophils**, particularly superficially and at sites **far from the gastroesophageal junction**, which helps distinguish it from GERD, Crohn's disease, and other forms of oesophagitis. The incidence is markedly increasing.

**Typical clinical characteristics:**
*   Failure of high-dose PPI treatment.
*   Absence of acid reflux.
*   The **majority are atopic**, presenting with atopic dermatitis, allergic rhinitis, asthma, and peripheral eosinophilia.

Treatment involves dietary restrictions (avoiding cow milk, soy products) and topical and systemic corticosteroids.

## 2.3 Barrett Oesophagus

**Barrett oesophagus** is a complication of **chronic GERD**, characterized by **intestinal metaplasia** within the oesophageal squamous mucosa. Its incidence is rising, estimated in up to **10% of persons with symptomatic GERD**. **White males** are most often affected, typically presenting between **40 and 60 years of age**. Diagnosis requires **endoscopic evidence of columnar mucosa** above the GEJ AND **histologic evidence of intestinal metaplasia** (goblet cells are key). Barrett oesophagus is a significant **risk factor for oesophageal adenocarcinoma**, with a 30- to 40-fold increased risk compared to the general population. Surveillance endoscopy with biopsies is standard for the detection of **dysplasia**. Low-grade dysplasia warrants surveillance, while high-grade dysplasia requires endoscopic ablation (radiofrequency, photodynamic therapy).

Grossly, it appears as patchy, tongues of red, velvety mucosa extending proximally from the GEJ, alternating with pale, smooth squamous mucosa. Histologically, the squamous epithelium is replaced by columnar cells containing **goblet cells** (intestinal metaplasia). Genetic changes include **p53 mutations**, aneuploidy, and increased proliferation.

SECTION 3: STOMACH

## 3.1 Acute Gastritis

**Acute gastritis** is an acute mucosal inflammatory process, usually transient. It involves inflammation without mucosal injury (erosion). When severe erosions and haemorrhage occur, it is termed **acute haemorrhagic erosive gastropathy**.

**Pathogenesis:** It results from an imbalance between mucosal protective factors (mucin, bicarbonate, blood flow, prostaglandins, epithelial regeneration) and damaging factors (acid, pepsin).

**Risk factors:**
*   **NSAIDs:** The most common cause; they inhibit COX, leading to decreased prostaglandins, which in turn reduces mucin, bicarbonate, and blood flow.
*   **Alcohol:** Disrupts the mucosal barrier.
*   **Stress:** Severe trauma, burns, sepsis, shock, surgery, and increased intracranial pressure can lead to "stress ulcers."
*   **Ischaemia/shock**.
*   **H. pylori infection:** In its acute phase.
*   **Direct mucosal injury:** Bile reflux, irradiation, cytotoxic chemotherapy.

**Clinical Features:** These are variable, ranging from asymptomatic to mild epigastric pain, nausea, and vomiting. Severe cases can present with haematemesis ("coffee-ground emesis"), melena, or even massive haemorrhage.

**Morphology:** Histology shows oedema, hyperaemia, and scattered neutrophils in the mucosa. Erosions (loss of epithelium) and haemorrhage are characteristic of acute haemorrhagic erosive gastropathy.

## 3.2 Chronic Gastritis

**Chronic gastritis** is characterized by chronic mucosal inflammation, leading to mucosal atrophy and intestinal metaplasia.

The **most important causes** are:
*   **H. pylori infection:** The most common cause worldwide.
*   **Autoimmune gastritis:** Accounts for <10% of cases.

### H. pylori Gastritis
**H. pylori gastritis** is the most common cause of chronic gastritis. **H. pylori** is a spiral-shaped, flagellated, gram-negative bacterium. It is associated with **PUD**, **gastric adenocarcinoma**, and **MALT lymphoma**. Infection is typically acquired in childhood and often persists lifelong.

**Virulence factors:**
*   **Urease:** Converts urea to ammonia, which neutralizes gastric acid locally and protects the bacteria.
*   **Flagella:** Allow movement in viscous mucus.
*   **Adhesins:** Bind to gastric epithelial cells.
*   **Toxins:** CagA (cytotoxin-associated gene A) and VacA (vacuolating cytotoxin A) contribute to host cell injury.

**Pathogenesis:** *H. pylori* induces intense inflammatory and immune responses.

**Morphology:** It typically affects the **antrum predominantly**. There is an intense inflammatory infiltrate, including neutrophils (indicating active inflammation), plasma cells, lymphocytes, and macrophages. **Intestinal metaplasia** is frequently present.

**Clinical Features:** Most individuals are asymptomatic.
**Diagnosis:** This can be made via urea breath test, stool antigen test, serology (antibodies), or endoscopic biopsy (histology, rapid urease test, culture).
**Treatment:** Antibiotics combined with proton pump inhibitor (PPI) therapy lead to eradication, which reverses gastritis and prevents recurrence of PUD.

### Autoimmune Gastritis
**Autoimmune gastritis** accounts for <10% of chronic gastritis cases. It involves **immune-mediated destruction of parietal cells** (located in the body/fundus) and is associated with other autoimmune diseases (e.g., Hashimoto thyroiditis, Addison disease). The **hallmark** is the presence of antibodies to parietal cells (H+/K+-ATPase) and/or intrinsic factor.

**Pathogenesis:** The loss of parietal cells leads to:
*   **Achlorhydria** (lack of gastric acid), which stimulates gastrin release from G cells, resulting in **hypergastrinemia**.
*   **Lack of intrinsic factor**, leading to impaired vitamin B12 absorption and subsequently **pernicious anaemia**.

**Morphology:** There is diffuse mucosal damage in the **body and fundus**. Histology shows a deep lympho-plasmacytic infiltrate and loss of parietal and chief cells. **Intestinal metaplasia** and **endocrine cell hyperplasia** (due to hypergastrinemia) are common.

**Clinical Features:** It often presents in older adults (median age 60) and progresses over decades. Symptoms include anaemia (fatigue, weakness) and neurological symptoms (due to B12 deficiency).
**Complications:** These include pernicious anaemia and a significantly increased risk of **gastric adenocarcinoma** and **carcinoid tumours**.

### Gastric Atrophy (Atrophic Gastritis)
**Gastric atrophy** is the end stage of chronic gastritis (both *H. pylori* and autoimmune). It is characterized by a marked reduction in mucosal thickness and glandular elements. It is associated with **intestinal metaplasia**, where gastric mucosa transforms into intestinal-type epithelium. This condition carries an increased risk of **dysplasia and adenocarcinoma**. Parietal and chief cell loss are prominent, and intestinal metaplasia may develop.

### H. pylori vs Autoimmune Gastritis — Comparison

| Feature           | H. pylori                                | Autoimmune                               |
| :---------------- | :--------------------------------------- | :--------------------------------------- |
| Location          | **Antrum** (predominantly)                   | **Body and fundus**                          |
| Acid secretion    | **Increased** (antral)                       | **Decreased (achlorhydria)**                 |
| Gastrin levels    | **Low (hypogastrinemia)**                    | **High (hypergastrinemia)**                  |
| Antibodies        | None specific                            | **Anti-parietal cell, anti-intrinsic factor** |
| Pernicious anaemia | No                                       | **Yes**                                      |
| Atrophic gastritis | Yes (multifocal)                         | Yes (diffuse, body/fundus)               |
| Intestinal metaplasia | Yes                                      | Yes                                      |
| Gastric cancer risk | Increased                                | Increased                                |
| Inflammatory pattern | Superficial; **neutrophils + plasma cells** | Deep; **lymphocytes + macrophages**          |

## 3.4 Peptic Ulcer Disease (PUD) **Peptic Ulcer Disease (PUD)** is most often associated with **H. pylori infection** or **NSAID use**. It is most commonly found in the **gastric antrum and first portion of the duodenum**. PUD can also occur in the oesophagus (from GERD/ectopic gastric mucosa) or small intestine (gastric heterotopia in Meckel diverticulum). The lifetime risk is approximately **10% for males** and **4% for females**.

**Pathogenesis:** PUD results from an imbalance between mucosal defences and damaging forces, often on a background of chronic gastritis. **>70% of PUD cases are associated with H. pylori**, but only **5–10% of H. pylori-infected persons** develop ulcers, indicating that host factors and strain variation determine the outcome. Gastric hyperacidity is fundamental, caused by *H. pylori* infection, parietal cell hyperplasia, excessive secretory responses, or impaired inhibition of gastrin release. **Zollinger-Ellison syndrome**, characterized by uncontrolled gastrin release from a gastrinoma, leads to massive acid production and multiple peptic ulcerations in the stomach, duodenum, and even the **jejunum**. Cofactors include chronic NSAID use, cigarette smoking, and high-dose corticosteroids. PUD is also more frequent in individuals with alcoholic cirrhosis, COPD, chronic renal failure, and hyperparathyroidism (where hypercalcaemia stimulates gastrin, increasing acid). Psychological stress may exacerbate PUD.

**Morphology:** **Duodenal ulcers** are **4 times more common** than gastric ulcers, typically found within a few centimetres of the pyloric valve, often on the **anterior duodenal wall**. Gastric peptic ulcers predominantly occur near the **interface of the body and antrum**. They are **solitary in >80%** of patients. The classic appearance is a **round to oval, sharply punched-out defect** with a **smooth, clean base** due to peptic digestion of exudate. Lesions less than 0.3 cm are considered shallow, while those greater than 0.6 cm are likely deeper. Histologically, the base consists of richly vascular **granulation tissue**. Ongoing bleeding can lead to life-threatening haemorrhage, and perforation demands emergent surgical intervention.

**Clinical Features:** PUD involves chronic, recurring lesions, typically affecting middle-aged to older adults. The primary symptom is **epigastric burning or aching pain**, which occurs **1–3 hours after meals**, is worse at night, and is **relieved by alkali or food**. Other symptoms include nausea, vomiting, bloating, and belching. Complications include iron-deficiency anaemia, frank haemorrhage, and perforation. The tendency to develop ulcers remains even after healing. Treatment involves **H. pylori eradication (antibiotics + PPI)**; surgery is reserved for bleeding or perforated ulcers. PUD causes much more **morbidity than mortality**.

## 3.5 Gastric Tumours

### Gastric Polyps
**Inflammatory and hyperplastic polyps** are reactive lesions associated with chronic gastritis; the risk of dysplasia increases with polyp size. **Gastric adenomas** develop in the background of chronic gastritis, particularly associated with intestinal metaplasia and mucosal atrophy. **Adenocarcinoma frequently arises in gastric adenomas**, requiring **complete excision and surveillance**.

### Gastric Adenocarcinoma
The incidence of **gastric adenocarcinoma** varies markedly with geography and is more common in lower socioeconomic groups. **H. pylori infection** is the most common aetiologic agent. Other associations include chronic atrophic gastritis and **EBV infection**, which can lead to multiple pathways of neoplastic transformation.

**Classification:**

| Type                       | Features                                                                         |
| :------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Intestinal pattern**     | Forms bulky tumours; may be ulcerated                                            |
| **Diffuse (signet ring cells)** | Diffuse infiltrative growth → thickens gastric wall without discrete mass = **linitis plastica** |

**Clinical Features:** These include abdominal swelling, abdominal pain, vomiting, and gastric outlet obstruction.

### Primary Gastric Lymphoma
**Primary gastric lymphoma** is most often derived from **MALT** (mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue). MALT development is induced by **chronic gastritis** (H. pylori). Importantly, **H. pylori eradication can lead to regression of early MALT lymphoma**.

### Carcinoid Tumour
**Carcinoid tumours** arise from diffuse components of the endocrine system. They are most common in the **gastrointestinal tract**, particularly the **small intestine**. The **most important prognostic factor is location**: small intestine tumours are the most aggressive, while appendix tumours are almost always benign. **Carcinoid syndrome**, caused by vasoactive substances released by the tumour, involves cutaneous flushing, sweating, bronchospasm, colicky abdominal pain, diarrhoea, and **right-sided cardiac valvular fibrosis**.

### Gastrointestinal Stromal Tumour (GIST)
A **Gastrointestinal Stromal Tumour (GIST)** is the **most common mesenchymal tumour of the abdomen**. It occurs most often in the **stomach** and arises from **interstitial cells of Cajal** (pacemaker cells). The **majority have activating mutations in c-KIT or PDGFRA tyrosine kinases**, making them **responsive to kinase inhibitors** (e.g., imatinib). A key examination point is that GISTs with a **c-KIT mutation** respond to **imatinib**.
