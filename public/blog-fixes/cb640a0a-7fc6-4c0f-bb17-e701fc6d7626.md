## SECTION 5: Cytology & Cytopathology, Special Techniques, IHC, Vitamins, Trace Elements & PEM (Pages 405–566)

## 1. Cytology vs Cytopathology

**Cytology:** The branch of biology studying cell structure, function, and chemistry in both normal and pathological states.

**Cytopathology:** A subspecialty of pathology that diagnoses disease by examining cells from body fluids or tissue samples — looking for changes in cell morphology that indicate cancer, infection, or inflammation.

**Key distinction:** Cytology is the broader science; cytopathology is the clinical diagnostic application.

### Cytology Slide Preparation — Steps
1. **Sample collection** — scraping, brushing, aspiration, or fluid collection
2. **Fixation** — alcohol or methanol immediately applied to prevent degradation
3. **Smearing** — thin uniform layer spread on a glass slide
4. **Staining** — Pap stain (gynaecological/general), Wright's/Giemsa (haematology), H&E (general)
5. **Air drying and coverslipping**
6. **Microscopic examination** — low to high magnification
7. **Interpretation and reporting** — cytopathologist identifies abnormalities, issues a diagnostic report

### Advantages of Cytopathology
- Minimally invasive compared to surgical biopsy
- Cost-effective and relatively quick
- Used in screening programmes (e.g., cervical cancer via Pap smear)

### Limitations
- No tissue architecture — only individual cells are seen
- May miss disease if sample is non-representative
- False negatives possible in early or focal disease

## 2. Exfoliative Cytology (Topic VII)

**Definition:** Examination of cells that have been naturally shed from body surfaces or collected by scraping/washing from mucosal surfaces.

### Techniques and Applications

**Pap Smear (Papanicolaou Smear):**
- Purpose: Cervical cancer screening
- Procedure: Spatula or brush scrapes cells from the cervical transformation zone; spread on a slide and stained with Pap stain
- Detects: Cervical dysplasia (pre-cancer) and invasive cervical cancer
- Reporting system: Bethesda system — NILM (normal), ASCUS, LSIL, HSIL, carcinoma

**Sputum Cytology:**
- Purpose: Diagnose lung disease, mainly cancer
- Procedure: Patient expectorates sputum; cells examined for malignant changes
- Detects: Squamous cell carcinoma of lung (most amenable to sputum cytology)

**Urine Cytology:**
- Purpose: Urinary tract evaluation
- Procedure: Voided urine or catheter specimen processed, concentrated, and stained
- Detects: Transitional (urothelial) cell carcinoma of the bladder

**FNA (as an exfoliative technique):**
- Aspirates cells from a mass using a thin needle
- Used for thyroid, lymph nodes, breast, salivary glands

**Body Fluid Cytology:**
- Examines cells in pleural, peritoneal, pericardial, CSF, synovial fluids
- Detects: Malignant effusions, infections, inflammatory conditions

### Principles of Exfoliative Cytology
- Cells collected from where they naturally shed or are accessible
- Fixed, stained, then examined for changes in: cell size, shape, nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio, nuclear hyperchromasia, abnormal mitoses, and arrangement

## 3. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) — Detailed

**Definition:** A laboratory technique using antigen-antibody binding to detect specific proteins (antigens) in tissue sections, allowing their localisation to be visualised under a microscope.

### Core Principle
- A **primary antibody** binds specifically to the target antigen in the tissue
- A **secondary antibody** (conjugated to an enzyme or fluorophore) binds to the primary antibody to amplify and visualise the signal
- **Detection:** Enzyme-based (HRP + DAB substrate → brown precipitate) or fluorescent (fluorophore emits light)
- **Counterstain:** Haematoxylin applied to show tissue architecture in blue/purple contrast

### Full IHC Process (Step by Step)

| Step | Detail |
|---|---|
| 1. Fixation | Formalin (10% NBF) — preserves morphology |
| 2. Embedding | Paraffin wax blocks |
| 3. Sectioning | Microtome, 3–5 µm sections on glass slides |
| 4. Deparaffinisation | Heated + xylene to remove wax; rehydrated through alcohol series |
| 5. Antigen retrieval | HIER (heat in buffer — microwave or water bath) or enzymatic retrieval — unmasks antigens masked by fixation |
| 6. Blocking | Serum applied to prevent non-specific antibody binding |
| 7. Primary antibody | Applied and incubated (1 hour to overnight) |
| 8. Washing | Buffer (PBS or TBS) to remove unbound antibody |
| 9. Secondary antibody | Conjugated antibody applied; shorter incubation |
| 10. Detection | DAB substrate → brown colour where antigen present |
| 11. Counterstaining | Haematoxylin → blue nuclei for background |
| 12. Dehydration & mounting | Alcohol series, xylene, coverslip applied |
| 13. Microscopy | Analysis and image capture |

### Types of IHC

**By detection method:**
- **Chromogenic** — enzyme-linked (HRP/AP + substrate); produces coloured precipitate; used in routine diagnostics
- **Fluorescent** — fluorophore-conjugated; allows multiplexing (multiple targets simultaneously)

**By antibody type:**
- **Monoclonal** — single clone, highly specific, one epitope (e.g., ER, HER2)
- **Polyclonal** — multiple clones, higher sensitivity, less specific

**By target:**
- **Tumour markers** — Ki-67 (proliferation), p53 (tumour suppressor), HER2 (breast cancer)
- **Cell lineage markers** — cytokeratins (epithelial), CD34 (haematopoietic stem cells), CD68 (macrophages), CD markers (lymphocytes)

**Special methods:**
- **Dual/multiplex IHC** — detects multiple antigens on same section
- **Tissue Microarray (TMA)** — many tissue cores on one slide; high-throughput analysis
- **ISH (In Situ Hybridisation)** — detects RNA/DNA targets in tissue; often combined with IHC

### Quality Control in IHC

| QC Area | Key Requirement |
|---|---|
| Antibody validation | Characterised, validated antibodies with correct specificity |
| Fixation consistency | Standardised formalin time (over-fixation blocks antigen retrieval) |
| Antigen retrieval | Standardised HIER protocol — same buffer, temperature, time |
| Controls | Positive and negative control slides on every run |
| Sectioning | Uniform 3–5 µm thickness; no folds/tears |
| Blocking | Effective blocking to eliminate background |
| Documentation | SOPs, lot numbers, incubation times all recorded |
| Staff | Trained personnel; periodic competency assessment |
| Equipment | Regular microtome and microscope calibration/maintenance |
| Interpretation | Blinded scoring where possible; consistent microscopy settings |

## 4. Special Techniques in Cytology (Topic X)

### Liquid-Based Cytology (LBC)
- Cells collected into a liquid preservative medium (e.g., ThinPrep, SurePath) instead of directly smeared
- Advantages: Better preservation, less obscuring debris, reduced cellular overlap, residual sample available for ancillary tests (HPV testing, ICC)
- Replaces conventional Pap smears in many modern cervical screening programmes

### Immunocytochemistry (ICC)
- Same principle as IHC but applied to cytology preparations (smears, LBC, cell blocks)
- Identifies specific antigens in individual cells
- Used to determine cell lineage (e.g., carcinoma vs lymphoma vs mesothelioma in effusions)

### Fluorescence In Situ Hybridisation (FISH)
- Uses fluorescent DNA probes that bind to complementary sequences on specific chromosomes
- Detects chromosomal abnormalities: deletions, amplifications, translocations
- Applications:
  - HER2 amplification in breast cancer
  - BCR-ABL translocation (Philadelphia chromosome) in CML
  - ALK rearrangement in lung adenocarcinoma
  - Urine FISH for bladder cancer detection
- **Dual-colour FISH** — two different probes simultaneously detect two targets; identifies specific rearrangements

### Comparative Genomic Hybridisation (CGH)
- Detects gains and losses of chromosomal DNA across the entire genome
- Used in cancer genetics and investigation of genetic disorders

### Karyotyping / Cytogenetic Analysis
- Examines number and structure of chromosomes
- Identifies aneuploidy, translocations, inversions
- Useful in haematological malignancies, genetic disorders, prenatal diagnosis

### Papanicolaou (Pap) Stain
- Multi-chromatic stain giving excellent nuclear detail
- Stains: nuclei dark blue/black (haematoxylin); cytoplasm shades of pink, green, orange depending on cell type and maturity
- Widely used in gynaecological cytology and body fluids

### Giemsa Stain
- Air-dried preparations; stains cytoplasm well
- Used in FNA, blood films, bone marrow smears
- Excellent for haematological cells and identifying microorganisms

### Rapid On-Site Evaluation (ROSE)
- Cytologist assesses FNA samples immediately during the procedure
- Confirms adequate cellularity before the patient leaves
- Reduces non-diagnostic rates and need for repeat procedures

### Electron Microscopy (EM)
- Ultra-high resolution imaging of cellular ultrastructure
- Visualises organelles at nm resolution — not achievable with light microscopy
- Applications in cytology:
  - Diagnosis of tumours with specific ultrastructural features (e.g., Weibel-Palade bodies in angiosarcoma, neurosecretory granules in neuroendocrine tumours)
  - Viral identification (virus morphology)
  - Ciliary defects (primary ciliary dyskinesia)
  - Renal biopsy interpretation (podocyte effacement in nephrotic syndrome)

### Flow Cytometry

**Principle:**
- Cells suspended in fluid stream pass single-file through a laser beam
- **Forward scatter (FSC)** — correlates with cell size
- **Side scatter (SSC)** — correlates with internal granularity/complexity
- Fluorescent-labelled antibodies bind surface/intracellular markers; lasers excite fluorophores → emitted light detected and analysed

**Key Applications:**
- **Immunophenotyping** — identify and classify cell populations (B vs T cells, mature vs immature) — essential for leukaemia/lymphoma diagnosis and classification
- **Cell counting** — rapid; used in haematology analysers
- **Cell cycle analysis** — determines proportion of cells in G0/G1, S, G2/M phases
- **Apoptosis detection** — Annexin V staining
- **CD4 counting** — monitors HIV disease progression and treatment
- **Stem cell identification** — CD34+ cells for transplant
- **Transplant monitoring** — immune cell populations post-transplant
- **Vaccine research** — measures immune response to vaccination

### Image Analysis Systems
- Automated algorithms analyse cytological slides
- Improve throughput and consistency; reduce inter-observer variability
- Used in cervical screening programmes (liquid-based cytology automation)

## 5. Vitamins — Deficiencies

### Water-Soluble Vitamins

| Vitamin | Deficiency Disease | Key Features |
|---|---|---|
| B1 (Thiamine) | Beriberi / Wernicke-Korsakoff | Wet beriberi: cardiac failure; Dry beriberi: peripheral neuropathy; Wernicke's: confusion, ophthalmoplegia, ataxia |
| B2 (Riboflavin) | Ariboflavinosis | Angular stomatitis, cheilosis, glossitis |
| B3 (Niacin) | Pellagra | 3 Ds: Dermatitis, Diarrhoea, Dementia |
| B6 (Pyridoxine) | Peripheral neuropathy | Especially in INH (anti-TB drug) use |
| B9 (Folate) | Megaloblastic anaemia | Neural tube defects in pregnancy |
| B12 (Cobalamin) | Megaloblastic anaemia + subacute combined degeneration of spinal cord | Neurological damage; causes: pernicious anaemia, strict veganism |
| C (Ascorbic acid) | Scurvy | Perifollicular haemorrhages, gum disease, poor wound healing, corkscrew hairs |

### Fat-Soluble Vitamins (A, D, E, K)

| Vitamin | Deficiency | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| A (Retinol) | Night blindness, xerophthalmia, Bitot's spots | Increased susceptibility to infections |
| D (Calciferol) | Rickets (children), Osteomalacia (adults) | Defective bone mineralisation; low Ca²⁺, low PO₄³⁻, raised ALP |
| E (Tocopherol) | Haemolytic anaemia, neuropathy | Rare; seen in fat malabsorption |
| K (Phylloquinone) | Bleeding tendency | Impaired clotting factor synthesis (II, VII, IX, X); prolonged PT |

### Etiology of Vitamin Deficiencies
- Inadequate dietary intake (poor diet, poverty, famine)
- Malabsorption (coeliac disease, Crohn's, chronic pancreatitis)
- Increased requirements (pregnancy, lactation, rapid growth)
- Chronic disease (liver, kidney, cancer)
- Alcoholism (especially B vitamins)
- Drug interactions (e.g., methotrexate → folate; INH → B6; anticonvulsants → D and K)

### Laboratory Evaluation
- Serum vitamin levels (B12, folate, vitamin D, vitamin A)
- CBC — macrocytic anaemia (B12/folate); normocytic or haemolytic (Vit E)
- Prothrombin time — prolonged in vitamin K deficiency
- Homocysteine and methylmalonic acid — elevated in B12 deficiency (functional tests)
- 24-hour urine — for B6 assessment
- Bone profile (Ca²⁺, PO₄³⁻, ALP) — for vitamin D deficiency

## 6. Trace Elements

### Types and Disorders

| Element | Deficiency | Excess |
|---|---|---|
| **Iron** | Iron deficiency anaemia — fatigue, pallor, koilonychia, low ferritin, high TIBC | Haemochromatosis — liver disease, diabetes, cardiomyopathy, bronze skin; ferritin very high, transferrin saturation >60% |
| **Zinc** | Impaired wound healing, immune dysfunction, taste/smell loss, skin lesions, growth retardation | Interferes with copper absorption → copper deficiency |
| **Copper** | Anaemia, connective tissue abnormalities, neurological symptoms | Wilson's disease — liver + brain damage, Kayser-Fleischer rings, psychiatric symptoms; low caeruloplasmin |
| **Selenium** | Keshan disease (cardiomyopathy), impaired immunity | Selenosis — GI, neurological, respiratory symptoms |
| **Iodine** | Goitre, hypothyroidism, cretinism (congenital) | Hyperthyroidism, thyroiditis |
| **Manganese** | Bone and glucose metabolism defects | Neurological symptoms resembling Parkinson's disease |

### Lab Evaluation of Trace Element Disorders
- **Iron:** Serum iron, ferritin, TIBC, transferrin saturation
- **Copper:** Serum caeruloplasmin (low in Wilson's), 24-hour urinary copper (high in Wilson's), liver biopsy copper quantification
- **Zinc:** Serum zinc
- **Selenium:** Serum selenium
- **Genetic testing** — Wilson's disease (ATP7B mutation), haemochromatosis (HFE gene — C282Y)
- **Tissue biopsy** — copper or iron quantification in liver

## 7. Protein-Energy Malnutrition (PEM)

### Types

**Kwashiorkor:**
- Cause: Severe protein deficiency with near-adequate calorie intake
- Common in: regions of Africa and Asia with low-quality protein diets
- Pathophysiology: Hypoalbuminaemia → reduced oncotic pressure → oedema; fatty liver from impaired lipoprotein synthesis
- Clinical features: Bilateral pitting oedema (hallmark), distended abdomen, hepatomegaly, skin changes (flaky paint dermatosis — hypo and hyperpigmentation patches), thin brittle hair that may depigment, growth retardation
- Labs: Low serum albumin; electrolyte imbalances; low pre-albumin

**Marasmus:**
- Cause: Severe deficiency of both calories AND protein (total starvation)
- Common in: extreme poverty, famine, neglect, chronic illness
- Pathophysiology: Body catabolises fat and muscle for energy; metabolic adaptation to conserve vital organs
- Clinical features: Severe generalised wasting — "skin and bones" appearance; loss of subcutaneous fat; muscle wasting; old man facies (loss of buccal fat pad); no oedema; growth failure, weakness
- Labs: Serum albumin often normal (unlike kwashiorkor); other markers of starvation

**Marasmic Kwashiorkor:**
- Combined — wasting (marasmus features) plus oedema (kwashiorkor features)
- Worst prognosis of the three types

**Secondary PEM:**
- Results from chronic illness, infection, GI malabsorption rather than dietary inadequacy alone

### Management Principles
- Gradual nutritional rehabilitation (refeeding syndrome risk if too rapid — hypophosphataemia, cardiac failure)
- Correct underlying infection or disease
- Micronutrient supplementation (zinc, potassium, magnesium, vitamins)
- Monitor electrolytes closely during refeeding
