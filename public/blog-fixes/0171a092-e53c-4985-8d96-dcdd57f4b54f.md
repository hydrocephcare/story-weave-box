## NEUROPATHOLOGY — Complete Study Notes
## Unit: CNS Cellular Pathology, Malformations & Cerebral Edema

## PART 1: CELLULAR PATHOLOGY OF THE CNS

## The Neuron — The Functional Unit
The neuron is the principal functional unit of the CNS. Key characteristics:
- **Postmitotic** — cannot divide or replace themselves once destroyed
- **Highly metabolically active** — require continuous oxygen and glucose
- **Selectively vulnerable** — different neuron populations are vulnerable to different insults based on shared properties (neurotransmitters, metabolic needs, connectivity)
- **Long-lived** — their lengthy lifespan makes them unusually prone to accumulation of **misfolded proteins**, triggering the **unfolded protein response**, central to many neurodegenerative diseases
> 💡 **Key Concept:** Because neurons cannot regenerate, even small focal losses can produce permanent neurological deficits. Clinical signs depend on **both** the pathological process **AND** the anatomical region affected.

## Reactions of Neurons to Injury
### 1. Acute Neuronal Injury — "Red Neurons"
Seen after **acute hypoxia/ischemia, hypoglycemia, or trauma**
- Earliest morphological marker of neuronal cell death
- Changes visible by **6–12 hours** after irreversible injury
**Morphological Features:**
| Feature | Change |
|---|---|
| Cell body | Shrinkage |
| Nucleus | Pyknosis (dark, shrunken) |
| Nucleolus | Disappears |
| Nissl substance | Lost |
| Cytoplasm | Intensely **eosinophilic** (hence "red") |

### 2. Subacute/Chronic Neuronal Injury — "Degeneration"
Occurs over **months to years** in progressive diseases e.g. ALS, Alzheimer disease
**Sequence of events:**
1. Loss of **synapses** — aberrant synaptic pruning
2. **Neuronal cell death** — selective for functionally related groups
3. **Reactive gliosis**
- At early stages, **glial changes are the best indicator** of neuronal injury
- Predominant cell death mechanism: **Apoptosis**

### 3. Axonal Reaction — Chromatolysis
Occurs during **axonal regeneration** after axon cutting or damage. Best seen in **anterior horn cells of the spinal cord**
**Morphological Features:**
- Cell body **enlarges and rounds up**
- Nucleus displaced to **periphery**
- Nucleolus **enlarges**
- Nissl substance disperses to periphery → **central chromatolysis**
> 💡 Reflects increased protein synthesis for axonal repair

### 4. Neuronal Inclusions
| Inclusion | Disease/Context |
|---|---|
| **Lipofuscin** | Normal aging |
| **Neurofibrillary tangles** | Alzheimer disease |
| **Lewy bodies** | Parkinson disease |
| **Cowdry A/B bodies** | Herpes virus infection |
| **Negri bodies** | Rabies |
| **CMV inclusions** | Cytomegalovirus infection |
| **Vacuolization** | Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (prion) |
> 💡 **Wallerian Degeneration** = degeneration of axon **distal** to the site of nerve fiber disruption

## Reactions of Astrocytes to Injury
### Structure and Function
- Star-shaped cells with multipolar branching processes
- Express **GFAP** (Glial Fibrillary Acidic Protein) — specific astrocyte marker
- **Functions:**
  - Metabolic buffering and detoxification
  - Blood-brain barrier maintenance via **foot processes** around capillaries
  - Extend to subpial and subependymal zones

### Gliosis
**Most important histopathologic marker of CNS injury, regardless of cause**
- Characterized by **hypertrophy and hyperplasia** of astrocytes
- Reactive astrocytes = **gemistocytic astrocytes**
  - Bright pink cytoplasm (↑ GFAP)
  - Large vesicular nuclei
  - Stout ramifying processes
> Two subtypes exist (morphologically identical but functionally distinct):
> - **Type A** → promotes CNS injury
> - **Type B** → contributes to CNS repair

### Special Astrocytic Pathological Forms
**Alzheimer Type II Astrocyte** *(not related to Alzheimer disease)*
- Large nucleus (2–3× normal), pale chromatin, intranuclear glycogen droplet, prominent nucleolus
- Seen in **hyperammonemia**: chronic liver disease, Wilson disease, urea cycle disorders

**Rosenthal Fibers**
- Thick, elongated, eosinophilic structures in astrocytic processes
- Contain: **αB-crystallin, hsp27, ubiquitin**
- Found in: long-standing gliosis, **pilocytic astrocytoma**
- Abundant in **Alexander disease** (GFAP gene mutation — leukodystrophy)
- Located periventricularly, perivascularly, subpially

**Corpora Amylacea (Polyglucosan Bodies)**
- Round, faintly basophilic, **PAS-positive**, concentrically lamellated (5–50 μm)
- Located at astrocytic end processes — subpial and perivascular zones
- Contain: glycosaminoglycan polymers, heat-shock proteins, ubiquitin
- Increase with **advancing age** — represent degenerative astrocyte change
- Similar structure to **Lafora bodies** (neurons, hepatocytes, myocytes in myoclonic epilepsy)

## Reactions of Microglia to Injury
### Normal Function
- **Phagocytic cells** — resident macrophages of the CNS
- Derived from **yolk sac or fetal liver** early in embryonic development
- Share surface markers with bone marrow–derived monocytes/macrophages
- At rest: **tiled** arrangement (non-overlapping territories)
- During development: **prune unused synaptic connections** via complement system
> ⚠️ Aberrant reactivation of microglial synaptic pruning implicated in **schizophrenia, Alzheimer disease, frontotemporal dementia, and encephalitis**
### Responses to Injury
Microglia respond by:
1. **Proliferating**
2. Developing **elongated nuclei**
3. Forming **microglial nodules** around small necrotic foci
4. **Neuronophagia** — congregating around dying neurons

## Reactions of Other Glial Cells
### Oligodendrocytes
- Form **myelin** by wrapping processes around axons
- One oligodendrocyte myelinates **multiple axons** (vs. Schwann cells in PNS — only one axon each)
- Injury → feature of **demyelinating disorders and leukodystrophies**
- **PML** → viral inclusions in oligodendroglial nuclei
- **Multiple System Atrophy (MSA)** → α-synuclein glial cytoplasmic inclusions in oligodendrocytes
### Ependymal Cells
- Ciliated columnar cells lining the ventricles
- No specific reaction pattern
- **Ependymal granulations** — disruption of lining by inflammation/dilation → subependymal astrocyte proliferation
- **CMV** → extensive ependymal injury with viral inclusions

## PART 2: MALFORMATIONS AND DEVELOPMENTAL DISORDERS

## Etiology
- Both **genetic and environmental** factors involved
- Many toxic compounds and infectious agents are **teratogenic**
> ⚠️ **Golden Rule:** The earlier in development a malformation occurs → the **more severe** the morphological and functional phenotype

## Neural Tube Defects (NTDs)
**Most common CNS malformations.** Two pathogenic mechanisms:
| Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|
| Failure of neural tube closure → secondary skeletal defects | Anencephaly, Myelomeningocele |
| Primary bony defects → secondary CNS abnormality | Encephalocele, Meningocele, Spina bifida |

### Specific NTDs
**Anencephaly**
- Malformation of **anterior neural tube**
- Forebrain development disrupted at ~**28 days gestation**
- Absence of most brain and calvarium
- Remnant = **area cerebrovasculosa** (disorganized brain tissue with ependyma, choroid plexus, meningothelial cells)

**Myelomeningocele (Meningomyelocele)**
- Extension of **CNS tissue** through vertebral column defect
- Most common in **lumbosacral region**
- *Meningocele* = only meningeal extrusion (no neural tissue)
- **Clinical features:** Lower limb motor/sensory deficits, bowel and bladder dysfunction
- **Complication:** Superimposed infection due to thin overlying skin

**Encephalocele**
- Extrusion of **malformed brain tissue** through midline cranial defect
- Most common in the **occiput**
- Nasofrontal variants sometimes misleadingly called **"nasal glioma"**

**Spina Bifida**
- Most common NTD
- Ranges from **spina bifida occulta** (asymptomatic bony defect) to severe malformation with meningeal outpouching

### Risk Factors and Prevention
- **Folate deficiency** during early gestation — well-established risk factor
- Neural tube closure complete by **day 28** — before most pregnancies are recognized
- **Folate supplementation must be given throughout reproductive years**
- Mechanism: effects on **DNA methylation** (epigenetic gene regulation) suspected
- Recurrence rate in subsequent pregnancies: **4–5%**

## Forebrain Anomalies
### Neuronal Migration
Neurons migrate from the **germinal matrix** (adjacent to ventricular system) via:
- **Radial migration** → excitatory neurons
- **Tangential migration** → inhibitory interneurons
Disruption → abnormalities in brain size, shape, and organization

### Major Forebrain Malformations
**Microencephaly**
- Small brain + small head circumference
- Causes: chromosomal abnormalities, fetal alcohol syndrome, in utero viral infection (**HIV-1, Zika virus**)
- Mechanism: reduced neurons reaching neocortex → simplified gyral folding

**Lissencephaly** — reduced number of gyri
| Type | Surface | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Type 1 | Smooth | Mutations in cytoskeletal motor proteins driving migration |
| Type 2 | Cobblestone/rough | Mutations disrupting migration "stop signal" (glycosylated proteins) |

**Polymicrogyria**
- Numerous small, irregularly formed convolutions with shallow sulci
- Cortex has **4 or fewer layers** (normal = 6 layers)
- Fusion of molecular layers between gyri
- Caused by localized injury at end of neuronal migration, or genetic mutations

**Neuronal Heterotopias** — neurons in wrong locations along migration pathway
| Mutation | Gene | Location | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Filamin A (X-linked) | Actin-binding protein | Periventricular | Males lethal; females → nodular heterotopias |
| Doublecortin DCX (X-linked) | Microtubule protein | Subcortical | Males → lissencephaly; Females → "double cortex" |

**Holoprosencephaly**
- Incomplete separation of cerebral hemispheres across midline
- Severe → midline facial abnormalities including **cyclopia**
- Mild (arrhinencephaly) → absence of olfactory cranial nerves
- Associated with **Trisomy 13** and **sonic hedgehog signaling pathway** mutations
- Severe forms diagnosed **in utero by ultrasonography**

**Agenesis of Corpus Callosum**
- Absence of white matter bundles connecting hemispheres
- Imaging: **"bat-wing" deformity** of lateral ventricles
- Histology: **"Probst bundles"** — anteroposteriorly oriented white matter
- May cause intellectual disability or be entirely asymptomatic
- Can be sporadic or familial, isolated or associated with other malformations

## Posterior Fossa Anomalies
### Arnold-Chiari Malformation — Chiari Type II
- **Small posterior fossa**, misshapen midline cerebellum
- **Downward extension of vermis** through foramen magnum
- Almost always associated with:
  - **Hydrocephalus**
  - **Lumbar myelomeningocele**
- Additional: caudal medullary displacement, aqueductal stenosis, cerebral heterotopias, hydromyelia

### Chiari Type I
- Less severe — low-lying **cerebellar tonsils** extend into vertebral canal
- May be silent or symptomatic (impaired CSF flow, medullary compression)
- Treatable by **neurosurgical intervention**

### Dandy-Walker Malformation
- Enlarged posterior fossa
- **Absent or rudimentary cerebellar vermis**
- Large **midline cyst** = expanded roofless 4th ventricle, lined by ependyma
- Associated brainstem nuclear dysplasias

### Joubert Syndrome
- Hypoplasia of cerebellar vermis
- Elongated superior cerebellar peduncles + altered brainstem → **"molar tooth sign"** on imaging
- Caused by mutations in **primary (nonmotile) cilia** genes

## Syringomyelia and Hydromyelia
| Feature | Hydromyelia | Syringomyelia |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Expansion of ependyma-lined central canal | Fluid-filled cleft in inner cord |
| Extension | — | Can extend to brainstem = **syringobulbia** |
| Associations | Chiari malformation | Chiari, intraspinal tumors, trauma |
- Histology: destruction of adjacent gray and white matter + dense **reactive gliosis**
- Onset: typically **2nd–3rd decade of life**
- Hallmark: **Cape-like loss of pain and temperature sensation in upper extremities** (disruption of anterior spinal commissural fibers)

## PART 3: CEREBRAL EDEMA, HYDROCEPHALUS, RAISED ICP & HERNIATION

## The Rigid Compartment
The brain is enclosed in the rigid skull and dural reflections. ICP rises in three main settings:
1. **Generalized brain edema**
2. **Increased CSF volume**
3. **Focal expanding mass lesion**
Consequences range from subtle neurological deficits to **death**, depending on degree and rapidity of pressure increase.

## Cerebral Edema
**Definition:** Increased fluid accumulation within the brain parenchyma
### Types of Cerebral Edema
| Feature | Vasogenic Edema | Cytotoxic Edema |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | Blood-brain barrier disruption | Cell membrane injury |
| Fluid location | **Extracellular** | **Intracellular** |
| Causes | Inflammation, neoplasms, ischemia | Hypoxia/ischemia, metabolic failure |
| Distribution | Localized or generalized | Generalized |
> ⚠️ The brain has **few lymphatics** → poor resorption of excess fluid → edema worsens rapidly
**Gross appearance of generalized edema:**
- Gyri **flattened**
- Sulci **narrowed**
- Ventricles **compressed**
- Can progress to **herniation**

## Hydrocephalus
**Definition:** Excessive accumulation of CSF within the ventricular system
### Normal CSF Circulation
Choroid plexus → ventricles → **foramina of Luschka and Magendie** → cisterna magna → cerebral convexities → absorbed by **arachnoid granulations**

### Types of Hydrocephalus
| Type | Mechanism | Example Causes |
|---|---|---|
| **Noncommunicating (Obstructive)** | Focal block within ventricular system | Aqueductal stenosis, 3rd ventricle mass |
| **Communicating** | Open system but impaired resorption/overproduction | Post-meningitis fibrosis, choroid plexus tumor |
| **Hydrocephalus ex vacuo** | Compensatory ventricular enlargement | Loss of brain parenchyma — NOT true hydrocephalus |
### Age-Dependent Effects
- **Before suture closure** (infancy) → **head enlarges**
- **After suture closure** → ventricles expand + **raised ICP**, no change in head size

## Raised Intracranial Pressure and Herniation
**Herniation** = displacement of brain tissue past rigid dural folds or through skull openings due to raised ICP
**Causes:**
- Diffuse: generalized edema
- Focal: tumors, abscesses, hemorrhages
> ⚠️ Raised ICP also **compresses vasculature** → reduced perfusion → ischemia → worsens edema — a dangerous vicious cycle

### Types of Brain Herniation
**1. Subfalcine (Cingulate) Herniation**
- Cingulate gyrus displaced **under the falx**
- Complication: **anterior cerebral artery** compression → secondary infarcts

**2. Transtentorial (Uncal) Herniation**
- Medial temporal lobe compressed against **free margin of tentorium**
- **CN III compression** → ipsilateral pupillary dilation + impaired eye movements
- **Posterior cerebral artery compression** → occipital lobe infarct (visual cortex)
- Contralateral cerebral peduncle compression → **ipsilateral hemiparesis** (false localizing sign)
- **Duret hemorrhages** — linear/flame-shaped hemorrhages in midbrain and pons; midline/paramedian; due to tearing of penetrating vessels; indicate severe downward displacement

**3. Tonsillar Herniation**
- Cerebellar tonsils displaced through **foramen magnum**
- Most **life-threatening** — compresses brainstem
- Compromises **medullary respiratory and cardiac centers**
- Can cause **sudden death**

## PRACTICE QUESTIONS & ANSWERS

**Q1. What are "red neurons" and when do they appear?**
Red neurons represent acute neuronal injury seen after hypoxia/ischemia, hypoglycemia, or trauma. They appear **6–12 hours** after irreversible insult. Features: cell shrinkage, nuclear pyknosis, nucleolar disappearance, Nissl substance loss, and intense cytoplasmic eosinophilia.

**Q2. What is central chromatolysis and what does it indicate?**
Central chromatolysis is the dispersal of Nissl substance from the cell center to the periphery. It indicates axonal injury and represents increased protein synthesis for axonal regeneration. Accompanied by cell body enlargement, peripheral nuclear displacement, and nucleolar enlargement.

**Q3. What is gliosis and why is it important?**
Gliosis is hypertrophy and hyperplasia of astrocytes following CNS injury. It is the **most important histopathologic marker of CNS injury** regardless of cause. Reactive gemistocytic astrocytes show bright pink cytoplasm (increased GFAP) and stout ramifying processes.

**Q4. Differentiate Rosenthal fibers from corpora amylacea.**
**Rosenthal fibers** — thick, elongated, eosinophilic; in astrocytic processes; contain αB-crystallin, hsp27, ubiquitin; found in long-standing gliosis and pilocytic astrocytoma.
**Corpora amylacea** — round, basophilic, PAS-positive, lamellated (5–50 μm); at astrocytic end processes; composed of glycosaminoglycans; increase with age as degenerative change.

**Q5. How do oligodendrocytes differ from Schwann cells in myelination?**
One **oligodendrocyte** myelinates multiple internodes on multiple axons (CNS). One **Schwann cell** myelinates only one internode of one axon (PNS).

**Q6. What is the role of folate in neural tube defects?**
Folate deficiency during early gestation is a well-established risk factor for NTDs, possibly through effects on DNA methylation. Neural tube closure is complete by **day 28** — before most pregnancies are recognized — so supplementation must be given **throughout reproductive years**.

**Q7. Differentiate vasogenic from cytotoxic edema.**
**Vasogenic** — blood-brain barrier disruption → excess extracellular fluid; seen with tumors, inflammation.
**Cytotoxic** — cell membrane injury → excess intracellular fluid; seen with hypoxia, metabolic failure.
Both can coexist in severe ischemic injury.

**Q8. What is the difference between communicating and noncommunicating hydrocephalus?**
**Noncommunicating** — focal block within the ventricular system (e.g., aqueductal stenosis) → proximal dilatation only.
**Communicating** — ventricular system connects freely with subarachnoid space but CSF is overproduced or inadequately resorbed (e.g., post-meningitis, choroid plexus tumor).

**Q9. What are Duret hemorrhages?**
Secondary linear or flame-shaped hemorrhages in the **midbrain and pons**, occurring in midline/paramedian regions during severe **transtentorial herniation**. Result from distortion or tearing of penetrating vessels supplying the upper brainstem as the brain is displaced downward.

**Q10. What is the "molar tooth sign"?**
Characteristic brain imaging finding in **Joubert syndrome**. Results from cerebellar vermis hypoplasia + elongated superior cerebellar peduncles + altered brainstem shape. Caused by mutations in **primary (nonmotile) cilia** genes.
