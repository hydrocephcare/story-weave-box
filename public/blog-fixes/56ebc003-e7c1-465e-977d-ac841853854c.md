# Glomerular Diseases

**Year 4 · Paediatrics · Nephrology**

> **In one line:** glomerular diseases are mostly immune-mediated injuries (autoantibodies or deposited immune complexes) to the glomerular capillary wall. Injury to the **podocyte** gives massive protein loss (**nephrotic** pattern); injury to the **endothelium, mesangium or basement membrane** gives inflammation with haematuria and falling GFR (**nephritic** pattern). The pattern points to the cause, and biopsy names the lesion.

## What you must know

- Normal urine protein is under **150 mg/day** (albumin 4 to 7 mg/day). **Nephrotic range** is over **3.5 g/24 h** in adults (in children, over 40 mg/m²/h or urine protein:creatinine ratio over 200 mg/mmol).
- **Nephrotic syndrome** = heavy proteinuria + hypoalbuminaemia (under 30 g/L) + generalised oedema (+ hyperlipidaemia). Urine has no red cells or casts.
- **Nephritic syndrome** = haematuria with **RBC casts and dysmorphic red cells** (an "active sediment"), hypertension, oedema, oliguria and raised creatinine. Proteinuria is present but variable.
- **Minimal change disease (MCD)** causes about 90% of nephrotic syndrome in children under 10 years. No biopsy is needed; treat empirically with steroids.
- **Post-streptococcal GN (PSGN)** follows a throat or skin infection by **1 to 3 weeks**, with low C3 and raised ASO. **IgA nephropathy** follows a mucosal infection by **1 to 3 days**, with normal complement.
- Biopsy is the most accurate test for a glomerular disease, though it is not always needed. Treatment is usually corticosteroids, with cyclophosphamide, mycophenolate, calcineurin inhibitors or rituximab added in resistant or severe disease.
- Crescentic (rapidly progressive) GN is an emergency: renal function is lost over days to weeks.

## 1. Overview and mechanism

- Glomerular disease accounts for a large share of acute and chronic kidney disease. Causes include immunological injury, inherited disease (**Alport syndrome**), metabolic disease (**diabetes mellitus**) and deposition of abnormal protein (**amyloid**).
- This note covers the **primary** (mostly immune) glomerular diseases. Injury comes from **autoantibodies against glomerular structures** or from **antigen-antibody (immune-complex) deposition** that damages the glomerular components. Often the exact trigger is unknown, but the pattern of damage shows the immune system is central.
- **Which cell is hit decides the picture:**
  - **Podocytes** are the main target: they lose their foot processes (cell bodies stay intact), so large amounts of protein leak. Result: **nephrotic syndrome**.
  - **Endothelium, mesangium or GBM** are the target: the filtration barrier is disrupted and inflamed. Result: **haematuria and abnormal renal function (nephritic pattern)**. Proteinuria is always present as well. These structures are exposed because they lie close to the circulating blood.
- Glomerular diseases are **named by their histology**, so a kidney biopsy is almost always needed to diagnose a suspected primary glomerular disease.
- They are usually chronic and treated with corticosteroids, often with extra immunosuppression (cyclophosphamide, mycophenolate).
- Proteinuria level matches severity and the likelihood of progression.

> **Remember:** Glomerular = **S**low = **S**ample (biopsy) = **S**teroids = Immuno**S**uppressive. And: nephr**O**tic has the **O**edema/prote**O**in loss; nephr**I**tic has **I**nflammation.

## 2. Nephrotic versus nephritic: comparison

| Feature | Nephrotic syndrome | Nephritic glomerular disease |
|---|---|---|
| Urine | Heavy proteinuria (over 3.5 g/24 h); fat in urine (lipiduria): fatty casts and oval fat bodies. **No RBCs, RBC casts or WBCs** | Haematuria; **RBC casts** (RBCs from the inflamed glomerulus trapped in Tamm-Horsfall mucoprotein in the distal tubule and collecting duct); **dysmorphic RBCs** (damaged passing through the inflamed GBM); proteinuria of variable amount. Together this is an **active urinary sediment** |
| Serum | Albumin under 30 g/L (normal 35 to 55 g/L); hyperlipidaemia | Raised creatinine; immune markers may be positive: ANA, anti-DNA, low complement, ANCA (depends on cause) |
| Oedema | Peripheral and generalised (everywhere, not just dependent areas) | Present, with periorbital puffiness |
| Other | Fatigue, frothy urine that persists after voiding, anorexia, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, weight gain, dyspnoea (pleural effusion), features of DVT or PE | Reduced urine output, **hypertension**, possible features of systemic vasculitis (skin rash, pulmonary haemorrhage) |
| Pathology | Podocyte abnormality, loss of foot processes, intact cell bodies | Inflammation: attraction of inflammatory mediators, cell proliferation, permanent glomerular dysfunction if untreated. Mesangium, endothelium and GBM targeted |
| Complications | Infection and sepsis (urinary loss of immunoglobulins and complement); **thrombosis** (loss of protein S, protein C and antithrombin); AKI; ESRD if proteinuria does not resolve | AKI (raised creatinine); ESRD; pulmonary oedema |
| Meaning | A **syndrome** (a measure of proteinuria severity in any glomerular disease) | A **pattern** shared by the glomerulonephritides; always means active glomerular inflammation needing urgent attention |

> **Remember:** heavy proteinuria + hypoalbuminaemia + generalised oedema is diagnostic of nephrotic syndrome.

### Why oedema, hyperlipidaemia and infection occur in nephrotic syndrome

- Increased glomerular permeability leads to massive proteinuria, then low serum albumin and low plasma oncotic pressure.
- Renin release from juxtaglomerular cells rises, activating the **renin-angiotensin-aldosterone axis**, with uncontrolled activation of **ENaC channels** in the renal tubules. Salt and water are retained.
- Heart failure causes oedema of dependent areas (legs), but nephrotic patients are oedematous everywhere.
- Hyperlipidaemia and hypercholesterolaemia result from **increased hepatic lipoprotein synthesis**.
- Nephrotic syndrome happens when proteinuria is so massive that the liver cannot raise albumin production enough to compensate.

## 3. Investigation approach

- **Best initial test:** urinalysis (dipstick and microscopy). **Most accurate:** renal biopsy.
- Urine: protein quantification (24-hour collection or spot protein:creatinine ratio), microscopy for RBCs, casts and fat.
- Blood: creatinine and electrolytes, albumin, lipids, complement (C3, C4), ASO titre and anti-DNase B, ANA, anti-dsDNA, ANCA, anti-GBM antibody, hepatitis B and C, HIV serology.
- Other: chest X-ray (especially if haemoptysis), ECG.
- Biopsy: light microscopy, immunofluorescence (IF) and electron microscopy (EM).

> **Exam tip:** the pattern of IF is a classic discriminator: **linear** IgG and C3 = anti-GBM disease; **granular** deposits = immune-complex disease (PSGN, lupus); **mesangial IgA** = IgA nephropathy.

## 4. Nephrotic syndrome: general management

- **Best initial therapy:** glucocorticoids. If there is no response after several weeks, add other immunosuppressants such as cyclophosphamide.
- **ACE inhibitors or ARBs** reduce proteinuria.
- **Oedema:** salt restriction and diuretics.
- **Hyperlipidaemia:** statins.
- Associations are better described than causes, because the exact cause of nephrotic syndrome is unknown. Diabetes and hypertension are the commonest conditions associated overall.

## 5. Causes of nephrotic syndrome

### 5.1 Minimal change disease (MCD)

*Mnemonic: MCD = **C**hil**D**ren.*

- **Why "minimal change":** light microscopy is normal ("nil disease"); EM shows **diffuse effacement of the epithelial (podocyte) foot processes** only. The key difference from FSGS is that there is **no glomerular sclerosis**.
- Current evidence points to systemic **T cell dysfunction** as the likely root cause.
- **Epidemiology:** the main cause of nephrotic syndrome in children (about 90% in children under 10 years; over 50% in older children). Causes 10 to 25% of adult cases. Incidence has two peaks: young adults and the elderly (60 to 70 years).
- **Types:** primary (idiopathic), or secondary (less common).

| Secondary causes | Examples |
|---|---|
| Drugs | NSAIDs, lithium, sulphasalazine, pamidronate, D-penicillamine, some antibiotics |
| Neoplasm | Hodgkin and non-Hodgkin lymphoma, leukaemia |
| Infection | TB, syphilis |
| Allergy | Asthma, allergic rhinitis |

- **Clinical features:** sudden-onset oedema; BP normal or slightly raised; nephrotic-range proteinuria; lipiduria; usually very low albumin; hyperlipidaemia. **Creatinine is normal or slightly raised and normalises with remission.**
- **Diagnosis:** a child under 10 with nephrotic syndrome is MCD until proven otherwise, so **no biopsy** is done. Adults with this presentation **must** have a biopsy (diffuse foot-process effacement on EM).
- **Treatment:**
  - Children: steroid-responsive in over 90%; treatment is empirical.
  - First line: **corticosteroids** for 3 to 4 months, then taper over 6 months (over 90% respond).
  - Second line: oral **cyclophosphamide** (relapsing MCD, as it prolongs remission); **cyclosporine** (steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome).

> **Check:** standard paediatric regimens (e.g. prednisolone 60 mg/m²/day for 4 to 6 weeks, then alternate days tapered over weeks) are shorter than the "3 to 4 months then taper over 6 months" quoted for the whole course; use your local protocol. Relapses are common in children.

> **Exam tip:** a child with nephrotic syndrome, no haematuria, normal BP and normal creatinine does not need a biopsy; start prednisolone. Biopsy is indicated if the child is under 1 year, over 10 years, has haematuria, hypertension, low complement, or is steroid resistant.

### 5.2 Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS)

- A common cause of nephrotic syndrome in **adults** (12 to 35% of cases); **more common in people of African descent**.
- **Light microscopy:** **focal** = only some glomeruli affected; **segmental** = only part (under 50%) of each affected glomerulus is sclerosed. IF may show C3 and IgM deposits. **EM: diffuse foot-process effacement in all glomeruli**, which explains the nephrotic syndrome.

| | Primary FSGS | Secondary FSGS |
|---|---|---|
| Presentation | Sudden onset of heavy proteinuria and other nephrotic features | Proteinuria less heavy; serum albumin not very low; renal impairment common (poor prognostic sign) |
| Cause | Unknown | Obesity; anabolic steroid abuse; nephron loss (over 75% of renal mass, e.g. renal agenesis); reflux nephropathy; healing of prior GN (e.g. IgA); severe pre-eclampsia; drugs (interferon, pamidronate, heroin); HIV infection |
| Treatment | First line: corticosteroids (but **80% do not respond**). Second line: cyclosporine or tacrolimus. ACEIs or ARBs commonly used | Not usually immunosuppressed. Treat the primary cause and protect the kidney: control BP, ACE inhibitor |

> **Remember:** pamidronate is a nitrogen-containing bisphosphonate used to prevent osteoporosis.

### 5.3 Membranous nephropathy

- Most common cause of **primary** nephrotic syndrome in **adults** (15% to 33% in Caucasians). **Mostly secondary in children** (hepatitis B antigenaemia, congenital HBV). *Mnemonic: Mem**B**ranous = hep**B**.*
- **Slowly developing** nephrotic syndrome (FSGS can develop within a week; here onset is very slow).
- **Pathology:** thickened capillary walls with **subepithelial immune deposits**.

| | Primary | Secondary |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency | Idiopathic; 75% of adult cases | SLE class V (10 to 20% of lupus nephritis; young females); drugs (penicillamine, IV gold, high-dose captopril, NSAIDs, anti-TNF); infections (hepatitis B, hepatitis C, syphilis); solid tumours (prostate, lung, GI; account for 20% in those over 60) |
| Treatment | Corticosteroids plus cyclophosphamide or cyclosporine (a third resolve spontaneously); possibly rituximab | Treat the primary disease and the nephrotic manifestations |

- Natural history (from the answers): one-third improve spontaneously, one-third stay stable, one-third progress to ESRD if untreated. It is fairly responsive to corticosteroid and cytotoxic therapy.
- Membranous nephropathy is associated with venous thrombosis, including **renal vein thrombosis**.

### 5.4 Other secondary causes of nephrotic syndrome (mostly adult)

- **Diabetes mellitus:** nephrotic syndrome in a diabetic first suggests diabetic nephropathy, but other glomerular diseases also occur in diabetics.
- **Amyloidosis** (with myeloma, rheumatoid arthritis, IBD and chronic infections): Congo red stain shows **apple-green birefringence**. Amyloidosis and diabetes **enlarge the kidneys** (as do HIV nephropathy and polycystic kidneys).
- **IgA nephropathy:** nephrotic syndrome is a rare presentation.
- **MPGN:** may present as nephrotic or nephritic.

## 6. Nephritic glomerular disease (glomerulonephritis)

"Nephritic" is a clinical pattern of presentation shared by a group of glomerulonephritides, not a single syndrome.

> **Remember:** rapidly progressive (crescentic) GN is characterised by loss of renal function over days to weeks. Biopsy shows crescents, often with focal segmental necrotising lesions. It indicates severe inflammation and a bad outcome if not treated rapidly. It is typical of **anti-GBM disease** and small-vessel vasculitides, and may be seen in SLE and occasionally IgA and other nephropathies. Proteinuria may be relatively little.

### 6.1 IgA nephropathy (Berger disease) and Henoch-Schönlein purpura (HSP)

- Most common primary GN in developed countries; most common cause of glomerular haematuria. Chronic course that can progress to ESRD. More common in Asians.
- **HSP** is a systemic vasculitis from IgA deposition in several organs: typically **skin, bowel and kidney**. In HSP there is also purpuric rash on the extensor surfaces, large joint arthritis and abdominal pain, which separates it from isolated IgA nephropathy.
- **Pathogenesis:** altered mucosal immunity leads to excessive IgA synthesis and deposition in the glomerular mesangium (mesangial expansion).
- **Clinical:** dark urine (haematuria) **1 to 3 days after an upper respiratory infection** ("**synpharyngitic**": with or immediately after pharyngitis, within a week of the infection). Often picked up incidentally by abnormal urinalysis (haematuria with or without proteinuria) without symptoms.
- **Diagnosis:** **biopsy** (most accurate), showing **mesangial IgA and C3 deposition**. Serum IgA is raised in only about 50%.
- **Treatment:** no effective immunosuppressive therapy except in severe cases, where it can be tried. About 30% resolve completely. **Controlling BP is the most important measure** and also lowers proteinuria. Severe proteinuria: ACE inhibitors and steroids. Fish oil is of uncertain benefit.

### 6.2 Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis (PSGN)

- A subtype of post-infectious GN, **much more common in children** than adults and now rare in developed countries. Follows a **throat or skin (impetigo) infection by 1 to 3 weeks** (latency about 10 days after pharyngitis, longer after skin infection), which points to an immune mechanism rather than direct infection.
- **Cause:** Group A beta-haemolytic *Streptococcus*. Staphylococcal soft tissue or bone infection can cause it in adults. Bacterial antigens cross-react with glomerular antigens, or an immune-complex response is responsible.
- **Clinical:** frank haematuria usually 1 to 3 weeks after infection starts; **dark cola-coloured urine**, **periorbital oedema**, **hypertension**, **oliguria**.
- **Diagnosis:** raised **ASO** titre and anti-DNase antibody; **low C3**, normal or slightly low C4; throat culture may be positive. Biopsy is the most accurate test but is not done routinely.
- **Treatment:** supportive. Children recover better and faster than adults. Management does not reverse the GN: antibiotics (to clear infection) and diuretics for fluid overload.

> **Exam tip:** IgA nephropathy = 1 to 3 **days** after infection, complement normal. PSGN = 1 to 3 **weeks**, complement (C3) low.

### 6.3 Lupus nephritis

- SLE ("the disease with a thousand faces") can affect the kidneys from mild, unnoticed disease to ESRD within months. High suspicion and immediate work-up and referral are most important.
- **Diagnosis:** low complement (C3, C4) with positive lupus markers, abnormal urinalysis and abnormal renal function should raise suspicion. **Kidney biopsy is mandatory**, not to diagnose lupus but to **stage the involvement and guide the intensity of therapy**. Long-standing SLE can simply scar the kidney (glomerulosclerosis without active inflammation) and may require dialysis.
- **Treatment:** depends on biopsy findings; usually heavy immunosuppression. Mild inflammatory changes may respond to glucocorticoids.

### 6.4 Anti-GBM disease (Goodpasture disease)

- **Cause:** autoantibody against the **alpha-3 chain of type IV collagen** in glomerular and alveolar basement membranes.
- **Clinical:** GN (may be the only finding) plus **pulmonary haemorrhage** (GN with lung involvement is Goodpasture disease). No upper respiratory tract involvement (unlike Wegener granulomatosis), and no skin, joint, GI, eye or neurological involvement. Anaemia from chronic blood loss in haemoptysis.
- **Diagnosis:** serum **anti-GBM antibodies**; biopsy IF shows **linear IgG and C3**. Chest X-ray is abnormal but cannot confirm the diagnosis.
- **Treatment:** start immediately: **plasmapheresis** (separating plasma from blood cells and replacing it with saline or albumin) to remove antibodies, plus **corticosteroids and cyclophosphamide** to stop further production.

### 6.5 ANCA-associated vasculitis

- ANCA = anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody.
  - **C-ANCA** (cytoplasmic): granulomatosis with polyangiitis (old name Wegener granulomatosis; granuloma-forming, small-vessel).
  - **P-ANCA** (perinuclear): microscopic polyangiitis and Churg-Strauss syndrome.
- **Clinical:** upper airway and lung involvement common; renal and pulmonary manifestations (GN plus pulmonary haemorrhage with haemoptysis, which may cause anaemia).
- **Diagnosis:** kidney biopsy plus positive ANCA titre.
- **Treatment:** aggressive disease; high-dose corticosteroids and cyclophosphamide.

### 6.6 Membranoproliferative GN (MPGN)

- A pathological description with many causes ("the black box"). May present as nephritic or nephrotic; mesangial expansion and GBM thickening. EM may show dense deposits in the basement membrane, and C3 is depressed.
- Primary (idiopathic) MPGN is mainly seen in children. Secondary causes: hepatitis B and C, endocarditis, lupus, Sjögren syndrome, cancer, complement deficiency.
- Progressive clinical course with erratic response to treatment.

## 7. Differentials worth separating

| Clue | Diagnosis |
|---|---|
| Child, oedema, no haematuria, normal BP | MCD |
| African descent, HIV, heroin, obesity | FSGS |
| Hepatitis B, adult with renal vein thrombosis | Membranous nephropathy |
| Haematuria days after sore throat, normal C3 | IgA nephropathy |
| Cola urine 2 to 3 weeks after throat or skin infection, low C3 | PSGN |
| Deafness, retinal flecks, haematuria, family history | Alport syndrome (type IV collagen defect) |
| Haematuria + haemoptysis, no upper airway or systemic features | Anti-GBM disease |
| Sinus disease, C-ANCA, crescents | Granulomatosis with polyangiitis |
| Low C3, nephrotic and nephritic features, dense deposits | MPGN |
| Young woman, multi-system, low C3 and C4 | Lupus nephritis |

## 8. Worked cases

- **17-year-old with generalised swelling, frothy urine, 15 g/day proteinuria, albumin 10 g/L, normal creatinine, no blood or casts, negative ANA and viral screen:** pure nephrotic picture in a young person. The slide answers lupus (answer E in the matching question series) for a different stem; for this case, MCD is the usual leading diagnosis, then FSGS.
- **70-year-old smoker, 4 g/day proteinuria, albumin 20 g/L, normal creatinine, negative serology:** think membranous nephropathy secondary to malignancy; look for a solid tumour (the slide lists FSGS as the differential).
- **Matching stems:** 50-year-old white man, nephrotic syndrome, renal vein thrombosis, subepithelial deposits = membranous nephropathy. 19-year-old, nephrotic, RBC casts, low C3, dense deposits within the basement membrane = MPGN. 43-year-old woman on daily ibuprofen, 3.9 g protein, normal light microscopy, foot-process effacement on EM = MCD (NSAID-associated).
- **21-year-old with macroscopic haematuria after a throat infection:** IgA nephropathy (HSP is distinguished by systemic features).
- **17-year-old with periorbital oedema, haematuria, proteinuria, throat infection 3 weeks ago:** post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis (nephritic syndrome).
- **6-year-old with tea-coloured urine 3 weeks after a sore throat:** PSGN.
- **21-year-old with sensorineural deafness, retinal flecks, haematuria:** Alport syndrome (X-linked type IV collagen defect).
- **64-year-old with sinus problems, oliguria, C-ANCA positive, anti-GBM negative, RBC casts, crescents:** granulomatosis with polyangiitis (Wegener).

## 9. Complications and prognosis

- Nephrotic: infection, thrombosis, AKI, ESRD if proteinuria persists.
- Nephritic: AKI, hypertensive complications, pulmonary oedema, ESRD.
- Prognosis depends on the lesion: MCD and PSGN in children usually do well; FSGS (steroid-resistant), crescentic GN and lupus nephritis can progress to ESRD without early treatment.

## Key points to revise

1. Podocyte injury gives nephrotic syndrome; endothelial, mesangial or GBM injury gives nephritic pattern; proteinuria occurs in both.
2. Nephrotic: protein over 3.5 g/24 h, albumin under 30 g/L, generalised oedema, hyperlipidaemia; no red cells or casts.
3. Nephritic: haematuria, RBC casts, dysmorphic RBCs, hypertension, oliguria, raised creatinine.
4. MCD: commonest in children, normal light microscopy, foot-process effacement on EM, over 90% steroid responsive, no biopsy in a child under 10.
5. FSGS: adults and people of African descent, 80% steroid non-responsive; membranous nephropathy: commonest primary cause in adults, associated with hepatitis B, SLE, drugs and tumours.
6. PSGN follows infection by 1 to 3 weeks with low C3 and high ASO; IgA nephropathy follows by 1 to 3 days with normal complement.
7. Anti-GBM disease: linear IgG on IF, treat with plasmapheresis, steroids and cyclophosphamide; ANCA vasculitis: C-ANCA with granulomatosis with polyangiitis.
8. Complications of nephrotic syndrome: infection and thrombosis (loss of immunoglobulins, protein C, S and antithrombin).
