This article provides a concise overview of key concepts in medical biochemistry, genetics, and molecular biology, essential for Year 2 MBChB students. It covers fundamental processes of energy metabolism, principles of inheritance and chromosomal disorders, and the core mechanisms of gene expression.

## Energy Metabolism

Metabolism encompasses all chemical reactions occurring within a living organism, broadly categorised into two main processes: anabolism and catabolism.

*   **Anabolism:** These are biosynthetic pathways where small, simple precursor molecules are assembled into larger, more complex organic molecules. Anabolic reactions typically require an input of energy (endergonic), often supplied by ATP. Examples include protein synthesis from amino acids, or glycogen synthesis from glucose.
*   **Catabolism:** These are degradative pathways where larger, complex organic molecules are broken down into smaller, simpler ones. Catabolic reactions typically release energy (exergonic), which is captured in molecules like ATP. Examples include the breakdown of glucose during glycolysis or the oxidation of fatty acids.

### ATP Production: Oxidative vs. Substrate-Level Phosphorylation

The primary energy currency of the cell is adenosine triphosphate (ATP). Its synthesis occurs through two main mechanisms:

*   **Oxidative Phosphorylation:** This is the major pathway for ATP synthesis in aerobic organisms. As electrons flow through the **electron transport chain (ETC)**, much of their free energy is conserved in the form of ATP. This process occurs on the inner mitochondrial membrane in eukaryotes (or plasma membrane in prokaryotes). Electrons from NADH and FADH2 are passed along a series of protein complexes, creating a proton gradient across the membrane. The potential energy stored in this gradient is then harnessed by **ATP synthase** to produce ATP.
*   **Substrate-Level Phosphorylation:** In this process, ATP is generated by the direct transfer of a phosphate group from a high-energy phosphorylated intermediate molecule to ADP. This occurs independently of the electron transport chain and does not require oxygen directly. Key examples include steps in glycolysis (e.g., the conversion of 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate to 3-phosphoglycerate) and the citric acid cycle (e.g., the conversion of succinyl CoA to succinate).

### Glucose Metabolism: Glycolysis and Fermentation

**Glycolysis** is the metabolic pathway that breaks down glucose into pyruvate, generating ATP and NADH. The most common pathway is the **Embden-Meyerhof pathway**. An alternative pathway, the **Entner-Doudoroff pathway**, is found in some bacteria, yielding different amounts of ATP and NADH.

**Fermentation** is an anaerobic process that allows for the regeneration of NAD+ from NADH, enabling glycolysis to continue in the absence of oxygen. The acquisition of energy by glucose fermentation requires **substrate-level phosphorylation** as its primary means of ATP generation, as it does not involve an electron transport chain or an external terminal electron acceptor.

### Respiration: Aerobic vs. Anaerobic

Respiration involves the transfer of electrons from fuel molecules to a terminal electron acceptor, releasing energy for ATP synthesis.

*   **Aerobic Respiration:** In aerobic respiration, the terminal electron acceptor is **oxygen (O2)**. This process yields a large amount of ATP through oxidative phosphorylation.
*   **Anaerobic Respiration:** In anaerobic respiration, various inorganic compounds serve as terminal electron acceptors instead of oxygen. Common electron acceptors include **nitrate, sulfate, and fumarate**. Hydrogen sulfide (H2S) is typically a product of sulfate reduction, not an electron acceptor itself.

### Carbon Dioxide Fixation and Photosynthesis

**Carbon dioxide fixation** is the process by which inorganic carbon (CO2) is converted into organic compounds. The **Calvin Cycle** is the most common pathway for CO2 fixation in chemolithotrophs (organisms that obtain energy from the oxidation of inorganic compounds) and photoautotrophs (organisms that use light energy).

**Anoxygenic photosynthesis** is a form of photosynthesis that does not produce oxygen. It is carried out by certain bacteria (e.g., purple and green sulfur bacteria). In these organisms, **bacteriochlorophyll** uses light energy to energize an electron, initiating electron flow. Unlike oxygenic photosynthesis (which uses water as an electron donor), anoxygenic photosynthetic bacteria oxidize electron donors other than water, such as **sulfide (H2S)**, elemental sulfur, or ferrous iron.

### Plasma Membrane Functions

The plasma membrane is a vital component of all cells, performing multiple critical functions:

*   **Selective Permeability:** Regulates the passage of substances into and out of the cell, maintaining intracellular homeostasis. This includes passive diffusion (driven by concentration difference), facilitated diffusion, and active transport.
*   **Responding to External Stimuli:** Contains receptors that bind to signaling molecules, initiating intracellular responses.
*   **Energy Transduction:** In many prokaryotes and in the mitochondria of eukaryotes, the plasma membrane (or inner mitochondrial membrane) is involved in generating electrochemical gradients for ATP synthesis (e.g., electron transport chain).
*   **Intracellular Interactions:** Mediates cell-cell recognition and adhesion.
*   **Protection:** Acts as a barrier, keeping toxic substances out of the cell.
*   **Assisting in Chromosome Segregation:** While primarily a nuclear function involving the spindle apparatus in eukaryotes, in prokaryotes, the plasma membrane can play a role in anchoring the bacterial chromosome during replication and segregation.

### Disinfectants and Microbial Control

Disinfectants target various microbial structures and processes. Some act on microbial membrane structures, disrupting their integrity and function. These include:

*   **Cationic detergents:** Disrupt cell membranes.
*   **Phenol compounds:** Denature proteins and disrupt cell membranes.
*   **Chlorhexidine:** Damages cell membranes and coagulates cytoplasmic contents.
*   **Alcohols:** Denature proteins and dissolve lipids in the cell membrane.

In contrast, **radiation** (e.g., UV, ionizing radiation) primarily damages DNA and proteins, rather than directly acting on membrane structures.

## Genetics and Inheritance

Genetics is the study of heredity and variation. Understanding genetic principles is crucial for diagnosing and managing inherited diseases.

### Population Genetics: Genetic Drift and Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium

**Genetic drift** refers to random fluctuations in allele frequencies within a population from one generation to the next. It has a more pronounced effect in **small populations**, leading to the rapid fixation (reaching 100% frequency) or loss of certain alleles, even if they are not under selective pressure.

The **Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium** describes a theoretical population where allele and genotype frequencies remain constant from generation to generation in the absence of evolutionary influences. The five main assumptions are:

1.  No mutation
2.  Random mating
3.  No gene flow (migration)
4.  No genetic drift (large population size)
5.  No natural selection

The Hardy-Weinberg equations are:

*   **Allele frequencies:** p + q = 1 (where p = frequency of the dominant allele, q = frequency of the recessive allele)
*   **Genotype frequencies:** p² + 2pq + q² = 1 (where p² = frequency of homozygous dominant, 2pq = frequency of heterozygous, q² = frequency of homozygous recessive)

**Example Calculations in Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium:**

*   If a population is in Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium and p = 0.7, then q = 1 - p = 1 - 0.7 = 0.3. The frequency of genotype aa (q²) would be 0.3² = **0.09**.
*   In a Hardy-Weinberg population where p = 0.7 (and thus q = 0.3), the frequency of heterozygotes (2pq) is 2 * 0.7 * 0.3 = 0.42. In a population of 100 individuals, the expected number of heterozygotes would be 0.42 * 100 = **42**.
*   Consider a population with 80 AA, 13 Aa, and 7 aa individuals (total 100 individuals). To find the expected number of aa in the next generation, first calculate the allele frequency of 'a' (q):
    *   Total 'a' alleles = (13 from Aa) + (2 * 7 from aa) = 13 + 14 = 27.
    *   Total alleles in the population = 2 * 100 individuals = 200.
    *   Frequency of 'a' (q) = 27 / 200 = 0.135.
    *   Expected frequency of 'aa' (q²) = (0.135)² = 0.018225.
    *   Expected number of 'aa' in the next generation (assuming 100 individuals) = 0.018225 * 100 = **1.82** (approximately).

### Mendelian Genetics

**Gregor Johann Mendel** is credited with deriving the fundamental laws of inheritance. His **Law of Dominance** states that in a heterozygote, one allele (the dominant allele) will mask the expression of the other allele (the recessive allele).

### Chromosomal Abnormalities

**Nondisjunction** is the failure of homologous chromosomes to separate during meiosis I, or sister chromatids to separate during meiosis II or mitosis. This is a major cause of aneuploidy (abnormal number of chromosomes).

Common chromosomal syndromes include:

*   **Cri-du-chat syndrome:** Caused by a partial deletion of the short arm of chromosome 5 (5p deletion). Characterized by a distinctive high-pitched cry resembling a cat's meow, intellectual disability, and microcephaly.
*   **Klinefelter's syndrome:** Caused by nondisjunction of sex chromosomes, resulting in a 47,XXY karyotype. Individuals are typically male with tall stature, hypogonadism, infertility, and sometimes learning difficulties.
*   **Down syndrome (Trisomy 21):** The most common chromosomal disorder, caused by the presence of an extra copy of chromosome 21. Most cases (approximately 95%) are due to meiotic nondisjunction, resulting in a karyotype of **47 chromosomes, trisomy 21**. Individuals exhibit characteristic facial features, intellectual disability, and an increased risk of congenital heart defects and other health issues.
*   **Familial Down syndrome:** A less common form (approximately 3-4% of cases) where individuals have **46 chromosomes, with a translocation involving chromosome 21**. Typically, a portion of chromosome 21 is translocated onto another chromosome (often chromosome 14 or 22). This form can be inherited from a parent who is a balanced carrier of the translocation, meaning they have 45 chromosomes but are phenotypically normal.

### Genetic Mapping

Genetic maps illustrate the relative positions of genes on a chromosome based on recombination frequencies. Distances are typically measured in **centimorgans (cM)**, where 1 cM corresponds to a 1% chance of recombination between two genes.

**Example of Gene Order and Distance Calculation:**

If genetic distances are given as Y-M = 3.4 cM, Y-W = 0.1 cM, and Y-R = 5.8 cM. To determine the gene order and distance between W and R:

1.  Identify the two genes furthest apart from Y: Y-R (5.8 cM).
2.  Place W relative to Y: Y-W is 0.1 cM, meaning W is very close to Y.
3.  The order must be Y-W-M-R (or R-M-W-Y).
4.  The distance between W and R = (Distance Y-R) - (Distance Y-W) = 5.8 cM - 0.1 cM = **5.7 cM**. Therefore, the order is Y-W-M-R and the distance is 5.7 cM.

## Molecular Biology

Molecular biology explores the molecular basis of biological activity, focusing on DNA, RNA, and protein synthesis.

### Nucleotides

**Nucleotides** are the fundamental building blocks of nucleic acids (DNA and RNA). They consist of a nitrogenous base, a pentose sugar, and one to three phosphate groups. Beyond their role in genetic information storage, nucleotides function as:

*   **Precursors for nucleic acid synthesis:** Essential for DNA replication and RNA transcription.
*   **Energy carriers:** ATP (adenosine triphosphate) and GTP (guanosine triphosphate) are crucial for cellular energy transfer.
*   **Enzyme cofactors:** NAD+ (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide), FAD (flavin adenine dinucleotide), and Coenzyme A are derived from nucleotides and play vital roles in metabolic reactions.
*   **Intracellular signals:** cAMP (cyclic adenosine monophosphate) and cGMP (cyclic guanosine monophosphate) act as secondary messengers in signal transduction pathways.

### DNA Structure and Gene Expression

**Double-stranded DNA** consists of two antiparallel polynucleotide strands wound into a double helix. Each strand has a distinct role during gene expression:

*   **Template strand (Antisense strand):** This strand is copied during transcription to synthesize an RNA molecule. Its sequence is complementary to the RNA transcript.
*   **Coding strand (Sense strand):** This strand has a sequence identical to the RNA transcript (with thymine (T) replaced by uracil (U)). It is not directly copied during transcription but serves as a reference for the gene's sequence.

### Transcription and Translation

**Transcription** is the process of synthesizing RNA from a DNA template. **Translation** is the process of synthesizing proteins from an mRNA template.

*   **Ribosomal Binding Site (Shine-Dalgarno sequence):** In prokaryotes, the ribosomal binding site on mRNA is known as the **Shine-Dalgarno sequence**. This purine-rich sequence, located upstream of the start codon, helps recruit the ribosome to the mRNA for translation initiation.
*   **Polysomes (Polyribosomes):** These are structures formed when multiple ribosomes simultaneously translate a single mRNA molecule. This allows for the efficient and rapid synthesis of many copies of the same protein from a single mRNA template.
*   **Peptidyl Transferase:** During translation, the role of **peptidyl transferase** (a ribozyme component of the large ribosomal subunit) is to catalyze the formation of a **peptide bond** between the growing polypeptide chain and the incoming amino acid.
*   **Genetic Code:** The codon-amino acid correspondence is referred to as the **genetic code**. It is a set of rules by which information encoded in genetic material (DNA or RNA sequences) is translated into proteins. The code is degenerate (most amino acids are specified by more than one codon) and nearly universal.
*   **Nonsense Codons (Stop Codons):** There are **3** nonsense codons in the genetic code: UAA, UAG, and UGA. These codons do not specify an amino acid but instead signal the termination of protein synthesis.
*   **Wobble Hypothesis:** Proposed by **Crick**, the wobble hypothesis explains how a single tRNA molecule can recognize more than one codon. It suggests that the base pairing between the third nucleotide of the mRNA codon and the first nucleotide of the tRNA anticodon is less stringent, allowing for some flexibility (wobble).
*   **Regulatory Proteins (Transcription Factors):** These are proteins that bind to specific DNA sequences (often near the gene's start site, such as promoters or enhancers) to regulate the rate of gene transcription. They can act as activators, increasing gene expression, or repressors, decreasing it.

### DNA Packaging: Histones

**Histones** are small, highly basic proteins (rich in positively charged amino acids like lysine and arginine) that are associated with DNA in eukaryotic cells. They play a crucial role in packaging DNA into compact structures called nucleosomes, which then fold further to form chromatin. This packaging is essential for fitting the long DNA molecules into the nucleus and for regulating gene expression.

## Key Metabolic Pathways Comparison

Understanding the differences between energy-generating pathways is fundamental in biochemistry:

| Feature               | Aerobic Respiration                               | Anaerobic Respiration                                 | Fermentation                                       |
| :-------------------- | :------------------------------------------------ | :---------------------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------- |
| **Oxygen Requirement** | Required                                          | Not required                                          | Not required                                        |
| **Terminal e- Acceptor** | O2 (Oxygen)                                       | Inorganic molecules (e.g., Nitrate, Sulfate, Fumarate) | Organic molecule (e.g., Pyruvate, Acetaldehyde)     |
| **Electron Transport Chain** | Present                                           | Present                                               | Absent                                              |
| **ATP Production**    | High (via Oxidative Phosphorylation)              | Moderate (via Oxidative Phosphorylation)              | Low (via Substrate-Level Phosphorylation only)      |
| **Primary Purpose**   | Maximize ATP yield from glucose                   | Generate ATP in absence of O2, using external e- acceptor | Regenerate NAD+ for glycolysis in absence of O2/ETC |
| **Examples**          | Most eukaryotes, many prokaryotes                 | Some bacteria, archaea                                | Many bacteria, yeast, muscle cells (lactic acid)    |
