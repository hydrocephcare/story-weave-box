## MYCOLOGY

### SECTION B — SHORT ANSWER QUESTIONS

**Q: List four characteristics of systemic mycoses.**
**A:**
1. Caused by dimorphic fungi
2. Acquired primarily by inhalation of spores
3. Asymptomatic in immunocompetent individuals (subclinical infection common)
4. Can infect both healthy and immunocompromised hosts
5. Microscopy reveals granulomatous tissue reaction on biopsy

**Q: Describe the diagnosis of paracoccidioidomycosis.**
**A:**
- **Microscopy:** KOH wet mount of sputum, skin scrapings, or biopsy material reveals the pathognomonic "ship's wheel" or "Mickey Mouse" appearance — large yeast cells surrounded by multiple narrow-necked buds
- **Culture:** Growth on Sabouraud dextrose agar at 25°C (mold form) and 37°C (yeast form); requires conversion to confirm dimorphism
- **Serology:** Immunodiffusion and complement fixation tests detect specific antibodies (confirmatory)
- **Histology:** Granulomatous inflammation with giant cells containing the budding yeast forms

**Q: List four clinical manifestations of Cryptococcosis.**
**A:**
1. Meningitis / meningoencephalitis (most serious) — headache, fever, neck stiffness
2. Pneumonia — cough, chest pain, dyspnoea
3. Skin lesions — papules resembling molluscum contagiosum
4. Paralysis / focal neurological deficits in disseminated disease

**Q: List two fungi that multiply within macrophages.**
**A:**
- *Histoplasma capsulatum*
- *Cryptococcus neoformans*
- *Aspergillus fumigatus*

**Q: List two fungi that produce melanin.**
**A:**
- *Paracoccidioides brasiliensis*
- *Wangiella (Exophiala) dermatitidis*

**Q: List two fungi that affect the CNS.**
**A:**
- *Cryptococcus neoformans*
- *Histoplasma capsulatum*

**Q: List one fungus whose etiology is a basidiomycete.**
**A:**
- *Cryptococcus neoformans* (its teleomorph/sexual form is *Filobasidiella neoformans*, a basidiomycete; pigeon droppings are the environmental reservoir)

**Q: List two fungi that form yeast-like cells.**
**A:**
- *Candida albicans*
- *Histoplasma capsulatum*

**Q: List one similarity between 5-flucytosine and fluconazole.**
**A:** Both are antifungal agents (ergosterol biosynthesis pathway interference). More precisely, both are used to treat systemic fungal infections including cryptococcal meningitis, and both can penetrate the CNS.

**Q: List one similarity between *Sporothrix schenckii* and *Blastomyces dermatitidis*.**
**A:** Both are thermally dimorphic fungi (mold at 25°C, yeast at 37°C). Both also cause subcutaneous/deep mycoses in geographically defined endemic regions.

**Q: List one similarity between *Epidermophyton* and *Microsporum*.**
**A:** Both are dermatophytes that cause cutaneous mycoses (tinea infections), and both have many common commensal-related characteristics.

**Q: List one similarity between Clotrimazole and Miconazole.**
**A:** Both are azole antifungals that inhibit fungal ergosterol biosynthesis by blocking the cytochrome P450-dependent enzyme lanosterol 14-α-demethylase.

**Q: List one similarity between White Piedra and Paracoccidioidomycosis.**
**A:** Both are fungal infections; White Piedra is caused by *Trichosporon* spp. and Paracoccidioidomycosis by *Paracoccidioides brasiliensis*, but both can involve granulomatous or inflammatory tissue responses.

**Q: Name one fungus with the following characteristics:**
**(a) Production of sclerotic bodies**
**A:** *Phialophora verrucosa* (agent of chromoblastomycosis)
**(b) Characterized by erythema nodosum**
**A:** *Coccidioides immitis* (coccidioidomycosis)
**(c) Associated with pigeon droppings**
**A:** *Cryptococcus neoformans*

**Q: Describe dimorphism in relation to systemic fungi.**
**A:** Dimorphic fungi exist in two distinct morphological forms depending on temperature and environment:
- At **25°C (environmental/room temperature):** they grow as **molds** (filamentous, with hyphae and conidia) — the infective form
- At **37°C (human body temperature):** they convert to **yeast** forms (or spherules in Coccidioides) — the pathogenic tissue form
This conversion is triggered by temperature, CO₂ concentration, and nutritional factors. Examples include *Histoplasma capsulatum*, *Blastomyces dermatitidis*, *Paracoccidioides brasiliensis*, *Sporothrix schenckii*, and *Coccidioides immitis*.

**Q: Outline risk factors to fungal infections.**
**A:**
1. **Immunosuppression** — HIV/AIDS, corticosteroid therapy, malignancy (patients cannot mount an effective immune response against fungal spores)
2. **Occupation** — Farmers, construction workers, and archaeologists are exposed to fungal spores in soil
3. **Obesity** — The skin folds provide an excellent warm, moist environment for fungal growth
4. **Poor hygiene** — Fungal infections manifest more frequently in people with poor hygiene and superficial fungal disease risk
5. **Indwelling catheters** — In hospitals, these provide excellent routes for fungal etiological agents into the body

**Q: Explain any two virulence factors used by fungi to cause disease in the host.**
**A:**
**1. Toxins:**
Fungi elaborate mycotoxins such as aflatoxins and ergot alkaloids. These toxins are harmful to host cells and lead to damage of normal cellular structures. They enable the fungi to colonise the host cells and use the dead cells to provide nutrition.
**2. Cytokines:**
Fungi mediate the release of cytokines that suppress the normal immunological attack against them, thereby enhancing their survival. The immune system is not able to mount an effective response.

**Q: Compare Eumycetoma and Actinomycetoma.**
| Feature | Eumycetoma | Actinomycetoma |
|---|---|---|
| **Etiology** | Fungal (e.g., *Madurella mycetomatis*) | Bacterial (e.g., *Nocardia spp.*, *Actinomadura*) |
| **Cell membrane** | Ergosterol | Phospholipids |
| **Cell wall** | Chitin | Peptidoglycan |
| **Nuclear membrane** | Present | Absent (prokaryote) |
| **Treatment** | Antifungals | Antibiotics |

**Q: Explain the clinical manifestation of Tinea versicolor.**
**A:**
Tinea versicolor is a superficial fungal mycosis caused by the fungi *Malassezia furfur* and *Malassezia globosa*.
It manifests as:
- **Hyperpigmentation** — the fungus causes hyperpigmentation of the superficial layer of the skin, causing the skin to thicken and become hyperpigmented
- **Hypopigmentation** — the fungus interferes with the biochemical synthesis of melanin from its tyrosine precursor; deficiency of melanin causes the manifestation of hypopigmentation
- **Irregular macules** — skin appears with irregular hyperpigmented or hypopigmented macules
- **Mild itching**
- **Non-contagious** — appears on back, chest, upper arm, and chest (widespread to skin)
- **Scaling** of affected areas

**Q: Explain any five modes of action of antifungals.**
**A:**
1. **Polyenes (Nystatin & Amphotericin B):** Bind to ergosterol in the fungal cell membrane and create leakage through the membrane, leading to fungal death. This means there is no ergosterol available for cell membrane synthesis, so the fungi cannot grow.
2. **Azoles (Ketoconazole, Fluconazole, Itraconazole):** Interfere with the synthesis of ergosterol by inhibiting the cytochrome P450-dependent enzyme lanosterol 14-α-demethylase. Lack of ergosterol means no synthesis of cell membranes, so the fungi cannot grow.
3. **Allylamines (Terbinafine):** Selective for the enzyme squalene oxidase. Lack of ergosterol means no synthesis of cell membranes.
4. **Flucytosine:** A pyrimidine analogue; interferes with the synthesis of nucleic acids by terminating their elongation.
5. **Griseofulvin:** Selective for the mitotic spindle by binding to microtubules and interfering with normal cellular reproduction.

**Q: Briefly describe the classification of dermatophytes.**
**A:**
Dermatophytes are classified into three genera based on morphology:
1. **Trichophyton** — infects hair, skin, and nails; produces both macroconidia and microconidia; most common genus in tinea pedis, tinea unguium, and tinea capitis
2. **Microsporum** — infects hair and skin (not nails); large, thick-walled, spindle-shaped macroconidia with rough walls; zoophilic species common (e.g., *M. canis*)
3. **Epidermophyton** — infects skin and nails only (not hair); smooth, thin-walled club-shaped macroconidia; no microconidia; *E. floccosum* is the only species
They are further classified ecologically as:
- **Anthropophilic** — infect humans primarily (chronic, mild inflammation)
- **Zoophilic** — infect animals, spread to humans (acute, intense inflammation)
- **Geophilic** — soil-dwelling, occasional human infection

**Q: Compare and contrast Madura foot and Chromoblastomycosis.**
| Feature | Madura Foot (Mycetoma) | Chromoblastomycosis |
|---|---|---|
| **Etiology** | *Madurella*, *Pseudallescheria* spp. | *Fonsecaea*, *Phialophora*, *Cladosporium* |
| **Entry** | Traumatic inoculation (thorns, splinters) | Traumatic inoculation |
| **Location** | Foot, leg | Lower extremities |
| **Tissue form** | Grains/granules in exudate | Sclerotic (Medlar) bodies — copper-penny cells |
| **Appearance** | Swelling, sinus tracts, bone destruction | Warty, cauliflower-like verrucous lesions |
| **Spread** | Local, into bone and deeper tissue | Local; rarely systemic |
| **Treatment** | Antifungals ± surgery | Itraconazole, flucytosine; surgery |

**Q: State the significance of dyes/stains in fungal disease diagnosis.**
**A:**
Stains are critical in the laboratory identification of fungi:
1. **KOH (Potassium Hydroxide) wet mount** — digests keratin and host tissue, revealing fungal hyphae and yeast cells in skin scrapings, nail clippings, and hair. Used for dermatophytes and *Malassezia*
2. **Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS) stain** — stains fungal cell walls magenta/red; used in histological sections
3. **Gomori's Methenamine Silver (GMS)** — stains fungi black against a green background; highly sensitive for *Pneumocystis jirovecii* and other fungi in tissue
4. **India Ink** — negative stain revealing the polysaccharide capsule of *Cryptococcus neoformans* in CSF as a clear halo around yeast cells
5. **Calcofluor White** — fluorescent dye binding to chitin in fungal cell walls; used with fluorescence microscopy for rapid detection

## SECTION C — ESSAY QUESTIONS

**Q: Discuss the causative agent, clinical manifestation, and treatment of Histoplasmosis.**

**CAUSATIVE AGENT**
*Histoplasma capsulatum* — a thermally dimorphic fungus:
- At **25°C:** exists as a mold with tuberculate macroconidia and oval microconidia (infective form)
- At **37°C (tissue):** converts to small, oval yeast cells (2–4 µm) that reproduce by budding and survive intracellularly within macrophages
**Ecology:** Found in soil enriched with bird and bat droppings (chicken coops, caves, construction sites). Endemic to the Ohio and Mississippi River valleys in the USA, and parts of Africa and Latin America.
**Transmission:** Inhalation of microconidia from disturbed soil.

**CLINICAL MANIFESTATIONS**
**Phase 1 — Acute Pulmonary Histoplasmosis:**
Mild manifestations occur when microconidia are inhaled into the lungs and develop into yeast. This attracts immune cells such as neutrophils. In the early stages, manifestations are mild and include headaches, nausea, and a mild fever.
**Phase 2 — Chronic Pulmonary Histoplasmosis:**
After the initial acute manifestations, the disease progresses to the second phase in which symptoms are now more severe. This is because the fungus is now active in granulomatous formation within the lungs and the body's immune system is working extremely hard to fight the pathogen. These include high fever, chronic productive cough, weight loss, and vomiting/diarrhoea. These manifestations closely resemble those of Tuberculosis.
**Disseminated Histoplasmosis:**
Through translocation in white blood cells and haematogenously, the yeast is transported through the body system. Manifests as hepatosplenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, as well as skin and mucocutaneous lesions. Seen in immunocompromised patients (HIV/AIDS).

**TREATMENT**
- **Oral Itraconazole** — first-line for mild to moderate disease
- **Amphotericin B** — for severe or disseminated disease, especially in immunocompromised patients

**Q: Discuss classes of fungi based on reproduction.**
**A:**
There are four classes of fungi based on reproduction:
**1. Ascomycota:**
- Resemble that of a sac
- Sexually, they reproduce through ascospores (contained in a structure called an ascus)
- Asexually, they reproduce through conidia
- Reproduction is located in an ascus
**2. Basidiomycota:**
- Resemble that of a basidium (mushroom/club-shaped)
- Sexually, they reproduce through basidiospores
- Asexually, they reproduce through conidia
- Examples: *Cryptococcus neoformans*, mushrooms
**3. Deuteromycota (Fungi Imperfecti):**
- Belong to the class of imperfect fungi
- Have a complex reproduction system — the sexual reproduction method is not clearly known
- They are imperfect fungi
- Examples include many dermatophytes and *Candida*
**4. Zygomycota:**
- Reproduce sexually through zygospores
- Reproduce asexually through conidia (sporangiospores)
- The conidia are thick-walled
- Examples: *Rhizopus*, *Mucor*

**Q: Discuss the clinical manifestation and treatment of Candidiasis.**
**A:**
**Causative Agent:** *Candida albicans* — an opportunistic mycosis caused by the fungus *Candida albicans*, which is normally a commensal of the skin, GI tract, and female genital tract.
**CLINICAL MANIFESTATIONS:**
**Mucocutaneous Manifestations:**
- **Oral (Thrush and Stomatitis):** Whitish tongue coloration; painful white plaques on oral mucosa
- **Vaginitis:** Vaginal inflammation, painful during sex and urination; whitish appearance; intense itching
- **Ocular:** When the candidiasis has spread to reach the tissues of the eye, it causes inflammation and vision difficulty
**Cutaneous Manifestations:**
- **Granulomatous Candidiasis:** Infection of the skin with subsequent mounting of an immune response. The response never contains the infection, and in return causes granulomas
- **Koilonychia:** Infection of the nails; they appear spooned
- **Diaper dermatitis:** In infants; manifests as a very erythematous papule eruption
**Systemic (Disseminated) Candidiasis:**
From disseminated candidiasis in the body it leads to manifestations such as:
- Osteoarthritis
- Genitourinary infection
- Endocarditis
- Pulmonary infection
- Meningitis
- Gastrointestinal infection
- Allergic eczematous candidiasis
**TREATMENT:**
- **Nystatin** — topical/oral mucocutaneous disease
- **Clotrimazole** — topical application
- **Amphotericin B** — severe systemic or disseminated candidiasis

**Q: Discuss five types of dermatophytoses.**
**A:**
**1. Tinea Capitis (Scalp Ringworm)**
Fungal infection of the scalp hair. Most commonly caused by *Trichophyton tonsurans* (endothrix pattern) and *Microsporum canis* (ectothrix). Presents with scaling, hair loss (alopecia), and in severe cases a boggy, inflammatory mass called a kerion. Diagnosed by Wood's lamp fluorescence (green fluorescence with *Microsporum*). Treated with oral griseofulvin or terbinafine.
**2. Tinea Corporis (Ringworm of the Body)**
Circular, dry, scaly, and pruritic lesion with raised advancing borders on smooth skin. Caused by *Trichophyton rubrum*, *T. tonsurans*, and *Microsporum canis*. KOH preparation of skin scrapings reveals branching, septate, nonpigmented hyphae. Treated with topical azoles or terbinafine.
**3. Tinea Pedis (Athlete's Foot)**
Most common dermatophytosis. Presents with itchy, white flaky patches between the toe webs and soles, characterized by inflammation and skin fissures. Caused primarily by *Trichophyton rubrum* and *T. mentagrophytes*. Risk factors include wearing tight shoes, sweating, and keeping feet wet for long periods. Treated with topical antifungals.
**4. Tinea Unguium / Onychomycosis (Nail Infection)**
Fungal infection of the nails causing thickened, discolored, brittle nails. Most commonly caused by *Trichophyton rubrum* and *T. mentagrophytes*. *Microsporum canis* does NOT typically infect nails. Requires prolonged oral antifungal therapy (itraconazole or terbinafine).
**5. Tinea Versicolor (Pityriasis Versicolor)**
Caused by *Malassezia furfur* and *M. globosa* (not a dermatophyte in the strict sense but classified under superficial mycoses). Presents with hypopigmented and hyperpigmented irregular macules on the trunk, back, chest, and upper arms. Non-contagious, not due to poor hygiene. Diagnosed by KOH preparation revealing hyphae and yeast ("spaghetti and meatballs" appearance). Treated with topical selenium sulphide or azoles.
