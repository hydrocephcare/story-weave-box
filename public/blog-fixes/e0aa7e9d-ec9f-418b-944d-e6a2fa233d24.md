### Liver Function Tests (LFTs) – Summary Notes
#### Introduction
- The liver is the second largest organ, weighing about **three pounds**, located under the **right rib cage**.
- Functions: **Metabolizes nutrients, detoxifies blood, and synthesizes proteins**.
- Liver Function Tests (LFTs) assess **liver health, detect damage, monitor disease, and evaluate treatment effectiveness**.

### 1. Common Liver Function Tests
#### a) Liver Enzyme Tests
- **Alanine Transaminase (ALT):** High levels indicate liver damage.
- **Aspartate Transaminase (AST):** Found in the liver and muscles; elevated levels suggest liver or muscle damage.
- **Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP):** Increased in liver or bile duct diseases.
- **Gamma-Glutamyl Transpeptidase (GGT):** High in liver and bile duct damage.

#### b) Liver Protein Tests
- **Total Protein:** Measures albumin and globulin.
- **Globulin:** Supports immunity; low levels may indicate liver disease.
- **Albumin:** Indicates liver’s protein synthesis function; low levels suggest liver failure.
- **Prothrombin Time (PT):** Measures clotting ability; prolonged PT suggests impaired liver function.

#### c) Bilirubin Tests
- **Bilirubin:** Breakdown product of red blood cells (RBCs); high levels cause **jaundice**.
- **Total Bilirubin:** Includes both conjugated (direct) and unconjugated (indirect) bilirubin.
- **Conjugated Bilirubin:** Water-soluble and excreted in bile; elevated levels suggest **bile duct obstruction**.
- **Unconjugated Bilirubin:** Fat-soluble; high levels indicate excessive RBC breakdown or impaired liver processing.

### 2. Bilirubin Formation and Detoxification
- RBCs are broken down in the **liver, spleen, and bone marrow** (Reticuloendothelial System - RES).
- **Heme → Choleglobin → Biliverdin (green) → Bilirubin (yellow, water-insoluble)**.
- **Bilirubin binds to albumin → enters the liver → conjugated with glucuronic acid → bilirubin diglucuronide (water-soluble)**.
- **Excretion Pathway:Bile → Small intestine → Converted to stercobilinogen → Oxidized to stercobilin (brown pigment in feces).**
- **Some stercobilinogen is reabsorbed → Liver → Excreted in urine as urobilinogen (oxidized to urobilin when exposed to air).**

### 3. Key Liver Function Test Analyses
- **Total Bilirubin Measurement:** Includes conjugated and unconjugated bilirubin.
- **Specific Bilirubin Determination:** Differentiates between **direct (conjugated)** and **indirect (unconjugated) bilirubin**.

### 4. Factors Affecting Liver Function Tests
- **Large Liver Reserve Capacity:** Liver can still function even with some damage.
- **Liver Regeneration:** The liver can **self-repair**, but chronic damage (e.g., cirrhosis) is irreversible.

### Diseases Related to Bilirubin Formation and Detoxification
#### 1. Jaundice (Icterus)
- Yellow **discoloration of skin, mucous membranes, and plasma** due to **elevated bilirubin levels** (>100 mL/L).
- Classified based on **cause and location** of bilirubin accumulation:

### 2. Types of Jaundice
#### A) Post-Hepatic Jaundice (Obstructive Jaundice)
- **Cause:** Blockage in bile flow due to **gallstones, tumors, or bile duct obstructions**.
- **Accumulated Bilirubin:** **Conjugated bilirubin (bilirubin diglucuronide)**.
- **Common Causes:Lumen obstruction:** Gallstones, parasitic worms.
- **Wall obstruction:** Bile duct cancer, strictures, atresia.
- **External compression:** Pancreatic cancer, lymph node enlargement at porta hepatis.
- **Symptoms:Severe jaundice, dark urine, pale stools**.
- **High conjugated bilirubin in serum and urine**.

##### Gallstones (Cholelithiasis)
- **Types:Cholesterol Stones** (most common) – Risk factors: obesity, age, female sex, diabetes, hyperlipidemia.
- **Pigment Stones** – Due to excessive bilirubin from hemolytic anemia.
- **Presentation:Biliary colic:** Severe **upper right quadrant pain**.
- **Intermittent jaundice, bilirubinuria**.
- **Elevated ALP, GGT levels**.
- **Prolonged painless obstructive jaundice**.

#### B) Pre-Hepatic Jaundice (Hemolytic Jaundice)
- **Cause:** Excess RBC breakdown → **increased bilirubin load on the liver**.
- **Accumulated Bilirubin:** **Unconjugated bilirubin**.
- **Causes:Hemolytic Disorders: **Sickle cell anemia.
- Hereditary spherocytosis.
- G6PD deficiency.
- Autoimmune hemolytic anemia.
- Blood group incompatibility (e.g., neonatal jaundice).
- Bacterial toxins and malaria.
- **Ineffective erythropoiesis:** Megaloblastic anemia.
- **Lab Findings:High unconjugated bilirubin (liver overwhelmed by RBC breakdown)**.
- **Normal conjugated bilirubin** (no bile duct obstruction).
- **No bilirubin in urine (unconjugated bilirubin is not water-soluble)**.
- **Increased urine urobilinogen** (increased bilirubin metabolism).
- **Hematological tests:** High reticulocyte count, RBC morphology changes.

#### C) Intrahepatic Jaundice (Hepatocellular Jaundice)
- **Cause:** **Liver cell damage → impaired bilirubin metabolism and excretion**.
- **Accumulated Bilirubin:** Both **unconjugated and conjugated bilirubin** (due to liver dysfunction and canaliculi obstruction).
- **Causes:Liver diseases:** Hepatitis, cirrhosis, drug toxicity, infections.
- **Inherited defects in bilirubin metabolism:Gilbert’s Syndrome: **Decreased UDP-glucuronosyltransferase (UDPGT) activity due to a **TA insertion mutation**.
- Mildly **elevated unconjugated bilirubin**, worsened by fasting or infections.
- **Crigler-Najjar Syndrome:Type I:** Complete absence of UDPGT enzyme → **severe unconjugated bilirubin elevation**.
- **Type II:** Partial UDPGT deficiency → milder symptoms.
- **Defective bilirubin excretion:Dubin-Johnson Syndrome & Rotor Syndrome:Benign inherited disorders** causing **increased conjugated bilirubin** in blood and urine due to impaired excretion.

### 3. Key Diagnostic Features of Jaundice Types
These conditions highlight the importance of **bilirubin metabolism** in diagnosing **liver function and systemic diseases**.
### Classification of Jaundice by Type of Bilirubin
#### 1. Unconjugated Hyperbilirubinemia
- **Physiological Jaundice**Occurs on days **3-5** after birth (never on day 1).
- Bilirubin levels may reach **150 μM/L** (all unconjugated).
- Managed with **phototherapy**.
- **Blood Group Incompatibility (Hemolytic Disease of the Newborn)**Develops on **day 1**.
- Caused by **Rhesus or ABO incompatibility**.
- Maternal **IgG antibodies** attack fetal RBCs → **hemolysis**.
- In severe cases, may cause **hydrops fetalis** (fetal anemia & heart failure).
- **Inherited Hemolytic Disorders**Similar to hemolytic diseases in adults.

##### Complications
- **Kernicterus**: Risk increases when **unconjugated bilirubin > 200 μM/L**.
- Risk factors:Low **albumin**.
- **Drugs** that displace bilirubin from albumin.
- **Acidosis, hypoxia, hypoglycemia, hypothermia, sepsis** (increase bilirubin entry into the brain).
- **Heparin** (releases free fatty acids that displace bilirubin).

##### Clinical Features
- **Lethargy**
- **Hypotonia**
- **Poor feeding**
- **Spasticity** → Can lead to **death**.

##### Treatment
- **Phototherapy**Light at **440-470 nm** converts bilirubin into water-soluble isomers excreted in urine.
- **Exchange Transfusion**Required if bilirubin > **305 μM/L** (term babies) or > **200 μM/L** (preterm babies).

#### 2. Conjugated Hyperbilirubinemia
- **Neonatal Hepatitis (Infectious Jaundice)**Causes: **Rubella, CMV, herpes simplex, toxoplasmosis**.
- Liver immaturity (especially in preterm infants) contributes to jaundice.
- **Shorter RBC lifespan & increased RBC mass** contribute to higher bilirubin load.
- **Biliary Atresia**Cause unclear; may be due to **toxic bile duct degeneration** rather than congenital malformation.
- **Metabolic Disorders**Various inherited conditions can lead to **impaired bilirubin metabolism**.

##### Treatment
- Treat the **underlying cause** (e.g., infection, metabolic disorder).

### Determination of Bilirubin Levels: Van den Bergh Test
- Detects **conjugated and unconjugated bilirubin** in circulation.

##### Reagents Used
- **Van den Bergh Reagent A** – Sulfuric acid (**H₂SO₄**)
- **Van den Bergh Reagent B** – Dilute sodium nitrite (**NaNO₂**)
- **Ehrlich’s Diazole Reagent** – Formed by mixing Reagent A & B in a **5:0.15 ratio**.

##### Procedure & Interpretation
- **Direct (Positive) Test** – Immediate **pink color** (within **30 sec**) → **High conjugated bilirubin**.
- **Indirect (Negative) Test** – No color change → Suggests:Presence of **unconjugated bilirubin** (water-insoluble).
- **No bilirubin at all**.
- **Confirmatory Step**Add **2.5 mL methanol** → If **pink color appears**, it confirms **unconjugated bilirubin**.

##### Differentiation of Jaundice Using Van den Bergh Test
- **Hemolytic (Pre-Hepatic) Jaundice** → **Indirect positive** (↑ Unconjugated bilirubin).
- **Obstructive (Post-Hepatic) Jaundice** → **Direct positive** (↑ Conjugated bilirubin).
- **Hepatocellular (Intrahepatic) Jaundice** → **Both direct & indirect positive** (Mix of both bilirubin types).

### Bile Acid (Bile Salt) Metabolism
#### 1. Source of Bile Acids
- Derived from **cholesterol metabolism**.

#### 2. Formation of Bile Acids
- **Primary bile acids** (formed in liver):**Cholic acid**
- **Chenodeoxycholic acid**
- Rate-limiting step: **7-α-hydroxylation of cholesterol**.
- Conjugated with **glycine or taurine** → Increases **solubility**.

#### 3. Enterohepatic Circulation
- **Bacteria in gut** convert primary bile acids into **secondary bile acids**:**Lithocholic acid**
- **Deoxycholic acid**
- **Reabsorption:75% in ileum, 10% in colon** → Transported back to liver via **portal circulation**.
- **Liver damage** impairs bile acid reuptake → Leads to **fat malabsorption & steatorrhea**.

### Short Important Notes on Bile Acids, Liver Function Tests & Plasma Ammonia
#### Circulating Bile Acids
- Bile acids are reabsorbed in the **ileum**, enter portal circulation, and return to the liver.
- **Obstruction in the biliary tree** → reflux of bile acids into systemic circulation → **severe pruritus (itching)**.
- **Liver damage** → impaired reabsorption → **high postprandial systemic bile acid levels**.
- **Ileal disease** → bile acid loss in feces → **low systemic bile acid levels**.

#### Structure of Bile Acids
- **Amphipathic**: Hydrophilic (-OH groups) & hydrophobic (steroid nucleus) → detergent properties.
- Helps in **micelle formation** for lipid digestion.

#### Functions of Bile Acids
- **Cholesterol excretion**
- **Fat emulsification** (aiding digestion & absorption)
- **Bile flow stimulation**

### Liver Enzymes & Liver Function Tests
#### Markers of Liver Cell Damage
- **AST (Aspartate transaminase)**: Found in **liver, RBCs, skeletal & cardiac muscle**.
- **ALT (Alanine transaminase)**: More **liver-specific** than AST.
- **ALT/AST Ratio>1** → Infectious hepatitis or cirrhosis
- **<1** → Myocardial infarction
- **=1** → Viral hepatitis
- **LDH (Lactate Dehydrogenase)**: Liver & muscle-specific isoenzyme **LD5**.

#### Markers of Cholestasis
- **ALP (Alkaline Phosphatase)**: Found in **liver, bone, placenta, GIT**.
- **GGT (Gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase)**: Liver-specific
- Increased by **cholestasis, alcohol, barbiturates, phenytoin**

### Plasma Proteins in Liver Disease
- **Albumin** ↓ in **chronic liver disease** but not useful in acute disease (long t½ ~18 days).
- **Clotting factors** (short t½, e.g., Factor VII = 4h) → **PT/PTT prolonged** in liver disease.
- **Immunoglobulins** ↑ in chronic liver disease due to **GIT antigen shunting**:**IgM ↑** → Primary biliary cirrhosis
- **IgA ↑** → Alcoholic cirrhosis (causes "beta-gamma bridging")
- **IgG ↑** → Autoimmune chronic active hepatitis
- **AFP (Alpha-fetoprotein)** ↑ in **hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)**.
- **α1-antitrypsin deficiency** → Childhood cirrhosis & early **emphysema**.
- **Ferritin** ↑ in **iron overload & liver necrosis**.

### Plasma Ammonia & Hepatic Encephalopathy
- **Normal plasma ammonia**: < 40 μM/L
- **Elevated ammonia** → **hepatic encephalopathy**
- **Causes of Hyperammonaemia**:**Liver failure**
- **Transient neonatal hyperammonaemia** (due to immature liver)
- **Urea cycle enzyme defects** (diagnosed by **specific amino acid abnormalities** in plasma/urine)
- **Plasma ammonia sample handling**:**Must be tested within 1 hour**
- Blood should be **sent on ice & centrifuged immediately**
- **CSF Glutamine**: Increased in **hepatic encephalopathy**.

### Kidney Function Tests (KFTs)
#### Normal Functions of the Kidney
- Maintains extracellular fluid balance by:Excreting waste products (urea, creatinine, urate, H⁺).
- Retaining necessary substances (proteins, glucose, amino acids, HCO₃⁻).
- Acts as an endocrine gland: **Erythropoietin** (stimulates RBC production).
- **Renin** (regulates blood pressure).
- **1-alpha-hydroxylation of Vitamin D** (activates calcitriol).
- Utilizes **¼ of cardiac output** and produces **180 L/day of filtrate** via ~1 million nephrons/kidney.

### Glomerular Function
- Three filtration layers: **Capillary endothelium, basement membrane, visceral epithelium**.
- The **basement membrane** is the primary barrier, with a **negative charge** preventing large anions (e.g., proteins) from passing.
- **Hydrostatic pressure** drives filtration; a drop in blood pressure can cause **oliguria** (low urine output).**Polyuria** = excessive urine output.
- **Oliguria** = reduced urine output.
- **Anuria** = absence of urine output.

### Tubular Function
#### Proximal Tubule
- **Reabsorbs 80% of Na⁺ and water**; Cl⁻ follows passively.
- **Low plasma chloride** can limit Na⁺ recovery, contributing to **alkalosis after prolonged vomiting**.
- Reabsorbs **glucose, amino acids, phosphate** (inhibited by PTH).
- **Secretes organic acids, urate, and drugs**.
- **Fanconi Syndrome**: Loss of proximal tubular function → **glycosuria, amino aciduria, phosphaturia, acidosis, polyuria**.

#### Loop of Henle
- **Counter-current multiplier** allows dilution/concentration of urine.
- **Thick ascending limb actively pumps NaCl**, but is **water impermeable**, making fluid **hypotonic** (~120-150 mosm/L).
- Further dilution can occur in the **collecting ducts** under conditions of water diuresis.

#### Distal Convoluted Tubule (DCT)
- **Little change in volume or concentration**.
- **Aldosterone** (via renin-angiotensin system) promotes **Na⁺ reabsorption in exchange for K⁺/H⁺**.
- Affected in: **Conn’s syndrome (↑ aldosterone)** → Na⁺ retention, K⁺ loss.
- **Addison’s disease (↓ aldosterone)** → Na⁺ loss, K⁺ retention.
- **Renal tubular acidosis (RTA Type I)** → H⁺ excretion defect.

#### Collecting Ducts
- **Antidiuretic Hormone (ADH)** increases water permeability, promoting reabsorption.
- ADH-related conditions: **Diabetes insipidus (polyuria, dilute urine):Pituitary form** → No ADH production.
- **Nephrogenic form** → Tubules unresponsive to ADH.
- **Syndrome of Inappropriate ADH (SIADH):Low urine output, concentrated urine, dilutional hyponatremia**.

### Tests of Renal Function
#### Glomerular Function Tests
- **Serum Creatinine**: Derived from **muscle creatine phosphate**.
- Filtered by glomerulus, minimally reabsorbed/secreted.
- **Increases in chronic renal disease & acute renal failure**.
- Affected by **muscle mass, diet (meat intake)**.

### Kidney Function Tests (KFT) - Important Notes
#### Kidney Functions
- **Maintains extracellular fluid balance**: Excretes waste (urea, creatinine, urate, H⁺)
- Retains essential substances (proteins, glucose, amino acids, HCO₃⁻)
- **Endocrine Functions**: Produces **erythropoietin** (stimulates RBC production)
- Produces **renin** (regulates BP)
- Activates Vitamin D (1-alpha hydroxylation)

#### Glomerular Function
- Glomerulus has **three layers**: Capillary endothelium
- Basement membrane (main filtration barrier, negatively charged)
- Visceral epithelium (podocytes)
- **Filtration pressure** is low (~1 kPa), meaning mild BP drops can cause **oliguria**.

#### Tubular Function
- **Proximal Tubule**: Reabsorbs **80% Na⁺, water, glucose, amino acids, HCO₃⁻**.
- Secretes **organic acids, urate, drugs**.
- **Fanconi Syndrome**: Loss of proximal tubular function → **glycosuria, amino aciduria, phosphaturia**.
- **Loop of Henle**:**Counter-current multiplier** creates concentrated urine.
- **Thick ascending limb** actively pumps NaCl but is water-impermeable.
- Filtrate exits **hypotonic (~120-150 mosm/L)**.
- **Distal Convoluted Tubule (DCT)**:**Aldosterone** promotes Na⁺ retention and K⁺/H⁺ excretion.
- Disorders affecting DCT: **Conn’s syndrome, Addison’s disease, Renal Tubular Acidosis (RTA) type I**.
- **Collecting Duct**:**ADH (Vasopressin)** increases water reabsorption.
- **Pathologies**:**Diabetes insipidus** (polyuria with dilute urine)
- **SIADH** (excess ADH, concentrated urine, dilutional hyponatremia)

### Tests of Renal Function
#### Glomerular Function Tests
- **Serum Creatinine**: Derived from **muscle metabolism**.
- Increases in **chronic kidney disease (CKD)**.
- Less reliable in **acute renal failure**.
- **Creatinine Clearance (CrCl)**: Measures **GFR**: CrCl=Urine Creatinine×VolumePlasma CreatinineCrCl = \frac{{Urine \,Creatinine \times Volume}}{{Plasma \,Creatinine}}
- **More reliable than serum creatinine** in tracking **chronic renal failure**.
- **Normal CrCl** = **~120 mL/min**.
- **Blood Urea**: Formed from **protein breakdown** in the liver.
- **Increased in**: High protein diet, GI bleeding, dehydration, catabolic states.
- **Decreased in**: Liver disease, dialysis, low protein diet.
- **Estimated GFR (eGFR)**: Uses formulas like **MDRD** to estimate kidney function.
- Takes into account **serum creatinine, age, sex, ethnicity**.
- Not accurate in **children (use Schwartz equation instead)**.

#### Causes of Abnormal Urea:Creatinine Ratio
- **Increased**: High protein intake, GI bleeding, dehydration, muscle wasting.
- **Decreased**: Low protein intake, dialysis, liver disease.

### Tubular Function Tests
- **Urinary Na⁺ concentration** (normally low unless on high salt diet).
- **Urinary osmolality tests** (concentration after water load or Pitressin).
- **Acidification tests** (NH₄Cl challenge for RTA diagnosis).

### Renal Disorders
#### Nephrotic Syndrome
- **Proteinuria > 2.5 g/day**, **hypoalbuminemia**, **oedema**, **hyperlipidemia**.
- **Selectivity index** (IgG/Albumin clearance ratio) tracks disease progression.
- **Liver compensates** by increasing α₂ globulins (e.g., α₂ macroglobulin).

#### Acute Renal Failure (ARF)
- Defined by **urine output < 450 mL/day** with rising **blood urea (>6.7 mMol/L)**.
- **Causes**:**Pre-renal failure**: Hypovolemia, low cardiac output, renovascular obstruction.
- **Intra-renal failure**: Acute tubular necrosis (ischemic/toxic), glomerulonephritis, interstitial nephritis.
- **Post-renal failure**: Urinary obstruction (e.g., prostate enlargement, stones).
- **Distinguishing Pre-renal vs. Intra-renal Failure**: Test Pre-Renal Intra-Renal **Urine Na⁺ (mMol/L)** < 20 > 40 **Urine/Plasma Osmolality Ratio** > 1.4 < 1.1 **Urine/Plasma Urea Ratio** > 14 < 10

### Management of Acute Renal Failure (ARF)
#### Post-Renal ARF
- Relieve obstruction to restore urine flow.
- Monitor for **post-obstructive diuresis**, which may cause **polyuria** and electrolyte imbalances.

#### Pre-Renal ARF
- Restore blood volume to normalize **blood pressure and glomerular filtration rate (GFR)**.

#### Acute Tubular Necrosis (ATN)
- **Fluid management: **If hypovolemic: cautiously replace fluids to avoid overload (**risk of cardiac failure**).
- Maintain balance: **500 mL/day for insensible loss + previous day’s urine output**.
- Monitor weight daily.
- **Electrolyte management:Sodium (Na⁺):** Restrict if oliguric; may require supplementation in the diuretic phase.
- **Potassium (K⁺):** Restrict if oliguric; may need dialysis if severe **hyperkalemia**. Supplement if **hypokalemia** occurs in the diuretic phase.
- **Acid-Base Balance: **Severe **metabolic acidosis** may need bicarbonate therapy but avoid sodium overload.
- **Indications for Dialysis:Blood urea > 50 mmol/L & rising**
- **Bicarbonate < 10 mmol/L**
- **K⁺ > 7.0 mmol/L** or ECG changes.
- **Course of ARF:**Oliguric phase (low urine output) → Polyuric phase (osmotic diuresis due to high urea).
- **Risk of hypokalemia and dehydration** in the recovery phase—requires close monitoring.

### Chronic Renal Failure (CRF)
- **Common causes:** Glomerulonephritis, **Diabetes Mellitus**, Hypertension.
- **Progressive nephron loss** leads to polyuria (**opposite of ARF**).

#### Early Signs
- Increased urea (**chronic uraemia/azotaemia**).
- **Elevated creatinine & reduced clearance**.
- Increased **urate, phosphate, sulfate**.

#### Later Features
- Tubular dysfunction → **Polyuria with fixed output**.
- Loss of **concentrating/diluting abilities**.
- **Metabolic acidosis (high anion gap)**.
- **Sodium instability** (overload or depletion).

#### Management of CRF
- **Water intake** controlled by thirst (since urine output is fixed).
- **Strict control of Na⁺, K⁺, and protein intake**.
- **Erythropoietin for anemia**.
- **Oral bicarbonate** if severe acidosis.
- **End-stage CRF Treatment: **Dialysis (**hemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis**).
- **Renal transplantation**.

### Polyuria** (Excessive Urine Output)#### 
Water Diuresis (Urine Osmolality < 200 mOsm/kg)**
- **Compulsive water drinking**.
- **Diabetes insipidus (neurogenic or nephrogenic)**.

#### Osmotic Diuresis (Urine Osmolality > 300 mOsm/kg)
- **Due to unabsorbed solutes in urine:Na⁺ excess** (dietary, diuretics, salt-losing nephritis).
- **Urea** (CRF, ATN recovery phase, post-renal failure).
- **Glucose** (**Diabetes Mellitus**).
- **Mannitol** (osmotic agent).

### Renal Stones (Nephrolithiasis)
#### Causes
- **High concentration of substances in urine** due to: **Low urine volume**.
- **High excretion rate** of solutes.
- **pH imbalance:Alkaline urine** → Calcium stones.
- **Acidic urine** → Uric acid stones.
- **Urinary stagnation** (due to obstruction).

#### Types of Stones
- **Calcium** (Oxalate & Phosphate).
- **Phosphate**.
- **Uric Acid** (10% of gout patients).
- **Rare Types:Cystine** (cystinuria, a transport defect).
- **Xanthine** (xanthine oxidase deficiency).
- **2,8-Dihydroxyadenine** (APRT enzyme deficiency).

#### Treatment
- **Increase fluid intake** to dilute urine.
- **Potassium citrate & thiazide diuretics** (to prevent calcium stones).
- **Alkalinization of urine** (to prevent uric acid stones).

### Renal Acidosis
#### Uraemic Acidosis
- Occurs in **acute & chronic renal failure** due to **reduced H⁺ excretion**.
- **High anion gap metabolic acidosis** due to **phosphate & sulfate retention**.
- Develops when **GFR < 20 mL/min**.
- Associated with **hyperkalemia**.

#### Renal Tubular Acidosis (RTA)
- **Normal anion gap metabolic acidosis** with normal or slightly decreased GFR.
- **Urine pH often inappropriately high** despite systemic acidosis.

#### Types of RTA
- **Type 1 (Distal RTA)Defective H⁺ secretion** in the distal tubule.
- **Urine pH > 5.5, hypokalemia, nephrocalcinosis, rickets**.
- Can be **genetic or acquired** (e.g., heavy metal toxicity).
- **Type 2 (Proximal RTA)Defective bicarbonate reabsorption** in the proximal tubule.
- **Hypokalemia, renal bicarbonate wasting, Fanconi syndrome**.
- Can be **genetic (e.g., cystinosis) or acquired (toxins)**.
- **Type 4 RTADue to mineralocorticoid deficiency or resistance**.
- Associated with **hyperkalemia**.

### Rare Inherited Renal Tubular Disorders
#### Liddle’s Syndrome (Pseudohyperaldosteronism)
- **Hypertension, hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis**.
- **Low renin & aldosterone**.
- Due to **excess Na⁺ reabsorption (ENaC channel defect)**.

#### Pseudohypoaldosteronism Type 1 (PHA1)
- **Failure to thrive, hyponatremia, salt wasting, hyperkalemia**.
- **Elevated renin & aldosterone**.
- **Genetic mutation in ENaC channels**.

### Bartter’s Syndrome
- **Features**: Failure to thrive (FTT), polyuria, nephrocalcinosis, short stature, mental retardation.
- **Electrolyte Imbalance**: Hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis, renal salt wasting, nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, hypercalciuria.
- **Cause**: Sporadic or autosomal recessive (AR) mutation.
- **Pathophysiology**: Overactivation of the **renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system** and hyperplasia of the **juxtaglomerular apparatus**.
- Increased production of **prostaglandin E2** in the kidney.
- Defects in epithelial cells of the **medullary thick ascending limb (mTAL)** of the nephron.
- **Key Mutations**:**NKCC2 (Na-K-Cl cotransporter)**: Impaired NaCl reabsorption → salt wasting, diuresis, kaliuresis, hypercalciuria.
- **ROMK1 (K+ channel)**: Prevents normal Na-K exchange, leading to metabolic alkalosis and hypovolaemia.
- **CLCKB (Basolateral Cl- channel)**: Causes further impairment of sodium and chloride handling.

### Gitelman’s Syndrome
- **Features**: Hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis, **hypocalciuria**, and **hypomagnesaemia**.
- **Inheritance**: Sporadic or autosomal recessive (AR).
- **Presentation**: Late childhood or early adulthood.
- **Pathophysiology**: Defect in the **thiazide-sensitive Na-Cl cotransporter (NCCT)** in the **distal convoluted tubule**.
- Similar effects to chronic thiazide diuretic use.
- Heterozygotes may develop **hypokalaemic alkalosis** if given loop or thiazide diuretics.

### Dent’s Disease (X-linked Hypercalciuric Nephrolithiasis)
- **Features**: Hypercalciuria, low-molecular-weight proteinuria, nephrocalcinosis, nephrolithiasis, renal failure.
- **Associated Conditions**: Fanconi syndrome-like tubulopathy, rickets.
- **Cause**: Mutation in **CLC5 (voltage-gated chloride channel)**.
- **Pathophysiology**: Impaired chloride transport may disrupt proximal tubule reabsorption.
- Carrier females may show mild **proteinuria** and **hypercalciuria**.
- **Treatment**: **Renal transplant** is effective in advanced disease.

## Tests to Assess Gastric Function
### 1. Fractional Test Meal (FTM)
- **Procedure**: Ryle’s tube inserted to aspirate **fasting** stomach contents.
- A test meal (e.g., rice, black coffee) is given.
- Gastric contents are collected **every 15 min for 2 hours**.
- Analysis of **free and total acidity**.
- **Use**: Rarely used today.

### 2. Pentagastrin Stimulation Test
- **Purpose**: Measures **gastric acid secretion** in response to pentagastrin (a gastrin analogue).
- **Procedure**: Fasting gastric contents aspirated (residual juice).
- Gastric juice collected for **1 hour** (basal acid output - BAO).
- **Pentagastrin (5 µg/kg)** administered.
- Gastric juice collected **every 15 min for 1 hour** (maximal acid output - MAO).
- Acidity measured by **titration with NaOH**.
- **Normal Values**:**BAO**: 4–10 mmol/hr
- **MAO**: 20–50 mmol/hr

### 3. Augmented Histamine Test Meal
- **Purpose**: Stimulates **maximal gastric acid secretion**.
- **Procedure**: Basal secretion collected for **1 hour**.
- Histamine injected **subcutaneously**.
- Gastric contents aspirated at **15 min intervals for 1 hour**.
- Acid content measured.

### 4. Insulin Test Meal (Hollander’s Test)
- **Purpose**: Assesses completeness of **vagotomy** (used in peptic ulcer surgery).
- **Procedure**:**Insulin (0.1 U/kg IV)** administered.
- Hypoglycemia induces **gastric acid secretion** via vagal stimulation.
- **No increase** in acid secretion → **successful vagotomy**.
- **Risk**: Hypoglycemia can be **dangerous**.

### 5. Tubeless Gastric Analysis
- **Principle**: Uses **cation exchange resin** that binds **H⁺ ions** in the stomach.
- Excreted in **urine** → Indirect measure of gastric acidity.
- **Use**: Screening test, less invasive.

## Abnormalities of Gastric Function
### Increased Gastric Acid Secretion
- **Causes**:**Zollinger-Ellison Syndrome** (gastrin-secreting tumor)
- **Chronic duodenal ulcer**
- **Gastric cell hyperplasia**
- **Excessive histamine production**

### Decreased Gastric Acid Secretion
- **Causes**:**Gastritis**
- **Gastric carcinoma**
- **Pernicious anemia** (loss of parietal cells, low intrinsic factor)
