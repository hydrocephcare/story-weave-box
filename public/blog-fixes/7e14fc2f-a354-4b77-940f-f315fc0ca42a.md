# Blood Transfusion

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Haematology and Transfusion Medicine**

> **In one line:** blood transfusion is the transfer of blood or blood components from a donor into a recipient's circulation to restore oxygen-carrying capacity, volume or haemostasis. Safety depends on correct patient identification, ABO and Rh compatibility, the right component at the right dose, close monitoring, and immediate recognition and management of transfusion reactions.

## What you must know

- Landsteiner discovered the **ABO groups in 1901** (Nobel Prize 1930). ABO and Rh are the clinically important systems.
- Group A has A antigen and anti-B; B has B antigen and anti-A; AB has both antigens and no antibodies; **O has no antigen and both anti-A and anti-B**.
- Red cell units: store at **2 to 6 degrees C**, up to **42 days** in SAG-M; **10 ml/kg raises Hb by about 2.5 g/dl**; give over 2 to 4 hours (maximum 4 hours per unit).
- Platelets: transfuse at **below 15 000**, below 50 000 with bleeding or before an invasive procedure; **1 unit/10 kg raises count by about 50 000**.
- FFP 20 ml/kg raises clotting factors by about 20%; cryoprecipitate for fibrinogen, VWD, factor VIII and XIII deficiency.
- Any suspected reaction: **stop the transfusion, keep the line open with 0.9% saline**, check the unit against the patient, inform the doctor and the laboratory.
- Irradiated products prevent **TA-GvHD**; CMV-negative products for selected patients.

## 1. Overview and history

- A procedure in which a patient receives blood or blood products through an IV line; the transfer of blood-based products from one person into another's circulation.
- Before 1901 transfusion caused many reactions and deaths, since all blood was thought to be the same. **Karl Landsteiner** showed that clumping was an immunological reaction, making blood typing possible.
- 450 ml of blood can save as many as 3 lives (it is separated into components). One in three people will need a transfusion in their lifetime.
- A donor can give blood every **90 days**. Recovery: plasma volume in 24 to 48 hours, red cells in about 3 weeks, platelets and white cells within minutes.

### Indications (purposes)

- Replace losses of circulating volume and oxygen-carrying capacity; restore metabolic homeostasis
- Replenish normal red cells in chronic disease, for example thalassaemia, sickle cell anaemia
- Cancer patients during or after chemotherapy
- Emergency surgery and major trauma
- Typical situations: premature babies, severe anaemia, cancer, bleeding disorders (haemophilia, thrombocytopenia), liver and cardiac surgery, major injury

> **Exam tip:** transfuse the patient, not the Hb number. A stable patient with chronic compensated anaemia (for example iron deficiency) should be treated with the cause, not blood.

## 2. Blood groups

- More than **20 genetically determined blood group systems**; **ABO and Rhesus** are the most important.
- Mixing incompatible blood causes **agglutination** (clumping) and haemolysis.

### ABO system

| Group | Red cell antigen | Plasma antibody |
|---|---|---|
| A | A | Anti-B |
| B | B | Anti-A |
| AB | A and B | None (universal recipient) |
| O | None | Anti-A and anti-B (universal red cell donor) |

### Rhesus (Rh) antigen

- Present = Rh positive; absent = Rh negative.
- A **transmembrane protein** with many loops exposed on the red cell surface; it appears to transport CO2 and/or ammonia across the membrane.
- Named after the rhesus monkey in which it was first found.
- Rh-negative women who receive Rh-positive blood, or carry an Rh-positive fetus, become sensitised and may cause haemolytic disease of the newborn in later pregnancies. Rh antigens are not on platelets, but a few red cells in a platelet unit can sensitise an Rh-negative patient.

## 3. Blood banking and donors

### Blood bank

- Collects, tests and stores blood.
- **Autologous transfusion** is possible if surgery is scheduled months in advance.
- Stored at **2 to 6 degrees C**; whole blood shelf life about 3 weeks (up to 35 days in preservative).
- After 24 to 72 hours of storage red cells release oxygen less well (2,3-DPG falls). For **massive transfusion** use blood **less than 7 days old**.

### Components of one unit of whole blood

- 450 ml donor blood plus 50 ml anticoagulant-preservative solution
- No functional platelets

### Donor selection

- Free of transmissible disease (TB, cancer, jaundice or other)
- No donation within the previous **90 days**
- Age **18 to 65 years**, physically active, average height and weight, normal vital signs
- Not pregnant within the last **6 months**
- **Hb above 12 g/dl**
- No recent dental surgery, major surgery, immunisation, or receipt of blood or products
- Not hungry or dehydrated
- After donation: offer a sweetened drink, rest for 1 to 2 hours, check vital signs frequently

Donor blood is screened for HIV, hepatitis B and C and syphilis (in Kenya, also malaria where relevant).

## 4. Responsibilities

### Requesting

- Check the patient's diagnosis and transfusion history.
- State special requirements, for example **irradiated** or **CMV negative**.
- Complete the request form.

### Special requirements

| Product | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **CMV negative** | Protects at-risk patients (neonates, transplant, immunosuppressed) |
| **Irradiated** | Prevents **transfusion-associated graft-versus-host disease** |
| **Antigen negative** | Avoids haemolytic reactions in patients with red cell antibodies |

### Pre-transfusion

- Assess laboratory values; verify the prescription.
- Assess vital signs, urine output, skin colour and history of previous reactions.
- Obtain venous access: central line if possible, or a **20-gauge** cannula.

### Sampling (crucial to avoid ABO-incompatible transfusion)

- Counter-check **name, date of birth and hospital number**; extra vigilance in unconscious patients.
- Label the sample at the bedside with name, DOB, hospital number, gender and the **signature of the person taking it**.
- Group and screen the recipient and donor cells for ABO and Rh; screen for atypical antibodies (about 1 to 2% of people have them). Crossmatching mixes donor cells with recipient serum and looks for agglutination.

### Prescribing

- Each unit is entered **separately** on the prescription sheet, with product type, special requirements and rate. **Maximum 4 hours per unit.**

### During transfusion

- Use appropriately filtered tubing.
- **Baseline** BP, pulse and temperature; repeat at **15 minutes**; further set at the end.
- Secure the cannula; review fluid balance and medicines.
- Stay with the patient for the **first 15 to 30 minutes**.

### Documentation

Reason for transfusion, current blood results, component and amount, anticipated outcome, any adverse reactions, and review after transfusion of how much was given.

### Warming blood

- Surgical patients lose heat via wound or cavity exposure; large volumes of cold blood cause **hypothermia and arrhythmia**. At rates above **100 ml/min**, cold blood may contribute to cardiac arrest.
- Warm blood in large, rapid transfusions and in infant exchange transfusion, only in a **blood warmer with a thermometer and an audible alarm**. Never microwave or place in hot water.

## 5. Types of transfusion

| Basis | Types |
|---|---|
| **Time** | Fresh whole blood; stored (CPD: citrate, phosphate, dextrose) |
| **Composition** | Whole blood; blood fractions (components) |
| **Donor** | **Autologous** (self donation); **allogeneic** (another donor) |

## 6. Blood components

### Whole blood

- Storage up to 35 days; 1 unit has 50 ml anticoagulant preservative and no functional platelets.
- Start within **30 minutes** of leaving the refrigerator; complete within **4 hours**.
- Use a filter: platelets and clotting factors are inactive after 3 to 5 days.
- **Indications:** massive blood loss, trauma, exchange transfusion.

### Red cell concentrate (packed cells)

- Storage up to **42 days** in **SAG-M** (saline-adenine-glucose-mannitol) at 2 to 6 degrees C; can be frozen.
- **Haematocrit 50 to 70%**; supplied in 250 ml bags.
- **Indications:** anaemia, hypoxia. The recipient must not have antibodies against donor cells.
- **Dose 10 ml/kg raises Hb by about 2.5 g/dl**; over 2 to 4 hours, **slower in chronic anaemia**; maximum 15 to 20 ml/kg in haemodynamically stable patients.
- May need premedication (for example an antipyretic); routinely leucodepleted.
- Complication: **pulmonary oedema** from rapid infusion, or a transfusion reaction.

### Platelets

- **Indications:** thrombocytopenia with platelets **below 15 000**; **below 50 000 with bleeding**; **below 50 000 before an invasive procedure**.
- Contain leucocytes and cytokines (hence febrile reactions).
- **1 unit per 10 kg raises platelets by about 50 000.**
- ABO antigens are on platelets, so ABO-compatible platelets are ideal; Rh antigens are absent.
- Single or apheresis units: **4 units/m2**; in children 1 to 2 apheresis units; one apheresis unit equals 6 to 8 whole-blood derived packs from a single donor.
- Infuse over **20 to 40 minutes** with a filter; premedicate if there is a history of reactions.

### Plasma and fresh frozen plasma (FFP)

- Contains clotting factors (1 unit/ml); 200 ml bags; stored 12 months at -18 degrees C or colder.
- **Indications:** clotting factor deficiency, fibrinogen replacement, **DIC**, **liver disease**, exchange transfusion, massive transfusion.
- Must be **ABO compatible**; in children also Rh compatible. Allow time to **thaw**.
- **Usual dose 20 ml/kg raises clotting factors by about 20%.**

### Cryoprecipitate

- Collected when FFP is thawed at 4 degrees C; refrozen and stored up to 1 year at -18 degrees C.
- **Use:** fibrinogen deficiency or dysfibrinogenaemia, **von Willebrand disease**, **factor VIII or XIII deficiency**, DIC (not used alone).
- ABO compatibility preferred but not essential.
- **Dose 1 unit per 5 to 10 kg.** Pooled units (3 to 6 g fibrinogen in 200 to 500 ml) raise fibrinogen by about 1 g/l.
- Infuse within **6 hours**.

### Granulocyte transfusions

- Prepared fresh for immediate use (no storage).
- **Use:** severe neutropenia with infection not responding to antibiotics, when marrow recovery is expected.
- Donor receives **G-CSF and steroids or hetastarch**.
- Complications: severe allergic reactions; can irradiate to prevent GvHD.

### Leucocyte-reduction filters

- Prevent febrile and alloimmune reactions and CMV transmission; used with red cells, platelets, FFP and cryoprecipitate.
- Not needed for albumin, colloids and factor concentrates.
- **Never use with stem cell or bone marrow infusions.**
- May reduce red cells by 5 to 10%. **They do not prevent GvHD** (irradiation does).

## 7. Transfusion reactions

### First response to any suspected reaction

1. **Stop the transfusion.**
2. Inform the medical and laboratory teams.
3. **Check** the unit against the patient's details.
4. Replace the product with **0.9% saline** through new tubing.
5. Send the discontinued unit with the giving set attached (capped with a white cap, previous bags sealed with blue plugs) in biohazard bags to the transfusion laboratory.
6. Document and complete the checklist.

### Types

| Reaction | Features | Management |
|---|---|---|
| **Acute haemolytic** (ABO mismatch) | Chills, fever, headache, dyspnoea, chest pain, cyanosis, hypotension, tachycardia; also loin pain, haemoglobinuria | Stop immediately; saline with different tubing; fluids, diuresis, manage DIC and renal failure |
| **Mild allergic** | Flushing, itching, urticaria, wheeze | Stop or slow; notify doctor; antihistamine |
| **Severe allergic / anaphylactic** | Dyspnoea, circulatory collapse, chest pain, cardiac arrest | Stop; saline; adrenaline; monitor; CPR, oxygen as needed |
| **Febrile non-haemolytic** | Fever, warm flushed skin, chills, headache, myalgia, anxiety | Stop; antipyretics; saline |
| **Sepsis** (contaminated blood) | High fever, chills, vomiting, diarrhoea, hypotension | Stop; IV fluids; antibiotics; blood cultures; send the remaining blood and tubing to the lab |
| **Circulatory overload (TACO)** | Cough, dyspnoea, raised JVP, tachycardia, hypertension | Sit upright with feet dependent; stop or slow; diuretics and oxygen |
| **TA-GvHD** | Donor T cells attack host skin, marrow, gut; onset within 1 to 2 weeks; thrombocytopenia, anorexia, nausea, vomiting, weight loss, hepatitis, infections | Prevent by irradiation; usually fatal |
| **DIC** | Abnormal activation of coagulation and fibrinolysis, consuming factors and platelets | Often due to the underlying cause (hypovolaemic shock, trauma, obstetric complications) rather than the blood; treat the cause |

> **Exam tip:** the commonest cause of an acute haemolytic reaction is a clerical error (wrong patient, wrong sample label). Know the **bedside check**.

> **Remember:** TRALI (transfusion-related acute lung injury) is non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema within 6 hours; distinguish it from TACO (raised JVP, fluid overload, responds to diuretics).

## 8. Autologous transfusion

- Patient's own blood donated before planned surgery (pre-deposit), or collected during surgery (cell salvage) or by acute normovolaemic haemodilution.
- Advantages: no disease transmission or alloimmunisation, no mismatch. Limitations: cost, logistics, wastage, not for bacteraemia or malignancy.

## Key points to revise

1. ABO was discovered by Landsteiner in 1901; O has no antigen but both antibodies; AB has no antibodies.
2. Verify patient identity and label the sample at the bedside; transfuse each unit within 4 hours.
3. Red cells: 10 ml/kg gives about 2.5 g/dl rise; store at 2 to 6 degrees C up to 42 days.
4. Platelets: transfuse below 15 000, or below 50 000 with bleeding or before procedures.
5. FFP 20 ml/kg raises factors by 20%; cryoprecipitate gives fibrinogen, factor VIII, XIII and vWF.
6. Irradiate products to prevent TA-GvHD; leucodepletion does not prevent it.
7. In any reaction: stop, check, saline, inform, return the unit and giving set.
8. Circulatory overload: sit up, slow or stop, diuretics, oxygen.
