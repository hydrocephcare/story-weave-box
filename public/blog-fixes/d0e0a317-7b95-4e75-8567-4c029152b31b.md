# Trypanosomiasis (African Sleeping Sickness and Chagas Disease)

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Tropical and parasitic infections**

> **In one line:** trypanosomiasis is infection with **Trypanosoma** protozoa. In Africa, **tsetse-fly-borne T. brucei gambiense (West/Central) and T. brucei rhodesiense (East/Southern)** cause **human African trypanosomiasis (HAT, sleeping sickness)**, which goes through an early **haemolymphatic stage** and a late **meningo-encephalitic stage**; in the Americas **T. cruzi** causes **Chagas disease**.

## What you must know

- **T. b. gambiense**: West/Central Africa, chronic (months to years). **T. b. rhodesiense**: East Africa (including Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania), **acute** (weeks to months), zoonotic with game and cattle reservoirs.
- Vector: **tsetse fly (Glossina)**. Chagas disease (T. cruzi) is spread by reduviid ("kissing") bugs in the Americas.
- Early sign of rhodesiense: a painful **trypanosomal chancre** at the bite site, then fever, rash and lymphadenopathy.
- Diagnosis: **trypanosomes on blood film, chancre aspirate or node aspirate**; CSF examination to stage.
- Treatment depends on **species and stage**: pentamidine (gambiense, early) or suramin (rhodesiense, early); late-stage drugs cross the blood-brain barrier.

## 1. Causative species

| Species | Disease | Region |
|---|---|---|
| **Trypanosoma brucei gambiense** | African trypanosomiasis (chronic) | West and Central Africa |
| **Trypanosoma brucei rhodesiense** | African trypanosomiasis (acute) | East and Southern Africa |
| **Trypanosoma cruzi** | Chagas disease | The Americas |

## 2. Epidemiology and transmission

- HAT is transmitted by the bite of an infected **tsetse fly**, confined to sub-Saharan Africa. Risk groups: people near game parks and tsetse habitat (hunters, safari tourists, fishermen, farmers).
- *Rhodesiense* is a zoonosis (cattle, antelope, wild game); *gambiense* is mainly human-to-human.
- Other routes: congenital, blood transfusion, laboratory accidents.
- Chagas disease: faeces of reduviid bugs rubbed into a bite wound or mucous membrane; also transfusion, congenital and oral.

## 3. Clinical features of HAT

### 3.1 Stage 1: haemolymphatic stage

- **Chancre** (more with rhodesiense): painful, tender, indurated purple-black or red lesion at the bite site, 5 to 14 days later.
- Fever (intermittent, irregular), chills, headache, malaise, severe myalgia and arthralgia.
- **Lymphadenopathy**: in gambiense, enlarged posterior cervical nodes (**Winterbottom's sign**). Skin rash: annular erythematous macules with central clearing (**trypanides**).
- Hepatosplenomegaly, oedema, tachycardia and myocarditis (more common in rhodesiense).
- Rhodesiense can kill from myocarditis or multi-organ failure before CNS disease develops.

### 3.2 Stage 2: meningo-encephalitic stage

- Parasites cross the blood-brain barrier.
- Behavioural change, personality change, severe headache, tremor, abnormal movements, **sleep-wake reversal (daytime sleepiness and night wakefulness)**, then stupor, coma and death if untreated.
- Gambiense: stage 2 after months to years. Rhodesiense: within weeks.

### 3.3 Lecture case (rhodesiense)

- A 67-year-old man on a **lion-hunting safari in Tanzania**, bitten by flies; 2 days later a tender **purple-black lesion** on the calf (chancre); 2 weeks later fever, chills, fatigue and severe myalgia; seen at an airport and told "flu".
- Examination: temperature 97.3 F, pulse 62, 1 cm non-tender left supraclavicular node, no hepatosplenomegaly, **annular erythematous macules on the trunk with central clearing**, induration without warmth at the bite site.
- Laboratory: WBC 8.3 with 28 bands, 14 atypical lymphocytes, haematocrit 40%, platelets 233, BUN/creatinine 24/1.3, ALP 125, GGT 148, **LDH 576**.
- Initial differential: falciparum malaria, East African trypanosomiasis, West African trypanosomiasis, relapsing fever (**Borrelia**), HIV.
- Lesson: any traveller or resident returning from game areas with a chancre and fever needs a blood film for both malaria and trypanosomes.

## 4. Investigations

- **Thick and thin blood film (Giemsa)**: motile trypanosomes with undulating membrane, repeated and concentrated if negative. Buffy coat examination, miniature anion-exchange centrifugation.
- **Chancre aspirate and lymph node aspirate**: high yield, especially in gambiense.
- **Serology**: card agglutination test (CATT) for gambiense screening (not rhodesiense).
- **CSF (lumbar puncture)** for staging: raised white cell count (above 5 cells/microlitre), raised protein, trypanosomes, **Mott cells** (morular cells). Stage 2 if CSF abnormal.
- General: anaemia, thrombocytopenia, raised ESR, raised IgM, LFTs.

## 5. Differential diagnosis

Falciparum malaria, relapsing fever, HIV seroconversion, typhoid, brucellosis, visceral leishmaniasis, TB meningitis and cryptococcal meningitis (for late disease).

## 6. Management of HAT

Drug choice depends on species and stage. Early treatment is crucial: untreated HAT is fatal.

### 6.1 Haemolymphatic stage (stage 1)

| Species | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **T. b. gambiense** | **Pentamidine isethionate** 4 mg/kg once daily, IM or IV, for 7 to 10 doses. Alternative: **eflornithine** 400 mg/kg/day IV in 4 divided doses for 2 weeks |
| **T. b. rhodesiense** | **Suramin** IV (slow): test dose, then 5 mg/kg on day 1, 10 mg/kg on day 3, 20 mg/kg on day 5, then on days 11, 23 and 30 |

> **Check:** pentamidine is the standard for stage 1 gambiense; eflornithine is normally reserved for stage 2 gambiense (combined with nifurtimox, NECT). Suramin does not cross the blood-brain barrier.

### 6.2 Meningo-encephalitic stage (stage 2)

- **Rhodesiense:** **melarsoprol** (arsenical, toxic: reactive encephalopathy in about 5 to 10%, given with prednisolone); fexinidazole is now an option for some forms.
- **Gambiense:** **NECT** (nifurtimox-eflornithine combination), or fexinidazole.
- Follow-up: lumbar puncture every 6 months for 2 years for relapse.

## 7. Chagas disease (T. cruzi), brief

- **Acute phase**: chagoma or Romana's sign (unilateral periorbital oedema), fever, myocarditis.
- **Chronic phase** (years later): dilated cardiomyopathy and arrhythmias, megaoesophagus, megacolon.
- Treatment: benznidazole or nifurtimox (best in acute disease); supportive for chronic organ disease.

## 8. Prevention

- Avoid tsetse bites: long, neutral-coloured clothing, repellents, avoid dense bush in the day.
- Vector control (traps, targets), treatment of cattle, active case finding and treatment of gambiense cases.
- There is no vaccine or chemoprophylaxis.

> **Exam tip:** rhodesiense is acute with a chancre and early cardiac involvement; gambiense is chronic with posterior cervical nodes and prominent sleep disturbance.

## Key points to revise

1. T. b. gambiense is chronic (West Africa); T. b. rhodesiense is acute (East Africa, zoonotic); both spread by tsetse flies.
2. Stage 1 is haemolymphatic (chancre, fever, nodes, rash); stage 2 is CNS (sleep reversal, coma).
3. Diagnose by finding trypanosomes in blood, chancre or node aspirate; do a lumbar puncture to stage.
4. Stage 1 treatment: pentamidine for gambiense, suramin for rhodesiense.
5. Stage 2 treatment needs a drug that crosses the blood-brain barrier (melarsoprol, eflornithine, NECT).
6. Consider HAT with malaria in anyone returning from game parks with fever and a bite lesion.
7. Chagas disease (T. cruzi, Americas) causes cardiomyopathy, megaoesophagus and megacolon.
