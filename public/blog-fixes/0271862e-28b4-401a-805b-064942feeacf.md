# Growth Assessment

**Year 4 · Paediatrics · Nutrition and growth**

> **In one line:** normal growth is the best single indicator of a child's general health and nutritional state. It is assessed by accurate, repeated measurement of weight, length or height, head circumference and mid-upper arm circumference, plotted on the same chart over time; the direction of the growth curve matters more than where it sits.

## What you must know

- Growth is the progressive increase in size of the whole body; normal growth fits established standards for the population.
- **Height-for-age** = chronic undernutrition (**stunting**); **weight-for-height** separates acute (**wasting**) from chronic malnutrition; **weight-for-age** is the most sensitive and widely used index but cannot tell acute from chronic.
- Weight falls by up to **10%** in the first days (loss of excess fluid) and is regained by **10 to 14 days**. Birth weight **doubles at 4 to 5 months** and **trebles at 1 year**.
- Length: **50 cm at birth, 75 cm at 1 year**; 25 cm gained in year one. For 2 to 12 years: height (cm) = age (years) x 6 + 77.
- Head circumference: **35 cm at birth**, about **1 cm/month** in the first year; measure from birth to 3 years (rapid brain growth).
- Interpret the **direction** of the curve: parallel to the printed curves is good; horizontal (static) is dangerous; downward is very dangerous.
- **BMI** is used from age 2 years: below 5th centile underweight, 85th to 94th at risk of overweight, 95th or above overweight.

## 1. Definition and importance

- Growth is the progressive increase in size of the whole body. **Normal growth** is progression of change in body size compatible with established standards for a given population.
- It is the best indicator of **general health and nutritional status**. Knowing the normal patterns allows early detection of pathological deviation: chronic disease, interpersonal and psychological stress.
- Accurate measurement and charting can prevent unnecessary investigation in a child with a normal pattern, for example **familial short stature** with normal growth velocity.

## 2. Determinants of growth

- **Environmental:** nutrition, intrauterine environment, postnatal illnesses.
- **Genetic** factors.
- **Hormonal** factors (growth hormone, thyroid hormone, insulin, sex steroids).
- **Psychosocial** factors (neglect, emotional deprivation can cause psychosocial short stature).
- **Gender.**

## 3. Measures of growth and what each tells you

The mainstay of nutritional assessment: height or length, weight, head circumference and mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC). They are useful only if the clinician interprets them correctly.

| Index | What it shows |
|---|---|
| **Height-for-age** | Linear growth; a measure of **chronic undernutrition (stunting)** |
| **Weight-for-height** | Distinguishes **acute (wasting)** from chronic malnutrition |
| **Weight-for-age** | Widely used and the most sensitive index, but **cannot distinguish acute from chronic** malnutrition |

## 4. Taking measurements correctly

General rules: standard procedure and **reliable equipment that is regularly calibrated**.

| Measurement | Technique |
|---|---|
| **Weight** | Properly calibrated scale. Infant weighed **without clothes or diaper**, best on a **beam balance**. Older child without shoes, in little or no outer clothing |
| **Length (infant)** | Firm, flat horizontal surface with perpendicular surfaces at each end (measuring board). Child **supine**; most accurate with **two examiners** (one positions the child) |
| **Height (older child)** | Stature using a **stadiometer** (firm vertical surface). Ideally take the measurement **three times** to improve accuracy |
| **Head circumference** | Maximum diameter through the **supra-orbital ridge** and around the most prominent part of the **occiput**, to capture the greatest cranial volume. Use a **non-stretch tape**; record the **mean of three measurements** |
| **MUAC** | Standing or sitting; **non-dominant arm** bare; measured at the **midpoint between the acromion and the olecranon**; non-stretch tape, not pulled tight, just fitting comfortably |

### Head circumference: when and why

- Measured from **birth to 3 years**, the period of rapid brain growth.
- Important in follow-up of **low birth weight infants** and in children with CNS abnormalities such as **post-meningitic hydrocephalus**.
- Newborn measurement errors are frequent because of **scalp oedema, overriding sutures, IV fluid infiltration and cephalohaematoma**.

## 5. Normal growth patterns

### 5.1 Weight

- Weight drops by up to **10%** a few days after birth from loss of excess fluid; regained in **10 to 14 days**. Breast-fed infants tend to regain a little later than bottle-fed infants.
- Average gain: **0 to 3 months about 1 kg/month; 3 to 6 months 0.5 kg/month; 6 to 9 months 0.33 kg/month; 9 to 12 months 0.25 kg/month**.
- Doubles by **4 to 5 months**, trebles by **1 year**. Then about **2 kg/year** from 2 years to puberty.

### 5.2 Length and height

| Period | Growth |
|---|---|
| Birth | 50 cm |
| First year | about 25 cm gained (0 to 6 months 2.5 cm/month; 7 to 12 months 1.25 cm/month); 75 cm at 1 year |
| Second year | about 12.5 cm |
| 4 years to puberty | 5 to 6 cm/year |
| 2 to 12 years | height (cm) = age (years) x 6 + 77 |

- There is a normal **deceleration of height velocity before the pubertal growth spurt**.
- Prepubertal growth is non-linear: growth spurts average 8 weeks, separated by slow periods averaging 18 days.

> **Check:** the slide says children grow about 1 cm/year at puberty. In fact the **pubertal growth spurt is the fastest of childhood after infancy**: peak velocity is roughly 8 to 10 cm/year (girls around age 12, boys around 14). The 1 cm figure appears to be a slip.

### 5.3 Height velocity

A period of **at least six months** is needed for reliable height velocity in children over 2 years. Average velocities:

| Age | Velocity |
|---|---|
| 0 to 6 months | 2.5 cm/month |
| 7 to 12 months | 1.25 cm/month |
| 12 to 24 months | over 10 cm/year |
| 24 to 36 months | 8 cm/year |
| 36 to 48 months | 7 cm/year |
| 4 to 10 years | 5 to 6 cm/year |

### 5.4 Head circumference (reflects brain growth)

- At birth the head is **1 to 2 cm larger than the chest**. Average head circumference is **35 cm at birth**, rising to about **47 cm by 1 year** (about 1 cm/month), then slowing to an average of about 55 cm by 6 years.
- Fastest in the first 6 months: **2 cm in the first month and 6 cm in the first four months**.
- **Brain weight doubles by 4 to 6 months and trebles by 1 year** (newborn brain about 350 to 400 g, adult 1.3 to 1.4 kg, about 2% of body weight). Most head growth is complete by **4 years**.

> **Check:** other standard references give about 45 cm at 12 months and about 51 to 52 cm at 6 years (50th centile). Use the child's own centile chart rather than a single number.

## 6. Evaluating a child's growth

### History

- **Birth:** weight, length, head circumference.
- **Prenatal:** maternal infection, intrauterine exposures (cigarettes, drugs, alcohol, other toxins).
- Medical, dietary and developmental history.
- **Family history:** parental heights, parental growth patterns, timing of pubertal onset in the parents.

### Examination

- Take the measurements, then do a full systemic examination.

## 7. Interpreting growth charts

- Watch the **direction** of the growth curve; it is more important than the position of the curve on the chart.
- The curve should run **parallel to the printed curves** (good curve).
- A **horizontal (static) curve is dangerous**; a **downward curve is very dangerous**.
- Whatever chart is used, the most useful information comes from careful measurement and plotting on the **same chart over time**. Healthy children usually stay within 1 to 2 percentile curves over time.
- Charts should be a reference for the population being measured. Serial measurements are needed to tell a true abnormality from a normal variant.

> **Exam tip:** a child who is "on the 5th centile" but tracking along it is usually normal; a child dropping from the 50th to the 10th centile is not, even though both are within limits.

## 8. Body mass index (BMI)

- BMI = weight (kg) / height (m)². Calculate it for children **older than 2 years**.
- A valid predictor of adiposity and the best clinical standard for defining obesity; BMI centile also screens for undernutrition.

| BMI centile for age | Category |
|---|---|
| Below 5th | Underweight |
| 5th to 84th | Normal weight |
| 85th to 94th | At risk of overweight |
| 95th or above | Overweight |

## Key points to revise

1. Normal growth is the best indicator of a child's general health and nutrition; measure weight, length or height, head circumference and MUAC.
2. Height-for-age shows stunting, weight-for-height shows wasting, weight-for-age is sensitive but non-specific.
3. Use calibrated equipment, a standard technique, two examiners for length and repeated (three) readings.
4. Weight: 10% loss, regained by 10 to 14 days; doubles at 4 to 5 months, trebles at 1 year, then about 2 kg/year.
5. Height: 50 cm at birth, 75 cm at 1 year, 25 cm gained in year one; velocity needs at least 6 months of data after age 2.
6. Head circumference: 35 cm at birth, about 1 cm/month in year one; brain weight doubles by 4 to 6 months; measure to 3 years.
7. The direction of the growth curve matters most: parallel is good, flat is dangerous, falling is very dangerous.
8. BMI is used from age 2: below 5th underweight, 85th to 94th at risk, 95th and above overweight.
