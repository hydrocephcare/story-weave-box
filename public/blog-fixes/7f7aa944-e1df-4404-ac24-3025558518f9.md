## Summary
Valvular heart disease involves either **stenosis**, a narrowing that impedes forward blood flow and causes pressure overload, or **regurgitation**, a valve's inability to fully close leading to backward blood leakage and volume overload. The left-sided **mitral** and **aortic** valves are most frequently affected due to higher systemic pressures. Common etiologies include **rheumatic heart disease**, **calcific degeneration**, **infective endocarditis**, and **mitral valve prolapse**, each presenting with characteristic pathophysiological consequences and clinical signs, often progressing to heart failure or systemic complications like stroke.

## Key Points
- **Stenosis** is characterized by a narrowed valve opening, leading to pressure overload and **hypertrophy** of the chamber behind the affected valve.
- **Regurgitation** results from a valve failing to close completely, causing blood to leak backward, leading to volume overload and **dilation** of the affected chamber.
- **Rheumatic heart disease** is the predominant cause of **mitral stenosis**, resulting in fibrosed, fused leaflets with a classic "fish mouth" appearance.
- **Mitral regurgitation** is commonly associated with **mitral valve prolapse** or **papillary muscle rupture** post-MI, causing both **left atrial** and **left ventricular enlargement**.
- **Aortic stenosis** in the elderly is primarily **calcific/degenerative**, leading to **concentric left ventricular hypertrophy** and manifesting clinically as the **SAD triad** (**Syncope, Angina, Dyspnea**).
- **Aortic regurgitation** results in significant **left ventricular dilation and hypertrophy** due to chronic volume overload, leading to a characteristic **wide pulse pressure** and various peripheral signs.
- **Infective endocarditis** involves bacterial vegetations on heart valves, with **Staph aureus** being common in acute cases (e.g., IV drug users, often affecting the **tricuspid valve**) and **Strep viridans** in subacute cases (e.g., dental procedures, damaged valves).
- **Acute Rheumatic Fever**, caused by **Group A Strep**, triggers an autoimmune response leading to **pancarditis** and **Aschoff bodies**, often progressing to **chronic rheumatic heart disease** predominantly affecting the **mitral valve**.

## Detailed Notes

### The Heart Has 4 Chambers
Blood flows sequentially through the heart's chambers: **RIGHT ATRIUM → RIGHT VENTRICLE → LUNGS → LEFT ATRIUM → LEFT VENTRICLE → BODY**.

### The 4 Valves & Their Positions
The heart contains four valves, each positioned to ensure unidirectional blood flow:
- Between **Right Atrium** & **Right Ventricle** → **TRICUSPID VALVE** (3 leaflets)
- Between **Right Ventricle** & **Pulmonary Artery** → **PULMONIC VALVE**
- Between **Left Atrium** & **Left Ventricle** → **MITRAL VALVE** (2 leaflets)
- Between **Left Ventricle** & **Aorta** → **AORTIC VALVE**

### Why Left Side Gets Damaged More
The left side of the heart pumps blood to the **ENTIRE body**, handling approximately 5 times more pressure than the right side. Consequently, its valves experience greater wear and tear, making them more susceptible to damage and disease.

### Two Types of Valve Problems
Valvular heart disease generally falls into two categories based on the functional deficit:

| Type | Definition | Effect on Heart |
|---|---|---|
| **Stenosis** | Valve opening is **NARROWED** | Chamber **BEHIND** valve works harder → **Pressure overload** → **Hypertrophy** |
| **Regurgitation/Insufficiency** | Valve doesn't close properly → blood leaks **BACK** | Chamber **BEHIND** valve overfills → **Volume overload** → **Dilation** |

### Pressure Overload vs Volume Overload
In **stenosis**, the chamber pushes against resistance, causing its walls to get **THICK** (**Hypertrophy**), similar to a weightlifter's muscles. Conversely, in **regurgitation**, the chamber fills with extra blood, leading to the chamber becoming **BIG** and **DILATED**, akin to a balloon being overfilled.

### PART 2: MITRAL STENOSIS (MS)

### Definition
**Mitral stenosis (MS)** is the narrowing of the mitral valve opening. The normal valve area is **4-6 cm²**, with symptoms typically beginning when it drops below **2 cm²**, and becoming critical at **1 cm²**.

### Cause
**Rheumatic Heart Disease** is responsible for nearly **99% of all MS cases**. Rarely, other causes include congenital defects, carcinoid syndrome, or lupus.

### Pathogenesis — Step by Step
The development of **mitral stenosis** is a multi-step process:
- **Acute Rheumatic Fever:** A **Group A Streptococcus** (**Strep pyogenes**) throat infection triggers the immune system to make antibodies against **Strep M-protein**. This **M-protein LOOKS LIKE cardiac proteins** (**molecular mimicry**), causing the antibodies to attack the **HEART** by mistake. This leads to inflammation of all heart layers, termed **PANCARDITIS**, and the mitral valve leaflets become inflamed, forming small vegetations.
- **Chronic Scarring (Years Later):** Repeated episodes of **rheumatic fever** cause the leaflets to become **FIBROSED** and **FUSED** at the commissures. Concurrently, the **chordae tendineae** become **SHORTENED** and **THICKENED**. This process narrows the valve opening, leading to **STENOSIS**, which gives the valve a classic "**FISH MOUTH**" or "**BUTTONHOLE**" appearance.
- **Consequences of Obstruction:** The narrowed mitral valve obstructs the free flow of blood from the **Left Atrium (LA)** to the **Left Ventricle (LV)**. Blood then backs up into the **Left Atrium**, causing **LA pressure** to rise and the **LA to DILATE** (enlarge). This increased LA pressure further backs up into the pulmonary veins, leading to **pulmonary venous hypertension** and eventually **PULMONARY EDEMA** (manifesting as **dyspnea**). Chronic **pulmonary hypertension** subsequently causes **RIGHT HEART STRAIN**, leading to **right ventricular hypertrophy** and ultimately **RIGHT HEART FAILURE**.
- **Atrial Fibrillation:** As the **LA dilates** and stretches, its electrical pathways become disrupted, leading to the development of **ATRIAL FIBRILLATION**. Blood then pools and stagnates in the **LA** (particularly in the LA appendage), which promotes **THROMBUS FORMATION**. If a thrombus breaks off, it can cause **SYSTEMIC EMBOLISM**, most dangerously resulting in a **STROKE**.

### Gross Pathology
The following findings are characteristic in the gross pathology of **mitral stenosis**:

| Finding | Description |
|---|---|
| Leaflet fusion | Commissures fused → "**fish mouth**" valve |
| Leaflet thickening | **Fibrosis** and **calcification** |
| Short chordae | Pulled down, thickened, fused |
| LA enlargement | Massive — can compress esophagus |
| LA thrombus | Especially in **LA appendage** |

### Clinical Features

#### Symptoms
Patients with **mitral stenosis** exhibit a range of symptoms due to the pathophysiological changes:

| Symptom | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Dyspnea on exertion** | **Pulmonary venous hypertension** |
| **Orthopnea/PND** | **Pulmonary edema** when lying flat |
| **Hemoptysis** | Rupture of bronchial veins from high pressure |
| **Palpitations** | **Atrial fibrillation** |
| **Stroke** | LA **thrombus embolization** |
| **Hoarse voice** | Enlarged LA compresses **recurrent laryngeal nerve** |
| **Dysphagia** | Enlarged LA compresses **esophagus** |

#### Signs
Physical examination of **mitral stenosis** reveals distinct signs:

| Sign | Description |
|---|---|
| **Malar flush** | Pinkish-purple cheeks from low cardiac output |
| **Loud S1** | Valve snaps shut from being held open |
| **Opening snap** | Stiff leaflets snap open in diastole |
| **Mid-diastolic rumble** | Turbulent flow through narrow valve |

### CXR Findings
Chest X-ray in **mitral stenosis** typically shows:
- Enlarged LA (double density sign, splaying of carina)
- Pulmonary venous congestion (upper lobe diversion)
- Kerley B lines (pulmonary edema)
- Calcified mitral valve
- Normal LV size (**LV is actually SMALL/normal in pure MS**)

### Key MCQ Facts
- The **most common cause** of **mitral stenosis** is **Rheumatic heart disease**.
- The **most common valve affected in rheumatic disease** is the **Mitral valve**, followed by the **Aortic valve**, then the **Tricuspid valve** (the pulmonic valve is almost never affected).
- The **most common complication** of **mitral stenosis** is **Atrial fibrillation**.
- The **most dangerous complication** is **Stroke from LA thrombus**.
- The **LV is NORMAL** in pure **mitral stenosis**; only the LA enlarges.
- **Mitral stenosis** is the **only valvular lesion more common in WOMEN than men**.

### PART 3: MITRAL REGURGITATION (MR)

### Definition
**Mitral regurgitation (MR)** occurs when the mitral valve fails to close completely during systole, causing blood to leak **BACK** from the **LV** into the **LA**.

### Causes — Remember "PRIME"
The primary causes of **mitral regurgitation** can be remembered with the acronym "**PRIME**":
- **P**apillary muscle rupture (post-MI)
- **R**heumatic heart disease
- **I**nfective endocarditis
- **M**itral valve prolapse (**MVP**)
- **E**ndocarditis / connective tissue disorders (Marfan, Ehlers-Danlos)

### Most Important Causes by Context
The most important causes of **mitral regurgitation** vary by clinical context:

| Cause | Key Context |
|---|---|
| **MVP** | Young woman, most common cause overall |
| **Papillary muscle rupture** | 2-7 days post-MI → **ACUTE** severe **MR** |
| **Rheumatic** | Always with mitral stenosis too |
| **IE** | IV drug user, recent dental procedure |
| **Marfan syndrome** | Tall, long fingers, lens dislocation |
| **Dilated cardiomyopathy** | LV dilation pulls leaflets apart |

### Pathophysiology
In **mitral regurgitation**, the mitral valve does not close properly, leading to blood leaking back into the **LA** during systole. The **LA** then receives blood from both the pulmonary veins and the leaking **LV**, causing the **LA to DILATE** (**volume overload**). The **LV** must pump an **EXTRA volume** each beat to maintain cardiac output, leading to **LV DILATION** and eventually **HYPERTROPHY**. Ultimately, the **LV fails**, resulting in **LEFT HEART FAILURE**.

### Acute vs Chronic MR
The clinical course and presentation of **mitral regurgitation** differ significantly between acute and chronic forms:

| Feature | Acute MR | Chronic MR |
|---|---|---|
| **Cause** | **Papillary muscle rupture**, **IE** | **Rheumatic**, **MVP** |
| **LA** | Normal size, no time to dilate | Massively dilated |
| **Symptoms** | Sudden, severe **pulmonary edema** | Gradual, compensated for years |
| **Prognosis** | Emergency, life-threatening | Slowly progressive |

### Key MCQ Facts
- **Papillary muscle rupture** is the **most common cause of ACUTE severe MR** post-MI.
- **MVP** is the **most common cause of MR overall** in the developed world.
- **Both LA and LV enlarge** in **mitral regurgitation** (unlike **mitral stenosis** where only the LA enlarges).
- A **holosystolic murmur** heard at the apex radiating to the axilla is characteristic.

### PART 4: MITRAL VALVE PROLAPSE (MVP)

### Definition
**Mitral valve prolapse (MVP)** is a condition where the mitral valve leaflets **BULGE BACK** (prolapse) into the **LA** during systole, typically due to excess leaflet tissue or weak chordae.

### Causes
The causes of **mitral valve prolapse** include:

| Cause | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Idiopathic** | Most common — young women |
| **Marfan syndrome** | Connective tissue weakness |
| **Ehlers-Danlos** | Connective tissue weakness |
| **Myxomatous degeneration** | Weakening of valve matrix |

### Key Features
- **MVP** is the **most common valvular abnormality overall**.
- It is usually **BENIGN**.
- Clinically, it presents with a **mid-systolic CLICK** followed by a late systolic murmur.
- It can progress to **mitral regurgitation** if chordae rupture.
- Rarely, complications include sudden cardiac death, stroke, and arrhythmias.

### Gross Pathology
In **mitral valve prolapse**, the leaflets are typically large, floppy, and billowing, giving them a "**parachute**" appearance.

### PART 5: AORTIC STENOSIS (AS)

### Definition
**Aortic stenosis (AS)** is the narrowing of the aortic valve, which obstructs blood flow from the **LV** to the aorta during systole.

### Causes by Age Group
The causes of **aortic stenosis** vary with age:

| Age | Cause |
|---|---|
| Children/Young adults | Congenital **bicuspid aortic valve** |
| Adults 40-60 | **Calcification** of **bicuspid valve** |
| Elderly >65 | **Calcific/Degenerative AS** — most common |
| Any age (developing world) | **Rheumatic AS** (always with mitral disease) |

### Calcific AS — Pathogenesis
**Calcific aortic stenosis** develops through a process similar to atherosclerosis:
- Normal wear and tear on valve leaflets, followed by lipid deposition and inflammation, leads to **calcium deposits** forming on the leaflets.
- These leaflets become **STIFF** and cannot open fully, causing the valve opening to **NARROW**.
- The **LV** must generate **MASSIVE pressure** to push blood through, resulting in **LV HYPERTROPHY** (**concentric**), a state of **pressure overload**.
- Eventually, the **LV FAILS**, leading to decompensation.

### The SAD Triad — Symptom Progression
The clinical progression of **aortic stenosis** is marked by the **SAD Triad** of symptoms, each indicating a worsening prognosis:
- **S** → **Syncope** (5 year survival without surgery)
- **A** → **Angina** (3 year survival without surgery)
- **D** → **Dyspnea/HF** (1-2 year survival without surgery)
Each new symptom signals a worse prognosis, necessitating urgent valve replacement.

### Pathophysiology of Each Symptom
The **SAD Triad** symptoms are mechanistically linked to the severity of **aortic stenosis**:

| Symptom | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Syncope** | Fixed outflow obstruction → ↓ CO to brain on exertion |
| **Angina** | **LVH** → increased O2 demand + compressed coronary arteries |
| **Dyspnea** | LV failure → **pulmonary edema** |

### Key Findings
Characteristic findings in **aortic stenosis** include:

| Finding | Reason |
|---|---|
| **LV hypertrophy on CXR** | Pressure overload |
| Normal or small aortic root | Unlike AR |
| **Slow rising pulse (pulsus parvus et tardus)** | Reduced, delayed aortic flow |
| **Narrow pulse pressure** | Low stroke volume |
| **Harsh systolic ejection murmur** | Radiates to carotids |
| **Soft/absent S2** | Stiff valve can't snap shut |

### Key MCQ Facts
- An elderly patient with no prior illness presenting with the **SAD triad**, **LVH**, and normal cholesterol should trigger suspicion of **Calcific Aortic Stenosis**.
- **Normal cholesterol** rules out atherosclerosis as the primary driver of the calcification.
- The **LV hypertrophies** (not dilates) in **aortic stenosis** due to **pressure overload**.
- **Aortic stenosis** is the **most common valvular disease requiring surgery** in the developed world.
- A **bicuspid aortic valve** tends to calcify **EARLIER** (40s-50s instead of 70s) compared to a tricuspid valve.

### PART 6: AORTIC REGURGITATION (AR)

### Definition
**Aortic regurgitation (AR)** is a condition where the aortic valve leaks during diastole, causing blood to flow **BACK** from the aorta into the **LV**.

### Causes
Causes of **aortic regurgitation** can be broadly categorized into problems with the valve leaflets or dilation of the aortic root:

#### Valve Leaflet Problems
- **Rheumatic disease**: Leaflet scarring
- **Infective endocarditis**: Leaflet destruction
- **Bicuspid aortic valve**: Structural abnormality
- **Calcific degeneration**: Elderly

#### Aortic Root Dilation (Valve ring stretches open)
- **Marfan syndrome**: **Cystic medial necrosis** → aortic root dilation
- **Syphilitic aortitis**: Tertiary syphilis destroys aortic wall
- **Hypertension**: Chronic aortic dilation
- **Ankylosing spondylitis**: Inflammatory aortic root disease

### Pathophysiology
In **aortic regurgitation**, blood leaks back from the aorta into the **LV** during diastole. The **LV** then receives blood from both the **LA** and the leaking aorta, leading to **LV VOLUME OVERLOAD**. This causes the **LV to dilate AND hypertrophy**, often resulting in a massively enlarged, ox-like heart, known as "**Cor Bovinum**." Eventually, the **LV FAILS**, leading to decompensation.

### Classic Signs — All Due to Wide Pulse Pressure
**Aortic regurgitation** causes a **HIGH systolic pressure** (due to a large stroke volume) and a **LOW diastolic pressure** (as blood leaks back into the LV during diastole), resulting in a **WIDE PULSE PRESSURE**, which is the hallmark of AR. This wide pulse pressure manifests in several classic physical signs:

| Sign | Description |
|---|---|
| **Corrigan's pulse** | Bounding, **water-hammer pulse** |
| **De Musset's sign** | Head bobbing with each heartbeat |
| **Quincke's sign** | Capillary pulsations in nail bed |
| **Duroziez's sign** | Pistol-shot sound over femoral artery |
| **Hill's sign** | BP in legs > arms by >20mmHg |
| **Traube's sign** | Double sound over femoral artery |

### Key MCQ Facts
- A **wide pulse pressure** is the hallmark of **aortic regurgitation**.
- The **LV dilates AND hypertrophies** in **aortic regurgitation** (due to both volume and pressure overload).
- **Syphilitic aortitis** is associated with **aortic regurgitation** and an ascending aortic aneurysm.
- For a tall patient with a **wide pulse pressure** and **aortic regurgitation**, consider **Marfan syndrome**.
- The characteristic murmur is an **early diastolic murmur** at the left sternal border.

### PART 7: INFECTIVE ENDOCARDITIS (IE)

### Definition
**Infective endocarditis (IE)** is an infection of the heart valves or the endocardium, typically caused by bacteria.

### Types

#### Acute IE
| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Organism** | **Staph aureus** — most common overall |
| **Course** | Rapid, destructive, days-weeks |
| **Valve** | Often **NORMAL** valves |
| **Context** | IV drug users, post-surgery, catheters |

#### Subacute IE
| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Organism** | **Strep viridans** — most common in subacute |
| **Course** | Slow, weeks-months |
| **Valve** | Usually **PREVIOUSLY DAMAGED** valves |
| **Context** | Dental procedures, **MVP**, **rheumatic disease** |

#### Special Contexts
Specific clinical contexts are often associated with particular causative organisms:

| Context | Organism |
|---|---|
| **IV drug user** | **Staph aureus** → **RIGHT sided** (**tricuspid**) |
| **Dental procedure** | **Strep viridans** |
| **GI/GU procedure** | **Enterococcus** |
| **Prosthetic valve early (<2 months)** | **Staph epidermidis** |
| **Colon cancer** | **Strep bovis** (**Strep gallolyticus**) |
| **Fungal** | **Candida** → IV drug users, immunocompromised |

### Pathology of Vegetations
When bacteria enter the bloodstream, they attach to a damaged or turbulent valve, forming **VEGETATIONS**. These vegetations are composed of bacteria, fibrin, platelets, and inflammatory cells, and are typically large, irregular, and destructive. They can **EMBOLIZE**, leading to complications such as stroke, pulmonary embolism, or splenic infarct. Vegetations can also directly **DESTROY** the valve, causing acute regurgitation, or spread locally, leading to abscess formation or fistulas.

### Non-Infective Endocarditis (Important for MCQs)
It is important to differentiate **infective endocarditis** from non-infective forms:

| Type | Cause | Vegetation Location | Key Association |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Libman-Sacks** | **SLE** | **BOTH** sides of mitral leaflet | **Lupus** — "**LSE = Lupus Sterile Endocarditis**" |
| **Marantic (NBTE)** | **Malignancy**, chronic illness | Along line of closure | **Cancer**, debilitating illness |
| **Rheumatic** | **Rheumatic fever** | Tips of mitral leaflets | **Strep**, **Aschoff bodies** |

### Osler's Nodes vs Janeway Lesions
Two distinct cutaneous manifestations are important in **infective endocarditis**:

| Feature | Osler's Nodes | Janeway Lesions |
|---|---|---|
| **Location** | Finger/toe **PADS** | Palms/soles |
| **Pain** | **PAINFUL** | **Painless** |
| **Cause** | Immune complex deposition | Septic emboli |
| **Memory** | "**Osler = Ouch**" | "**Janeway = Just walks on them**" |

### PART 8: RHEUMATIC HEART DISEASE

### Pathogenesis
**Rheumatic heart disease** begins with a **Group A Strep throat infection** (**Strep pyogenes**). Antibodies form against the **Strep M-protein**, which then exhibit **molecular mimicry** by attacking cardiac proteins. This leads to **Pancarditis**, an inflammation of all heart layers, characterized by the formation of **Aschoff bodies**, which are pathognomonic granulomas. These **Aschoff bodies** contain **Aschoff cells**, which are large macrophages with "caterpillar/owl eye" nuclei. The inflammation eventually heals with **fibrosis**, leading to **CHRONIC RHEUMATIC HEART DISEASE**.

### Jones Criteria (Diagnosis of Acute RF)
The **Jones Criteria** are used for the diagnosis of **Acute Rheumatic Fever**:

#### Major Criteria — "JONES"
- **J**oints — migratory polyarthritis (most common)
- **O** — cardi**O**megaly/carditis
- **N**odules — subcutaneous nodules
- **E**rythema marginatum — skin rash
- **S**ydenham's chorea — involuntary movements

#### Minor Criteria
Fever, elevated ESR/CRP, prolonged PR interval, previous history of **rheumatic fever**.

Diagnosis requires 2 Major criteria **OR** 1 Major + 2 Minor criteria, along with evidence of preceding **Strep infection**.

### Valve Involvement Order
In **rheumatic heart disease**, the valves are affected in a specific order of frequency:
**MITRAL** → **AORTIC** → **TRICUSPID** → **PULMONIC** (the pulmonic valve is almost never affected).

### Summary Table

| Feature | MS | MR | AS | AR |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Main cause** | **Rheumatic** | **MVP**/Post-MI | **Calcific/Bicuspid** | **Marfan/Syphilis/Rheumatic** |
| **Pressure or Volume** | Pressure on LA | Volume on LA+LV | Pressure on LV | Volume on LV |
| **Chamber enlargement** | LA only | LA + LV | LV **hypertrophy** | LV dilates + hypertrophies |
| **Key symptom** | **Dyspnea** + **A-fib** | Fatigue + **dyspnea** | **SAD triad** | **Wide pulse pressure** signs |
| **Murmur** | **Mid-diastolic rumble** | **Holosystolic** | **Systolic ejection** | **Early diastolic** |
| **Key complication** | **Stroke from LA clot** | LV failure | Sudden death | LV failure |

### High Yield MCQ Triggers
- If you see this... Elderly + **SAD triad** + **LVH** + normal cholesterol → Think... **Calcific Aortic Stenosis**
- If you see this... Middle-aged woman + fused leaflets + **A-fib** + **stroke** → Think... **Rheumatic Mitral Stenosis**
- If you see this... Post-MI day 2-7 + sudden **pulmonary edema** → Think... **Papillary muscle rupture** → **Acute MR**
- If you see this... Young woman + **mid-systolic click** → Think... **MVP**
- If you see this... IV drug user + fever + tricuspid vegetation → Think... **Staph aureus IE** (**right sided**)
- If you see this... Dental procedure + fever + **Strep viridans** → Think... **Subacute IE**
- If you see this... **SLE** + vegetations on **BOTH** sides of mitral → Think... **Libman-Sacks endocarditis**
- If you see this... Cancer patient + sterile vegetations → Think... **Marantic endocarditis** (**NBTE**)
- If you see this... Tall patient + **wide pulse pressure** + **AR** → Think... **Marfan syndrome**
- If you see this... **Syphilis** + **AR** + ascending aneurysm → Think... **Syphilitic aortitis**
- If you see this... Child + migratory arthritis + carditis + **Strep** → Think... **Acute Rheumatic Fever**
- If you see this... **Colon cancer** + **IE** → Think... **Strep bovis**

## Practice Questions
1.  **Question:** What is the most common cause of mitral stenosis? → **Answer:** Rheumatic Heart Disease.
2.  **Question:** Which two types of valvular problems lead to "pressure overload" versus "volume overload" on the heart chambers? → **Answer:** Stenosis causes pressure overload; Regurgitation causes volume overload.
3.  **Question:** A 70-year-old patient presents with syncope, angina, and dyspnea. What is this clinical presentation known as, and which valvular disease is most likely? → **Answer:** The "SAD triad" for Aortic Stenosis.
4.  **Question:** A young woman is diagnosed with a valvular abnormality characterized by a mid-systolic click. What is this condition? → **Answer:** Mitral Valve Prolapse (MVP).
5.  **Question:** What is the most common cause of acute, severe mitral regurgitation that occurs 2-7 days post-myocardial infarction? → **Answer:** Papillary muscle rupture.
6.  **Question:** Which organism is the most common cause of infective endocarditis in IV drug users, and which heart valve is typically affected? → **Answer:** Staph aureus, affecting the tricuspid valve.
7.  **Question:** What are Aschoff bodies, and which condition are they pathognomonic for? → **Answer:** Granulomas containing macrophages with "caterpillar/owl eye" nuclei, pathognomonic for Acute Rheumatic Fever.
8.  **Question:** A patient exhibits Corrigan's pulse and De Musset's sign. These findings are characteristic of which valvular heart disease and are due to what hemodynamic change? → **Answer:** Aortic Regurgitation, due to wide pulse pressure.
