## Part 1: Hepatitis Viruses

## 1. Introduction

Hepatitis means inflammation of the liver. Viral hepatitis is caused by a group of viruses that primarily target hepatocytes — liver cells. Despite being caused by completely unrelated viruses from different families, they all share the common clinical feature of hepatitis. Understanding their differences in transmission, natural history, chronicity, and prevention is essential for clinical practice. At MKU, hepatitis viruses appear consistently in MCQs testing transmission routes, diagnostic methods, and specific clinical features.

## 2. Overview — The Five Hepatitis Viruses

| Feature | HAV | HBV | HCV | HDV | HEV |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Family | Picornaviridae | Hepadnaviridae | Flaviviridae | Deltaviridae | Hepeviridae |
| Genome | ssRNA | dsDNA (partial) | ssRNA | ssRNA | ssRNA |
| Envelope | No | Yes | Yes | Yes | No |
| Transmission | Fecal-oral | Parenteral, sexual, vertical | Parenteral | Parenteral (needs HBV) | Fecal-oral |
| Chronicity | Never | Yes (5–10% adults) | Yes (75–85%) | Yes | Never (except immunocompromised) |
| Vaccine | Yes | Yes | No | HBV vaccine prevents | No licensed vaccine |
| Oncogenic | No | Yes — HCC | Yes — HCC | Yes | No |

## 3. Hepatitis A Virus (HAV)

### Classification
- **Family:** Picornaviridae
- **Genome:** Single-stranded positive-sense RNA
- **Non-enveloped** — survives in water, acidic environments, and on surfaces
- **Transmission:** Fecal-oral — contaminated food and water, poor sanitation

### Pathogenesis
- Ingested virus survives stomach acid
- Infects intestinal epithelial cells → enters portal circulation → reaches liver
- Infects hepatocytes — virus replicates in hepatocytes and is shed into bile → excreted in faeces
- Liver damage is primarily immune-mediated — cytotoxic T cells destroy infected hepatocytes

### Clinical Features
- **Incubation period:** 2–6 weeks
- Prodrome — fever, malaise, anorexia, nausea, right upper quadrant pain
- Jaundice — appears after prodrome; tea-coloured urine, pale stools, pruritis
- Hepatomegaly and tenderness
- Self-limiting — complete recovery in most cases
- No chronic infection — ever
- Fulminant hepatitis — rare but life-threatening

### High-Risk Groups
- Children in developing countries — most infections subclinical
- Travellers to endemic areas
- Men who have sex with men
- Injection drug users

### Diagnosis — High Yield
- **IgM anti-HAV** — gold standard for acute infection; appears early, lasts 3–6 months
- **IgG anti-HAV** — indicates past infection or vaccination; persists lifelong
- **Detection of faecal HAV by immunoelectron microscopy** — research setting
- **Raised ALT and AST** — liver enzymes elevated; ALT more specific for hepatocyte damage

### Treatment
- No specific antiviral
- Supportive — rest, adequate nutrition, avoid hepatotoxic drugs and alcohol
- Hospitalization for severe cases

### Prevention
- **Improved sanitation and clean water** — most important
- **Handwashing** — especially food handlers
- **HAV vaccine** — inactivated; two doses; highly effective; recommended for travellers and high-risk groups
- **Passive immunization** — normal human immunoglobulin (HNIG) for post-exposure prophylaxis within 2 weeks

## 4. Hepatitis B Virus (HBV)

### Classification
- **Family:** Hepadnaviridae
- **Genome:** Partially double-stranded circular DNA — unique among human hepatitis viruses
- **Enveloped virus**
- **Replication:** Uses reverse transcriptase — unique for a DNA virus; HBV replicates through an RNA intermediate

### Transmission — High Yield
HBV is present in blood, semen, vaginal secretions, saliva, breast milk, and other body fluids.

- **Parenteral** — blood transfusion, sharing needles, needlestick injuries
- **Sexual contact** — highly efficient sexually transmitted infection
- **Vertical transmission** — mother to child during delivery (most important route globally)
- **Horizontal transmission** — child-to-child in endemic areas via minor skin breaks

### Virology — Key Antigens and Antibodies

| Marker | Meaning |
|---|---|
| HBsAg (surface antigen) | Present in active infection — acute or chronic; first marker to appear |
| Anti-HBs | Antibody to surface antigen; indicates immunity — from vaccination or recovery |
| HBeAg (e antigen) | Marker of active viral replication; high infectivity |
| Anti-HBe | Seroconversion from HBeAg; lower replication, lower infectivity |
| HBcAg (core antigen) | Not detectable in serum — only in liver tissue |
| Anti-HBc IgM | Acute HBV infection |
| Anti-HBc IgG | Past or chronic infection |
| HBV DNA | Viral load — measures active replication |

**Window period:**
- HBsAg disappears but anti-HBs not yet detectable
- Only anti-HBc IgM positive — the only marker of infection during window period

### Natural History

**Acute HBV infection:**
- Incubation: 1–6 months
- 70% subclinical or mild
- 30% symptomatic hepatitis — jaundice, malaise, anorexia
- Less than 1% fulminant hepatitis

**Outcomes after acute infection:**
- 95% of immunocompetent adults — complete recovery, virus cleared, anti-HBs develops
- 5% of adults — progress to chronic infection
- 90% of neonates infected at birth — chronic infection (immature immune system)
- 30–50% of children infected aged 1–5 — chronic infection

**Chronic HBV infection:**
- HBsAg positive for more than 6 months
- Phases: immune tolerant → immune active → inactive carrier → reactivation
- Complications: liver cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)
- HBV causes HCC through: chromosomal integration, HBx protein activating oncogenes, chronic inflammation

### Diagnosis
- **HBsAg** — first marker; confirms infection
- **Anti-HBc IgM** — acute infection
- **HBeAg and HBV DNA** — active replication, infectivity
- **Liver function tests** — ALT/AST elevated
- **Liver biopsy** — assess fibrosis and inflammation in chronic disease

### Treatment
- **Acute HBV** — supportive; no antivirals unless fulminant
- **Chronic HBV:**
  - **Tenofovir** — first-line nucleotide analogue; inhibits HBV reverse transcriptase
  - **Entecavir** — first-line nucleoside analogue
  - **Pegylated IFN-α** — 48-week course; induces immune response; HBeAg seroconversion
  - Lamivudine, adefovir — older agents, high resistance rates

### Prevention
- **HBV vaccine** — recombinant HBsAg; 3 doses (0, 1, 6 months); highly effective; part of national immunisation schedules
- **Neonatal vaccination** — within 24 hours of birth + HBIG for babies of HBsAg-positive mothers
- **HBIG (Hepatitis B Immunoglobulin)** — passive immunization for post-exposure prophylaxis
- **Safe injection practices** — needle exchange programmes
- **Blood screening** — all donated blood screened for HBsAg
- **Safe sex** — condom use

## 5. Hepatitis C Virus (HCV)

### Classification
- **Family:** Flaviviridae
- **Genome:** Single-stranded positive-sense RNA
- **Enveloped virus**
- **Highly variable** — 7 genotypes; high mutation rate due to error-prone RNA polymerase

### Transmission
- **Parenteral** — sharing needles (most common in developed world), blood transfusion (before screening), needlestick injuries
- **Sexual transmission** — less efficient than HBV; increases with HIV co-infection
- **Vertical transmission** — less common than HBV
- **NOT transmitted** by fecal-oral route

### Natural History
- Incubation: 2–12 weeks
- Acute infection often asymptomatic — 80% no symptoms
- **75–85% progress to chronic infection** — highest chronicity rate of all hepatitis viruses
- Chronic HCV → cirrhosis (20–30% over 20 years) → hepatocellular carcinoma
- HCV is a leading indication for liver transplantation globally

### Diagnosis
- **Anti-HCV antibody** — screening test; ELISA; appears 8–12 weeks after infection
- **HCV RNA by PCR** — confirms active infection; used in window period and to monitor treatment
- **HCV genotype** — guides treatment duration and drug choice
- No antigen test widely used clinically

### Treatment
- **Direct-Acting Antivirals (DAAs)** — revolutionary; cure rates over 95%
  - Sofosbuvir (NS5B polymerase inhibitor)
  - Ledipasvir, Daclatasvir (NS5A inhibitors)
  - Simeprevir (NS3/4A protease inhibitor)
  - Combinations: Sofosbuvir/Ledipasvir, Sofosbuvir/Velpatasvir
- **Pegylated IFN-α + Ribavirin** — older regimen; largely replaced by DAAs

### Prevention
- **No vaccine available** — high mutation rate makes vaccine development difficult
- Needle exchange programmes
- Blood screening
- Safe sex practices
- Testing and treatment of infected individuals — reduces transmission

## 6. Hepatitis E Virus (HEV)

### Classification
- **Family:** Hepeviridae
- **Genome:** Single-stranded positive-sense RNA
- **Non-enveloped**
- **Transmission:** Fecal-oral — contaminated water; zoonotic (pigs, deer)

### Clinical Features
- Self-limiting acute hepatitis — similar to HAV
- No chronic infection in immunocompetent
- **Special feature:** Very high mortality in pregnant women — up to 20–25% in third trimester
- Causes fulminant hepatic failure in pregnancy — most important clinical fact

### Diagnosis
- Anti-HEV IgM — acute infection
- HEV RNA by PCR — most sensitive

### Prevention
- Clean water and sanitation
- HEV vaccine — licensed in China; not widely available

## Part 2: Oncogenic Viruses

## 1. Introduction

Oncogenic viruses are viruses capable of causing cancer in humans. They account for approximately 15–20% of all human cancers worldwide. Understanding viral oncogenesis is important both clinically and scientifically — it explains the geographic distribution of certain cancers, provides targets for cancer prevention through vaccines, and illuminates the molecular mechanisms of cancer in general. At MKU, oncogenic viruses appear in MCQs and one essay question — comparing normal cells with cancer cells.

## 2. Mechanisms of Viral Carcinogenesis

Viruses cause cancer through several distinct mechanisms:

### Direct Mechanisms

**1. Integration of viral DNA into host genome:**
- Viral DNA integrates near or within host proto-oncogenes or tumour suppressor genes
- Disrupts normal gene regulation
- Example: HBV integration near oncogenes → HCC

**2. Viral oncoproteins:**
- Viral proteins directly inactivate tumour suppressor proteins
- **HPV E6 protein** — binds and degrades p53 tumour suppressor
- **HPV E7 protein** — binds and inactivates Rb (retinoblastoma) protein
- p53 and Rb are the two most important tumour suppressor proteins in human cells
- Loss of both → uncontrolled cell proliferation

**3. Insertional mutagenesis:**
- Viral genome inserts next to a cellular proto-oncogene
- Viral promoter drives overexpression of the proto-oncogene → oncogene
- Example: HTLV-1 Tax protein activates NF-κB → promotes T cell proliferation

**4. B cell immortalization:**
- EBV immortalizes B lymphocytes — they proliferate indefinitely
- Combined with chromosomal translocation → malignant lymphoma

### Indirect Mechanisms

**5. Chronic inflammation:**
- Chronic viral infection → persistent inflammation → mutagenic environment
- Reactive oxygen and nitrogen species damage DNA
- Example: HBV and HCV → chronic hepatitis → cirrhosis → HCC

**6. Immunosuppression:**
- HIV destroys CD4+ T cells → severe immunosuppression
- Impaired immune surveillance → other oncogenic viruses cause cancer unopposed
- Example: HIV → KS (HHV-8), lymphoma (EBV), cervical cancer (HPV)

## 3. Normal Cell vs Cancer Cell — High Yield Essay Topic

| Feature | Normal Cell | Cancer Cell |
|---|---|---|
| Growth control | Controlled by growth factors and cell cycle checkpoints | Uncontrolled — ignores growth signals |
| Cell cycle | Regulated by cyclins, CDKs, p53, Rb | Cell cycle checkpoints bypassed |
| Contact inhibition | Stops dividing when touching other cells | Loses contact inhibition — piles up |
| Apoptosis | Undergoes programmed death when damaged | Resists apoptosis — p53 inactivated |
| Differentiation | Fully differentiated — performs specific function | Poorly differentiated — loses normal function |
| Telomeres | Shorten with each division — limits lifespan | Telomerase activated — immortal |
| Angiogenesis | Normal vascular supply | Induces new blood vessel formation (angiogenesis) via VEGF |
| Invasion | Stays within tissue boundaries | Invades surrounding tissues |
| Metastasis | Does not spread to distant sites | Spreads via blood and lymphatics |
| Antigenic profile | Normal surface antigens | Expresses tumour-associated antigens |
| Energy metabolism | Oxidative phosphorylation | Warburg effect — aerobic glycolysis |

## 4. Key Oncogenic Viruses and Their Associated Cancers

### Human Papillomavirus (HPV)

- **Family:** Papillomaviridae
- **Genome:** dsDNA, non-enveloped
- **Oncogenic types:** HPV 16 and HPV 18 — cause 70% of cervical cancers
- **Low-risk types:** HPV 6 and 11 — cause genital warts (condylomata acuminata)

**Mechanism of oncogenesis:**
- HPV infects basal epithelial cells of cervical transformation zone
- E6 protein → degrades p53
- E7 protein → inactivates Rb protein
- Loss of p53 and Rb → cells cannot arrest or undergo apoptosis → uncontrolled proliferation
- Accumulation of further mutations → invasive carcinoma

**Associated cancers:**
- Cervical carcinoma — most important
- Vulvar, vaginal, penile carcinoma
- Anal carcinoma
- Oropharyngeal carcinoma (base of tongue, tonsil) — increasing incidence

**Prevention:**
- **HPV vaccine** — Gardasil (types 6, 11, 16, 18) or Cervarix (types 16, 18)
- Most effective when given before sexual debut
- **Cervical screening** — Pap smear detects precancerous CIN lesions
- Condom use — reduces but does not eliminate transmission

### Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV) — HHV-4

- **Associated cancers:**
  - **Burkitt's lymphoma** — most common childhood cancer in equatorial Africa; jaw mass; t(8;14) translocation activating c-myc oncogene; EBV in 95% of endemic cases
  - **Nasopharyngeal carcinoma** — especially in Southern China; EBV in virtually all cases
  - **Hodgkin's lymphoma** — EBV in 40–50% of cases
  - **Post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder (PTLD)**
  - **Primary CNS lymphoma** — in AIDS

### Hepatitis B Virus (HBV) and Hepatitis C Virus (HCV)

- Both cause **hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)**
- HBV — direct oncogenesis via integration and HBx protein; also chronic inflammation
- HCV — primarily through chronic inflammation and cirrhosis
- HCC is the third most common cause of cancer death worldwide
- Prevention: HBV vaccine, DAAs for HCV

### Human Herpesvirus 8 (HHV-8) — Kaposi's Sarcoma Herpesvirus (KSHV)

- Causes **Kaposi's sarcoma** — vascular tumour; purple/red skin lesions
- Three forms:
  - Classic KS — elderly Mediterranean men
  - Endemic KS — Sub-Saharan Africa
  - AIDS-related KS — most common AIDS-defining malignancy; aggressive
- Also causes: primary effusion lymphoma, multicentric Castleman disease
- Treatment: ART for AIDS-related KS (restores immune control), chemotherapy for disseminated disease

### HTLV-1 (Human T-cell Lymphotropic Virus Type 1)

- **Family:** Retroviridae
- **Associated cancer:** Adult T-cell leukaemia/lymphoma (ATL)
- Transmitted by: sexual contact, blood, breast milk
- Endemic in: Japan, Caribbean, Sub-Saharan Africa
- Tax protein activates NF-κB → T cell proliferation → lymphoma
- Also causes: HTLV-1-associated myelopathy (tropical spastic paraparesis)

### Merkel Cell Polyomavirus (MCPyV)

- Causes **Merkel cell carcinoma** — rare aggressive skin cancer in elderly and immunocompromised

## Part 3: Paramyxoviruses — Measles, Mumps, RSV, Parainfluenza, and Rubella

## 1. Introduction

Paramyxoviruses are a family of enveloped RNA viruses that cause some of the most important childhood infections in history — measles, mumps, and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV). Rubella, though technically a togavirus, is traditionally grouped with these in clinical teaching because of its similar epidemiology and the MMR vaccine. These viruses caused massive childhood morbidity and mortality before vaccination, and remain important in unvaccinated populations. At MKU they appear primarily in MCQs testing clinical features, vaccines, and distinguishing features of each virus.

## 2. General Features of Paramyxoviruses

- **Family:** Paramyxoviridae
- **Genome:** Single-stranded negative-sense RNA — non-segmented
- **Enveloped virus**
- **Two key surface glycoproteins:**
  - **Haemagglutinin-neuraminidase (HN)** — attachment and neuraminidase activity
  - **Fusion protein (F)** — mediates cell-to-cell fusion → multinucleated giant cells (syncytia) formation — characteristic cytopathic effect
- Transmitted primarily by respiratory droplets

## 3. Measles Virus

### Key Facts
- **Genus:** Morbillivirus
- **Only one serotype** — effective lifelong immunity after infection or vaccination
- **Receptor:** CD46 and SLAM (signalling lymphocyte activation molecule) on immune cells
- **Most contagious human virus known** — R0 of 12–18

### Pathogenesis
- Inhaled droplets → infects respiratory epithelium and regional lymph nodes
- Primary viremia → spreads to lymphoid tissue throughout body
- Secondary viremia → skin, respiratory tract, CNS, eyes
- Giant cell (syncytia) formation in respiratory epithelium — Warthin-Finkeldey giant cells in lymphoid tissue
- Rash caused by immune-mediated damage to skin blood vessels — not direct viral effect

### Clinical Features
- **Incubation:** 10–14 days
- **Prodrome (3 Cs):** Cough, Coryza, Conjunctivitis — plus high fever
- **Koplik's spots** — pathognomonic; white spots on bright red buccal mucosa opposite lower molars; appear 1–2 days before rash
- **Rash** — maculopapular; starts behind ears and hairline → spreads downward to face, trunk, limbs; lasts 4–7 days
- **Fever** — high; may reach 40°C

### Complications
- **Otitis media** — most common complication
- **Pneumonia** — most common cause of death; primary viral or secondary bacterial
- **Encephalitis** — 1 in 1000 cases; acute post-infectious demyelinating encephalitis
- **Subacute sclerosing panencephalitis (SSPE)** — rare, fatal; occurs years after measles; progressive CNS degeneration; caused by persistent measles virus in brain
- **Measles inclusion body encephalitis** — in immunocompromised
- **Vitamin A deficiency** worsens measles severity — supplement routinely in developing countries
- **Immunosuppression** — measles causes lymphopenia and immune suppression lasting weeks → window for secondary infections

### Diagnosis
- Clinical — characteristic prodrome and rash
- IgM serology — acute infection
- PCR — from throat, urine, blood
- Viral culture

### Treatment
- Supportive
- **Vitamin A supplementation** — reduces mortality in developing countries
- Ribavirin — for severe measles in immunocompromised

### Vaccine
- **MMR vaccine** — live attenuated; given at 12–15 months and 4–6 years
- Highly effective — one dose 93% protective, two doses 97% protective
- Contraindicated in immunocompromised and pregnancy

## 4. Mumps Virus

### Key Facts
- **Genus:** Rubulavirus
- **Receptor:** Sialic acid on ductal epithelial cells — explains tropism for glands
- Transmitted by respiratory droplets and saliva

### Clinical Features
- **Incubation:** 14–25 days
- **Parotitis** — painful swelling of parotid glands; bilateral in 70%; pathognomonic of mumps
- Fever, malaise, headache
- Submandibular and sublingual gland swelling possible

### Complications
- **Orchitis** — most important; occurs in 20–30% of post-pubertal males; usually unilateral; can cause testicular atrophy; rarely causes sterility
- **Oophoritis** — in post-pubertal females
- **Aseptic meningitis** — most common CNS complication; usually benign
- **Encephalitis** — rare but serious
- **Pancreatitis** — elevated amylase
- **Sensorineural hearing loss** — rare but important

### Diagnosis
- Clinical — bilateral parotitis
- Serology — IgM anti-mumps
- PCR — from saliva, urine, CSF

### Treatment
- Supportive only
- Scrotal support and analgesics for orchitis

### Vaccine
- MMR — live attenuated; same schedule as measles

## 5. Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV)

### Key Facts
- **Genus:** Pneumovirus
- **Most important cause of lower respiratory tract infection in infants and young children worldwide**
- Two subtypes — A and B; both circulate simultaneously
- No haemagglutinin or neuraminidase — distinguished from other paramyxoviruses
- **Fusion protein (F)** — mediates syncytia formation; major target of immune response and therapeutics
- Transmitted by respiratory droplets and direct contact with secretions

### Pathogenesis
- Infects upper respiratory epithelium first
- Spreads to lower respiratory tract — bronchioles
- F protein causes cell fusion → syncytia formation → airway obstruction
- Immune response contributes to inflammation and bronchospasm
- Narrow bronchioles of infants → severe disease with obstruction

### Clinical Features

**In infants and young children (most severe):**
- **Bronchiolitis** — most characteristic; wheeze, tachypnoea, hyperinflation, feeding difficulty
- **Pneumonia** — fever, cough, respiratory distress
- Peak age: 2–6 months
- Most common cause of hospitalisation in infants in winter

**In older children and adults:**
- Common cold-like illness — mild upper respiratory tract infection
- More severe in elderly and immunocompromised

### High-Risk Groups for Severe RSV

- Premature infants
- Infants with congenital heart disease
- Infants with chronic lung disease
- Immunocompromised
- Elderly

### Diagnosis
- **Rapid antigen detection** — nasopharyngeal swab; quick; used in clinical setting
- **PCR** — most sensitive
- **Viral culture** — shows syncytia formation — characteristic
- **Direct immunofluorescence** — from nasopharyngeal aspirate

### Treatment
- **Supportive** — oxygen, hydration, feeding support
- **Ribavirin** — inhaled; limited evidence; used in severe cases in immunocompromised
- **Bronchodilators** — limited benefit in bronchiolitis

### Prevention
- **Palivizumab** — monoclonal antibody against RSV F protein; given monthly during RSV season to high-risk infants; passive immunization
- **Nirsevimab** — newer long-acting monoclonal antibody; single dose; now recommended for all infants
- RSV vaccines — now available for adults (Abrysvo, mRESVIA); approved 2023
- Handwashing and contact precautions in hospitals

## 6. Rubella Virus

### Classification
- **Family:** Togaviridae — not a paramyxovirus; grouped here clinically
- **Genus:** Rubivirus
- **Genome:** Single-stranded positive-sense RNA
- **Enveloped**
- Transmitted by respiratory droplets

### Clinical Features — Postnatal Rubella
- **Incubation:** 14–21 days
- Mild illness in children — low-grade fever, lymphadenopathy (especially suboccipital and postauricular), mild rash
- **Rash** — pink maculopapular; starts on face → spreads downward; fades within 3 days
- Arthralgia — common in adult women
- Forchheimer spots — petechiae on soft palate — rare but characteristic

### Congenital Rubella Syndrome (CRS) — Most Important Feature

Rubella infection in pregnancy, especially first trimester, causes severe fetal damage.

**Mechanism:** Rubella infects placental endothelium → crosses to fetus → infects fetal cells → disrupts organogenesis

**Risk of CRS by trimester:**
- First trimester — 85% risk of fetal abnormality
- Second trimester — 25% risk
- After 20 weeks — risk very low

**Classic triad of CRS:**
- **Cataracts** — most characteristic eye finding
- **Cardiac defects** — patent ductus arteriosus (most common), pulmonary artery stenosis, VSD
- **Sensorineural deafness** — most common single defect

**Other features of CRS:**
- Microcephaly
- Mental retardation
- Retinopathy (salt and pepper retinopathy)
- Hepatosplenomegaly
- Thrombocytopenic purpura — blueberry muffin rash
- Glaucoma
- Bone lesions

### Diagnosis
- **IgM serology** — acute rubella infection
- **PCR** — from throat, urine, blood
- **Prenatal diagnosis** — PCR on amniocentesis or chorionic villus sampling

### Treatment
- No specific antiviral
- Supportive

### Prevention
- **MMR vaccine** — most important prevention strategy
- Vaccination of all children and seronegative women of childbearing age
- Contraindicated in pregnancy — live vaccine
- Screen all pregnant women for rubella immunity in first antenatal visit

## Part 4: Arboviruses

## 1. Introduction

Arboviruses (Arthropod-Borne Viruses) are a diverse group of viruses transmitted to humans through the bites of arthropod vectors — primarily mosquitoes and ticks. They belong to multiple unrelated viral families but share the common feature of transmission by arthropod vectors. They cause a wide spectrum of diseases — from mild febrile illness to haemorrhagic fever, encephalitis, and severe congenital defects. Arboviruses are particularly relevant in tropical and subtropical regions including sub-Saharan Africa and Kenya. At MKU they appear primarily in MCQs.

## 2. Key Arboviruses — Overview

| Virus | Family | Vector | Geographic Distribution | Key Disease |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dengue | Flaviviridae | Aedes aegypti | Tropics and subtropics globally | Dengue fever, DHF, DSS |
| Yellow Fever | Flaviviridae | Aedes aegypti | Africa, South America | Yellow fever |
| Zika | Flaviviridae | Aedes aegypti | Americas, Africa, Pacific | Zika fever, microcephaly |
| West Nile | Flaviviridae | Culex mosquitoes | Africa, Middle East, Europe, Americas | West Nile fever, encephalitis |
| Chikungunya | Togaviridae | Aedes aegypti, Aedes albopictus | Africa, Asia, Americas | Chikungunya fever |
| Rift Valley Fever | Phenuiviridae | Aedes, Culex mosquitoes | Sub-Saharan Africa | Fever, hepatitis, haemorrhage |
| Japanese Encephalitis | Flaviviridae | Culex mosquitoes | Asia | Encephalitis |

## 3. Dengue Virus

### Classification
- **Family:** Flaviviridae
- **Genome:** ssRNA positive-sense
- **Enveloped**
- **Four serotypes** — DENV-1, 2, 3, 4; infection with one confers lifelong immunity to that serotype but not others
- **Vector:** Aedes aegypti mosquito — bites during daytime

### Pathogenesis
- Mosquito bite → virus deposited in skin → infects dendritic cells
- Spreads to regional lymph nodes → viremia → disseminates to liver, spleen, bone marrow, endothelium
- **Antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE)** — critical concept:
  - After first infection, antibodies form against that serotype
  - If infected by a different serotype, cross-reactive antibodies bind virus but cannot neutralize it
  - Instead facilitate viral entry into Fc receptor-bearing monocytes → higher viral loads → more severe disease
  - This explains why second dengue infection with a different serotype causes DHF/DSS

### Clinical Syndromes

**Classic Dengue Fever (Break-bone fever):**
- Incubation: 4–10 days
- Sudden high fever — biphasic (saddle-back) pattern
- Severe headache, retro-orbital pain
- Severe myalgia and arthralgia — break-bone fever
- Maculopapular rash — appears 3–4 days after fever onset
- Lymphadenopathy
- Leukopenia and thrombocytopenia on blood count
- Self-limiting — recovery in 7–10 days

**Dengue Haemorrhagic Fever (DHF):**
- Occurs with second infection (different serotype) — ADE mechanism
- Thrombocytopenia — below 100,000/mm³
- Evidence of plasma leakage — rising haematocrit, pleural effusion, ascites
- Haemorrhagic manifestations — petechiae, purpura, mucosal bleeding

**Dengue Shock Syndrome (DSS):**
- Severe plasma leakage → hypovolaemic shock
- Life-threatening
- Rapid deterioration — circulatory failure

### Diagnosis
- **NS1 antigen** — detectable in first 5 days; rapid test
- **IgM serology** — appears after day 5
- **RT-PCR** — most sensitive in first 5 days; detects viral RNA
- **FBC** — leukopenia, thrombocytopenia

### Treatment
- No specific antiviral
- Supportive — IV fluids, careful fluid management
- Avoid aspirin and NSAIDs — worsen bleeding
- Platelet transfusion — only for severe bleeding

### Prevention
- **Dengvaxia vaccine** — only for seropositive individuals aged 9–45; risk of severe disease in seronegative recipients
- **Mosquito control** — eliminate breeding sites (stagnant water), insecticides
- **Personal protection** — repellents, long sleeves, bed nets

## 4. Yellow Fever Virus

### Classification
- **Family:** Flaviviridae
- **Vector:** Aedes aegypti (urban) and forest mosquitoes (sylvatic)
- **Three transmission cycles:**
  - Sylvatic (jungle) — forest monkeys to humans via forest mosquitoes
  - Intermediate (savannah) — semi-domestic mosquitoes in Africa
  - Urban — Aedes aegypti human-to-human

### Clinical Features
- **Incubation:** 3–6 days
- **Phase 1 (Infection):** Fever, headache, myalgia, nausea — 3–4 days
- **Phase 2 (Remission):** Symptoms improve — 24–48 hours
- **Phase 3 (Intoxication)** — occurs in 15% of patients:
  - **Jaundice** — liver failure; the yellow in yellow fever
  - **Haemorrhage** — black vomit (haematemesis), bleeding gums, epistaxis
  - **Renal failure**
  - **Bradycardia despite high fever** — Faget's sign; characteristic
  - **Case fatality rate:** 20–50% in this phase

### Diagnosis
- **Serology** — IgM anti-yellow fever
- **RT-PCR** — acute phase
- **Liver histology** — Councilman bodies (eosinophilic degeneration of hepatocytes); midzonal necrosis

### Treatment
- Supportive only
- No specific antiviral

### Prevention
- **Yellow fever vaccine** — live attenuated (17D strain); single dose; highly effective; lifelong protection
- Required for entry to many countries — international certificate of vaccination
- Mosquito control

## 5. Zika Virus

### Classification
- **Family:** Flaviviridae
- **Vector:** Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus
- **Also transmitted by:** Sexual contact (unique among arboviruses — persists in semen), blood transfusion, vertical transmission

### Clinical Features
- Most infections (80%) asymptomatic
- Mild fever, rash, arthralgia, conjunctivitis — self-limiting

### Most Important Clinical Associations — High Yield

**Congenital Zika Syndrome:**
- Zika in pregnancy → crosses placenta → infects fetal neurons
- **Microcephaly** — most characteristic; severe brain underdevelopment
- Cortical malformations
- Eye defects
- Joint contractures
- Most severe with first trimester infection

**Guillain-Barré Syndrome:**
- Post-infectious immune-mediated polyneuropathy
- Ascending flaccid paralysis
- Follows Zika infection by 2–4 weeks

### Diagnosis
- **RT-PCR** — from blood, urine (urine positive longer than blood), saliva
- **IgM serology** — cross-reacts with other flaviviruses (dengue, yellow fever)

### Treatment
- No specific antiviral
- Supportive

### Prevention
- Mosquito control
- Condom use — prevents sexual transmission
- Pregnant women advised to avoid travel to Zika-endemic areas
- No vaccine available

## 6. Chikungunya Virus

### Classification
- **Family:** Togaviridae (same family as rubella — but an arbovirus)
- **Genus:** Alphavirus
- **Vector:** Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus

### Clinical Features
- **Incubation:** 2–12 days
- High fever — sudden onset
- **Severe arthralgia and arthritis** — most characteristic feature; affects multiple joints; can persist for months to years
- Maculopapular rash
- Headache, myalgia
- Name means "that which bends up" in Makonde language — describes the stooped posture from joint pain

### Diagnosis
- RT-PCR — acute phase (first 5 days)
- IgM serology — after day 5

### Treatment
- Supportive
- NSAIDs for arthralgia
- No specific antiviral

### Prevention
- Mosquito control
- Personal protection
- No licensed vaccine

## 7. West Nile Virus

### Classification
- **Family:** Flaviviridae
- **Vector:** Culex mosquitoes (unlike dengue and Zika which use Aedes)
- **Reservoir:** Birds — mosquitoes transmit from birds to humans; humans are dead-end hosts

### Clinical Features
- 80% asymptomatic
- 20% — West Nile fever: mild febrile illness, rash, lymphadenopathy
- Less than 1% — **neuroinvasive disease:**
  - West Nile meningitis
  - West Nile encephalitis
  - Acute flaccid paralysis — polio-like; due to anterior horn cell infection

### High-Risk for Neuroinvasive Disease

- Elderly
- Immunocompromised
- Organ transplant recipients

### Diagnosis
- IgM in CSF — confirms neuroinvasive disease
- RT-PCR — blood or CSF

### Treatment
- Supportive
- No specific antiviral or vaccine for humans
