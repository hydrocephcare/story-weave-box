# CNS Malignancies (Brain Tumours)

**Year 4 · Paediatrics · Paediatric Oncology and Neurology**

> **In one line:** brain tumours are primary (arising from CNS cells) or secondary (metastases), and in children they are mostly gliomas, medulloblastoma, ependymoma and craniopharyngioma. Symptoms come from raised intracranial pressure and from the site of the tumour (supratentorial gives focal deficits and seizures; infratentorial gives ataxia, vomiting and hydrocephalus); MRI with gadolinium is the investigation of choice and treatment combines surgery, radiotherapy and chemotherapy.

## What you must know

- Brain and CNS tumours are the **commonest solid tumours of childhood**; **80%** are juvenile pilocytic astrocytoma, medulloblastoma/PNET, diffuse astrocytoma, ependymoma and craniopharyngioma.
- **Age and site:** infants mostly supratentorial (choroid plexus tumours, teratoma); **1 to 10 years mostly infratentorial** (pilocytic astrocytoma, medulloblastoma); **over 10 years** supratentorial (diffuse astrocytoma).
- **Medulloblastoma** is the commonest malignant childhood brain tumour (about 20% of paediatric CNS tumours).
- Classic triad: **headache, vomiting and papilloedema**, linked to midline and infratentorial tumours.
- **Cranial MRI with gadolinium** is the preferred test; CT only if MRI is not possible.
- Established risk factors are only **ionising radiation** and **immunosuppression**; several **genetic syndromes** (NF1, NF2, VHL, TSC, Li-Fraumeni) predispose.
- Treatment depends on type, site and stage; a **combination** of surgery, radiotherapy and chemotherapy is most effective.

## 1. Introduction and incidence

- Brain tumours are **primary** (from CNS cells) or **secondary** (metastases).
- **Adult primary tumours (USA):** about 52,000 diagnosed a year; at least half are malignant. **Glial tumours** make up about **60%** (80% of those malignant). **Meningiomas** 25%, **vestibular schwannomas** 10%, **CNS lymphoma** about 2%.
- **CBTRUS:** all primary CNS tumours 20.6 per 100,000 (malignant 7.3, non-malignant 13.3); commoner in females (22.3) than males (18.8).
- **IARC (2008):** worldwide malignant brain and CNS incidence 3.8 per 100,000 in males and 3.1 in females; higher in developed countries (males 5.8, females 4.4) than less developed (males 3.2, females 2.8).
- **Childhood (CBTRUS):** 5.1 per 100,000, slightly higher in males (5.2 vs 5.1).
- **Metastases** are **3 times more common** than all primary brain tumours combined (about 150,000 diagnoses a year). Leptomeningeal and spinal epidural metastases each occur in 3 to 5% of patients with systemic cancer. Commonest sources in adults: **lung, melanoma, renal cell carcinoma, breast**.
- Incidence of CNS tumours appears to have risen over 50 years (partly better imaging).

### Distribution in children

| Site | Proportion |
|---|---|
| **Infratentorial** | 43.2% |
| **Supratentorial** | 40.9% |
| Spinal cord | 4.9% |
| Multiple sites | 11% |

| Age | Predominant location and tumours |
|---|---|
| **Infants** | Supratentorial: **choroid plexus tumours, teratomas** |
| **1 to 10 years** | **Infratentorial**: juvenile pilocytic astrocytoma, medulloblastoma |
| **Over 10 years** | Supratentorial: **diffuse astrocytomas** |

Tumours of the **optic pathway/hypothalamus, brainstem and pineal-midbrain** region are commoner in children and adolescents than in adults.

## 2. Risk factors

- No cause is found in most primary tumours.
- **Established:** **ionising radiation** (meningiomas, gliomas, schwannomas) and **immunosuppression** (primary CNS lymphoma).
- **Unproven:** mobile phone and electromagnetic field exposure, head injury, N-nitroso compounds in food, occupational exposures.
- A small minority have a **family history**, some within genetic syndromes:

| Syndrome | Associated tumour |
|---|---|
| **NF1** (von Recklinghausen) | Optic pathway glioma, spinal nerve root schwannomas |
| **NF2** (gene deletion on chromosome 22) | **Bilateral vestibular schwannomas**, meningiomas |
| **Von Hippel-Lindau (VHL)** | Capillary haemangioblastoma |
| **Tuberous sclerosis (TSC)** | Subependymal giant cell astrocytoma |
| **Li-Fraumeni** | Gliomas, choroid plexus carcinoma |
| Cowden disease, Turcot syndrome, naevoid basal cell carcinoma (Gorlin) syndrome | Various CNS tumours (Turcot and Gorlin: medulloblastoma) |

## 3. Classification

### By tissue of origin

1. Tumours of neuroepithelial tissue
2. Tumours of cranial and paraspinal nerves
3. Tumours of the meninges
4. Tumours of the haematopoietic system
5. Germ cell tumours
6. Tumours of the sellar region
7. Metastatic tumours

### By behaviour

- **Intrinsic "malignant" tumours** (arise within brain tissue): gliomas, embryonal tumours, ependymoma.
- **Extrinsic "benign" tumours** (arise outside brain and compress it): meningioma, schwannoma, pituitary tumours, craniopharyngioma.

### By compartment

| Supratentorial | Infratentorial |
|---|---|
| Choroid plexus tumours, teratoma, astrocytoma | **Medulloblastoma**, cerebellar astrocytoma, **ependymoma**; (craniopharyngioma is suprasellar) |

> **Remember:** "benign" extrinsic tumours can still kill by raised ICP or by compressing vital structures.

## 4. Intrinsic tumours

### 4.1 Gliomas

- Make up about **80% of primary CNS malignancies**: astrocytomas and oligodendrogliomas.
- Graded by light microscopy; **diffusely infiltrative**, invading brain parenchyma.
- **Astrocytes** nourish neurons and help repair damage. **Oligodendrocytes** make myelin.

**WHO grading of astrocytomas**

| Grade | Tumour | Group |
|---|---|---|
| I | **Pilocytic astrocytoma**, subependymal giant cell astrocytoma | Low grade |
| II | Diffuse astrocytoma | Low grade |
| III | Anaplastic (malignant) astrocytoma | High grade |
| IV | **Glioblastoma multiforme** | High grade |

- **Low grade** gliomas are commonest in children; the classic one is **juvenile pilocytic astrocytoma of the cerebellum**.
- **Anaplastic astrocytoma (III):** about 15 to 20% of high-grade astrocytomas; usually in the fourth and fifth decades, variably enhancing.
- **Glioblastoma (IV):** the majority of high-grade astrocytomas, about 50% of astrocytomas, and the commonest primary malignant brain tumour; usually in the sixth and seventh decades with headache, seizures or focal deficits; highly infiltrative.
- **Gliomatosis cerebri:** highly infiltrating, **non-enhancing** tumour involving more than two lobes; does not meet criteria for glioblastoma but behaves as aggressively with a similar poor outcome.

**Oligodendroglioma**

- About 15 to 20% of gliomas. Grade II (well differentiated) or grade III (anaplastic).
- **Mixed oligoastrocytomas** (grade II, or grade III anaplastic).
- May spread through CSF pathways but rarely outside the CNS.
- Grade II tumours have a **better prognosis than pure astrocytic tumours**; anaplastic forms present in the fourth and fifth decades and **respond better to treatment than grade III astrocytomas**.

### 4.2 Ependymoma

- From **ependymal cells** lining the ventricles.
- About **5% of childhood tumours**; often arise from the **wall of the fourth ventricle** (posterior fossa).
- In adults, commoner in the spine, especially the **filum terminale** (myxopapillary histology).
- **Subependymomas** are slow-growing benign ventricular lesions that often need no treatment.

### 4.3 Choroid plexus tumours

- 2 to 4% of childhood CNS tumours; **the commonest CNS tumour in children under 1 year** (10 to 20% of infant CNS tumours).
- Intraventricular papillary tumours of choroid plexus epithelium, mostly in the **lateral ventricles** (supratentorial).
- Present with **raised ICP**; infants may have **macrocephaly** and focal deficits (overproduction of CSF and obstruction cause hydrocephalus).
- Variants: **choroid plexus papilloma** (WHO I, commonest, well circumscribed, resembles normal choroid plexus), **atypical papilloma**, and **choroid plexus carcinoma** (WHO III: nuclear pleomorphism, high mitotic index, high cell density, seeds along CSF pathways).
- Associated with **Li-Fraumeni syndrome**; **SV40** may play a role.

### 4.4 Primary CNS lymphoma (PCNSL)

- Rare non-Hodgkin lymphoma; **under 3%** of primary brain tumours.
- Immunocompetent patients: usually **diffuse large B-cell**; older (median 60 years).
- Immunocompromised (**HIV**, transplant recipients): large-cell immunoblastic, more aggressive; typically **CD4 < 50/mL**; median age 31 years; **EBV** frequently involved in pathogenesis.
- In Kenya, think of it in an HIV-positive patient with ring-enhancing lesions (differential: toxoplasmosis).

### 4.5 Embryonal tumours (PNET)

- The **commonest group of malignant CNS tumours of childhood** (20 to 25% of paediatric CNS tumours).
- Can **metastasise along the neuraxis** (CSF seeding).
- Includes **medulloblastoma**, supratentorial PNET, ependymoblastoma, medulloepithelioblastoma and **atypical teratoid/rhabdoid tumour (ATRT)**; all **WHO grade IV**.
- **Medulloblastoma:** commonest malignant childhood brain tumour (about 20% of primary paediatric CNS tumours); arises from **granule cell progenitors** or multipotent progenitors of the ventricular zone. Typically a midline cerebellar (vermis) mass that blocks the fourth ventricle.

### 4.6 Pineal region tumours

Many types: **pineocytoma, benign teratoma, germinoma, pineoblastoma, malignant germ cell tumours**. They present with **Parinaud syndrome** and hydrocephalus. Germ cell tumours may raise serum/CSF **hCG and AFP**.

## 5. Extrinsic ("benign") tumours

| Tumour | Key points |
|---|---|
| **Meningioma** | From **arachnoidal cap cells** of the dura. Usually over cerebral convexities, near the **sagittal sinus**; also skull base and spine. **WHO grade I** (benign), **II** (atypical), **III** (malignant). Often incidental. **MRI: dural-based with a dural tail**, compresses but does not invade the brain. Main differential: **dural metastasis** (or dural lymphoma) |
| **Schwannoma** | From Schwann cells. **Vestibular schwannoma (acoustic neuroma)** from the vestibular part of CN VIII, about 9% of primary brain tumours. **NF2: frequently bilateral.** Trigeminal schwannomas are far rarer. **NF1** is linked to spinal nerve root schwannomas |
| **Pituitary tumours** | About 9% of primary brain tumours. **Functioning** (usually microadenomas < 1 cm): acromegaly (GH), Cushing disease (ACTH), galactorrhoea, amenorrhoea and infertility (prolactin). **Non-functioning** (usually macroadenomas > 1 cm): headache, **bitemporal hemianopia**, hypopituitarism by mass effect |
| **Craniopharyngioma** | Rare, usually **suprasellar**, partly **calcified**, solid or cystic, from **Rathke's pouch** remnants. **Bimodal age** (children and 55 to 65 years). Children: headache, visual impairment, **growth failure**; adults: hypopituitarism |
| **Others** | Dermoid cysts, epidermoid cysts, colloid cysts, dysembryoplastic neuroepithelial tumours (DNT) |

> **Exam tip:** calcified suprasellar mass in a child with short stature and bitemporal hemianopia = craniopharyngioma.

## 6. Secondary (metastatic) brain tumours

- Spread is **haematogenous** from the primary site.
- **Common sources:** lung, breast, GIT malignancies, melanoma, germ cell tumours, thyroid. **Rare:** prostate, ovary, Hodgkin lymphoma.
- **In children:** **sarcomas, neuroblastoma and germ cell tumours** are the commonest sources.
- Meningeal spread from brain tumours or leukaemia mimics infratentorial tumour symptoms.

## 7. Clinical features

Depend on **tumour location, type and the child's age**. In young children diagnosis is often delayed because symptoms mimic common illnesses. Hydrocephalus may cause **macrocephaly**; personality change can occur.

| Location | Features |
|---|---|
| **Midline / infratentorial** | Classic triad: **headache, nausea/vomiting, papilloedema**. Disorders of **equilibrium, gait and coordination** (ataxia); blurred vision, diplopia, nystagmus; **torticollis** with cerebellar tonsil herniation |
| **Brainstem** | Gaze palsy, cranial nerve palsies, upper motor neuron signs (**hemiparesis, hyperreflexia, clonus**) |
| **Supratentorial** | Focal: **motor weakness, sensory change, speech disorders, seizures**, reflex changes. Infants may show early **hand preference** |
| **Optic pathway** | Reduced acuity, **Marcus Gunn pupil** (relative afferent pupillary defect), nystagmus, visual field defects |
| **Suprasellar / third ventricle** | **Diabetes insipidus**, hypothyroidism and other neuroendocrine deficits |
| **Hypothalamic (infants)** | **Diencephalic syndrome**: failure to thrive, emaciation, **increased appetite**, euphoric affect |
| **Pineal region** | **Parinaud syndrome**: (1) paresis of upward gaze, (2) pupils dilated, reacting to accommodation but not light, (3) convergence-retraction nystagmus, (4) eyelid retraction |
| **Spinal cord tumour or drop metastases** | Long tract motor and sensory deficits, bowel and bladder dysfunction, back or radicular pain |

> **Remember:** early morning headache and vomiting, worse lying down or on coughing, suggest raised ICP. A child with headache plus new squint, ataxia or head tilt needs imaging.

## 8. Diagnosis

Work-up: history, examination (including fundoscopy and visual fields), laboratory tests, neuroimaging and biopsy.

- **Cranial MRI with gadolinium** is the preferred test; **CT** only if MRI is not possible.
- **Malignant tumours** enhance with gadolinium, may have central necrosis, and are surrounded by white matter oedema.
- **Low-grade gliomas** usually do not enhance and are best seen on **FLAIR**.
- **Meningiomas:** dural-based, dural tail, compress but do not invade.
- **Biopsy** gives definitive diagnosis; when a tumour is strongly suspected it can be taken as an **intra-operative frozen section** before resection.
- **Functional MRI** for pre-surgical planning (eloquent motor, sensory, language cortex). **PET** shows metabolic activity; **MR perfusion and spectroscopy** give blood flow and tissue composition.
- **Laboratory tests** are rarely helpful, except tumour markers in metastases (e.g. **hCG** from testicular cancer; **AFP and hCG** in germ cell tumours).
- **Angiography, EEG and lumbar puncture** are rarely indicated (LP is dangerous with raised ICP and a posterior fossa mass).
- Medulloblastoma, ependymoma and germ cell tumours need **spine MRI and CSF cytology** (after surgery) to stage for dissemination.

## 9. Treatment

Depends on **type, location and stage**. Options are **surgery, radiotherapy and chemotherapy; a combination is more effective**.

- **Supportive:** dexamethasone for peritumoural oedema, anticonvulsants for seizures, CSF diversion (shunt or endoscopic third ventriculostomy) for hydrocephalus.
- **Surgery:** maximal safe resection; the first step for most (and gives tissue).
- **Radiotherapy:** avoided or delayed in very young children because of severe neurocognitive and endocrine effects.
- **Chemotherapy:** used in medulloblastoma, high-grade glioma and infant tumours.
- Long-term follow-up for endocrine, growth, cognitive and second-tumour effects.

## 10. Prognosis

| Measure | Figure (SEER, 1995 to 2009 data) |
|---|---|
| 5-year relative survival, all primary malignant brain/CNS tumours | **33.8%** (males 32.4%, females 35.5%) |
| Age 0 to 19 years | **73.0%** |
| Age 20 to 44 years | 57.7% |
| Age 45 to 54 years | 31.7% |
| Age 55 to 64 years | 17.6% |
| Age 65 to 74 years | 10.0% |
| Age 75 or older | 5.7% |

- **Mortality (USA):** an estimated 13,700 deaths from primary malignant brain and CNS tumours in 2012.
- **Lifetime risk:** males 0.7% of diagnosis and 0.5% of death; females 0.6% and 0.4%.

Survival falls steeply with age; paediatric survival is much better, but late effects are common among survivors.

## Key points to revise

1. Childhood brain tumours: pilocytic astrocytoma, medulloblastoma/PNET, diffuse astrocytoma, ependymoma, craniopharyngioma make up 80%.
2. Infants supratentorial, 1 to 10 years infratentorial, over 10 years supratentorial.
3. Medulloblastoma is the commonest malignant childhood brain tumour; PNET are grade IV and seed along the CSF.
4. Headache, vomiting and papilloedema mean raised ICP; ataxia means infratentorial; seizures and focal deficits mean supratentorial.
5. Parinaud syndrome = pineal region; diencephalic syndrome = hypothalamic tumour in infants; bitemporal hemianopia = pituitary/suprasellar.
6. Risk factors: radiation, immunosuppression, NF1, NF2, VHL, TSC, Li-Fraumeni.
7. MRI with gadolinium is the investigation of choice; biopsy confirms.
8. Treatment is surgery plus radiotherapy and chemotherapy; paediatric 5-year survival 73%.
