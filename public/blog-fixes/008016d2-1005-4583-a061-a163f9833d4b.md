## STREPTOCOCCUS — CLASSIFICATION
- Classified primarily by **haemolytic properties**: alpha (partial/green haemolysis), beta (complete haemolysis), non-haemolytic (gamma)
- This — not Lancefield grouping — is the single most important method for classifying/speciating streptococci (tested directly)
- *Streptococcus pneumoniae* (pneumococci) — alpha-hemolytic, can be serotyped based on their **polysaccharide capsule**
- Enterococci — group D streptococci, classified by ability to grow in **6.5% sodium chloride** and **45% bile**; most strains sensitive to penicillin, sulfonamides, cephalosporins, gentamicin, streptomycin (NOT resistant, as a distractor claims)
- Viridans streptococci — alpha-hemolytic; **NOT** identified by Lancefield grouping (that's the wrong statement in the "except" question) — Lancefield grouping is based on the C-carbohydrate in the cell wall and applies to other groupable streptococci
- Pneumococci vs viridans streptococci — both alpha-hemolytic but differentiated by **bile solubility test** and **susceptibility to optochin** (pneumococci are bile-soluble and optochin-sensitive; viridans strep are not)
- Group B Streptococcus (*S. agalactiae*) — part of normal vaginal flora, but can cause **neonatal meningitis** — a key exception to "normal flora doesn't cause disease"
**STREPTOCOCCUS PYOGENES — VIRULENCE FACTORS AND PATHOGENESIS (5–20 marks, appears repeatedly)**
- **Toxins:**
 - Streptolysin O and S (hemolysins) — responsible for hemolysis and leukocidal activity (kill white blood cells)
 - Pyrogenic/erythrogenic toxin (scarlet fever toxin) — a superantigen, causes the scarlet fever rash and anti-inflammatory/immune-inhibiting effects
- **Enzymes (spreading factors):**
 - Streptokinase — promotes lysis of fibrin by converting plasminogen to plasmin
 - DNase (streptodornase) — depolymerizes DNA, liquefying pus
 - Hyaluronidase — breaks down hyaluronic acid in connective tissue, aiding spread through tissue
 - Lipase — degrades lipids/phospholipids on host cell membranes
- **Cell wall components:**
 - Capsule — antiphagocytic (protects from being engulfed by phagocytes)
 - M protein — major virulence factor, gives rigidity, resists phagocytosis, and is the antigen basis for immune classification (also key in rheumatic fever pathogenesis via molecular mimicry)
 - Pili — mediate attachment to host cells
- **Clinical complications caused by *S. pyogenes*:** pharyngitis, scarlet fever, rheumatic fever — "all of the above" is the typical MCQ answer
- **Complication of streptococcal pharyngitis specifically:** rheumatic fever (not pseudomembrane formation, not meningococcemia — those belong to other organisms)
**RHEUMATIC FEVER vs ACUTE GLOMERULONEPHRITIS (post-streptococcal complications — 10 mark comparison, repeated)**
- Site of infection that precedes each:
 - Glomerulonephritis — can follow either **skin or throat** infection
 - Rheumatic fever — follows **throat** infection only
- Latent period before onset:
 - Glomerulonephritis — 1–3 weeks
 - Rheumatic fever — 2–5 weeks
- Prior sensitization needed:
 - Glomerulonephritis — not essential
 - Rheumatic fever — essential
- Repeated attacks:
 - Glomerulonephritis — absent; one heals completely, doesn't recur from the same episode pattern
 - Rheumatic fever — repeated attacks are common
- Serotypes responsible:
 - Glomerulonephritis — nephritogenic strains, associated with skin and throat serotypes
 - Rheumatic fever — associated with throat serotypes specifically
- Clinical manifestation:
 - Glomerulonephritis — hematuria, albuminuria, edema; pathology at the base of the glomerulus
 - Rheumatic fever — swelling of joints, inflammation of heart muscle (myocarditis, pericarditis, endocarditis)
- Pathogenesis:
 - Glomerulonephritis — antibody-antigen immune complex deposition at the glomerulus (type III hypersensitivity)
 - Rheumatic fever — antibody against the streptococcal antigen cross-reacts with heart tissue antigen (molecular mimicry, type II hypersensitivity)

## STAPHYLOCOCCUS
- Coagulase test — differentiates *S. aureus* (coagulase-positive) from *S. epidermidis* (coagulase-negative)
- Nose is the most likely source of *S. aureus* in an outbreak of neonatal nursery sepsis (normal flora reservoir)
- Toxin-mediated diseases from *S. aureus*:
 - Scalded skin syndrome — caused by exfoliative toxin (exfoliatin)
 - Toxic shock syndrome — caused by TSST-1 (a superantigen); classic scenario: nasal tampon left in place post-surgery, fever, hypotension, diffuse erythematous rash including palms/soles, elevated liver enzymes, renal failure
 - Food poisoning (food intoxication, not infection) — caused by a preformed **enterotoxin** in food; rapid onset (~2 hours), vomiting-predominant
- Biofilm-related infection (e.g., long-term central venous catheter with *S. epidermidis*):
 - The complex molecular interactions within the biofilm make it difficult to provide effective antimicrobial therapy — catheter often needs removal to cure infection
 - Biofilm does NOT wash off easily, and bacteria in it are NOT more susceptible to antibiotics from slowed metabolism (both are wrong distractors)
- Associated with formation of a bacterial biofilm: classic example is airway colonization in a cystic fibrosis patient with a mucoid (alginate-producing) strain of ***Pseudomonas aeruginosa*** (this exact scenario is used to test biofilm concept generally, not just Staph)

## CLOSTRIDIUM SPECIES
## Clostridium tetani (tetanus)
- Spores germinate in dirty, neglected wounds with some tissue necrosis
- Produces **tetanospasmin** (tetanus toxin)
- Mechanism of action: toxin binds to gangliosides at the neuromuscular junction, is taken up and transported retrograde along the axon to the CNS, then travels via **cranial nerve/cranial pathway** transsynaptically to reach the spinal cord/brainstem
- In the CNS, the toxin **blocks release of inhibitory neurotransmitters** (glycine and GAB
A) from inhibitory interneurons
- Loss of inhibitory signals → unopposed, sustained muscle contraction → **spastic/rigid paralysis** (opposite mechanism to botulism, which causes flaccid paralysis)
- Clinical picture: trismus (lockjaw), facial muscle spasms, inability to open mouth, generalized rigidity
- Necrotic tissue significance: necrotic/anaerobic tissue provides the low-oxygen environment *
C. tetani* spores need to germinate and produce toxin
- How infection is acquired: through a contaminated wound (e.g., stepping on a nail, dirty cut) that creates an anaerobic pocket
- Treatment: tetanus antitoxin (passive immunization) plus antibiotics such as **erythromycin or tetracycline** (metronidazole/penicillin also acceptable), plus wound debridement
- Should treatment wait for lab confirmation? — **No**; treatment should be started immediately on clinical suspicion because tetanus is rapidly fatal and lab confirmation takes too long
- Prevention: **tetanus toxoid immunization** induces effective protection (note: antitoxin against tetanus does NOT cross-protect against botulism — they are different toxins with different antigenic sites, a common wrong-answer trap)
## Clostridium botulinum (botulism)
- Toxin ingested (preformed in improperly canned/preserved food) — classic scenario involves home-canned goods
- Mechanism: toxin is absorbed from the gut, enters the bloodstream, and blocks **release of acetylcholine** at the neuromuscular junction (presynaptic)
- This causes **flaccid paralysis** (opposite of tetanus)
- Treatment for ingested botulism toxin: large doses of **antitoxin** must be administered (not antibiotics — the disease is toxin-mediated, not an ongoing bacterial infection at that stage)
- Type of exotoxin most commonly associated with human botulism disease: **Type A and Type C** (from the exam: "A and C" was the marked correct answer)
## Clostridium perfringens
- Causes **gas gangrene** (myonecrosis) and food poisoning
- Produces an **enterotoxin**
- Produces a **double zone of beta-hemolysis** on blood agar (this is a classic identifying lab feature)
- Some strains are aerotolerant, though the organism is fundamentally anaerobic and **multiplies in foods only under anaerobic conditions**
- It is **NOT** the most common cause of antibiotic-associated diarrhea (that's *
C. difficile* — this is the "EXCEPT" trap in that question)
- Classic clinical scenario: wound with gas felt in the tissue (crepitus), blackened/necrotic skin, severe pain, fever — gram-positive rod on gram stain of exudate
- Toxin mechanism: alpha-toxin is a **lecithinase** that degrades cell membranes, causing tissue necrosis and hemolysis; the lecithinase specifically does NOT cause diarrhea (that's a distractor — the enterotoxin, a separate protein, causes the diarrheal illness)
- This organism contaminates both meats AND vegetables and is the sporeformer responsible for gas gangrene specifically among the options given
## General Clostridium immunization fact
- Antitoxin against tetanus does NOT also protect against botulism, even though both are clostridial toxins — they have different antigenic sites and require separate antitoxins
- Immunization with **tetanus toxoid** induces effective protection specifically against tetanus toxin

## CORYNEBACTERIUM DIPHTHERIAE (Diphtheria)
- Causes a **pseudomembrane** (grayish exudate) over the tonsils/pharynx — classic presentation in an 8-year-old with severe sore throat
- Organism: **club-shaped, gram-positive bacillus**, sometimes described as showing metachromatic (Babes-Ernst) granules
- Diphtheria toxin mechanism:
 - The **A subunit** causes **ADP-ribosylation of elongation factor 2 (EF-2)**
 - This **inhibits protein synthesis** in host cells → cell death, and formation of the pseudomembrane
 - It does NOT form pores in RBCs, degrade lecithin, or increase cAMP (all wrong distractors used repeatedly)
- Elek's test — used to test for **toxin/antitoxin reaction** (confirms toxin production by the organism); a strip of filter paper impregnated with diphtheria antitoxin is placed on blood agar, the organism is streaked at right angles to it, and after incubation a line of precipitation forms where toxin meets antitoxin, confirming a toxigenic strain
- Diagnosis of diphtheria is confirmed by **demonstration of toxin production** by a suspicious isolate (i.e., Elek's test) — NOT simply by microscopy or culture alone

## MYCOBACTERIUM TUBERCULOSIS
- Route of entry: inhalation of aerosols/droplets or dust particles containing tubercle bacilli
- Pathogenesis:
 - Bacilli lodge in the lungs, engulfed by alveolar macrophages
 - Replicate inside macrophages using virulence factors: **mycolic acid, mycosides, lipoarabinomannan, trehalose dimycolate (cord factor)**
 - This causes death of macrophages, forming an inflammatory lesion called the **Ghon focus**
 - Activated macrophages form a **granuloma** around the primary infection site to contain it
 - Bacilli also spread to adjacent lymph nodes; dendritic cells present the bacilli to T cells, causing T cell activation and cytokine release, which activates further immune cells
- Cell wall protective factors:
 - Bacterial inhibition of phagosome-lysosome fusion, and interference with endosomal acidification — this is how the bacterium **survives after being ingested by macrophages** (correct answer over complement inhibition, poor immunogenicity, or oxidative resistance)
- Types of disease caused: pulmonary TB, miliary TB, bone/joint TB, renal/urogenital TB, tuberculous meningitis, tuberculous lymphadenitis
- Diagnosis: sputum for **acid-fast staining** (Ziehl-Neelsen), viewed under microscope to demonstrate the organism
- Mantoux (tuberculin) test — used to identify **TB exposure** (not active disease); a positive result (induration) means the person was previously exposed to the organism, not necessarily that they have active disease
- *M. tuberculosis* is classified as a **true pathogen** — never considered part of normal flora, unlike *
E. coli* or *S. aureus*
- Why penicillin doesn't work against Mycobacterium: **the cell wall of these organisms is not affected by penicillin** (high mycolic acid content makes the cell wall impermeable to many antibiotics)
- Classic clinical vignette clue: plant nursery worker (soil exposure), chronic cough, weight loss, bilateral upper lobe cavitary infiltrates, acid-fast bacilli on sputum → acquired by **inhaling aerosolized droplets**, not from soil contact directly (the soil-exposure detail is a distractor pointing toward a different organism in some vignettes, but for classic TB the answer is always inhalation)

## NEISSERIA GONORRHOEAE
- Gram-negative diplococci — classic finding in urethral discharge (kidney-bean shaped, seen within neutrophils)
- Classic vignette: young man with penile discharge, dysuria, gram-negative diplococci on Gram stain → gonorrhea
- Antibiotics used: **ceftriaxone/rifampin/azithromycin type regimens** (dual therapy is standard due to resistance concerns; exam answer key noted rifampin/azithromycin in one instance — go with current dual-therapy logic but know ceftriaxone is the backbone)
- Complications:
 - In men: can ascend to cause epididymitis
 - In women: Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID) → can lead to **infertility** (the classic sequela tested)
 - Salpingitis is associated with **gonorrhea** and can lead to **sterility**
 - Ophthalmia neonatorum is associated with **gonorrhea AND chlamydia** (two organisms, both must be known as the answer)
- Why partners must be treated even if asymptomatic: prevents reinfection and prevents complications like PID/infertility in the partner, even without symptoms
- Gonococcal conjunctivitis of newborns is now rare because of **silver nitrate or erythromycin prophylaxis** given at birth
- Definitive diagnosis is NOT by detecting antibodies in serum (this is the wrong/EXCEPT answer) — diagnosis is by culture (Thayer-Martin agar) or Gram stain/NAAT, not serology

## CHLAMYDIA TRACHOMATIS
- Causes: PID, ophthalmia (with gonorrhea), trachoma (via mechanical vector *Musca sorbens* — see parasitology notes)
- Life cycle (asked as a 6-mark essay) — obligate intracellular organism with two forms:
 - **Elementary body (EB)** — the infectious, metabolically inactive form; enters the host cell by endocytosis
 - Inside the cell, EB converts into the **reticulate body (RB)** — the metabolically active, replicating form
 - RB divides repeatedly by binary fission inside a vacuole (inclusion body)
 - RBs eventually condense back into infectious EBs
 - The host cell ruptures, releasing new EBs to infect adjacent cells — cycle repeats
- Diagnosis of PID caused by Chlamydia: tissue culture inoculum is used because Chlamydia is an obligate intracellular organism and cannot be grown on standard bacterial media — it needs living host cells
- Staining shows **cell inclusions** — this is the classic clue pointing to Chlamydia in a PID case with a "single sexual partner and condom use" history (patient may not realize partner-transmitted infections can persist/recur)

**TREPONEMA PALLIDUM (Syphilis) vs HAEMOPHILUS DUCREYI (Chancroid) — comparison, repeated 5–10 marks**
- **Similarities:**
 - Both originate as pustules at the site of inoculation, progressing to ulcerated lesions
 - Both are caused by sexually transmitted organisms
 - Both typically appear on the genitals of infected individuals
 - Both can present with multiple sites/multiple lesions
- **Differences:**
| Feature | Syphilitic chancre | Chancroid |
|---|---|---|
| Organism | *Treponema pallidum* | *Haemophilus ducreyi* |
| Pain | Painless | Painful |
| Exudate | Non-exudative | Soft, green-yellow purulent exudate |
| Edge | Hard/indurated edge | Soft edge |
| Healing | Heals spontaneously in 3–6 weeks | Does NOT resolve spontaneously |
| Extragenital sites | Chancre can involve extragenital sites | Mostly confined to genitals |
- **Syphilis clinical stages (5 marks, repeated as its own short note):**
 - Primary stage — painless chancre at inoculation site, heals spontaneously
 - Secondary stage — characterized by widespread skin lesions, including involvement of palms and soles, with desquamation; lesions frequently ulcerate
 - Tertiary stage — widespread bone, joint, and soft tissue destruction; extensive destruction of bone/cartilage (e.g., gummas, saddle nose)
- Serology interpretation: non-treponemal test (RPR) non-reactive + treponemal test (TP-P
A) reactive in a pregnant woman previously treated for syphilis → indicates past treated infection, **but the baby is still considered at risk for congenital syphilis** and must be evaluated (this exact scenario has appeared)

## HELICOBACTER PYLORI
- Survives the acidic stomach environment via multiple mechanisms (5-mark short note, repeated):
 - **Urease enzyme** — converts urea to ammonia and CO2, neutralizing acid locally around the organism
 - **Flagella** — allow motility to burrow through the mucus layer toward the less acidic gastric epithelium, escaping the most acidic luminal environment
 - **Adhesins** — allow attachment to gastric epithelial cells, resisting being washed away
 - Ability to sense the pH gradient and swim toward more neutral pH
 - Produces urease-generated ammonia which also helps neutralize local acid and can damage epithelium
- Urease specifically neutralizes stomach acid (HCl) — this is a direct fill-in-the-blank MCQ answer

## VIBRIO CHOLERAE
- Causes profuse "rice water" watery diarrhea — classic vignette: 15-year-old girl, >1L diarrhea in 90 minutes, no fever
- Toxin mechanism: cholera toxin has A and B subunits; the toxin **increases intracellular cAMP** (yields increase in intracellular cAMP levels) — this drives massive chloride/water secretion into the gut lumen, NOT cleavage of AMP, NOT cleaving SNARE (that's botulinum's mechanism), NOT blocking EF-1
- Cholera toxin is **not** attached to the flagella (a wrong distractor) — it's a separately secreted AB toxin
- Most important treatment step: **fluid and electrolyte replacement** — this takes priority over antibiotics, antitoxin, or even confirming the exact organism first, because the mortality risk is from dehydration
- Classic outbreak vignette: airline passengers from Peru who ate crab salad, two developed severe watery diarrhea after landing — likely cause: **Vibrio cholerae O139** (crab/seafood-associated, lipopolysaccharide O antigen 139)

## ESCHERICHIA COLI
- UTI pathogenesis: **P-fimbriae (P-pili)** are the virulence factor associated with ability to cause urinary tract infection, specifically in patients with the **P blood group antigen** (fimbriae bind to a receptor related to this blood group)
- *
E. coli* O157:H7 — associated with **lipopolysaccharide O antigen 157, flagellar antigen H7**; a cause of hemorrhagic colitis (Shiga-toxin producing strain), not typically the answer for classic cholera-like outbreaks (see Vibrio above, where O157:H7 was the wrong answer in favor of *V. cholerae* O139)
- LPS (lipopolysaccharide) — part of the cell wall of *
E. coli*; interacts with macrophages and monocytes, triggering **release of cytokines** — this is the toxic mechanism of endotoxin generally (correct statement across multiple repeated MCQs); LPS does NOT form pores in RBCs, does NOT cause hypothermia, does NOT cause paralysis (all wrong distractors)
- Verotoxin of *
E. coli* is similar to **Shiga toxin** (both inhibit protein synthesis by attacking the ribosome, structurally/functionally related toxins)

## LISTERIA MONOCYTOGENES
- Pathogenesis:
 - Enters host cells by phagocytosis
 - Produces **listeriolysin O**, which damages the phagosomal membrane, allowing the bacterium to escape into the cytoplasm
 - Grows and multiplies **intracellularly** (in the cytoplasm)
 - Produces actin-based motility proteins that help it spread **cell-to-cell** without ever being exposed to the extracellular environment/antibodies (this is the correct mechanism — NOT simply "produces an enzyme that helps spread cell to cell" in a vague sense; it specifically hijacks host actin polymerization)
- Clinical relevance: can cross the placenta, causing miscarriage or neonatal meningitis; also causes meningitis in immunocompromised adults

## BACILLUS ANTHRACIS
- First microorganism to satisfy Koch's postulates (late 19th century)
- Vaccination facts: current vaccine is well tolerated but not routinely given to the general public; recombinant vaccine trials undergoing evaluation
- Woolsorter's disease = **inhalational anthrax** (occupational exposure to contaminated wool/animal hair)
- Cutaneous anthrax — classic black eschar lesion

## GENERAL BACTERIOLOGY CONCEPTS (recurring across all papers)
*Normal flora vs true pathogens*
- Organisms **never** considered normal flora, always pathogens when found: *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* (the repeated correct answer over *S. pneumoniae,
E. coli, S. aureus, N. meningitidis*, which CAN be part of normal/carrier flora in some contexts)
*Lipopolysaccharide (LPS/endotoxin)*
- Part of the cell wall of gram-negative bacteria like *
E. coli*
- Interacts with macrophages/monocytes → cytokine release (correct, repeated statement)
- Toxic component is **lipid A**, not the O side chain (the O side chain is the antigenic/serotyping portion, not the toxic part) — watch for this distinction if asked
*Type III secretion systems*
- Directly inject bacterial proteins into host cells across both bacterial and host cell membranes, promoting pathogenesis of infection — this is the correct mechanism (not simply "release into extracellular environment," which describes a different secretion type)
*Biofilms*
- Complex molecular interactions within a biofilm make it difficult to treat with antimicrobials effectively — often require device/catheter removal to cure
- Classic biofilm example: mucoid alginate-producing *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* in cystic fibrosis airway colonization
*Bacterial growth and resistance*
- Secondary metabolites (including some antibiotics produced by bacteria themselves) are formed during the **stationary phase** of bacterial growth
- Microbial resistance to drugs is acquired through **conjugation, transformation, and transduction** — "all of the above" is the typical correct answer
*Gastrointestinal/food safety organisms*
- *Bacillus cereus* — spore-forming, motile rod causing foodborne illness in diarrheal or emetic form (classic vignette: reheated fried rice)
- Whipple disease — caused by *Tropheryma whipplei*; diagnosis confirmed by **PCR amplification and sequencing** of an appropriate DNA segment (not culture, since it's difficult to culture)
*Miscellaneous clinical vignette organisms (each tested once, know the pairing)*
- Nocardia/Actinomyces — branching, partially acid-fast gram-positive rods; classic vignette is a breast cancer patient with pulmonary nodules and brain lesions → ***Nocardia*** (partially acid-fast distinguishes it from *Actinomyces*, which is NOT acid-fast); treated with **trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole**
- Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae — gram-positive rod causing violaceous nodular skin lesions after a fishhook injury (fish handling exposure), forms long filaments, causes greenish discoloration of agar
- Corynebacterium urealyticum — short, irregular gram-positive rod causing UTI with struvite/bladder stones in a catheterized patient
- Mycoplasma pneumoniae — treated with tetracycline or erythromycin (no cell wall, so beta-lactams don't work)
- Gardnerella vaginalis — bacterial vaginosis; thin gray-white discharge, pH >4.5, clue cells; the organism **decreased** in bacterial vaginosis is **Lactobacillus species** (normal flora that's lost)
- Whooping cough (*Bordetella pertussis*) — paroxysm (rapid-fire cough) is characteristic of pertussis; disease stages: catarrhal (mild, runny nose) → paroxysmal (severe cough, apnea/vomiting) → convalescent (recovery); pertussis toxin is an A-B toxin causing tissue damage/inflammation; adhesin (filamentous hemagglutinin) mediates attachment to upper respiratory epithelium
- Brain abscess 1 month after dental extraction — most likely organism: **anaerobic streptococci**

## NEISSERIA MENINGITIDIS
- Portal of entry: **inhalation** (droplet spread person-to-person)
- Attachment: organism attaches to nasopharyngeal epithelial cells with the help of **pili**
- Can exist as **part of normal flora** in the nasopharynx without causing symptoms (asymptomatic carriage is common)
- From the nasopharynx, the organism can invade the bloodstream, producing symptoms — this progression from upper respiratory tract colonization to bloodstream invasion is the key pathogenic step
- Once in blood → **meningococcemia**
- **Fulminant meningococcemia** — the severe form — presents with:
 - High fever
 - Hemorrhagic (petechial) rash
 - Intravascular coagulation (DIC)
 - Circulatory collapse
- During meningococcemia, there is thrombosis of small blood vessels, perivascular infiltration, and petechial hemorrhage
- Major virulence factor: **capsular polysaccharide** — serogroups A, B, C, D, X, Y, Z, W135, 29E (these letters/numbers are the actual serogroup names, occasionally asked to identify)
- Epidemic waves specifically associated with **mass gatherings, religious pilgrims, sub-Saharan Africa** (the "meningitis belt")
- Once organism reaches the meninges: meninges become acutely inflamed, with thrombosis of blood vessels and exudation of polymorphonuclear leukocytes — so the surface of the brain becomes covered with thick purulent exudate
- Clinical onset: usually begins with intense headache, vomiting, stiff neck (classic meningeal signs), progressing to coma within a few hours in severe cases
- Waterhouse-Friderichsen syndrome — bilateral adrenal hemorrhage/failure specifically associated with fulminant meningococcemia (this is a distractor option in some questions asking about strep pharyngitis complications — it belongs to *N. meningitidis*, NOT *S. pyogenes*)

**CAUSES OF BACTERIAL MENINGITIS BY ORGANISM (20-mark essay — "discuss the major causes of bacterial meningitis")**
- **Neisseria meningitidis** — as detailed above; a major cause across all age groups, especially adolescents/young adults, epidemic potential
- **Streptococcus pneumoniae** — most important cause of acute bacterial meningitis in adults generally
- **Haemophilus influenzae** — historically a major cause in children before vaccination programs
- **Streptococcus agalactiae (Group B Streptococcus)** — the **most common cause of neonatal meningitis** in the developed world/West; part of normal vaginal flora, transmitted to the newborn during delivery
- **Listeria monocytogenes** — important cause in:
 - Neonates (can cross the placenta during pregnancy, causing miscarriage or premature delivery; newborns infected during delivery can develop acute meningitis)
 - Immunocompromised adults (adults with weakened immunity are also at risk)
- **Escherichia coli** — also a cause, particularly in neonates (has K1 capsular antigen associated with neonatal meningitis specifically)
- **Mycobacterium tuberculosis** — causes **tuberculous meningitis**; tubercle bacilli reach the meninges via the bloodstream; occurs frequently in **non-immune infants and young children**

## STREPTOCOCCUS PNEUMONIAE (expanding beyond classification)
- Most common cause of **"typical" (classic, lobar) pneumonia** — correct answer over *H. influenzae, M. pneumoniae, L. pneumophila,
B. pertussis*
- Alpha-hemolytic
- Serotyped based on its **polysaccharide capsule** — the capsule is the major virulence factor, antiphagocytic
- Distinguished from viridans streptococci (also alpha-hemolytic) by:
 - **Bile solubility test** — pneumococci are bile-soluble, viridans strep are not
 - **Optochin sensitivity** — pneumococci are optochin-sensitive, viridans strep are resistant
- Important cause of bacterial meningitis in adults (see above)
- Complication of pneumococcal pharyngitis/URTI can include otitis media, sinusitis, and pneumonia itself as the progression

## SALMONELLA AND SHIGELLA
## Infective dose (ID50) comparison — repeatedly tested
- ***Shigella sonnei*** has the **lowest ID50** (as few as 10–100 organisms can cause disease) — this is the correct answer whenever compared against *Salmonella typhi, Vibrio cholerae, Campylobacter jejuni*
- *Salmonella typhi* — ID50 around 10^5 to 10^6 organisms (higher than Shigella)
- *Vibrio cholerae* — needs a much larger inoculum, around 10^6–10^11 organisms (large numbers needed because most are destroyed by stomach acid before reaching the intestine)
- Why this matters clinically: Shigella's very low infective dose explains why it spreads so easily person-to-person and in contaminated food/water even with minimal contamination
## Host defense against Salmonella-caused dysentery
- Most important host defense preventing dysentery caused by *Salmonella*: **gastric juice** (stomach acid barrier) — this is the correct answer over salivary enzymes, normal mouth flora, or alpha interferon
- This is why conditions that reduce stomach acid (e.g., antacid use, achlorhydria) increase susceptibility to Salmonella infection
**Shigella virulence factors** (if asked as a short note)
- Shiga toxin — inhibits protein synthesis by acting on the ribosome (60S subunit), causing cell death; contributes to bloody dysentery and can cause hemolytic uremic syndrome in severe cases
- Very low infective dose (as above) is itself a virulence-related factor — allows efficient person-to-person spread
- Invasion of colonic epithelial cells causing local inflammation and ulceration

## ENTEROCOCCUS (expanding beyond Streptococcus classification)
- Normal flora of the **lower intestinal tract and vagina**
- Can grow in **6.5% sodium chloride** and **45% bile** — this combination of tests is the classic identifying feature separating enterococci from other streptococci
- Can be **alpha-hemolytic or non-hemolytic** (not strictly one or the other)
- Most strains are **sensitive** to penicillin, sulfonamides, cephalosporins, gentamicin, and streptomycin — the wrong/EXCEPT-style distractor claims resistance, which is incorrect for typical strains (though clinically some enterococci do show acquired resistance, e.g., VRE — but for this exam's purposes, "sensitive" is the marked correct answer)

## PSEUDOMONAS AERUGINOSA
- Why Pseudomonas infections are difficult to treat and prevent: **the bacteria is highly resistant to detergents and antibiotics** — this is the specific marked correct answer over "metabolically versatile," "found everywhere," or "causes nosocomial infection" (those are true statements about Pseudomonas but not the reason infections are hard to treat)
- Associated with **biofilm formation** — classic example: airway colonization in a cystic fibrosis patient with a **mucoid, alginate-producing strain** of *P. aeruginosa*
- Toxin: **lecithinase** — degrades cell membranes, causes tissue damage (this is the answer for "which toxin accounts for tissue damage" when Pseudomonas is the organism in question — same enzyme class as *
C. perfringens'* alpha toxin, but a different organism context)
- Common cause of nosocomial (hospital-acquired) infections — particularly in burns, catheters, and immunocompromised patients

**TOXIC SHOCK SYNDROME vs SCALDED SKIN SYNDROME (5-mark comparison, both caused by *S. aureus* toxins)**
| Feature | Toxic Shock Syndrome (TSS) | Staphylococcal Scalded Skin Syndrome (SSSS) |
|---|---|---|
| Toxin | TSST-1 (a superantigen) | Exfoliative toxin (exfoliatin) |
| Mechanism | Superantigen causes massive, non-specific T-cell activation and cytokine release | Toxin cleaves desmoglein, causing separation within the epidermis |
| Key clinical features | High fever, hypotension (shock), diffuse erythematous rash resembling sunburn (including palms/soles), multiorgan involvement (elevated liver enzymes, renal failure) | Widespread blistering and peeling of the skin, resembling a burn/scald; usually in infants/young children; no hypotension or multiorgan failure typically |
| Classic scenario | Retained nasal tampon/foreign body, or menstruation-associated | Neonates/young children, localized staph infection triggering toxin release systemically |
| Systemic shock present? | Yes | No |
- Both are classified as **toxin-generated *S. aureus* diseases resulting from contact** — this grouping ("both B and C are correct," referring to scalded skin syndrome and toxic shock syndrome together) has been the marked answer when listed alongside purpuric fever and pseudomembranous colitis as distractors
