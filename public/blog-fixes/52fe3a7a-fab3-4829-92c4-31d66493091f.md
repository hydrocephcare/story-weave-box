## Medical Virology — Section B: Short Answer Questions
*Unit: MBMM 3300 | Medical Virology & Mycology | MBChB Year 3*

**Q: In what respect do mycoses caused by true pathogenic fungi differ from mycoses caused by fungi associated with opportunistic infections? What are the general consequences of these infections to the human host?**
**A:**
**True Pathogenic Fungi** cause disease in healthy, immunocompetent individuals. They possess intrinsic virulence factors that allow them to overcome normal host defenses. Infection is primarily by inhalation of conidia from an environmental source. Examples include *Histoplasma capsulatum*, *Coccidioides immitis*, and *Blastomyces dermatitidis*.
**Opportunistic Fungi** cause disease only in immunocompromised hosts — HIV/AIDS patients, transplant recipients, diabetics, or those on prolonged corticosteroids. They have low intrinsic virulence and rely entirely on a weakened host immune system. Examples include *Candida albicans*, *Cryptococcus neoformans*, *Aspergillus fumigatus*, and *Pneumocystis jirovecii*.
## General Consequences to the Human Host:
- Pulmonary involvement — pneumonia, cavitation, respiratory failure
- Disseminated infection spreading to the brain, liver, spleen, and adrenal glands
- Chronic granulomatous inflammation
- Severe immunological damage in susceptible individuals
- Death in untreated or severely immunocompromised patients

**Q: A child born in Muranga was vaccinated with an attenuated vaccine. Explain briefly how it is supposed to protect the child from the particular viral infection.**
**A:**
1. The attenuated (live, weakened) vaccine strain is administered and undergoes limited replication in the host without causing full disease
2. This mimics natural infection and stimulates both **humoral immunity** (B cells produce specific IgG, IgM, and IgA antibodies) and **cell-mediated immunity** (CD4+ T-helper and CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes are activated)
3. **Long-lived memory B and T cells** are generated that retain specific recognition of viral antigens
4. Upon natural exposure to the virulent pathogen, the immune system mounts a **rapid, amplified secondary immune response** before the virus can cause significant disease
5. Neutralizing antibodies block viral entry into host cells; cytotoxic T lymphocytes destroy already-infected cells
6. The child is therefore protected from the disease

## Q: Name the portal of entry of the herpes simplex viruses HSV-1 and HSV-2.
**A:**
- **HSV-1:** Oral mucosa, conjunctiva, respiratory mucosa, and breaks in skin
- **HSV-2:** Genital mucosa (primary route via sexual contact); also skin and mucous membranes
Both viruses enter through mucous membranes or broken skin, replicate locally, and then travel retrogradely along sensory nerve axons to establish latency in sensory ganglia — the trigeminal ganglion for HSV-1, and the sacral ganglia S2–S4 for HSV-2.

## Q: Mention three diseases caused by herpes simplex viruses HSV-1 and HSV-2.
**A:**
1. **Orolabial herpes (cold sores / herpes labialis)** — HSV-1; recurrent vesicular lesions on lips and perioral skin
2. **Genital herpes** — primarily HSV-2; painful genital vesicles, ulcers, and dysuria
3. **Herpes encephalitis** — HSV-1; most common cause of fatal sporadic encephalitis, involving the temporal lobe
4. **Neonatal herpes** — HSV-2, acquired during delivery; potentially fatal disseminated infection in the newborn
5. **Herpes keratitis** — corneal infection that can lead to blindness

## Q: Explain how interferon helps virus-infected cells fight back the infection.
**A:**
When a cell is infected by a virus, it detects viral nucleic acid through pattern recognition receptors (TLR3, RIG-I, MDA5), triggering the production of **Type I interferons (IFN-α and IFN-β)**.
## Mechanism of Action:
## Step 1 — Interferon Production and Secretion:
The infected cell produces and secretes interferons that act on the same cell (autocrine) and on neighbouring uninfected cells (paracrine).
## Step 2 — JAK-STAT Signalling:
Interferon binding to surface receptors activates the JAK-STAT pathway, driving transcription of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) that establish an **antiviral state**.
## Step 3 — Antiviral Mechanisms Activated:
- **Protein Kinase R (PKR)** is activated → phosphorylates eIF-2α → inhibits viral protein synthesis
- **RNase L** is activated → degrades viral RNA
- **Mx proteins** directly interfere with viral replication machinery
- **2',5'-oligoadenylate synthetase** amplifies RNase L activation
## Step 4 — Immune Cell Activation:
- Stimulate B cells to produce antiviral antibodies
- Activate Natural Killer (NK) cells to destroy virus-infected cells
- Upregulate MHC Class I molecules on all cells, enhancing recognition and killing by cytotoxic T lymphocytes
The net result is that interferon-treated cells become highly resistant to viral replication, limiting viral spread throughout the body.

**Q: Give the main characteristics that differentiate prions from other pathogens and describe the diseases caused to humans.**
**A:**
## Characteristics That Differentiate Prions:
Prions are **misfolded protein particles** (PrP^Sc) that are fundamentally different from all other pathogens:
- They contain **no nucleic acid** (no DNA or RNA) — they are purely proteinaceous
- They are **resistant** to heat, radiation, formaldehyde, and standard sterilization procedures that destroy conventional pathogens
- They replicate by inducing normal host prion protein (PrP^C) to adopt the abnormal misfolded conformation
- They do **not** trigger a conventional immune response
- They have extremely long incubation periods (years to decades)
- They are not visualised by standard light microscopy
## Diseases Caused to Humans:
All prion diseases cause fatal, progressive **spongiform encephalopathies** — neurodegenerative disorders characterised by vacuolation of brain tissue:
1. **Creutzfeldt-Jakob Disease (CJD)** — most common; sporadic, familial, or iatrogenic; rapidly progressive dementia, myoclonus, ataxia
2. **Variant CJD (vCJD)** — linked to consumption of BSE-infected beef; affects younger patients; psychiatric symptoms prominent
3. **Kuru** — transmitted through ritual cannibalism (Fore people, Papua New Guinea); cerebellar ataxia, tremor
4. **Gerstmann-Sträussler-Scheinker Syndrome (GSS)** — familial; cerebellar ataxia, late dementia
5. **Fatal Familial Insomnia (FFI)** — familial; intractable insomnia, autonomic dysfunction, dementia

**Q: Describe the pathogenesis of viral encephalitis. Name two viruses which cause encephalitis.**
**A:**
## Pathogenesis:
1. **Entry and Primary Replication:** Virus enters via a portal (respiratory, oral, skin, or arthropod bite) and undergoes primary replication at the entry site
2. **Primary Viremia:** Virus enters the bloodstream and seeds the reticuloendothelial system (lymph nodes, spleen, liver) where amplification occurs
3. **Secondary Viremia:** Large amounts of virus re-enter the bloodstream
4. **CNS Invasion:** The virus crosses the blood-brain barrier by infecting cerebrovascular endothelial cells, being carried within infected leukocytes (Trojan horse mechanism), or by retrograde axonal transport (HSV-1, Rabies)
5. **CNS Damage:**
   - Direct viral cytopathic destruction of neurons and glial cells
   - Immune-mediated inflammation — T-cell infiltration, cytokine storm, and cerebral oedema
   - Neuronal necrosis and haemorrhage in severe cases
6. **Clinical Features:** Fever, severe headache, altered consciousness, seizures, focal neurological deficits, and coma
## Two Viruses That Cause Encephalitis:
- *Herpes Simplex Virus type 1 (HSV-1)*
- *Cytomegalovirus (CMV)*

## Q: Outline risk factors to fungal infections.
**A:**
1. **Immunosuppression** — HIV/AIDS, organ transplantation, malignancy, and corticosteroid therapy prevent patients from mounting an effective immune response against fungal spores
2. **Occupation** — Farmers, construction workers, and cave explorers are regularly exposed to fungal spores in soil and bat/bird droppings
3. **Obesity** — Skin folds provide a warm, moist environment that is ideal for fungal growth and colonisation
4. **Poor hygiene** — Fungal infections manifest more frequently in people with poor personal hygiene, particularly superficial mycoses
5. **Indwelling catheters** — In hospital settings, these provide excellent routes for fungal organisms to enter the bloodstream and deeper tissues

**Q: Explain any two virulence factors used by fungi to cause diseases in the host.**
**A:**
## 1. Toxin Production:
Fungi elaborate mycotoxins such as aflatoxins and ergot alkaloids. These toxins are directly harmful to host cells and lead to damage of normal cellular structure. They enable the fungus to colonise the host and use dead cells as a nutritional substrate. For example, *Aspergillus flavus* produces aflatoxin B1, a potent hepatotoxin and carcinogen.
## 2. Cytokine Manipulation:
Fungi mediate the release of cytokines that suppress the normal immunological attack mounted against them. By enhancing the secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines (such as IL-10) and suppressing pro-inflammatory responses, the fungus enhances its own survival because the immune system is not able to mount an effective response. *Cryptococcus neoformans* is a classic example, using its polysaccharide capsule to drive this immunosuppression.

## Q: Explain the clinical manifestation of Tinea versicolor.
**A:**
Tinea versicolor is a superficial fungal mycosis caused by *Malassezia furfur* and *Malassezia globosa*. It manifests as:
- **Irregular macules** — the skin appears with irregular hypo- or hyperpigmented macules distributed across the back, chest, upper arms, and abdomen
- **Hyperpigmentation** — the fungus causes hyperpigmentation of the superficial skin layer by stimulating abnormal melanin distribution
- **Hypopigmentation** — the fungus interferes with the biochemical synthesis of melanin from its tyrosine precursor; deficiency of melanin in affected areas produces the characteristic pale patches
- **Mild itching** — pruritus is present but usually mild
- **Scaling** — fine superficial scaling of affected lesions
- **Non-contagious** — the condition is not transmitted between individuals and is not associated with poor hygiene
- **Appearance** — lesions appear on the back, chest, upper arms, and abdomen, and become more noticeable during or after sun exposure when normal skin tans but affected areas do not

## Q: Explain any five modes of action of antifungals.
**A:**
1. **Polyenes (Nystatin and Amphotericin B):** Bind directly to ergosterol in the fungal cell membrane, creating pores that cause membrane leakage. Cellular contents escape, leading to fungal cell death. Because human membranes use cholesterol rather than ergosterol, selective toxicity is achieved.
2. **Azoles (Fluconazole, Itraconazole, Voriconazole, Ketoconazole):** Inhibit the cytochrome P450-dependent enzyme lanosterol 14-α-demethylase, blocking the conversion of lanosterol to ergosterol. Depletion of ergosterol disrupts membrane integrity and function, impairing fungal growth.
3. **Allylamines (Terbinafine):** Inhibit squalene epoxidase, an earlier enzyme in the ergosterol biosynthesis pathway. This prevents conversion of squalene to ergosterol, leading to squalene accumulation (which is toxic) and ergosterol depletion.
4. **Echinocandins (Caspofungin, Micafungin):** Inhibit β-1,3-glucan synthase, the enzyme responsible for synthesising β-glucan, a major structural component of the fungal cell wall. Disruption of cell wall integrity leads to osmotic lysis. They target the fungal cell wall rather than the membrane, offering good selectivity.
5. **Flucytosine (5-Fluorocytosine):** A pyrimidine analogue taken up by fungi via a permease that human cells do not possess. Inside the fungal cell it is converted to 5-fluorouracil, which is incorporated into RNA (disrupting protein synthesis) and inhibits thymidylate synthase (blocking DNA synthesis). Selective because human cells lack the activating permease.
