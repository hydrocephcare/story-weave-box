# HIV and the Lung

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Infectious Diseases and HIV / Respiratory Medicine**

> **In one line:** HIV destroys CD4 T cells, so the lung is the commonest site of opportunistic disease; the infection to expect depends on the CD4 count (bacterial pneumonia and TB at any level, *Pneumocystis* pneumonia below 200, atypical fungi and CMV below 100 to 50). Presentation is often atypical, so TB and PCP must always be considered.

## What you must know

- The **CD4 count** is the best predictor of which opportunistic infection (OI) occurs; rising viral load also adds risk, especially when CD4 is above 300.
- Commonest pulmonary problems in the developing world: **bacterial (lobar) pneumonia, PCP, tuberculosis and Kaposi sarcoma**.
- **PCP** is a **fungus** (*Pneumocystis jirovecii*), occurs mostly at **CD4 below 200**, causes subacute dry cough, exertional dyspnoea and hypoxaemia with a normal chest examination, and a "butterfly" bilateral perihilar CXR. Treat with **co-trimoxazole 15 mg/kg/day for 21 days** plus **prednisone** if hypoxic.
- **TB in HIV** is atypical: smears often negative, cavities rare with CD4 below 200, CXR normal in up to 1 in 3, and extrapulmonary disease in over 50%. **GeneXpert** is the key rapid test.
- Empiric **macrolide monotherapy** must not be used for bacterial pneumonia in HIV (macrolide-resistant pneumococcus).
- Early HIV diagnosis, early **ART**, and **primary prophylaxis** prevent most of these.

## 1. Background: untreated HIV and opportunistic infections

### Natural history of untreated HIV

| Stage | Duration |
|---|---|
| Transmission, then acute retroviral syndrome | 2 to 3 weeks |
| Recovery and seroconversion | 2 to 3 weeks |
| Asymptomatic infection | 2 to 4 weeks after seroconversion starts the latent phase; averages about 8 years (over 30 years in elite controllers, under 2 years in rapid progressors) |
| Symptomatic HIV / AIDS | About 1.3 years |
| Death | OIs (including TB, pneumonia) and AIDS-related cancers are the usual cause |

### Opportunistic infection

- An infection caused by a pathogen that does not usually cause disease in healthy hosts, or an **atypical or more severe presentation** of a disease that can infect healthy hosts.

### How HIV predisposes to OIs

- HIV infects **CD4 cells** and replicates in them. CD4 cells are destroyed and replaced at about 2 × 10⁹ cells/day until capacity is lost.
- Mechanisms: **increased destruction** (direct infection, apoptosis, increased lymphocyte turnover), **decreased production**, and **redistribution**.
- Without enough functioning CD4 cells, cellular immunity falls, with disturbed humoral and cytotoxic immunity.

### Lung defences in HIV

- Progressive HIV reduces **lung CD4 T cells**.
- There is intense infiltration of **CD8+ T cells** in the interstitium and alveoli (**lymphocytic alveolitis**), most marked in early to middle disease, owing to compartmentalisation of HIV-specific cytotoxic T cells.

## 2. CD4 count and the risk of OIs

OIs form a continuum of risk and can occasionally occur above their usual thresholds.

| CD4 count (cells/µL) | Typical diseases |
|---|---|
| Above 500 | Candida vaginitis, pulmonary TB, *Streptococcus pneumoniae* |
| 200 to 500 | Pneumococcal disease, HSV, VZV, pulmonary TB, **Kaposi's sarcoma**, thrush |
| Below 200 | ***Pneumocystis jirovecii***, miliary and extrapulmonary TB, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, disseminated histoplasmosis, recurrent bacterial disease |
| Below 100 | Toxoplasmosis, cryptococcosis, chronic cryptosporidiosis, microsporidiosis, candidal oesophagitis |
| Below 50 | **CMV**, ***Mycobacterium avium* complex (MAC)**, CNS lymphoma, progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML) |

### Classification of OIs

- **By organism:** bacterial (TB, MAC, bacterial pneumonia, septicaemia); fungal (candidiasis, PCP, cryptococcosis, aspergillosis); viral (CMV, HSV, VZV, JC virus); parasitic (toxoplasmosis, cryptosporidium, microsporidium, leishmania).
- **By site:** brain (cryptococcus, toxoplasmosis, lymphoma, PML, CMV, HIV dementia); eye (CMV, VZV); mouth (HSV, Candida); **lungs** (PCP, TB, bacterial pneumonia, fungal pneumonia); gut (MAC, CMV, Salmonella, cryptosporidium, microsporidia); bone marrow (TB, MAC, histoplasmosis, leishmania); skin (HSV, VZV, pyogenic); genitals (HSV, HPV, Candida).

## 3. Pulmonary opportunistic infections

- **Common in the developing world:** bacterial (lobar) pneumonia, PCP, TB, Kaposi's sarcoma.
- **Uncommon:** histoplasmosis, *Penicillium* (talaromycosis), coccidioidomycosis, *Mycobacterium avium*.

## 4. Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PCP)

### Organism and epidemiology

- A **fungus** (not a protozoan), formerly *P. carinii* (the rodent species). Ubiquitous; first infection usually in early childhood. PCP follows reactivation or new exposure; airborne spread is possible in immunosuppressed patients.
- Before ART, PCP occurred in 70 to 80% of patients with AIDS; mortality in treated cases was 20 to 40%; **90% of cases occurred at CD4 below 200**.

### Risk factors

CD4 below 200 cells/µL; CD4 percentage below 14%; previous PCP; oral thrush; recurrent bacterial pneumonia; unintentional weight loss; high HIV RNA.

### Clinical features

- **Progressive exertional dyspnoea**, fever (may be predominant), **non-productive cough**, chest discomfort.
- **Subacute** onset, worsening over days to weeks (fulminant pneumonia is uncommon).
- **Desaturation on exercise.**
- Chest examination may be **normal** early; later diffuse dry crackles, tachypnoea, tachycardia, hypoxia, fever.
- Extrapulmonary disease is rare (linked to aerosolised pentamidine prophylaxis).

### Diagnosis

- Clinical picture, blood tests and radiographs are suggestive but not diagnostic; the organism cannot be cultured.
- **Hypoxaemia** is characteristic (PO2 below 70 mmHg or A-a gradient above 35 mmHg).
- **LDH above 500 mg/dL** is common but non-specific.
- Organism detection:

| Specimen | Sensitivity |
|---|---|
| Induced sputum | Less than 50% to over 90% |
| Expectorated sputum | Low |
| **Bronchoscopy with bronchoalveolar lavage** | 90 to 99% |
| Transbronchial biopsy | 95 to 100% |
| Open-lung biopsy | 95 to 100% |

- In resource-limited settings these cannot be done, so the **diagnosis is made clinically**.

### Imaging

- CXR may be normal early. **Typical:** diffuse bilateral symmetrical **interstitial perihilar infiltrates (butterfly appearance)**, granular opacities.
- Atypical: nodules, asymmetric disease, blebs, **cysts**, **pneumothorax**. Cavitation, hilar lymphadenopathy and pleural effusion are **uncommon** (unless a second process is present).
- **HRCT:** bilateral patchy **ground-glass opacities**.

### Treatment

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| **First line** | **Co-trimoxazole (TMP-SMX) 15 mg/kg/day** (trimethoprim component) in 3 divided doses; oral dose **2 double-strength tablets three times a day**; **21 days** |
| **Adjunctive steroids** in severe disease (PO2 below 70 mmHg, A-a gradient above 35, or very hypoxic) | **Prednisone 40 mg twice daily for 5 days, then 40 mg daily for 5 days, then 20 mg daily for 11 days** |
| Response | Clinical response in 5 to 7 days |
| Alternatives (TMP-SMX failure or intolerance) | **Clindamycin plus primaquine**; **atovaquone 750 mg twice daily** (mild to moderate; contraindicated in pregnancy per the slide); **IV pentamidine** |

> **Remember:** start prophylaxis (co-trimoxazole) in all HIV patients with CD4 below 200 and continue until CD4 is above 200 for 3 to 6 months on ART. Start ART within 2 weeks of PCP treatment.

## 5. Bacterial pneumonia

- Organisms: *S. pneumoniae* (drug-resistant strains increasingly common), *H. influenzae*, *P. aeruginosa*, *S. aureus*, atypicals (less common); no organism identified in up to 33%.
- **Clinical:** acute, like the HIV-negative patient. A **subacute** illness suggests an alternative diagnosis (PCP, TB, chronic fungal disease). Focal consolidation or pleural effusion; WBC usually raised.
- **CXR:** focal, segmental or lobar consolidation (right middle lobe consolidation in pneumococcal pneumonia); atypical forms (multilobar, nodular, reticulonodular).
- **Diagnosis:** routine pathogen testing is controversial; send expectorated sputum Gram stain and culture and **two blood cultures** (low sensitivity but very specific). **Consider TB**: if the clinical and X-ray picture fits, manage as TB. Evaluate for PCP if indicated (it may coexist). Diagnostic thoracentesis for effusion.
- **Treatment:** empiric treatment aimed at *S. pneumoniae* and *H. influenzae*, with the same principles as in HIV-negative patients, according to severity; narrow when a pathogen is identified. **Do not use empiric macrolide monotherapy** (macrolide-resistant pneumococci). Use **fluoroquinolones** cautiously if TB is suspected but untreated (they act as TB monotherapy and mask the diagnosis).

## 6. Tuberculosis in HIV

### TB in HIV-negative patients (typical)

Cough for more than 3 weeks, weight loss, night sweats, **upper-lobe cavitary disease** on CXR, **smear positive**.

### TB in the era of HIV (atypical)

- Atypical symptoms (no cough; acute or very long history).
- **Smears often negative.**
- Disease can occur anywhere in the lung; **cavities are rare with CD4 below 200**.
- **CXR may be normal in up to 1 in 3.**
- **Extrapulmonary TB is common:** in HIV-negative patients 85% of TB is pulmonary; in HIV-positive patients **over 50% have extrapulmonary disease** (a Nairobi autopsy study).
- Screening data from a South African ART clinic: relying on cough alone missed half of TB cases; **19%** of patients entering the ART clinic had TB; 22% of TB patients had no symptoms; only **9%** of proven cases were sputum AFB positive.

> **Exam tip:** in advanced HIV, a negative smear and a normal chest X-ray do **not** exclude TB. Use the symptom screen (cough, fever, night sweats, weight loss), GeneXpert and urine LAM.

### Diagnostics

- **GeneXpert (cartridge-based nucleic acid amplification test, CBNAAT):** rapid detection of *M. tuberculosis* **and rifampicin resistance**, useful in smear-negative cases and early disease.
- **ADA (adenosine deaminase)** is helpful for TB effusions, but is raised in many conditions with cellular immune stimulation: typhoid, infectious mononucleosis, liver disease, sarcoidosis, leukaemia, brucellosis, acute pneumonia, malignancies.

### Treatment and prevention

- Treated **as in HIV-negative patients** (standard regimen, co-trimoxazole prophylaxis, early ART).
- **Isoniazid preventive therapy (IPT)** is used where TB has been ruled out (for latent TB).

## 7. Histoplasmosis

- *Histoplasma capsulatum*; endemic in the US Midwest, Puerto Rico, Central America, the Amazon basin, sub-Saharan Africa and Asia. Up to 5% of AIDS patients in endemic areas not on ART. In non-endemic areas it is seen in those who lived in endemic areas.
- Acquired by **inhalation**; reactivation of latent infection occurs. **Disseminated** disease at CD4 below 150; isolated pulmonary disease may occur at CD4 above 300. Incidence has fallen with potent ART.

**Clinical features:**
- Disseminated multi-organ disease: fever, fatigue, weight loss, **hepatosplenomegaly**, lymphadenopathy, pallor, abdominal tenderness; cough, chest pain, dyspnoea in 50%; skin lesions.
- CNS (fever, headache, seizures, focal deficit, altered mental state); GI (fever, diarrhoea, abdominal pain, weight loss).
- Labs: anaemia, leucopenia, **LDH greater than AST greater than ALT**, **ferritin often above 1000**. Blood smear may show intracellular organisms.

**Diagnosis:**
- **Histoplasma antigen** in serum (over 85% sensitive) or urine (over 95%) for disseminated disease; insensitive for pulmonary disease.
- Biopsy: budding yeast.
- Culture of blood, bone marrow, respiratory secretions or lesions (over 85% sensitive; takes 2 to 4 weeks).
- Serology is usually not useful when immunosuppressed.

**Treatment:**

| Severity | Regimen |
|---|---|
| Moderate to severe | **Induction (2 weeks or until improved):** preferred **liposomal amphotericin B 3 mg/kg IV daily**; alternatives amphotericin B 0.7 mg/kg IV daily or amphotericin B lipid complex 5 mg/kg IV daily. **Maintenance:** itraconazole 200 mg three times daily for 3 days, then twice daily (liquid preferred) |
| Less severe | Itraconazole 200 mg three times daily for 3 days, then twice daily |
| Duration | At least 12 months |

**Prophylaxis:**
- Primary: itraconazole if CD4 below 150 and living in an endemic area.
- Secondary: for 1 year; can stop when treated for over 12 months, on ART over 6 months, CD4 above 150 and urine/serum antigen below 4.1.

## 8. Other pulmonary conditions in HIV

- **Viral pneumonitis:** CMV, HSV, VZV.
- **Malignancies:** **Kaposi's sarcoma** (pulmonary KS: nodules, effusion, haemoptysis; KSHV/HHV-8), lymphomas, **primary effusion lymphoma**.
- **Others:** lymphocytic interstitial pneumonitis (common in children), non-specific interstitial pneumonitis, **pulmonary hypertension**.

## 9. Approach and summary

1. Think of the CD4 count first; ask about ART, prophylaxis and previous infections.
2. Pulmonary manifestations are common and often atypical.
3. Rule out TB in every patient, even with a normal film.
4. Prompt diagnosis matters: treat empirically in resource-limited settings.
5. Prevent: early HIV diagnosis, early ART and primary prophylaxis (co-trimoxazole, IPT) of OIs.

## Key points to revise

1. CD4 below 200: PCP, extrapulmonary TB; below 100: cryptococcus, toxoplasma; below 50: CMV, MAC, PML.
2. PCP: fungus, dry cough, exertional dyspnoea, hypoxia, normal chest signs, butterfly CXR, raised LDH.
3. PCP treatment: co-trimoxazole for 21 days; add prednisone if PO2 below 70; alternatives clindamycin plus primaquine, atovaquone, pentamidine.
4. TB in HIV: smear-negative, no cavities, extrapulmonary in over 50%; GeneXpert.
5. Bacterial pneumonia: *S. pneumoniae* and *H. influenzae*; no macrolide monotherapy.
6. Histoplasmosis: disseminated at CD4 below 150; urine antigen; liposomal amphotericin then itraconazole.
7. Kaposi sarcoma and lymphoma are the pulmonary malignancies.
