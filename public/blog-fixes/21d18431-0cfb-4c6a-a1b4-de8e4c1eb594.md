# Birth Asphyxia and Hypoxic-Ischaemic Encephalopathy

**Year 4 · Paediatrics · Neonatology**

> **In one line:** birth asphyxia is failure of placental or pulmonary gas exchange around birth. The resulting hypoxia and ischaemia injure the brain in two waves (energy failure with glutamate excitotoxicity, then reperfusion injury with delayed apoptosis) and cause hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy (HIE) plus multi-organ damage. Treatment is prompt resuscitation, organ support, seizure control and, where available, therapeutic hypothermia within 6 hours.

## What you must know

- **Asphyxia** = placental or pulmonary gas exchange compromised or stopped. **HIE** = abnormal neurological behaviour in the neonate after a hypoxic-ischaemic event.
- Other organs are affected too: kidney, lungs, liver, heart, gut.
- Criteria for asphyxia severe enough to cause neurological injury (AAP/ACOG): **cord arterial pH below 7**, **Apgar 0 to 3 for more than 5 minutes**, neurological sequelae (seizures, coma, hypotonia), and **multi-organ involvement**.
- **Sarnat staging** (mild, moderate, severe) predicts outcome: mild normalises by 3 to 4 days; severe means stupor or coma and often ventilation.
- **MRI** is the imaging of choice; basal ganglia and thalamus injury has a worse outlook than a white-matter pattern.
- HIE is the **commonest cause of neonatal seizures**.
- **Therapeutic hypothermia** (core temperature 33 to 35 C for 72 hours) is the standard of care, started within 6 hours.
- Avoid hypoglycaemia, hyperglycaemia, hyperthermia and hypocapnia.

## 1. Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Hypoxia / anoxia** | Partial (hypoxia) or complete (anoxia) lack of oxygen in the brain or blood |
| **Asphyxia** | Placental or pulmonary gas exchange is compromised or ceases altogether |
| **Ischaemia** | Reduced or absent blood flow to an organ, compromising both oxygen and substrate (glucose) delivery |
| **Hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy** | Abnormal neurological behaviour in the neonatal period arising from a hypoxic-ischaemic event |

> **Remember:** asphyxia is a multi-system problem, not only a brain problem.

## 2. Epidemiology

- Occurs in **1 to 6 per 1000 live term births** in developed countries; the burden is higher in low-resource settings such as Kenya.
- The **5th leading cause of under-5 mortality**.
- Significant morbidity among survivors: in school-aged children with a history of moderately severe HIE, **15 to 20% had significant learning difficulties**, even without obvious brain injury.
- Commoner in term newborns; no sex or race predilection.

## 3. Aetiology and risk factors

Causes are grouped by where gas exchange fails:

- **Inadequate perfusion of the maternal side of the placenta** (for example maternal hypotension).
- **Failure of gas exchange across the placenta** (for example placental abruption).
- **Interruption of umbilical blood flow** (for example cord compression, prolapse).
- A **compromised fetus** who cannot tolerate the transient hypoxia of normal labour (for example the anaemic or growth-retarded fetus).
- **Failure to inflate the lungs** and to complete the changes in ventilation and lung perfusion that must occur at birth.

Other risk factors include prolonged or obstructed labour, maternal diabetes or pre-eclampsia, prematurity or post-maturity, and congenital anomalies.

## 4. Pathophysiology

### Systemic response

1. Systemic hypoxia leads to CNS hypoxaemia, hypercapnia and acidosis.
2. Blood flow is **redistributed to the brain, heart and adrenals** (diving reflex), with a **catecholamine surge** and raised blood pressure.
3. When compensation fails, ischaemic brain injury follows.

### Loss of autoregulation

- Normally cerebral blood flow is constant despite changes in systemic BP (in adults over a mean BP of 60 to 100 mmHg; poorly studied in newborns but thought to be narrower).
- After decompensation, flow becomes **pressure-passive**: cerebral perfusion depends directly on systemic BP, so hypotension or hypertension both harm the brain.

### Early phase (0 to 6 hours): primary energy failure and excitotoxicity

1. Energy failure raises synaptic **glutamate** (excitatory amino acid).
2. Glutamate activates **NMDA, AMPA and kainate** receptors; calcium and sodium flood in.
3. The **Na+/K+ pump fails**, intracellular sodium and water rise: **cellular oedema and necrotic cell death**.
4. High intracellular calcium (including uptake into the nucleus) triggers **programmed cell death (apoptosis)**.

### Reperfusion injury

- Restored perfusion and oxygen activate **cyclooxygenase, lipoxygenase and xanthine oxidase**, generating **reactive oxygen species**.
- Nitric oxide combines with superoxide to form **peroxynitrite**, causing **lipid peroxidation, DNA damage** and further cell death.

### Late (delayed) phase: 6 to 24 hours

- **Mitochondrial dysfunction**, activation of **caspases**, and further programmed cell death.
- **Inflammatory mediators** (cytokines, chemokines such as IL-1 beta) contribute.
- This secondary injury correlates with adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes at 1 and 4 years.

> **Exam tip:** the 6-hour window between primary injury and secondary energy failure is the "therapeutic window" in which cooling works.

## 5. Clinical features

### Diagnostic criteria (history and findings)

- Profound metabolic or mixed acidaemia (**pH below 7**) in an umbilical artery sample, if obtained.
- **Apgar 0 to 3 persisting for more than 5 minutes**.
- Neonatal neurological sequelae (seizures, coma, hypotonia).
- **Multiple organ involvement** (kidney, lungs, liver, heart, intestines).

### Sarnat classification of HIE

| Grade | Features | Course |
|---|---|---|
| **Mild** | Slightly increased tone, brisk deep tendon reflexes in the first days; transient poor feeding, irritability, excessive crying or sleepiness | Neurological examination normalises by **3 to 4 days** |
| **Moderate** | Lethargic, significant hypotonia, reduced reflexes; grasp, Moro and suck sluggish or absent; occasional apnoea; **seizures within the first 24 hours** | Full recovery within 1 to 2 weeks is possible and carries a better outcome |
| **Severe** | Stupor or coma; irregular breathing, usually needs ventilation; generalised hypotonia, depressed reflexes; **neonatal reflexes absent**; abnormal eye movements, dilated fixed pupils; seizures (initially resistant to treatment); heart rate and BP irregularities | Seizures are generalised and relate to reperfusion injury; later they subside, wakefulness may worsen and a **bulging fontanelle** suggests increasing cerebral oedema |

> **Remember:** an initial period of well-being or mild HIE can be followed by **sudden deterioration** (ongoing cell injury and death), with increased seizure intensity.

## 6. Investigations

| Test | Role |
|---|---|
| **Blood gas** | Essential for diagnosis; assesses acid-base status; avoid hypoxia, hyperoxia, hypercapnia and hypocapnia |
| **Urea, electrolytes, creatinine** | Daily in severe cases. Low sodium, potassium and chloride with reduced urine flow and excess weight gain (day 2 to 3) suggest **acute tubular necrosis or SIADH**. Increased urine flow may mean ongoing tubular damage with sodium loss, or recovery if later |
| **Liver function tests, coagulation profile, cardiac enzymes** | According to clinical suspicion (liver, clotting and myocardial injury) |
| **MRI** | **Imaging of choice** for diagnosis, follow-up and prognosis in moderate-to-severe HIE. **Basal ganglia and thalamus** predominant injury has an unfavourable outcome compared with a white-matter pattern |
| **Cranial ultrasound** | Low sensitivity (about 50%). Shows global increased echogenicity and obliterated CSF spaces (oedema). Useful on admission to exclude intracerebral or intraventricular haemorrhage, particularly before cooling |
| **CT head** | Confirms cerebral oedema (narrow lateral ventricles, flattened gyri, blurred sulci) and evolving infarcts, but exposes the child to radiation |

## 7. Management

### Initial resuscitation and stabilisation

- Follow standard neonatal resuscitation protocols: be prepared (warmth, airway, breathing, circulation), with close attention to oxygen delivery, perfusion, and avoiding **hypoglycaemia and hyperthermia**.
- **Room air vs 100% oxygen:** trials show room-air resuscitation is as effective as 100% oxygen and gives lower oxidative stress markers. ILCOR recommends starting with oxygen concentrations between **21 and 100%**.

### Supportive care by system

| Problem | Management |
|---|---|
| **Respiratory** | Most infants with severe HIE need ventilation in the first days. Keep blood gases and pH in normal range. **Hypocapnia causes severe brain hypoperfusion** and cellular alkalosis and worsens outcome. Watch for **persistent pulmonary hypertension** |
| **Circulation** | Keep mean BP **above 35 to 40 mmHg**. Hypotension is common (myocardial dysfunction, capillary leak, hypovolaemia). Use **dopamine or dobutamine**. Avoid iatrogenic hypertension |
| **Fluids and electrolytes** | Risk of ATN and SIADH, so **fluid restriction** until renal function and urine output are known; individualise by weight, urine output and electrolytes. Avoid both hypoglycaemia and hyperglycaemia |
| **Temperature** | **Hyperthermia** raises risk of death 3.6 to 4-fold for each 1 C increase in the mean of the highest quartile of skin or oesophageal temperature |
| **Feeding** | In moderate-to-severe HIE keep **nil by mouth for the first 3 days** or until alertness improves; then start trophic feeds (about 5 ml every 3 hours). Monitor for **necrotising enterocolitis** |

### Seizures

- HIE is the **most common cause of neonatal seizures**; they are usually self-limited to the first days but can compromise ventilation, oxygenation and BP, may add to brain injury and raise later epilepsy risk.
- Options: **phenobarbital, phenytoin, benzodiazepines** (especially lorazepam). Phenobarbital works in only 29 to 50%; phenytoin adds about 15%.

### Therapeutic hypothermia

- Now the **standard of care**. Whole-body and head cooling are both available; neither is clearly superior.
- Aim for core (rectal) temperature **33 to 35 C for 72 hours**, then **rewarm gradually over 6 to 8 hours**.
- Mild hypothermia (3 to 4 C below baseline) is neuroprotective when applied **within a few hours, no later than 6 hours**, of injury.
- **Mechanisms:** reduced metabolic rate and energy depletion, less excitatory transmitter release, less ion flux disturbance, reduced apoptosis, reduced vascular permeability, oedema and blood-brain barrier breakdown.
- **Concerns (mainly theoretical):** coagulation defects, leucocyte dysfunction, pulmonary hypertension, worsening metabolic acidosis, arrhythmias (especially during rewarming). Long-term follow-up is ongoing.

> **Exam tip:** cooling must start by 6 hours; it is contraindicated in practice if the facility cannot monitor and control temperature safely. In many Kenyan hospitals supportive care is the mainstay.

## 8. Follow-up and prognosis

- Aim to detect impairments early and begin early intervention.
- Monitor growth, including **head circumference**.
- Moderate-to-severe HIE with abnormal neurological examination or feeding difficulty needs **intensive follow-up** with a developmental paediatrician and paediatric neurologist.
- **Ophthalmology review** (damage to the posterior visual cortex can occur).
- **Hearing test before discharge**, repeated in infants at risk of late-onset hearing loss (pulmonary hypertension, antibiotic use).
- Outcome depends on HIE grade, MRI pattern (basal ganglia/thalamus worse) and recovery time; mild HIE has an excellent outlook, severe HIE has high mortality and risk of cerebral palsy, epilepsy and learning difficulty.

## Key points to revise

1. Asphyxia is failed gas exchange; HIE is the neurological consequence; other organs are also injured.
2. Severe-injury criteria: cord pH below 7, Apgar 0 to 3 beyond 5 minutes, seizures or coma, multi-organ involvement.
3. Pathophysiology: energy failure, glutamate excitotoxicity, Na/Ca influx and necrosis (0 to 6 h), reperfusion free radicals, then delayed apoptosis (6 to 24 h).
4. Sarnat grading: mild normal by 3 to 4 days, moderate has seizures in the first 24 hours, severe has coma and absent reflexes.
5. MRI is the best imaging; basal ganglia and thalamus injury carries a poor prognosis; cranial ultrasound is only about 50% sensitive.
6. Supportive care: avoid hypoglycaemia, hyperglycaemia, hyperthermia and hypocapnia; maintain mean BP above 35 to 40 mmHg; restrict fluids because of ATN and SIADH.
7. Cooling to 33 to 35 C for 72 hours, started within 6 hours, then rewarm over 6 to 8 hours.
8. Phenobarbital helps only 29 to 50% of seizures; follow-up needs head circumference, neurology, ophthalmology and hearing checks.
