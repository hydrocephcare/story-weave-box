# Cardiac Disease in Pregnancy

**Year 4 · Obstetrics & Gynaecology · Medical Conditions in Pregnancy**

> **In one line:** a normal pregnancy raises cardiac output by about 40 to 50% and a damaged heart may not cope. The danger times are **30 to 32 weeks, the second stage of labour, and the hours straight after delivery** (when the contracted uterus empties its blood into the circulation). Plan, monitor, deliver in hospital, and avoid anything that overloads the heart.

## What you must know

- Heart disease complicates about **1%** of pregnancies. In Kenya and the developing world the commonest lesion is **rheumatic heart disease (RHD)**, followed by congenital heart disease.
- Risk depends on the **lesion**, not just the symptoms: stenotic valves (mitral, aortic), pulmonary hypertension (Eisenmenger), cyanotic heart disease, severe cardiomyopathy and mechanical valves are the worst.
- Classify with the **NYHA grade**. Grades III and IV are admitted for the whole pregnancy.
- Delivery: **vaginal delivery is preferred** with a shortened second stage (assisted vacuum or forceps). Caesarean only for obstetric reasons or a few cardiac ones.
- **Never give ergometrine** (use oxytocin slowly). Avoid spinal anaesthesia for caesarean.
- Antibiotic prophylaxis and anticoagulation for valves, and lifelong follow-up.

## 1. Definition and scope

Cardiac disease in pregnancy covers disorders of the heart muscle, valves or vessels that reduce the heart's ability to supply tissues with oxygen. It includes:

- Pre-existing disease (rheumatic, congenital, cardiomyopathy)
- Conditions that develop in pregnancy or the puerperium (**peripartum cardiomyopathy**, pre-eclampsia heart failure, spontaneous coronary dissection)
- Congenital or acquired structural abnormalities
- Arrhythmias

## 2. Normal changes that can be mistaken for heart disease

| Normal finding in pregnancy | Why |
|---|---|
| Hyperdynamic circulation, tachycardia | Cardiac output and blood volume rise |
| **Systolic ejection murmur** at the left sternal edge | More flow across the aortic and pulmonary valves |
| **Mammary soufflé** (continuous murmur, 2nd to 4th intercostal space) | Increased breast blood flow |
| Loud, split first heart sound; apex displaced | Raised diaphragm and increased volume |
| Breathlessness, tiredness, reduced exercise tolerance, syncope | Haemodilution, raised diaphragm, vasodilatation |
| Ankle oedema | Venous pressure from the uterus |

> **How to separate disease from pregnancy:** a diastolic murmur, a loud systolic murmur with a thrill, cyanosis or clubbing, chest pain on exertion, an irregular rhythm or signs of heart failure are **never** normal.

## 3. Classification

### NYHA functional class

| Grade | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **I** | No limitation of physical activity |
| **II** | Slight limitation; comfortable at rest, ordinary activity causes discomfort |
| **III** | Marked limitation; comfortable at rest, less than ordinary activity causes symptoms |
| **IV** | Symptoms **at rest** |

- Grades III and IV have high rates of heart failure, arrhythmias, stroke and maternal death.
- **Stenotic valves with minimal symptoms** (mitral or aortic stenosis in NYHA I or II) can deteriorate suddenly, so manage them **as grade III or IV**.

### Conditions with high maternal risk

- Severe cardiomyopathy that leads to heart failure
- Symptomatic **mitral, aortic or pulmonary stenosis**
- **Cyanotic heart disease** (e.g. tetralogy of Fallot)
- **Pulmonary hypertension** (including Eisenmenger syndrome)
- **Artificial (mechanical) valves**

## 4. Effects of pregnancy on the heart

**Cardiac failure** can develop in pregnancy, labour or the puerperium. The risk periods are:

1. **30 to 32 weeks** (peak blood volume and cardiac output)
2. **Labour, especially the second stage** (pushing, pain, anxiety)
3. **Immediately after delivery** (autotransfusion from the uterus and relief of caval compression)

**Factors that trigger failure:** advanced age, arrhythmias, ventricular hypertrophy, a previous history of heart failure, and "precipitants": **anaemia, respiratory or urinary tract infection, any fever, excessive exercise, emotional upset, hypertension, multiple pregnancy and obesity**.

**Effects on the fetus.** Cyanosis and poor functional capacity are markers of high maternal and fetal risk: **preterm labour, IUGR and intrauterine death**.

## 5. Diagnosis

Remember that symptoms and signs overlap with normal pregnancy.

**Symptoms:** breathlessness, nocturnal cough, orthopnoea and PND, syncope, chest pain, palpitations, swelling, exercise intolerance.

**Warning signs of cardiac disease:** worsening dyspnoea on exertion or at rest, chest pain with exertion, syncope preceded by palpitations or exertion, a **loud systolic or any diastolic murmur**, **cyanosis or clubbing**, raised JVP, cardiomegaly or a ventricular heave.

**Signs:** oedema, cyanosis, tachycardia, tachypnoea, murmurs (pansystolic, late systolic, loud ejection or diastolic with a thrill), basal crepitations, cardiac enlargement, arrhythmia.

### Investigations

- **Chest X-ray** (with an abdominal lead shield): cardiomegaly, increased pulmonary vascular markings, enlarged pulmonary veins
- **ECG:** T-wave inversion, biatrial enlargement, dysrhythmias
- **Echocardiography with colour Doppler** (the key test): structural defects (ASD, VSD), valve anatomy and area, ventricular function and ejection fraction, pulmonary artery pressure
- **Cardiac MRI** for complex anatomy
- **Full blood count** (anaemia, infection) and **urinalysis** (UTI)
- BNP / NT-proBNP can help if failure is suspected

## 6. Management

### Principles

1. Early diagnosis and a clear functional classification
2. Prevent, detect early and treat cardiac failure
3. Treat anaemia and any other precipitating condition
4. Follow-up for obstetric complications through pregnancy, labour and the puerperium
5. Joint care by an **obstetrician and a cardiologist**
6. **Delivery in hospital is mandatory**

### Pre-conception

- Counsel on the maternal and fetal risks. Some conditions make pregnancy **absolutely contraindicated** (severe pulmonary hypertension / Eisenmenger syndrome, severe symptomatic mitral or aortic stenosis, severe LV dysfunction with EF under 30%, Marfan syndrome with aortic root over 4 cm).
- Treat anaemia, hypertension and other conditions beforehand.
- Offer termination on medical grounds when the risk is very high.
- Corrective surgery or valvotomy before pregnancy, where possible.

### Antenatal care

- Start early and keep **one provider** for continuity
- Monitor fetal growth and wellbeing
- Rest, and avoid the aggravating factors listed above
- Treat infections vigorously; **prevent anaemia** with prophylactic iron and folate and treat it aggressively
- Good dental care (to prevent endocarditis)
- **Anticoagulation** for mechanical valves (see the note below)

| Group | Plan |
|---|---|
| **NYHA I and II** | Outpatient care; **admit at about 34 weeks** (earlier if social circumstances are poor); admit if the cardiac state worsens or there is any obstetric complication |
| **NYHA III and IV** | **Admit as soon as the pregnancy is diagnosed, until delivery**; complete bed rest; treat any worsening aggressively |

**Emergency admission** if: the functional grade worsens; new dyspnoea, cough, basal crepitations or tachyarrhythmia; anaemia or pre-eclampsia develops.

> **Mechanical valves:** warfarin is effective but teratogenic in the first trimester (6th to 12th weeks) and risks fetal bleeding near term; heparin (LMWH or unfractionated) does not cross the placenta but is less protective for the valve. The regimen is chosen with the cardiologist, with a switch to heparin around 36 weeks.

### Labour

**First stage**

- Prop the patient up and tilt her to the left (avoid supine compression of the vena cava)
- **Continuous oxygen** by mask (5 to 6 L/min)
- Good analgesia: pethidine 25 to 50 mg IM, or morphine, or **epidural**
- Strict fluid balance; limit IV fluids (**under 75 mL/hour**) to avoid overload
- If oxytocin is needed use a **more concentrated solution at a slower rate**
- **Antibiotic prophylaxis** (e.g. crystalline penicillin 1 MU)
- Watch pulse and respiratory rate closely; if the pulse is above 110/minute between contractions, or failure develops, give **digoxin**
- For pulmonary oedema, give **furosemide 40 mg IV**

**Second stage**

- Keep her propped up
- **Shorten the second stage** with a vacuum or forceps delivery, to avoid prolonged bearing down which worsens failure

**Third stage**

- Active management with **oxytocin** (given slowly), controlled cord traction and uterine massage
- **DO NOT GIVE ERGOMETRINE**: it constricts vessels and squeezes extra blood into the circulation, causing sudden overload

### Caesarean section

There is **no cardiac reason** for caesarean in most women. It is done for obstetric indications, and for these cardiac ones: **coarctation of the aorta** (to prevent rupture), **aortic dissection or aneurysm**, **aortopathy with aortic root over 4 cm**, and women on warfarin in early labour. **Avoid spinal anaesthesia** (sudden vasodilatation) and prefer epidural or general anaesthesia as advised by the anaesthetist.

### Puerperium

- Close monitoring for the first **24 hours** (the highest risk of pulmonary oedema)
- Stay in hospital for **10 to 14 days** depending on grade
- Restrict exercise in the first week and mobilise slowly
- **Antibiotic prophylaxis** IV for 24 to 48 hours then orally for 10 days, to prevent **subacute bacterial endocarditis**
- Treat the cardiac condition; watch for **pulmonary oedema, PPH** and venous thromboembolism
- Encourage **breastfeeding** (check each drug for safety)

### Contraception

| Method | Comment |
|---|---|
| **Combined oestrogen pills** | Avoid: thrombosis risk |
| **Progestin-only pills or injections** | Safe and effective; may cause irregular bleeding, especially on anticoagulants |
| **IUD (copper or LNG-IUS)** | Often avoided for fear of infection, although WHO permits it |
| **Condoms** | Good as a back-up |
| **Sterilisation** | Consider after the family is complete; vasectomy for the husband if the heart is poorly compensated |

## 7. Prognosis

**Maternal**

- Depends on the lesion, functional capacity, quality of supervision, other risk factors, and whether corrective surgery has been done.
- Maternal mortality is **lowest (under 1%)** in rheumatic and acyanotic congenital lesions, and may be as high as **50% in Eisenmenger syndrome**.
- Most deaths are from cardiac failure and happen **after delivery**. Other causes: pulmonary oedema, pulmonary embolism, active rheumatic carditis, subacute bacterial endocarditis and rupture of a cerebral aneurysm in coarctation.

**Fetal**

- In rheumatic lesions the outcome is usually good.
- In **cyanotic** lesions fetal loss is about **45%** (abortion, IUGR, prematurity).
- If either parent has a congenital heart lesion, the baby's risk of congenital heart disease is **3 to 10%**.

## Key points to revise

1. The commonest lesion is **RHD**; the highest risk lesions are Eisenmenger, stenotic valves, cyanotic disease, mechanical valves.
2. Risk windows: **30 to 32 weeks, second stage, immediately postpartum.**
3. NYHA III and IV: admit throughout. NYHA I and II: admit at 34 weeks.
4. Labour: sit up, oxygen, restrict fluids, antibiotic cover, shorten the second stage. **No ergometrine.**
5. Caesarean only for obstetric reasons (or coarctation, aortic root over 4 cm, dissection).
6. Prophylactic antibiotics against endocarditis; progestin or barrier contraception.
