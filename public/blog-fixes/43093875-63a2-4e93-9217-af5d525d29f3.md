## Summary
This document provides a comprehensive overview of various hematologic malignancies, including Hodgkin lymphoma, other lymphoid and myeloid neoplasms, and Langerhans cell histiocytoses, detailing their pathogenesis, clinical features, and treatment. It also explores bleeding disorders, categorizing them by underlying mechanisms such as vascular fragility, platelet defects, coagulation factor deficiencies, and mixed defects like Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC). Key molecular insights, diagnostic features, staging, and prognostic indicators are highlighted across these diverse conditions.

## Key Points
- Hodgkin lymphoma is pathologically defined by the presence of **Reed-Sternberg (RS) giant cells** and typically spreads in an orderly, contiguous fashion.
- The histological diagnosis of Hodgkin lymphoma necessitates the definitive identification of RS cells or variants within an appropriate reactive background, often supported by immunophenotyping.
- Extranodal marginal zone lymphomas, frequently associated with chronic antigenic stimulation from autoimmune disorders or infections like *H. pylori*, may regress with eradication of the inciting agent.
- **Hairy Cell Leukaemia** is an uncommon but highly treatable indolent B cell neoplasm, exhibiting excellent response to purine nucleoside chemotherapy due to its characteristic **BRAF mutation**.
- **Mycosis Fungoides and Sézary Syndrome** represent cutaneous T cell lymphomas where malignant CD4+ T cells primarily infiltrate the skin, leading to characteristic rashes, plaques, and tumors.
- **Acute Myeloid Leukaemia (AML)** is marked by the accumulation of immature myeloid blasts in the bone marrow and often involves specific genetic mutations, with **Acute Promyelocytic Leukaemia** being notably responsive to targeted differentiation therapy.
- **Myelodysplastic Syndromes (MDS)** are characterized by ineffective hematopoiesis, leading to peripheral cytopenias despite a frequently hypercellular marrow, and carry a risk of transformation to AML.
- **Chronic Myelogenous Leukaemia (CML)** is pathognomonically linked to the **BCR-ABL fusion gene**, typically from the **Philadelphia chromosome**, and its treatment has been revolutionized by targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitors.
- **Langerhans Cell Histiocytoses** are rare proliferative disorders of immature dendritic cells, often driven by the **BRAF V600E mutation**, presenting in varied clinical forms from localized bone lesions to aggressive multisystem disease.
- Bleeding disorders arise from defects in the vessel wall, platelets, or coagulation factors, necessitating distinct diagnostic approaches and management strategies based on their etiology.
- **Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC)** is a paradoxical condition caused by systemic coagulation activation, simultaneously leading to microthrombotic tissue ischemia and a severe bleeding diathesis due to consumptive coagulopathy.

## Detailed Notes

### Hodgkin Lymphoma
**Key concepts:** Reed-Sternberg cells · Subtypes · Pathogenesis · Staging · Clinical features · Prognosis

Hodgkin lymphoma is characterised by the presence of **Reed-Sternberg (RS) giant cells** — the neoplastic cell — which are typically vastly outnumbered by non-neoplastic inflammatory cells. Unlike most NHLs, it arises in a **single lymph node or chain** and spreads in a **stepwise, contiguous fashion** to anatomically adjacent nodes.

**Reed-Sternberg cell morphology** — very large cells (15–45 µm) with an enormous multilobate nucleus, exceptionally prominent nucleoli, and abundant slightly eosinophilic cytoplasm. The classic form has two mirror-image nuclear lobes each containing a large acidophilic nucleolus surrounded by a clear zone — the **"owl-eye" appearance.** RS cells express **CD15 and CD30** and fail to express CD45, B cell antigens, and T cell antigens.

**Origin of RS cells** — long mysterious, but molecular studies on single microdissected RS cells showed every RS cell in a given case carries identical immunoglobulin gene rearrangements that have undergone somatic hypermutation → Hodgkin lymphoma is a **neoplasm of germinal centre B cells** that has lost normal B cell gene expression programmes.

**EBV and pathogenesis** — EBV is present in RS cells in up to **70% of mixed-cellularity cases** and a smaller fraction of other classical subtypes. EBV genome integration is identical in all RS cells within a case, indicating infection precedes transformation. RS cells secrete several cytokines generating the characteristic inflammatory background: **IL-5** (attracts eosinophils), **TGF-β** (fibrogenic), and **IL-13** (may stimulate RS cells via autocrine loop). In turn, responding inflammatory cells produce CD30 ligand which aids RS cell growth and survival.

### Subtypes
**Nodular Sclerosis** — most common subtype; equally frequent in men and women; strong predilection for lower cervical, supraclavicular, and mediastinal lymph nodes; most patients are adolescents or young adults; overall prognosis excellent.
- Characteristic RS cell variant: **lacunar cell** — large cell with single multilobate nucleus, multiple small nucleoli, and abundant pale cytoplasm; in formalin-fixed tissue, cytoplasm retracts leaving nucleus in an empty space (lacune)
- Collagen bands divide involved lymphoid tissue into circumscribed nodules; fibrosis may be scant or abundant; cellular infiltrate contains lymphocytes, eosinophils, histiocytes, and lacunar cells

**Mixed Cellularity** — most common subtype in patients >50 years; comprises ~25% of all cases; male predominance. Classic RS cells are plentiful within a heterogeneous inflammatory infiltrate of small lymphocytes, eosinophils, plasma cells, and macrophages. More likely to be disseminated and associated with systemic manifestations than nodular sclerosis.

**Lymphocyte-Rich** — abundant lymphocytes; rare RS cells; best prognosis among classical subtypes.

**Lymphocyte Depletion** — few lymphocytes; many RS cells or fibrosis; worst prognosis; often advanced stage at presentation.

**Lymphocyte Predominance** — accounts for ~5% of Hodgkin lymphoma; set apart as a distinct entity. Characteristic RS cell variant: **lymphohistiocytic (L&H) "popcorn cell"** — delicate multilobed puffy nucleus. L&H variants are found within large nodules of mainly small resting B cells with variable macrophages; eosinophils, neutrophils, and plasma cells are scanty or absent. Unlike classical RS cells, **L&H variants express CD20** and usually fail to express CD15 and CD30. Most patients present with isolated cervical or axillary lymphadenopathy; prognosis typically excellent.

⚠️ Histologic diagnosis of Hodgkin lymphoma **rests on definitive identification of RS cells or variants in the appropriate reactive background.** Immunophenotyping plays an important adjunct role — some forms closely mimic inflammatory reactive processes.

### Clinical Features and Staging

| Feature | Hodgkin Lymphoma | Non-Hodgkin Lymphoma |
|:--------|:----------------|:--------------------|
| Node involvement | Single axial group (cervical, mediastinal, para-aortic) | Multiple peripheral nodes |
| Spread | Orderly, contiguous | Noncontiguous |
| Mesenteric nodes / Waldeyer ring | Rarely involved | Commonly involved |
| Extranodal involvement | Uncommon | Common |

**Ann Arbor Staging:**

| Stage | Distribution |
|:------|:------------|
| I | Single lymph node region (I) or single extralymphatic organ (IE) |
| II | Two or more lymph node regions on same side of diaphragm (II) ± limited contiguous extralymphatic organ (IIE) |
| III | Lymph node regions on both sides of diaphragm (III) ± spleen (IIIS) ± extralymphatic organ (IIIE) |
| IV | Multiple or disseminated extralymphatic organ involvement ± lymphatic involvement |

All stages further divided: **A** (no systemic symptoms) vs **B** (fever, night sweats, unexplained >10% weight loss).

Younger patients with favourable subtypes tend to present at stage I or II without systemic symptoms. Advanced disease (stages III and IV) more likely to present with systemic complaints, fever, weight loss, pruritus, and anaemia.

**Treatment** — even stage I disease is now treated with systemic chemotherapy due to long-term complications of radiotherapy. Advanced disease is treated with chemotherapy ± involved-field radiotherapy at sites of bulky disease. **Anti-CD30 antibodies** have produced excellent responses in disease failing conventional therapy.

**Prognosis** — outcomes are very good even with advanced disease:
- Stage I-A or II-A → **~100% 5-year survival**
- Stage IV-A or IV-B → **~50% 5-year disease-free survival**

⚠️ Long-term survivors treated with radiotherapy have significantly higher risk of secondary malignancies including **lung cancer, melanoma, and breast cancer** — this has driven development of less toxic treatment regimens.

### Miscellaneous Lymphoid Neoplasms

### Extranodal Marginal Zone Lymphoma
Indolent B cell tumour arising most commonly in epithelial tissues — stomach, salivary glands, small and large bowel, lungs, orbit, and breast. Develops in the setting of **autoimmune disorders** (Sjögren syndrome, Hashimoto thyroiditis) or **chronic infection** (*H. pylori* gastritis) — sustained antigenic stimulation contributes to development.

In ***H. pylori*-associated gastric marginal zone lymphoma**, eradication of the organism with antibiotic therapy often leads to tumour regression — tumour cells depend on cytokines secreted by *H. pylori*-specific T cells for growth and survival. When arising at other sites, often cured by local excision or radiotherapy.

Most common recurrent genetic abnormality: **(11;18) translocation** involving MALT1 and IAP2 genes. Presence of t(11;18) is highly predictive of **failure to respond to antibiotic treatment** in gastric tumours — an important clinical point.

### Hairy Cell Leukaemia
Uncommon, indolent B cell neoplasm. Tumour cells have **fine hairlike cytoplasmic projections** and express pan-B cell markers (CD19, CD20), surface immunoglobulin, **CD11c and CD103** — the latter two antigens are diagnostically useful as they are absent from most other B cell tumours. Virtually all cases are associated with **activating mutations in BRAF** serine/threonine kinase (same mutation found in diverse other cancers including melanoma).

Occurs mainly in **older males**. Manifestations result from bone marrow and spleen infiltration. **Massive splenomegaly** is the most common and sometimes only physical finding. Pancytopenia occurs in >50% of cases from marrow infiltration and splenic sequestration. Lymphadenopathy is rare. Leukaemia is present in only 15–20% but scattered hairy cells can be identified on peripheral blood smear in most cases.

Indolent but progressive if untreated. Unlike most other indolent lymphoid neoplasms, this tumour is **extremely sensitive to purine nucleoside chemotherapy** → complete durable responses are the rule; overall prognosis is excellent.

### Mycosis Fungoides and Sézary Syndrome
Tumours of neoplastic **CD4+ T cells** that home to the skin — collectively called **cutaneous T cell lymphoma.**

**Mycosis fungoides** — usually manifests as a nonspecific erythrodermic rash → progresses to a **plaque phase** → then a **tumour phase.** Histologically, neoplastic T cells with **cerebriform nuclei** (produced by marked nuclear membrane infolding) infiltrate the epidermis and upper dermis (**Pautrier microabscesses**). With disease progression, nodal and visceral dissemination appears.

**Sézary syndrome** — clinical variant characterised by (1) generalised **exfoliative erythroderma** and (2) circulating tumour cells (**Sézary cells**) in the peripheral blood. Circulating tumour cells are also present in up to 25% of plaque- or tumour-phase mycosis fungoides.

**Prognosis** — patients with early-phase mycosis fungoides often survive many years; tumour-phase disease, visceral disease, or Sézary syndrome → average survival 1–3 years.

### Adult T Cell Leukaemia/Lymphoma
Neoplasm of **CD4+ T cells** caused by **HTLV-1** (human T cell leukaemia virus type 1), a retrovirus. HTLV-1 infection is endemic in southern Japan, the Caribbean basin, and West Africa; occurs sporadically elsewhere including southeastern United States. In addition to lymphoid malignancies, HTLV-1 can cause **transverse myelitis** — a progressive demyelinating disease of the CNS and spinal cord.

**Clinical features** — skin lesions, lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, hypercalcaemia, and variable numbers of malignant lymphocytes in peripheral blood. Leukaemic cells express CD4 and high levels of **CD25** (IL-2 receptor α chain).

**Prognosis** — most cases are very aggressive and respond poorly to treatment; median survival ~8 months. In 15–20% of patients, disease follows a chronic course resembling mycosis fungoides.

### Peripheral T Cell Lymphomas
Heterogeneous group comprising **10–15% of adult NHLs.** Most tumours are unclassifiable. Generally aggressive and respond poorly to therapy. Because these are tumours of functional T cells, patients often suffer symptoms from **tumour-derived inflammatory products** even at low tumour burden.

### Myeloid Neoplasms
**Conditions covered:** Acute Myeloid Leukaemia · Myelodysplastic Syndromes · Chronic Myeloproliferative Disorders (CML · Polycythaemia Vera · Primary Myelofibrosis)

Three broad categories:
- **Acute myeloid leukaemias (AML)** — neoplastic cells blocked at an early stage of myeloid development; immature blasts accumulate in marrow and peripheral blood
- **Myeloproliferative disorders** — neoplastic clone retains terminal differentiation capacity; increased production of formed elements; commonly associated with activating tyrosine kinase mutations
- **Myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS)** — terminal differentiation occurs but is disordered and ineffective; dysplastic marrow precursors + peripheral blood cytopenias

All myeloid neoplasms arise from early multipotent progenitors; myelodysplastic syndromes and myeloproliferative disorders both can transform to AML.

### Acute Myeloid Leukaemia (AML)
Primarily affects older adults; **median age 50 years.** Clinical signs and symptoms closely resemble ALL — fatigue, pallor, abnormal bleeding, and infections — usually presenting within weeks of symptom onset. Splenomegaly and lymphadenopathy are generally less prominent than in ALL. On rare occasions AML mimics a lymphoma by manifesting as a discrete tissue mass (**granulocytic sarcoma**).

**Pathogenesis** — most AMLs harbour mutations in genes encoding transcription factors required for normal myeloid cell differentiation → maturation arrest → accumulation of myeloid precursors (blasts). These mutations are not sufficient alone; complementary mutations that enhance proliferation and survival are also required. Examples include **FLT3** (receptor tyrosine kinase) activating mutations and RAS mutations.

**Morphology** — by definition, myeloid blasts or promyelocytes compose **>20% of bone marrow cellularity.** Myeloblasts have delicate nuclear chromatin, 3–5 nucleoli, and fine azurophilic cytoplasmic granules. **Auer rods** — distinctive red-staining rodlike structures — are specific for neoplastic myeloblasts and are a helpful diagnostic clue when present; particularly numerous in acute promyelocytic leukaemia.

**Acute Promyelocytic Leukaemia — a key subtype:**
- **(15;17) translocation** → fusion of RARA (chromosome 17) with PML (chromosome 15) → PML/RARα fusion protein → blocks myeloid differentiation at promyelocytic stage
- Pharmacologic doses of **all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA)** overcome the differentiation block → neoplastic promyelocytes rapidly differentiate into neutrophils → which die within 6 hours → tumour rapidly cleared
- Combination of **ATRA + arsenic trioxide** (which degrades the PML/RARα fusion protein) is even more effective → **cures >80% of patients**
- This is an important example of highly effective targeted therapy directed at a tumour-specific molecular defect
- AMLs without RARA translocations do NOT respond to ATRA

**WHO Classification of AML:**

| Class | Examples | Prognosis |
|:------|:---------|:----------|
| AML with recurrent chromosomal translocations | t(8;21), inv(16) — favourable; t(15;17) — favourable; t(11q23) MLL fusions — poor | Varies |
| AML with multilineage dysplasia | With or without prior MDS | Very poor to poor |
| AML, therapy-related | Alkylating agent–related; epipodophyllotoxin-related | Very poor |
| AML, not otherwise classified | Subclassed by predominant differentiation | Intermediate |

**Immunophenotype** — most tumours express myeloid-associated antigens: CD13, CD14, CD15, CD64, CD117 (cKIT), and CD33. Useful in distinguishing AML from ALL.

**Prognosis** — devastating overall. Tumours with good-risk karyotypes [t(8;21), inv(16)] → ~50% long-term disease-free survival. Overall survival with conventional chemotherapy is only **15–30%.** Increasing use of allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation for eligible patients.

### Myelodysplastic Syndromes (MDS)
Bone marrow partly or wholly replaced by the clonal progeny of a transformed multipotent stem cell that retains capacity to differentiate into red cells, granulocytes, and platelets but in a **disordered and ineffective** manner. Marrow is usually **hypercellular or normocellular** but peripheral blood shows one or more **cytopenias** — a hallmark paradox. The abnormal clone is genetically unstable → prone to additional mutations → eventual transformation to AML.

Most cases are **idiopathic**; some develop after chemotherapy with alkylating agents or exposure to ionising radiation.

**Pathogenesis** — poorly understood. Clonal cytogenetic abnormalities in up to 70% of cases. Translocations are generally lacking; losses or gains of whole chromosomes or parts thereof are frequent. Common abnormalities: monosomy 5 or 7; deletions of 5q, 7q, and 20q; trisomy 8. The **5q− syndrome** — deletion of 5q containing genes encoding a ribosomal protein and several microRNAs — occurs more often in women, is associated with severe anaemia and preserved/elevated platelet counts, and often responds to thalidomide analogues.

**Morphology** — abnormal-appearing haematopoietic precursors including:
- Megaloblastoid erythroid precursors
- **Ringed sideroblasts** — erythroid forms with iron deposits within mitochondria
- Granulocyte precursors with abnormal granules or nuclear maturation
- Small megakaryocytes with single small nuclei or multiple separate nuclei

**Clinical features** — MDS is about as common as AML, affecting up to **15,000 patients per year** in the United States. Most patients are between 50 and 70 years. Cytopenias lead to infections, anaemia symptoms, and haemorrhage. Response to conventional chemotherapy is usually poor. Transformation to AML occurs in **10–40%.** Median survival ranges from 9–29 months; worse with increased marrow blasts or cytogenetic abnormalities at diagnosis.

### Chronic Myelogenous Leukaemia (CML)
Principally affects adults between **25 and 60 years**; peak incidence in the fourth and fifth decades. ~4,500 new cases per year in the United States.

**Pathogenesis** — CML is **always** associated with a **BCR-ABL fusion gene.** In ~95% of cases, this results from a balanced **(9;22) translocation** — the **Philadelphia (Ph) chromosome** — moving ABL from chromosome 9 to chromosome 22 adjacent to BCR. In remaining 5%, cytogenetically cryptic or complex rearrangements are responsible. The BCR-ABL fusion gene is present in granulocytic, erythroid, megakaryocytic, B cell, and sometimes T cell precursors → tumour arises from a transformed **haematopoietic stem cell.** Ph chromosome is also present in 25% of adult B cell ALLs and a small subset of AMLs.

BCR-ABL encodes a fusion tyrosine kinase that **constitutively activates growth factor signalling pathways** → greatly decreased growth factor dependence of CML progenitors. Importantly, BCR-ABL does **not** inhibit differentiation → early disease course marked by excessive haematopoiesis rather than blast accumulation.

**Morphology:**
- Peripheral blood: leukocyte count elevated, often exceeding **100,000 cells/µL;** predominantly neutrophils, metamyelocytes, and myelocytes; basophils and eosinophils prominent; platelets usually increased; myeloblasts usually <5%
- Bone marrow: hypercellular with increased granulocytic and megakaryocytic precursors
- Spleen: markedly enlarged from extensive **extramedullary haematopoiesis** resembling bone marrow in the red pulp; proliferation may compromise local blood supply → splenic infarcts

**Clinical features** — onset is often insidious with nonspecific symptoms (fatigue, weakness, weight loss) or a dragging sensation in the abdomen from splenomegaly. Distinguished from leukemoid reaction by testing for the **BCR-ABL fusion gene** (by karyotyping, FISH, or PCR).

**Natural history:**
- Initially slow progression; even without treatment, median survival is 3 years
- After a variable period, ~50% enter an **accelerated phase** → increasing anaemia, new thrombocytopenia, additional cytogenetic abnormalities → **blast crisis**
- In remaining 50%, blast crisis occurs abruptly without accelerated phase
- In 30% of blast crises, the picture resembles **precursor-B cell ALL** (attesting to stem cell origin); in 70%, it resembles AML
- Less commonly, CML progresses to extensive bone marrow fibrosis resembling primary myelofibrosis

**Treatment** — the natural history has been dramatically altered by **BCR-ABL tyrosine kinase inhibitors** (imatinib, nilotinib) → complete remission in a high fraction of patients with little toxicity. Treatment may prevent blast crisis by suppressing the proliferative drive that leads to the acquisition of additional mutations that confer blast crisis.

### Histiocytic Neoplasms — Langerhans Cell Histiocytoses
**Key concepts:** Pathogenesis · Three clinical forms · BRAF mutation

"Histiocytosis" encompasses a variety of proliferative disorders of dendritic cells or macrophages. The Langerhans cell histiocytoses are a group of relatively rare tumours composed of **Langerhans cells** — immature dendritic cells normally found in the epidermis that capture antigens and display them to T cells.

The proliferating Langerhans cells express **MHC class II antigens, CD1a, and langerin.** Langerin is found in **Birbeck granules** — cytoplasmic pentalaminar rodlike tubular structures with a characteristic periodicity and sometimes a dilated terminal end giving a **"tennis racket" appearance** on electron microscopy. Under light microscopy, proliferating Langerhans cells have abundant vacuolated cytoplasm and vesicular nuclei resembling tissue macrophages rather than their normal dendritic counterparts.

**BRAF mutation** — different clinical forms are frequently associated with an acquired **BRAF V600E mutation** (valine to glutamate at residue 600) → kinase hyperactivity → drives cellular proliferation and survival. The same mutation is found in hairy cell leukaemia, benign nevi, malignant melanoma, papillary thyroid carcinoma, and some colon cancers.

### Three Clinical Forms

**1. Multisystem Langerhans Cell Histiocytosis (Letterer-Siwe Disease)**
Usually occurs in children **<2 years of age.** Manifests with multifocal cutaneous lesions resembling seborrheic skin eruptions composed of Langerhans cells. Most affected patients have hepatosplenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, pulmonary lesions, and destructive osteolytic bone lesions. Extensive marrow infiltration → pancytopenia → recurrent infections (otitis media, mastoiditis). **Rapidly fatal if untreated;** with intensive chemotherapy, 50% survive 5 years.

**2. Unisystem Langerhans Cell Histiocytosis (Eosinophilic Granuloma)**
May be unifocal or multifocal. Characterised by expanding, erosive accumulations of Langerhans cells within medullary cavities of bones (most commonly calvaria, ribs, and femur) or less commonly skin, lungs, or stomach. Langerhans cells are admixed with lymphocytes, plasma cells, neutrophils, and usually prominent eosinophils.

- **Unifocal disease** — most often skeletal; may be asymptomatic or cause pain, tenderness, and pathologic fractures; indolent and may heal spontaneously or be cured by local excision or irradiation
- **Multifocal unisystem disease** — usually affects children; multiple erosive bony masses sometimes extending into soft tissues; in ~50% of cases, involvement of the posterior pituitary stalk leads to **diabetes insipidus;** the combination of **calvarial bone defects + diabetes insipidus + exophthalmos = Hand-Schüller-Christian triad;** many experience spontaneous regression; others treated effectively with chemotherapy

### Bleeding Disorders — Overview
**Conditions covered:** Vascular fragility disorders · Platelet disorders (thrombocytopenia, qualitative defects) · Coagulation factor deficiencies · DIC

Normal clotting involves the vessel wall, platelets, and clotting factors. Abnormalities in any of these components can lead to clinically significant bleeding.

**Key laboratory tests:**
- **Prothrombin time (PT)** — assesses the **extrinsic and common** coagulation pathways. Measures time (in seconds) for plasma to clot after addition of tissue thromboplastin and Ca²⁺. Prolonged PT → deficiency of factor V, VII, X, prothrombin, or fibrinogen, or an acquired inhibitor of the extrinsic pathway.
- **Partial thromboplastin time (PTT)** — assesses the **intrinsic and common** coagulation pathways. Prolonged PTT → deficiency of factor V, VIII, IX, X, XI, or XII, prothrombin, or fibrinogen, or an acquired inhibitor of the intrinsic pathway.
- **Platelet count** — reference range 150,000–450,000/µL. Counts outside this range must be confirmed by visual inspection of a peripheral blood smear.
- **Tests of platelet function** — platelet aggregation tests; qualitative and quantitative tests of von Willebrand factor. The older bleeding time test has some value but is difficult to standardise and rarely performed.

### Categories of Bleeding Disorders

**1. Vascular fragility** — petechiae and ecchymoses appear "spontaneously" in skin and mucous membranes. Coagulation tests are usually normal. Causes include vitamin C deficiency (scurvy), systemic amyloidosis, chronic glucocorticoid use, inherited connective tissue disorders, and infectious/hypersensitivity vasculitides (meningococcaemia, infective endocarditis, rickettsial diseases, typhoid, Henoch-Schönlein purpura).

**2. Thrombocytopenia and qualitative platelet defects** — easy bruising, nosebleeds, excessive bleeding from minor trauma, menorrhagia. Qualitative defects may be acquired (uraemia, myeloproliferative disorders, aspirin ingestion) or inherited (von Willebrand disease and other rare congenital disorders).

**3. Coagulation factor deficiencies** — PT, PTT, or both are prolonged. Petechiae and mucosal bleeding are usually absent. Instead, haemorrhages tend to occur at trauma-prone sites such as lower extremity joints. Massive haemorrhage may occur after surgery, dental procedures, or severe trauma. Includes the **haemophilias.**

**4. Mixed defects** — bleeding from a combination of mechanisms; examples include DIC (thrombocytopenia + coagulation factor deficiency) and von Willebrand disease (both platelet function and coagulation factor function impaired).

### Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC)
**Key concepts:** Pathogenesis · Consequences · Clinical features · Associations

DIC occurs as a complication of a wide variety of disorders. It is caused by **systemic activation of coagulation** throughout the microcirculation → formation of widespread microthrombi → consumption of platelets and coagulation factors → secondary activation of fibrinolysis. DIC therefore produces **two opposing consequences simultaneously:** tissue hypoxia/microinfarcts from microthrombi AND bleeding from depletion of haemostatic elements (**consumptive coagulopathy**). DIC probably causes bleeding more commonly than all congenital coagulation disorders combined.

**Pathogenesis — two main triggers:**

**1. Release of tissue factor / thromboplastic substances into the circulation:**
- Obstetric complications — tissue factor from placenta, retained dead fetus, or amniotic fluid
- Certain cancers — particularly acute promyelocytic leukaemia and adenocarcinomas; tumour cells release thromboplastic substances, proteolytic enzymes, and express tissue factor
- Gram-negative and gram-positive sepsis — endotoxins/exotoxins stimulate tissue factor release from monocytes; activated monocytes release IL-1 and TNF → stimulate tissue factor expression on endothelial cells and simultaneously decrease thrombomodulin expression → net result is enhanced thrombin generation and blunted inhibitory pathways

**2. Widespread endothelial cell damage:**
- Releases tissue factor and exposes subendothelial collagen and von Willebrand factor
- Even subtle endothelial damage can increase tissue factor expression on cell surfaces
- Causes include antigen-antibody complex deposition (e.g. SLE), temperature extremes (heat stroke, burns), infections (meningococci, rickettsiae), and endotoxaemia

**Most common clinical associations:** Sepsis, obstetric complications, malignancy, and **major trauma** (especially brain trauma — releases fat and phospholipids which activate the intrinsic coagulation cascade).

**Two main consequences:**

**1. Widespread fibrin deposition in microcirculation:**
- Obstruction → **ischaemia** in severely affected organs
- Red cells traumatised passing through fibrin-narrowed vessels → **microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia**

**2. Bleeding diathesis:**
- Depletion of platelets and clotting factors
- Secondary release of plasminogen activators → **plasmin** cleaves fibrin (fibrinolysis) AND factors V and VIII → further reduces their concentration
- Fibrin degradation products inhibit platelet aggregation, have antithrombin activity, and impair fibrin polymerisation → all contribute to haemostatic failure
