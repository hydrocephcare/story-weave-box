## Part 1: Influenza Virus

## 1. Introduction

Influenza is an acute respiratory illness caused by Influenza viruses. It is one of the most clinically and epidemiologically important viruses in medicine, responsible for seasonal epidemics and occasional global pandemics. Its unique ability to change its surface antigens — antigenic drift and antigenic shift — is why it remains a major global health threat and why the influenza vaccine must be reformulated every year.

At MKU, influenza is examined almost every year, primarily through the essay question on antigenic shift versus antigenic drift and their clinical significance.

## 2. Classification and Structure

- **Family:** Orthomyxoviridae
- **Genome:** Single-stranded, negative-sense RNA — segmented into 8 segments
- **Enveloped virus**
- **Types:** Influenza A, B, and C
  - Influenza A — causes epidemics and pandemics; infects humans, birds, pigs
  - Influenza B — causes epidemics; infects humans only; less severe
  - Influenza C — causes mild illness; not clinically significant

**Surface glycoproteins — the two most important structures:**

- **Haemagglutinin (H or HA)**
  - Binds sialic acid receptors on respiratory epithelial cells
  - Mediates viral attachment and fusion with host cell
  - Target of neutralizing antibodies — antibodies against H prevent infection
  - 18 subtypes identified (H1 to H18)
- **Neuraminidase (N or NA)**
  - Cleaves sialic acid to release newly formed virions from infected cells
  - Prevents viral aggregation
  - Facilitates spread through mucus
  - Target of neuraminidase inhibitor drugs
  - 11 subtypes identified (N1 to N11)

**Internal proteins:**
- **M2 protein** — ion channel; involved in uncoating; target of adamantane drugs
- **Nucleoprotein (NP)** — determines type (A, B, or C)
- **RNA polymerase complex** — error-prone; no proofreading; drives antigenic drift

## 3. Antigenic Variation — The Highest Yield Topic

This is the single most important concept in influenza and has appeared as an essay question in multiple MKU past papers.

### Antigenic Drift — Minor Antigenic Change

**Definition:** Small, gradual, cumulative changes in the H or N antigens caused by point mutations in the viral RNA during replication.

**Mechanism:**
- The RNA polymerase of influenza has no proofreading ability
- Random point mutations accumulate in the genes encoding H and N
- Over time, the surface antigens change enough that existing antibodies no longer recognize them
- The immune system is partially but not fully effective against the new strain

**Clinical significance:**
- Occurs continuously and gradually
- Responsible for seasonal influenza epidemics every year
- Explains why the influenza vaccine must be updated annually
- Affects both Influenza A and Influenza B

### Antigenic Shift — Major Antigenic Change

**Definition:** Sudden, dramatic change in the H or N antigens resulting from genetic reassortment between two different influenza A strains infecting the same host simultaneously.

**Mechanism:**
- Influenza A infects multiple species — humans, birds, pigs
- When two different Influenza A strains infect the same cell simultaneously (e.g., a human strain and a bird strain infecting a pig)
- The 8 RNA segments from each strain mix randomly during replication — reassortment
- A new hybrid virus emerges with a completely new combination of H and N antigens
- Human population has no pre-existing immunity to the new H or N subtype

**Clinical significance:**
- Occurs suddenly and unpredictably
- Produces a completely new subtype that the human population has never encountered
- No existing immunity in the population
- Responsible for influenza pandemics
- Only affects Influenza A — because only Influenza A infects multiple species
- Historical pandemics caused by antigenic shift:
  - 1918 Spanish flu — H1N1 — killed 50 million people
  - 1957 Asian flu — H2N2
  - 1968 Hong Kong flu — H3N2
  - 2009 Swine flu pandemic — H1N1 (new reassortant)

### Key Comparison

| Feature | Antigenic Drift | Antigenic Shift |
|---|---|---|
| Type of change | Minor, gradual | Major, sudden |
| Mechanism | Point mutations | Genetic reassortment |
| Viruses affected | Influenza A and B | Influenza A only |
| Result | New variant of same subtype | Entirely new subtype |
| Immunity | Partial pre-existing immunity | No pre-existing immunity |
| Consequence | Seasonal epidemics | Pandemics |
| Vaccine impact | Annual reformulation needed | Existing vaccine ineffective |

## 4. Replication Cycle

1. Haemagglutinin binds sialic acid receptors on respiratory epithelial cells
2. Virus enters by endocytosis
3. Low pH in endosome activates H — triggers fusion of viral and endosomal membranes
4. M2 ion channel allows H+ ions into virion — acidifies interior — uncoating occurs
5. 8 RNA segments released into cytoplasm, transported to nucleus
6. Viral RNA polymerase transcribes RNA segments → mRNA → viral proteins
7. Genome RNA also replicated in nucleus
8. New virions assembled and bud from cell surface
9. Neuraminidase cleaves sialic acid — releases new virions

## 5. Transmission

- Respiratory droplets — primary route
- Airborne transmission in enclosed spaces
- Fomites — virus survives on surfaces for several hours
- Direct contact with infected secretions

## 6. Clinical Features

**Incubation period:** 1–4 days

**Typical influenza:**
- Sudden onset — distinguishes it from common cold
- High fever (38–40°C)
- Severe myalgia and arthralgia
- Headache
- Dry cough
- Sore throat
- Nasal congestion
- Fatigue and malaise — can be severe and prolonged

**Complications:**
- Primary viral pneumonia — most serious
- Secondary bacterial pneumonia — Streptococcus pneumoniae, Staphylococcus aureus, H. influenzae
- Myocarditis
- Encephalitis
- Reye's syndrome — in children given aspirin during influenza infection (avoid aspirin in children)

**High-risk groups for severe disease:**

- Elderly (over 65)
- Young children
- Pregnant women
- Immunocompromised
- Chronic cardiopulmonary disease

## 7. Diagnosis

- **Clinical diagnosis** — typical presentation during epidemic season
- **Rapid antigen test** — nasal swab; quick but less sensitive
- **RT-PCR** — gold standard; detects viral RNA; highly sensitive and specific
- **Viral culture** — from nasopharyngeal swab; slow but definitive
- **Serology** — four-fold rise in antibody titres between acute and convalescent samples

## 8. Treatment

### Neuraminidase Inhibitors — First Line
- **Oseltamivir (Tamiflu)** — oral; most widely used
- **Zanamivir** — inhaled
- **Mechanism:** Block neuraminidase → new virions cannot be released from infected cells → spread is halted
- **Effective against:** Influenza A and B
- Must be started within 48 hours of symptom onset for maximum benefit

### Adamantanes — Second Line
- **Amantadine** and **Rimantadine**
- **Mechanism:** Block M2 ion channel → prevent uncoating of virus inside endosome → virus cannot release its RNA into the cell
- **Effective against:** Influenza A only — Influenza B has no M2 protein
- High resistance rates — not recommended as monotherapy currently

### Supportive Treatment
- Rest, hydration, antipyretics (paracetamol — not aspirin in children)

## 9. Vaccines

**Seasonal influenza vaccine:**
- Reformulated annually based on WHO predictions of circulating strains
- Contains H and N antigens from predicted strains — usually 3 or 4 strains (trivalent or quadrivalent)
- Two types available:
  - **Inactivated influenza vaccine (IIV)** — killed virus; injected; suitable for all including immunocompromised
  - **Live attenuated influenza vaccine (LAIV)** — weakened live virus; nasal spray; not for immunocompromised

**Why the vaccine does not confer lifelong protection:**
- Antigenic drift continuously changes the surface antigens
- Antibodies from last year's vaccine may not recognize this year's strains
- Annual reformulation and revaccination required

## Part 2: Viral Receptors and Spikes

## 1. Introduction

Viral receptors and spikes are among the most clinically important concepts in virology. Every virus must attach to a specific receptor on a host cell before it can infect it — this step is called adsorption or attachment, and it is the first and most critical step in viral pathogenesis. The viral surface proteins (spikes) that mediate this attachment are also the primary targets of the host immune system and of antiviral drugs. Understanding viral receptors and spikes explains tissue tropism, disease manifestations, transmission routes, and the basis of antiviral therapy and vaccines.

At MKU, this topic has appeared as a major Section C essay question multiple times, asking students to discuss the clinical importance of viral receptors and spikes with specific examples.

## 2. What Are Viral Spikes

Viral spikes are glycoprotein projections that extend from the surface of enveloped viruses. They are encoded by the viral genome and inserted into the viral envelope. They serve as the primary means by which a virus recognizes and binds to host cell receptors.

**Functions of viral spikes:**
- Attachment to specific host cell receptors — determines which cells can be infected
- Fusion with host cell membrane — allows viral genome to enter the cell
- Immune evasion — some spikes help virus evade antibody recognition
- Targets of neutralizing antibodies — antibodies against spikes can block infection
- Targets of antiviral drugs

## 3. What Are Viral Receptors

Viral receptors are normal host cell surface molecules — proteins, glycoproteins, carbohydrates, or lipids — that viruses have evolved to exploit for entry. The virus does not create these receptors — it hijacks molecules that the cell uses for its own normal functions.

**The receptor determines:**
- Which cell types can be infected — tissue tropism
- Which species can be infected — host range
- Which route of transmission is possible
- What disease the virus causes

## 4. Clinical Importance of Viral Receptors and Spikes — With Examples

### HIV — gp120 and CD4

- **Viral spike:** gp120 (surface) and gp41 (transmembrane) — together form the envelope glycoprotein complex
- **Host receptor:** CD4 molecule on T-helper lymphocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells
- **Co-receptors:** CCR5 (macrophage-tropic strains) and CXCR4 (T-cell tropic strains)
- **Clinical importance:**
  - HIV can only infect CD4+ cells — explains selective destruction of T-helper cells
  - Loss of CD4+ cells → immunodeficiency → AIDS
  - Individuals with CCR5 deletion mutation (CCR5-delta32) are naturally resistant to HIV infection
  - Maraviroc (CCR5 antagonist) blocks the co-receptor — used as an antiretroviral drug
  - Enfuvirtide (Fuzeon) blocks gp41-mediated fusion — another drug target at this step

### Influenza — Haemagglutinin and Sialic Acid

- **Viral spike:** Haemagglutinin (H)
- **Host receptor:** Sialic acid residues on respiratory epithelial cells
  - Human influenza strains bind alpha-2,6-linked sialic acid — found in human upper respiratory tract
  - Avian influenza strains bind alpha-2,3-linked sialic acid — found in human lower respiratory tract and bird intestines
- **Clinical importance:**
  - Explains why human influenza primarily causes upper respiratory symptoms
  - Explains why avian influenza (H5N1) causes severe pneumonia — it infects deep lung tissue
  - Pigs have both receptor types — can be infected by both human and avian strains simultaneously — this is why pigs are mixing vessels for antigenic shift
  - Neutralizing antibodies against H prevent attachment — basis of influenza vaccine

### Rabies — Glycoprotein G and Acetylcholine Receptor

- **Viral spike:** Glycoprotein G
- **Host receptor:** Nicotinic acetylcholine receptor at neuromuscular junctions; also NCAM (neural cell adhesion molecule) and p75 neurotrophin receptor
- **Clinical importance:**
  - Explains why rabies virus infects neurons specifically — neurotropism
  - Virus travels along peripheral nerves to the CNS — explains the pathogenesis of rabies
  - Explains why bite wounds near the CNS (face, neck) cause faster disease progression than distal wounds (feet)
  - Glycoprotein G is the target of rabies vaccine — induces protective neutralizing antibodies

### Epstein-Barr Virus — gp350 and CD21

- **Viral spike:** gp350/220
- **Host receptor:** CD21 (complement receptor 2, CR2) on B lymphocytes and epithelial cells
- **Clinical importance:**
  - EBV specifically infects B cells — explains B cell immortalization and lymphoma development
  - Explains why EBV causes infectious mononucleosis — massive B cell proliferation
  - CD21 is also involved in complement regulation — virus exploits this normal immune molecule
  - EBV latency in B cells is the basis of Burkitt's lymphoma and nasopharyngeal carcinoma

### Rhinovirus — Viral Protein and ICAM-1

- **Viral spike:** VP1, VP2, VP3 capsid proteins forming a canyon structure
- **Host receptor:** ICAM-1 (Intercellular Adhesion Molecule 1) on upper respiratory epithelial cells
- **Clinical importance:**
  - Explains why rhinovirus causes common cold — ICAM-1 is abundant in nasal epithelium
  - ICAM-1 is also a normal immune cell adhesion molecule — virus exploits it
  - Attempts to develop drugs blocking ICAM-1 binding have been explored

### Poliovirus — Viral Protein and PVR

- **Viral spike:** VP1 capsid protein
- **Host receptor:** PVR (Poliovirus Receptor — CD155) on motor neurons and intestinal cells
- **Clinical importance:**
  - Explains why poliovirus specifically destroys anterior horn motor neurons → flaccid paralysis
  - Virus enters via gut but travels to CNS via bloodstream and neural routes
  - Only primates express PVR — explains host range of poliovirus

## 5. Tissue Tropism

Tissue tropism means the preference of a virus for specific tissues or cell types. It is determined almost entirely by the match between the viral spike and the host receptor.

| Virus | Receptor | Tissue Tropism | Disease |
|---|---|---|---|
| HIV | CD4 / CCR5 / CXCR4 | T-helper cells, macrophages | AIDS |
| Influenza | Sialic acid (alpha-2,6) | Respiratory epithelium | Flu |
| Rabies | Acetylcholine receptor | Neurons | Encephalitis |
| EBV | CD21 | B lymphocytes | Mononucleosis, lymphoma |
| Poliovirus | CD155 (PVR) | Motor neurons | Paralysis |
| Rhinovirus | ICAM-1 | Nasal epithelium | Common cold |
| Hepatitis B | NTCP receptor | Hepatocytes | Hepatitis |

## 6. Receptors as Drug and Vaccine Targets

Because viral spikes and host receptors mediate the first and most critical step of infection, they are prime targets for antiviral strategies:

- **Vaccines** — most viral vaccines work by inducing antibodies against surface spike proteins, which block receptor binding and prevent infection
- **Fusion inhibitors** — Enfuvirtide blocks HIV gp41 fusion
- **CCR5 antagonists** — Maraviroc blocks HIV co-receptor
- **Neuraminidase inhibitors** — Oseltamivir blocks influenza NA, preventing viral release
- **Monoclonal antibodies** — can be designed to block specific spike-receptor interactions

## Part 3: Interferons

## 1. Introduction

Interferons are a family of signalling proteins produced by host cells in response to viral infection. They are part of the innate (non-specific) immune system — the body's first line of defence against viruses. They do not directly kill viruses but instead create an antiviral state in surrounding cells, slow viral replication, and activate immune cells to destroy infected cells. Interferons are critically important in limiting the spread of viral infection in the early days before the adaptive immune response (antibodies and T cells) has time to develop.

At MKU, interferons have appeared as a standalone essay question in almost every past paper — typically asking students to explain how interferons help virus-infected cells fight back the infection.

## 2. Types of Interferons

**Type I Interferons — Antiviral Interferons**
- **Interferon-alpha (IFN-α)**
  - Produced by: infected leukocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells
  - Main antiviral interferon
  - Multiple subtypes (IFN-α1, IFN-α2, etc.)
- **Interferon-beta (IFN-β)**
  - Produced by: infected fibroblasts and epithelial cells
  - Also antiviral
- Both IFN-α and IFN-β bind the same receptor — IFNAR (Type I interferon receptor)

**Type II Interferon — Immune Interferon**
- **Interferon-gamma (IFN-γ)**
  - Produced by: activated T lymphocytes and NK cells
  - Primarily an immune activator rather than direct antiviral
  - Activates macrophages to kill intracellular pathogens
  - Binds a different receptor — IFNGR

**Type III Interferons**
- **IFN-lambda (IFN-λ)**
  - Produced at mucosal surfaces — especially respiratory and gastrointestinal tract
  - Important in antiviral defence at mucosal barriers

## 3. How Interferons Are Produced — The Trigger

1. Virus infects a cell
2. Viral components — particularly double-stranded RNA (dsRNA), a byproduct of viral replication — are detected by pattern recognition receptors inside the cell
3. Key receptors: **TLR3** (in endosomes), **RIG-I** and **MDA5** (in cytoplasm)
4. These receptors trigger signalling cascades → activation of transcription factors **IRF3, IRF7, and NF-κB**
5. These transcription factors enter the nucleus and switch on **IFN-α and IFN-β genes**
6. Interferons are synthesized and secreted from the infected cell

## 4. How Interferons Work — Mechanism of Action

Interferons do not directly destroy viruses. Instead they act on both infected and neighbouring uninfected cells to create an antiviral state.

### Step 1: Interferon Secretion
- Infected cell secretes IFN-α and IFN-β
- These act in an autocrine manner (on the infected cell itself) and paracrine manner (on neighbouring cells)

### Step 2: Receptor Binding and Signalling
- IFN-α/β binds IFNAR on the surface of target cells
- Triggers JAK-STAT signalling pathway:
  - JAK1 and TYK2 (kinases) are activated
  - They phosphorylate STAT1 and STAT2
  - Phosphorylated STATs form a complex with IRF9 → called ISGF3
  - ISGF3 enters the nucleus → binds ISRE (Interferon Stimulated Response Element) sequences in DNA
  - Switches on hundreds of Interferon Stimulated Genes (ISGs)

### Step 3: Antiviral Proteins Produced

The ISGs encode several key antiviral proteins:

**Protein Kinase R (PKR):**
- Activated by double-stranded RNA
- Phosphorylates eIF2-alpha → blocks protein synthesis in the cell
- Stops viral protein production — also stops host protein production (cell accepts this sacrifice to stop viral spread)

**2'-5' Oligoadenylate Synthetase (OAS) and RNase L:**
- OAS activated by dsRNA → produces 2'-5' oligoadenylates
- These activate RNase L — an enzyme that degrades all RNA in the cell, both viral and host
- Destroys viral mRNA → stops viral replication

**Mx Proteins (Myxovirus Resistance Proteins):**
- GTPases that specifically interfere with viral RNA synthesis
- Particularly effective against influenza and other RNA viruses
- Block transport of viral components within the cell

**TRIM proteins and ISG15:**

- Further restrict viral replication through multiple mechanisms

### Step 4: Activation of Immune Cells
- Interferons activate **Natural Killer (NK) cells** — NK cells kill virus-infected cells directly
- Interferons increase **MHC class I expression** on infected cells — makes them more visible to cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CD8+ T cells)
- Interferons activate macrophages — enhance phagocytosis of infected cells and viral debris
- Interferons promote maturation of dendritic cells — bridge innate and adaptive immunity

### Step 5: Apoptosis of Infected Cells
- Interferons can trigger apoptosis (programmed cell death) in heavily infected cells
- This is a sacrifice — the infected cell destroys itself to prevent further viral spread
- Neighbouring cells are warned and enter antiviral state before they get infected

## 5. Summary — How Interferons Fight Viral Infection

| Action | Effect |
|---|---|
| Block protein synthesis (PKR) | Stops viral protein production |
| Degrade RNA (OAS/RNase L) | Destroys viral genome and mRNA |
| Mx proteins | Interfere with viral RNA synthesis |
| Activate NK cells | Kill infected cells directly |
| Upregulate MHC I | Infected cells targeted by CD8+ T cells |
| Activate macrophages | Enhanced clearance of infected cells |
| Trigger apoptosis | Infected cells self-destruct |
| Warn neighbouring cells | Create antiviral state before infection spreads |

## 6. Viral Evasion of Interferons

Many viruses have evolved mechanisms to block interferon production or action:

- **Influenza NS1 protein** — blocks RIG-I sensing of viral RNA; prevents IFN production
- **Hepatitis C virus** — blocks JAK-STAT signalling; interferons cannot activate ISGs
- **Adenovirus VA RNA** — blocks PKR activation
- **HIV Vif protein** — degrades host antiviral proteins
- **Herpesviruses** — multiple mechanisms including blocking IRF3 activation

This is why some viral infections are severe — the virus disables the first line of defence before the adaptive immune system can respond.

## 7. Clinical Use of Interferons

Interferons are used therapeutically:

- **IFN-α (Pegylated)** — treatment of chronic Hepatitis B and Hepatitis C (now largely replaced by direct-acting antivirals for HCV)
- **IFN-β** — treatment of Multiple Sclerosis (reduces relapse frequency)
- **IFN-γ** — treatment of chronic granulomatous disease
- **IFN-α** — treatment of certain malignancies (hairy cell leukaemia, Kaposi's sarcoma)

## Part 4: Viral Pathogenesis

## 1. Introduction

Viral pathogenesis refers to the sequence of events that occur from the moment a virus enters the host body to the point where disease is produced. Understanding pathogenesis explains why different viruses cause different diseases, why some infections are mild and others fatal, why some viruses are localized while others spread throughout the body, and how the host immune system responds. It also forms the scientific basis for designing antiviral drugs, vaccines, and other prevention strategies.

At MKU, the stages of viral pathogenesis have appeared as both MCQ and essay questions repeatedly — it is one of the most fundamental topics in the entire virology course.

## 2. Stages of Viral Pathogenesis — The Core Framework

### Stage 1: Entry and Inoculation

The virus must first gain access to the body through a portal of entry.

**Portals of entry:**
- **Respiratory tract** — most common; influenza, measles, mumps, RSV, rhinovirus, adenovirus
- **Gastrointestinal tract** — fecal-oral route; poliovirus, hepatitis A, rotavirus, adenovirus
- **Genital/urogenital mucosa** — sexual transmission; HIV, HSV-2, HPV, HBV
- **Skin** — through breaks, bites, or injections; rabies (animal bite), arbovirus (insect bite), HIV (needlestick)
- **Conjunctiva** — adenovirus, HSV
- **Transplacental** — vertical transmission; CMV, rubella, HIV, HSV

**The portal of entry influences:**
- Which tissues are first infected
- How the disease begins
- What prevention strategies are possible

### Stage 2: Primary Replication

- After entry, the virus replicates locally at the site of entry
- This is called primary replication or local infection
- Produces the initial viral load
- May cause local symptoms — e.g., sore throat in influenza, vesicles in HSV, diarrhea in rotavirus
- Some viruses remain localized here and never spread further — e.g., rhinovirus (common cold), HPV (warts)
- Other viruses use this as a launching pad for systemic spread

### Stage 3: Spread to Regional Lymph Nodes

- Virus drains from local site to regional lymph nodes via lymphatics
- Replicates further in lymph nodes
- Lymph nodes may become enlarged — lymphadenopathy
- Some viruses are cleared here by the immune system — infection remains localized

### Stage 4: Primary Viremia

- Virus escapes lymph nodes and enters the bloodstream — **viremia**
- This is called primary viremia — usually a brief, low-level phase
- Allows virus to reach distant organs
- Patient may have non-specific symptoms — fever, malaise, myalgia
- Virus may be carried by:
  - Free in plasma
  - Inside blood cells — HIV in CD4+ cells, EBV in B cells, CMV in monocytes

### Stage 5: Replication in Target Organs

- Virus leaves bloodstream and infects specific target organs — determined by receptor expression
- Extensive replication in target organs produces high viral loads
- This is where organ-specific disease develops
- Examples:
  - Poliovirus — anterior horn cells of spinal cord → paralysis
  - Hepatitis viruses — hepatocytes → hepatitis
  - HIV — CD4+ T cells → immunodeficiency
  - Rabies — neurons → encephalitis
  - Influenza — lower respiratory epithelium → pneumonia

### Stage 6: Secondary Viremia

- After replication in target organs, virus may re-enter bloodstream — secondary viremia
- Usually higher viral loads than primary viremia
- Spreads to even more distant organs
- Responsible for disseminated disease — e.g., CMV dissemination in immunocompromised

### Stage 7: Disease and Tissue Damage

Tissue damage in viral infections occurs through several mechanisms:

**Direct viral cytopathic effect:**
- Virus kills infected cells directly during replication
- Cell lysis when new virions burst out
- Examples: poliovirus, influenza, herpes viruses

**Immune-mediated damage:**
- The host immune response itself causes much of the damage
- Cytotoxic T cells kill infected cells — necessary but causes tissue destruction
- Inflammatory response — cytokines cause fever, tissue damage
- Immune complexes deposited in tissues — complement activation → damage
- Examples: hepatitis B liver damage is primarily immune-mediated

**Inclusion bodies:**
- Accumulations of viral components inside infected cells
- Visible on microscopy — useful for diagnosis
- Examples: Negri bodies in rabies (in neurons), owl-eye inclusions in CMV, Cowdry type A bodies in HSV

**Transformation:**
- Some viruses do not kill the cell but instead transform it into a cancer cell
- Examples: EBV, HPV, HBV, HCV, HTLV-1

### Stage 8: Resolution or Persistence

**Resolution:**
- Immune system clears the virus
- Adaptive immunity — neutralizing antibodies and cytotoxic T cells eliminate infected cells
- Patient recovers
- Immunological memory established — protection against reinfection

**Persistent Infection:**
Some viruses are not cleared and establish persistent infection:

- **Latent infection** — virus remains in host cells in a dormant state, no active replication
  - Examples: HSV in sensory ganglia, VZV in dorsal root ganglia, EBV in B cells
  - Reactivation possible when immunity drops
- **Chronic infection** — virus continues replicating at low levels indefinitely
  - Examples: Hepatitis B, Hepatitis C, HIV
  - Ongoing immune activation → chronic liver damage, immunodeficiency
- **Slow infection** — very long incubation period, then progressive fatal disease
  - Examples: HIV (years before AIDS), prion diseases (decades)
- **Transforming infection** — virus integrates and transforms cells into tumour cells
  - Examples: HPV (cervical cancer), EBV (Burkitt's lymphoma), HTLV-1 (T-cell leukaemia)

## 3. Factors Determining Disease Outcome

**Viral factors:**
- Virulence — ability to cause disease
- Inoculum size — larger dose → more severe disease
- Tissue tropism — determined by receptor-spike interaction
- Rate of replication

**Host factors:**
- Age — extremes of age more susceptible
- Immune status — immunocompromised patients get severe disease
- Nutritional status
- Genetic factors — e.g., CCR5-delta32 mutation in HIV resistance
- Prior immunity — vaccines or prior infection

## 4. Host Defenses Against Viral Infection

**Non-specific (Innate) defenses:**
- Physical barriers — skin, mucus, cilia
- Fever — high temperature inhibits viral replication
- Interferons — create antiviral state in neighbouring cells
- Natural Killer cells — kill infected cells without prior sensitization
- Macrophages and dendritic cells — phagocytosis, antigen presentation
- Complement system — direct viral lysis, opsonization

**Specific (Adaptive) defenses:**
- **Humoral immunity (antibodies):**
  - Neutralizing antibodies bind viral spikes → block attachment to host receptors
  - Opsonizing antibodies enhance phagocytosis of virions
  - Complement-fixing antibodies activate complement → viral lysis
  - Most effective against extracellular virus — before it enters cells
- **Cell-mediated immunity:**
  - Cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CD8+ T cells) — kill infected cells directly
  - Helper T cells (CD4+) — coordinate and amplify immune response
  - Most effective against intracellular virus — kills infected cells
- **Immunological memory:**
  - Long-lived memory B and T cells
  - Rapid, amplified response on re-exposure
  - Basis of vaccination

## 5. Persistent Viral Infections — Summary Table

| Type | Characteristics | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Latent | Dormant; no active replication; periodic reactivation | HSV, VZV, EBV, CMV |
| Chronic | Ongoing low-level replication; not cleared | HBV, HCV, HIV |
| Slow | Very long incubation; progressive fatal disease | Prions, HIV |
| Transforming | Integration; cell transformation to tumour | HPV, EBV, HTLV-1 |
