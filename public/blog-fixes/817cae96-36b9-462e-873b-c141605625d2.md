# Croup and Other Acute Upper Airway Infections in Children

**Year 4 · Paediatrics · Respiratory paediatrics**

> **In one line:** croup (laryngotracheobronchitis) is a viral inflammation of the larynx and subglottic airway that causes a barking cough, hoarseness and inspiratory stridor in children aged 3 months to 5 years. It is a clinical diagnosis, and the key task is to separate it from life-threatening **epiglottitis**, then treat with steroids and nebulised adrenaline according to severity. Bronchiolitis and acute bronchitis are the other common acute lower-tract conditions that are confused with it.

## What you must know

- Croup = barking ("brassy") cough, hoarse voice, inspiratory stridor, with or without respiratory distress.
- **Parainfluenza types 1, 2 and 3** cause about 75% of cases. Peak age is the **2nd year of life**; most cases occur between 3 months and 5 years.
- The problem is **upper airway obstruction**, so alveolar gas exchange stays normal until obstruction is nearly complete. Hypoxia, cyanosis, pallor and obtundation mean **impending respiratory failure**.
- Symptoms are **worse at night**, and **agitation and crying make them worse**.
- Neck X-ray: **steeple sign** (subglottic narrowing, PA view). Epiglottitis shows the **thumb sign** (lateral view).
- Treatment: **dexamethasone 0.6 mg/kg IM stat** (or nebulised budesonide), **nebulised adrenaline** for moderate to severe disease, humidified air, oxygen if hypoxic.
- **Epiglottitis** is an emergency: secure the airway first, do not distress the child, give ceftriaxone or cefotaxime for 7 to 10 days.
- **Bronchiolitis**: RSV in over 50%, supportive care and oxygen; **acute bronchitis** is self-limiting and needs no specific treatment.

## 1. Definitions

- **Croup** is a heterogeneous group of mainly acute infectious processes characterised by a **barking or brassy cough**, and possibly hoarseness, **inspiratory stridor** and respiratory distress.
- Inflammation of the vocal cords and structures **below** the cords is called **laryngitis, laryngotracheitis or laryngotracheobronchitis**.
- Inflammation **above** the vocal cords is **supraglottitis** (epiglottitis).

## 2. Aetiology and epidemiology

| Group | Agents |
|---|---|
| Viral (most cases) | **Parainfluenza 1, 2, 3** (about 75%), influenza A and B, adenovirus, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), measles |
| Bacterial | Diphtheria, bacterial tracheitis, epiglottitis (*Haemophilus influenzae* type b) |

- Age 3 months to 5 years, peak in the second year. Boys are slightly more affected, with an autumn peak in temperate climates.
- Croup is a disease of the upper airway, so alveolar gas exchange may remain normal.
- **Measles** and **diphtheria** remain relevant in Kenya where immunisation is incomplete; always check immunisation status.

## 3. Pathophysiology

- Viral infection causes mucosal oedema and inflammation of the **subglottis**, the narrowest part of a child's airway (the cricoid ring is a complete ring and cannot expand).
- Narrowing produces turbulent airflow: stridor on inspiration, a hoarse voice and a barking cough.
- Crying and agitation increase airflow turbulence and oedema, which is why distress worsens symptoms.
- By Poiseuille's law, a small amount of oedema in an infant's small airway causes a large rise in resistance.

## 4. Clinical features

- **Prodrome (1 to 3 days):** rhinorrhoea, mild pharyngitis, cough and low-grade fever.
- Then the characteristic **barking cough, hoarse voice and inspiratory stridor** appear.
- Symptoms are **worse at night**, recur with decreasing intensity over several days and resolve completely within about a week.
- Agitation and crying aggravate symptoms.

### Severity assessment

| Severity | Features |
|---|---|
| Mild | Barking cough, no stridor at rest, no or minimal chest recession |
| Moderate | Stridor at rest, recession, but child is calm |
| Severe | Marked stridor and recession, agitation or lethargy, tachycardia, **cyanosis, pallor, obtundation** |

> **Remember:** a child who is **hypoxic, cyanosed, pale or obtunded** needs **immediate airway support**. Falling stridor in a tiring, quiet child is a bad sign, not an improvement.

## 5. Investigations

- **Croup is a clinical diagnosis.** Avoid upsetting the child with unnecessary tests.
- Pulse oximetry guides oxygen need.
- Neck radiograph (PA view) may show **subglottic narrowing, the "steeple sign"**. It is not needed routinely and should never delay treatment in a distressed child.
- Throat swab or culture only if diphtheria is suspected (grey adherent membrane, bull neck).

## 6. Management of croup

- Keep the child **calm**, in the position of comfort, ideally on the parent's lap. Avoid examining the throat with a spatula.
- **Oxygen** if saturation is low.
- **Corticosteroids:** dexamethasone **0.6 mg/kg IM stat** (oral and nebulised budesonide are alternatives). Benefit begins in a few hours.
- **Nebulised adrenaline** for moderate to severe croup:
  - Racemic adrenaline 2.25%, 0.25 to 0.75 ml in 3 ml normal saline, repeated every 20 minutes (**not available in Kenya**).
  - In Kenya use **nebulised L-adrenaline (epinephrine) 5 ml of 1:1000**.
- Humidified mist or steam may help, though the evidence is weak.
- Observe after adrenaline: its effect lasts 2 hours or so and **rebound** obstruction can occur.
- Intubation (smaller tube than expected) for impending respiratory failure.

## 7. Acute epiglottitis (supraglottitis)

A dramatic and potentially lethal condition, usually from *H. influenzae* type b, now much less common where Hib vaccine is in the schedule (pentavalent vaccine in Kenya).

- Previously well child develops sore throat and high fever, and within **hours** looks toxic with difficulty swallowing and respiratory distress.
- **Drooling**, hyperextended neck and the **tripod position** (sitting upright, leaning forward, chin up, mouth open, bracing on the arms).
- **Stridor is a late sign** and indicates near-complete obstruction.
- A barking cough is rare, and there is usually no illness in other family members.

### Diagnosis and treatment

- Direct visualisation of a large **"cherry red" swollen epiglottis** by laryngoscopy, done only where the airway can be secured immediately.
- Lateral neck X-ray: **"thumb sign"**.
- **Avoid anxiety-provoking procedures** (examining the throat, cannulation, lying the child flat) until the airway is secure.
- **Establish an airway in every patient** regardless of degree of distress: nasotracheal intubation or tracheostomy, by the most experienced person (anaesthetist) in theatre.
- Antibiotics: **ceftriaxone or cefotaxime for 7 to 10 days**.

### Croup versus epiglottitis

| Feature | Croup | Epiglottitis |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | Viral (parainfluenza) | Bacterial (Hib) |
| Onset | Gradual, 1 to 3 days prodrome | Rapid, hours |
| Cough | Barking | Rare |
| Fever | Low grade | High, toxic |
| Drooling and dysphagia | Absent | Present |
| Position | Comfortable | Tripod, neck extended |
| Stridor | Early | Late sign |
| Neck X-ray | Steeple sign | Thumb sign |

> **Exam tip:** steeple sign = croup (PA view); thumb sign = epiglottitis (lateral view). Drooling plus tripod in a toxic child = epiglottitis until proven otherwise.

## 8. Acute bronchiolitis

- Predominantly viral. **RSV causes over 50%**; others are parainfluenza, adenovirus, *Mycoplasma* and human metapneumovirus.
- Usually follows exposure to an older child with a respiratory tract infection within the previous week.
- Begins with a mild URTI (sneezing, clear rhinorrhoea, reduced appetite, fever), then respiratory distress with a **paroxysmal wheezy cough, dyspnoea and irritability**.
- The infant is often tachypnoeic. Very small infants (**under 2 months**) or former preterm infants may have **apnoea**.
- Diarrhoea and vomiting are usually absent.

### Management

- **Oxygen** for all hypoxic infants; adequate feeding or fluids (nasogastric if needed).
- A trial of **nebulised salbutamol**; **nebulised adrenaline**.
- Add **ipratropium** if the infant has tracheomalacia or bronchomalacia.
- **Nebulised hypertonic saline** (3%, 3 ml).
- Trial of steroids in moderate to severe cases and in children with a history of atopy.
- **Ribavirin** for infants with congenital heart disease or chronic lung disease.
- Episodes can recur; about **60% of infants who wheeze stop wheezing** later.
- **Prevention:** **palivizumab**, a monoclonal antibody to RSV, for children under 2 years with chronic lung disease or prematurity.

> **Check:** most guidelines treat bronchiolitis supportively (oxygen, feeds), because bronchodilators, steroids and hypertonic saline have inconsistent benefit. The drugs above are the slide content and are used as a trial, continued only if there is a clear response.

## 9. Acute bronchitis

- Commonly preceded by a viral URTI.
- Rhinitis, low-grade fever, **barking cough** and chest pain; the whole episode lasts about **2 weeks**.
- The clinician must **exclude pneumonia**.
- **No specific treatment**; the disease is self-limited. **Avoid cough syrups and antihistamines.**

## 10. Complications

- Croup: respiratory failure, bacterial tracheitis, pneumonia, rarely pulmonary oedema after severe obstruction.
- Epiglottitis: complete airway obstruction and death, septicaemia.
- Bronchiolitis: apnoea, respiratory failure, later recurrent wheeze.

## Key points to revise

1. Croup is viral (parainfluenza 1, 2, 3 about 75%), age 3 months to 5 years, peak in the second year.
2. Triad: barking cough, hoarseness, inspiratory stridor; worse at night and with crying.
3. Diagnosis is clinical; steeple sign on PA neck film; do not distress the child.
4. Treat with dexamethasone 0.6 mg/kg IM, nebulised adrenaline (L-adrenaline 5 ml of 1:1000 in Kenya), oxygen and humidification.
5. Epiglottitis: toxic child, drooling, tripod, thumb sign; secure the airway first, then ceftriaxone or cefotaxime for 7 to 10 days.
6. Bronchiolitis: RSV over 50%; oxygen and supportive care; palivizumab prevents RSV in high-risk infants.
7. Acute bronchitis is self-limiting; exclude pneumonia; avoid cough syrups and antihistamines.
