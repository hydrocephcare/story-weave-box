# Tuberculosis

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Infectious diseases and Respiratory medicine**

> **In one line:** tuberculosis is an infection with **Mycobacterium tuberculosis**, spread by **airborne droplet nuclei**; most infected people contain it as **latent TB**, but about 10% develop active disease (mostly in the lungs, but any organ can be hit) when cell-mediated immunity fails. Cure needs **multiple drugs for at least 6 months**.

## What you must know

- Spread is **airborne** (droplet nuclei from open pulmonary or laryngeal TB). Active TB needs **airborne isolation in a negative-pressure room**, not droplet isolation.
- About **10% of infected people** develop active disease; about half of active cases are infectious. **Latent TB is not infectious.**
- Immunity is **cell-mediated** (delayed hypersensitivity); granuloma alone is not diagnostic of TB.
- Pulmonary TB is about **80%**, extrapulmonary about **20%** (lymph node commonest). Always check the lungs in extrapulmonary TB.
- Pulmonary TB tests: **CXR (initial), sputum smear (mainstay), sputum culture (gold standard)**; NAAT (Xpert MTB/RIF) gives rapid answers and rifampicin resistance.
- **Mantoux/TST** and IGRA show infection, not disease.
- Standard regimen: **2 months of isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, ethambutol (HRZE), then 4 months of isoniazid and rifampicin (HR)**.
- **MDR-TB** = resistance to at least isoniazid and rifampicin; **XDR-TB** adds a quinolone and a second-line injectable.
- HIV is the strongest risk factor for active TB; in Africa about 60% of TB patients are HIV positive.

## 1. Definition and epidemiology

- Tuberculosis is a bacterial infection caused by **M. tuberculosis** (the tubercle bacillus). It damages the lungs or other organs and is fatal if not properly treated.
- Worldwide disease; **90% of cases and 95% of deaths** occur in developing countries. In developed countries incidence fell with case finding, treatment and better nutrition.
- Kenya is a high-burden country for both TB and TB/HIV co-infection.
- Of infected people, **10% develop active disease**; about **50% of active cases are contagious**. Open pulmonary TB is the most infectious form, with laryngeal TB second.
- Take a history of nationality, residence, occupation, living conditions and travel.

### 1.1 What increases spread

1. Overcrowding (prisons, schools, hostels, slums).
2. Migration from endemic areas.

### 1.2 What increases the risk of active disease after infection

- **Infecting dose** and host factors:
  - Age: under 5 years and the elderly
  - Debilitating illness and poor nutrition
  - Alcoholism
  - Long-term steroids or other immunosuppression
  - **Gastrectomy** (malnutrition, loss of gastric acid protection) or jejunoileal bypass
  - **Diabetes mellitus**
  - Silicosis, chronic renal failure, malignancy (leukaemia, lymphoma, head and neck cancer), **HIV**
  - Infection in the last 2 years; previous TB on chest X-ray

### 1.3 People at higher risk of infection

Close contacts of infectious cases; people born in or travelling to endemic areas; residents and staff of congregate settings; poor access to healthcare; injecting drug users; health workers; the elderly.

## 2. Microbiology

- **Aerobic** (so upper lobe apices are favoured), non-spore-forming, non-motile **rod** about 2 to 5 micrometres long.
- **Acid- and alcohol-fast** (resists decolourisation after staining): Ziehl-Neelsen stain; resistant to many disinfectants.
- Facultative intracellular: lives inside and outside macrophages.
- Slow multiplication, which is why treatment lasts 6 months.
- **Humans are the main reservoir.**

## 3. Transmission and pathogenesis

1. **Transmission:** an infectious person coughs, speaks, laughs, sneezes or sings and releases **droplet nuclei (aerosols)**. Inhaled nuclei pass the airways and reach the alveoli.
2. **Initial focus (Ghon focus):** the bacilli multiply in the terminal airspace; the focus is usually **subpleural** and single in 75%.
3. **Spread:** bacilli drain to hilar nodes (Ghon complex) and can seed through lymph and blood to distant sites (lung apices, brain, larynx, nodes, spine, bone, kidney).
4. **Immune response (2 to 8 weeks):** macrophages ingest the bacilli; T cells activate macrophages, forming a **granuloma (tubercle)** with caseous centre. The majority of immunocompetent hosts control infection here: this is **latent TB infection (LTBI)**.
5. **Outcome:** about 5 to 10% progress to disease during life (half within 2 years). If immunity fails, bacilli multiply, granulomas enlarge and break down, **cavities** form, erode vessels (haemoptysis) and spread bronchially.

**Immunological points**
- Control needs **cell-mediated immunity (delayed hypersensitivity)**. Antibody response is rich but plays no role.
- Multiplication continues for weeks until cell-mediated immunity develops, both at the initial focus and at the lymphohaematogenous metastatic foci.

> **Remember:** "Granuloma is not diagnostic of TB." Sarcoidosis, fungal infections, Crohn's disease and others also form granulomas; caseating necrosis with acid-fast bacilli is what points to TB.

## 4. Latent TB versus active TB

| Feature | Latent TB infection (LTBI) | Active TB disease |
|---|---|---|
| Status | Infected, no disease; healthy | Unwell |
| Bacilli | Few, alive but inactive, confined in granulomas | Many, multiplying, broken out of tubercles |
| Infectious | **No** | Often yes (open pulmonary, laryngeal) |
| Symptoms | None | Cough, fever, weight loss, night sweats |
| TST / IGRA | Positive | Usually positive |
| Chest X-ray | Normal | Often abnormal |
| Sputum smear/culture | Negative | May be positive |
| Isolation | Not needed | May be needed |
| Treatment | Consider LTBI treatment (prophylaxis) | Full treatment for TB disease |
| Is it a "TB case"? | No | Yes |

## 5. Clinical features

### 5.1 Pulmonary TB (about 80%): primary and post-primary

| | Primary TB | Post-primary (adult) TB |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | First infection in a tuberculin-negative person | Exogenous reinfection or **reactivation** of a dormant focus in a sensitised person |
| Who | Children, the elderly, the immunocompromised; few adults | Most adults in endemic countries (including Kenya) |
| Course | Often self-limiting febrile illness; disease only with hypersensitivity or progressive infection | Insidious over weeks |
| Site | Subpleural focus with hilar nodes | **Apex of an upper lobe** (high oxygen tension favours the aerobe) |
| Imaging | Hilar lymphadenopathy, effusion | Upper lobe opacities, then consolidation, collapse, **cavitation** |

- Symptoms: **cough (dry, then productive) for 3 weeks or longer**, haemoptysis, fever (usually low-grade), **night sweats**, weight loss, loss of appetite, fatigue, chills, pleuritic pain, swollen glands. Signs: crackles; often few signs.
- A **cavity means high infectivity**.

### 5.2 Extrapulmonary TB (about 20%)

Any organ can be involved. Look for lung disease in all of them.

| Site | Key points |
|---|---|
| **Lymph node (commonest)** | About 25% of extrapulmonary cases. Painless localised swelling, usually **cervical and supraclavicular**. Early: discrete nodes. Late: matted nodes, sometimes with a discharging sinus (scrofula). Diagnosis: FNA, but **excision biopsy** is the most accurate (histology and culture) |
| **Pleural** | Few bacilli enter the pleural space, giving effusion and fever. Aspirate: **exudate** (lymphocytes, high protein). **Pleural biopsy: granuloma**. AFB rarely seen; culture positive in about 30% |
| **Bone and joint** | From reactivation of a blood-borne focus or spread from a node. Sites: spine, hips, knees. **Spinal TB (Pott's disease):** thoracic (dorsal) spine commonest; two vertebral bodies and the disc between them are destroyed; advanced disease gives collapse, **kyphosis/gibbus**, cord compression and paralysis (worse with higher lesions), **paravertebral (cold) abscess** that may track to the psoas, and root pain. CT/MRI; biopsy with histology, AFB stain and culture confirms |
| **Meninges (TB meningitis)** | Mainly children, also adults. By blood spread or rupture of a sub-ependymal tubercle (Rich focus) into the subarachnoid space. Fever, nausea, vomiting, headache, photophobia, neck stiffness; **subacute over about 2 weeks** (unlike acute bacterial meningitis). CSF: AFB stain, **lymphocytes, high protein, low glucose**, culture. Complications: hydrocephalus, cranial nerve palsies (especially VI), infarcts |
| **Peritoneum** | Abdominal TB: ascites (exudate, high ADA), abdominal pain, mass |
| **Others** | Kidney (sterile pyuria, haematuria), larynx (hoarseness, highly infectious), pericardium (effusion, constriction), genital tract, adrenals |

**Factors favouring extrapulmonary TB:** malnutrition, HIV, severe pulmonary TB (cavitating pneumonia, lymphatic spread with lobar collapse from enlarged nodes), and haematogenous dissemination (about 40%).

### 5.3 Miliary TB

- Blood-borne dissemination. Presents acutely or, more often, with 2 to 3 weeks of fever, night sweats, anorexia, weight loss and dry cough.
- Hepatosplenomegaly; headache suggests coexisting meningitis. Chest is often clear; crackles in advanced disease. **Choroidal tubercles** on fundoscopy.
- CXR: fine 1 to 2 mm **"millet seed"** nodules throughout both lungs. Anaemia and leucopenia from marrow involvement.
- In children an asymptomatic primary infection may progress to miliary TB or meningitis.

## 6. TB and HIV

- Active TB patients are more often HIV positive. About 60% of active TB patients in Africa are HIV positive (50% in New York).
- TB can occur at any CD4 count but looks different:
  - **Early HIV:** typical upper-lobe infiltrate with or without cavitation.
  - **Late HIV:** diffuse infiltrates, **no cavitation**, intrathoracic lymphadenopathy.
- Sputum smear is positive less often; **extrapulmonary TB occurs in about 40%**.
- Diagnosis is difficult: smear-negative in about 40%, atypical CXR, **negative tuberculin test** (anergy from loss of CD4 cells).
- Adverse drug reactions are more common in HIV. Treat TB and start ART early (see management).

## 7. Drug-resistant TB

- Resistance to one or more first-line drugs; transmitted the same way and **not more infectious**, but delayed detection prolongs infectiousness.
- **MDR-TB:** resistance to at least **rifampicin and isoniazid**, with or without other resistance.
- **XDR-TB:** MDR plus resistance to a **fluoroquinolone** and at least one injectable second-line agent (amikacin, kanamycin or capreomycin).
- Commoner after previous TB treatment (especially inadequate) and in HIV. Cure is possible but needs prolonged treatment with less effective, more toxic, more expensive drugs. Mortality is high for MDR and higher for XDR.
- A patient who refuses treatment of infectious TB may be treated under public health law because of the danger to others.

## 8. Diagnosis

### 8.1 History

- Pulmonary: cough of 3 weeks or longer, haemoptysis, chest pain, anorexia, weight loss, night sweats, fever, fatigue.
- Extrapulmonary clues: haematuria (kidney), headache/confusion (meningitis), back pain (spine), hoarseness (larynx).

### 8.2 Pulmonary TB tests

1. **Chest X-ray: initial test.** Cavities (upper lobe), upper lobe opacities, consolidation, collapse, miliary pattern, hilar nodes, effusion.
2. **Sputum:** a good sample has fewer than 10 epithelial cells and contains alveolar macrophages (not saliva).
   - **Ziehl-Neelsen smear** for acid-fast bacilli: the mainstay.
   - **Culture: gold standard.** Solid media (Lowenstein-Jensen) take 3 to 6 weeks, allow sensitivity testing. Liquid media (BACTEC) 4 to 14 days, faster but costlier.
   - Culture all specimens even if smear or NAAT is negative; culture monthly until two consecutive negative cultures (conversion).
3. **NAAT (Xpert MTB/RIF):** rapid DNA detection, also detects rifampicin resistance. Do at least one on each suspect; a single negative does not exclude TB. Earlier confirmation allows earlier isolation and treatment.
4. Other samples by site: CSF, pleural, peritoneal and pericardial fluid, early morning urine, node aspirate, tissue biopsy, blood.

### 8.3 Tuberculin skin test (Mantoux)

- **Not diagnostic** of disease. 0.1 ml PPD (5 tuberculin units) given intradermally in the forearm.
- Read at **48 to 72 hours**; measure the **induration** (not redness) across the forearm in millimetres; record "0" if none. Positive 2 to 8 weeks after infection.
- Limited by low sensitivity and specificity.

| False positive | False negative (up to 20% of active TB) |
|---|---|
| Non-tuberculous mycobacteria, **BCG vaccination**, errors in administration or reading | Anergy, viral/bacterial/fungal co-infection, recent TB infection, very young or old age, live-virus vaccines, overwhelming TB, renal failure, lymphoma, malnutrition, steroids/immunosuppressants, sarcoidosis, **HIV**, administration errors |

### 8.4 Interferon-gamma release assays (IGRAs)

- Blood tests (QuantiFERON-TB Gold In-Tube, T-SPOT.TB) that measure T-cell response to TB-specific antigens. **Cannot distinguish LTBI from active disease.**
- Used instead of (not in addition to) TST; preferred if BCG-vaccinated or unlikely to return for a reading. Generally not used under 5 years unless with TST.

> **Exam tip:** the PPD/TST is used only for an **at-risk, asymptomatic person with a normal CXR**. If symptomatic or the CXR is abnormal, go straight to sputum.

## 9. Management

### 9.1 Principles

- Cure the patient, reduce death and disability, prevent transmission.
- **Never use a single drug, and never add a single drug to a failing regimen.**
- Use multiple drugs to which the organism is susceptible (rapidly reduces bacterial load, kills bacilli, slows resistance).
- Ensure adherence: **directly observed therapy (DOT)**.

### 9.2 Drugs

| First line | Second line |
|---|---|
| Isoniazid (H), rifampicin (R), pyrazinamide (Z), ethambutol (E), rifapentine, streptomycin (sometimes replaces ethambutol) | Cycloserine, capreomycin, para-aminosalicylic acid, ethionamide (plus fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides, bedaquiline, linezolid, delamanid in modern MDR regimens) |

Key adverse effects (add detail for exams):

| Drug | Main adverse effects |
|---|---|
| Isoniazid | Hepatitis, **peripheral neuropathy** (give pyridoxine) |
| Rifampicin | Hepatitis, **orange-red secretions**, **enzyme induction** (reduces oral contraceptive, antiretroviral and warfarin levels) |
| Pyrazinamide | Hepatitis, hyperuricaemia and gout, arthralgia |
| Ethambutol | **Optic neuritis** (colour vision); reduce dose in renal failure |
| Streptomycin | Ototoxicity, nephrotoxicity |

### 9.3 Standard regimen for drug-susceptible pulmonary TB (6 months)

- **Intensive phase: isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, ethambutol daily for 8 weeks (2 months).**
- **Continuation phase: isoniazid + rifampicin daily for 18 weeks (4 months)**; intermittent regimens are an option elsewhere. In Kenya the standard is 2RHZE/4RH given as fixed-dose combinations.
- Extend to **9 to 12 months** if HIV positive (in some guidelines) or if drug intolerance requires a second-line substitute. **TB meningitis needs at least 12 months** (same drugs, longer course; steroids are added).
- **Pyridoxine** for pregnant women and the malnourished to prevent isoniazid neuropathy.
- Warn about **orange-red urine and tears** with rifampicin; women on the pill need alternative contraception.
- Ethambutol: caution in renal failure; adverse reactions occur in about 10% of patients and are commoner in HIV.

### 9.4 TB and HIV management

- Start TB treatment first, then ART early (within 2 to 8 weeks, within 2 weeks if CD4 below 50). Watch for IRIS and drug interactions (rifampicin with nevirapine and protease inhibitors; dolutegravir dose is adjusted).
- Give cotrimoxazole prophylaxis.

> **Check:** the lecture answer key says treat TB together with ART in a patient with CD4 of 50 (option D). Current guidance is to start TB treatment first and ART within 2 weeks when CD4 is below 50.

### 9.5 Monitoring and failure

- Pulmonary TB: sputum smear at **2 months and 5 months**. A **positive smear at 5 months defines treatment failure**. Extrapulmonary TB is assessed clinically and radiologically.
- Causes of failure: **non-compliance (commonest)**, inappropriate drug (for example non-tuberculous mycobacteria), drug resistance (MDR suspected if culture still positive after 2 months or MDR contact).

## 10. Prevention and control

### 10.1 Infection control

- Based on a risk assessment of the setting. Aims: detect disease early, isolate known or suspected cases, start treatment promptly.
- Active pulmonary TB: **airborne isolation, negative-pressure room**, for about 2 weeks and until three negative smears with clinical improvement.

### 10.2 Treatment of latent TB (chemoprophylaxis)

- Regimens: **isoniazid for 6 months**, or rifampicin + isoniazid for 3 months.
- Candidates: high-risk people with a positive IGRA or TST of **10 mm or more**, including silicosis, diabetes, chronic renal failure, certain cancers, gastrectomy or jejunoileal bypass, weight loss of 10% or more, children under 4 years.
- Also: asymptomatic TST-positive contacts with normal CXR, children under 16 with a strongly positive test found in contact tracing, children under 2 in close contact with smear-positive cases, **babies of mothers with pulmonary TB**, and **HIV-positive close contacts** of smear-positive patients.

### 10.3 BCG vaccine

- Live attenuated **M. bovis**, given intradermally; first used in humans in 1921.
- Protects infants against **disseminated disease and TB meningitis**; efficacy in adults is inconsistent. Given at birth in Kenya.
- **Contraindicated** in impaired immunity: HIV infection, congenital immunodeficiency, leukaemia, lymphoma, generalised malignancy, high-dose steroids, alkylating agents, antimetabolites, radiation therapy, and in pregnancy.

## 11. Practice points from the lecture MCQs

- IV drug user with fever, night sweats, haemoptysis, AFB-positive and HIV with CD4 50: treat TB and HIV together (keyed answer D; see the note above).
- Respiratory isolation is needed for smear-positive pulmonary or laryngeal disease; the AFB-positive node biopsy case in advanced HIV warrants isolation until pulmonary disease is excluded.
- Roommates of a pulmonary TB patient are at increased risk; a classmate of osteomyelitis or a mother of a child with TB meningitis are not (these are not open pulmonary cases).

## Key points to revise

1. M. tuberculosis is an aerobic, acid-fast bacillus spread by droplet nuclei; use airborne isolation with negative pressure.
2. About 10% of infected people develop disease; LTBI is not infectious; immunity is cell-mediated.
3. Pulmonary TB: cough over 3 weeks, haemoptysis, fever, night sweats, weight loss; post-primary disease affects upper lobe apices with cavitation.
4. Extrapulmonary TB: nodes commonest; also pleura, spine (Pott's), meninges, miliary; check the lungs every time.
5. Pulmonary TB tests: CXR first, smear (mainstay), culture (gold standard), Xpert for rapid detection and rifampicin resistance; TST/IGRA do not diagnose disease.
6. Treatment: 2 months HRZE then 4 months HR; meningitis at least 12 months; use pyridoxine with isoniazid; warn about rifampicin colour and contraceptive interaction.
7. MDR is resistance to isoniazid plus rifampicin; XDR adds a quinolone and an injectable.
8. HIV makes TB atypical and extrapulmonary; prevent with isoniazid prophylaxis for high-risk contacts and BCG at birth.
