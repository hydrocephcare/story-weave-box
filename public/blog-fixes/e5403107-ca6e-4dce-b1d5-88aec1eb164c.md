# Examination of the Newborn

**Year 4 · Paediatrics · Neonatology**

> **In one line:** the newborn examination checks general well-being, detects congenital malformations and confirms a smooth transition to extra-uterine life. Examine every baby within 24 hours and again before discharge (before the end of the first week), using a systematic head-to-toe checklist, assessment of posture, hips, primitive reflexes and gestational age, and screening for genetic and metabolic disease.

## What you must know

- Objectives: assess **general well-being**, detect **congenital malformations**, observe **transition to extra-uterine life**.
- Examine **within the first 24 hours** and **again before discharge** (before the end of the first week). Take a full history (antenatal, natal, postnatal, family) and **ask for records**.
- Passage of **meconium within 24 hours**; urine may be delayed up to **48 hours**.
- The **umbilical cord** has **2 arteries and 1 vein**.
- **Caput succedaneum** crosses suture lines; **cephalohaematoma** does not.
- Most primitive reflexes disappear by **4 months**; persistence beyond this suggests developmental delay.
- Screen hips (Ortolani and Barlow); treat developmental dysplasia with a **Pavlik harness**.
- Newborn screening: PKU, sickle cell disease, hypothyroidism, cystic fibrosis, congenital adrenal hyperplasia.

## 1. Preparation and prerequisites

- Mother and baby together, in a **warm room** with a clean sheet or clothes.
- Thermometer, weighing scale, watch with seconds hand, stethoscope.
- A **skilled, knowledgeable** examiner: the eyes see what the mind knows.
- Many parts of the examination are the same as for older babies and children.

## 2. History

- **Antenatal:** maternal illness, infections, drugs, alcohol, HIV and syphilis status, scans.
- **Natal:** gestation, mode of delivery, duration of labour, liquor, resuscitation.
- **Postnatal:** feeding, passage of urine and meconium, jaundice.
- **Family history:** genetic disease, consanguinity, previous neonatal deaths.

## 3. Checklist for examination

| Item | What to look for |
|---|---|
| **Apgar score** | Recorded at 1, 5 and 10 minutes (minimum) |
| **Oxygen saturation** | Four limbs (screen for critical congenital heart disease) |
| **Measurements** | Weight, length, head circumference |
| **Urine and meconium** | Meconium within 24 hours; urine may take up to 48 hours |
| **Alertness and wakefulness** | State, cry, activity |
| **Skin colour** | Cyanosis, jaundice (jaundice in the first 24 hours is pathological) |
| **Birthmarks** | Capillary haemangiomas, hyper- or hypopigmented patches |
| **Sacral region** | Sacral dimple |
| **Skull and fontanelles** | Caput succedaneum, cephalohaematoma, sagittal suture, fontanelle |
| **Eyes** | Pupillary and red reflex; **subconjunctival haemorrhages** are common after vaginal delivery |
| **Mouth and tongue** | Cleft palate, bifid uvula |
| **Neck** | Branchial cysts, dermoid cysts, thyroglossal cysts |
| **Chest** | As in older children; breast enlargement with milk exudate can be normal |
| **Abdomen and umbilicus** | Stump should contain **2 arteries and 1 vein** |
| **Back and spine** | Spina bifida |
| **Perineum and genitalia** | Hypospadias, hydroceles, hernias, undescended testis, **patent anus**, abnormal genitalia in girls; white vaginal discharge and slight PV bleeding in girls is normal (maternal hormones) |
| **Neurological** | Primitive reflexes, movements, tone, cry |
| **Limbs** | Digits, palmar crease, talipes |
| **Hips** | Developmental dysplasia of the hip |
| **Gestational age** | Clinical scoring |
| **Genetic screening** | See section 8 |

> **Exam tip:** failure to pass meconium within 24 hours suggests Hirschsprung disease, meconium ileus (cystic fibrosis) or anorectal malformation. Always confirm the anus is patent.

### Caput succedaneum versus cephalohaematoma

| Feature | Caput succedaneum | Cephalohaematoma |
|---|---|---|
| Site | Serous, bloody oedema of the scalp over the presenting part | Subperiosteal blood collection |
| Crosses suture lines | **Yes** | **No** (limited by the periosteum of one bone) |
| Onset | Present at birth | Appears hours to days after birth |
| Resolution | Within days | Weeks to months; may calcify; may cause jaundice |

## 4. Posture and colour

- **Normal resting posture of a term baby:** loosely clenched fists; **flexed arms, hips and knees**.
- **Small babies** (under 2.5 kg or under 37 weeks) may have **extended limbs**.
- **Breech-born babies** may have fully flexed hips and knees with the feet near the mouth.
- Look for abnormal arm and hand positions (for example Erb palsy "waiter's tip", or a persistent clenched fist with thumb in the palm).
- Colour: pink is normal; central cyanosis, pallor, plethora and jaundice are abnormal. Hands and feet may be mildly blue (acrocyanosis) in the first day or so.

## 5. Umbilicus

- Normal stump: two arteries and one vein, moist then drying and separating at about 1 to 2 weeks.
- Abnormal: a single umbilical artery (associated with renal and other anomalies), umbilical hernia, exomphalos or gastroschisis, discharge or redness (omphalitis).

## 6. Examination of the hips

- **Barlow:** adduct the flexed hip and push posteriorly to dislocate an unstable hip.
- **Ortolani:** abduct the flexed hip while lifting the femoral head forward; a "clunk" means a dislocated hip is relocating.
- Risk factors: breech presentation, family history, female sex, oligohydramnios, firstborn.
- Treatment of congenital hip dislocation: a **Pavlik harness** (holds the hips flexed and abducted); ultrasound confirms.

## 7. Infant reflexes

- **Involuntary, stereotyped movement responses to a particular stimulus.** They dominate movement in the last 4 months of prenatal life and the first 4 months after birth.
- They are **subcortical** (below higher brain centres) and are **primitive** or **postural**.
- **Most primitive reflexes disappear by 4 months**, as the maturing CNS inhibits them or integrates them into voluntary movement.
- **Absence** may indicate a brain abnormality, a local limb abnormality (for example brachial plexus injury, fracture) or a neuromuscular disorder. **Persistence beyond 4 months** should alert you to possible developmental delay.
- Test carefully and only when the baby is **quiet and awake**; a restless, crying, sleepy or distracted baby may not respond.
- Terminology varies (rooting = search = cardinal points reflex), and reflexes are more complex than once thought (palmar grasp versus traction response).

### Primitive reflexes

| Reflex | Elicitation and response |
|---|---|
| **Palmar grasp** | Finger placed in the palm; fingers flex around it |
| **Sucking** | Object in the mouth; rhythmic sucking |
| **Rooting (search)** | Stroke the cheek; head turns towards the stimulus and mouth opens |
| **Moro (startle)** | Sudden head drop or loud noise; arms abduct and extend, then adduct (embrace), baby cries |
| **Asymmetric tonic neck** | Head turned to one side; arm and leg extend on that side, flex on the opposite ("fencer") |
| **Symmetric tonic neck** | Neck flexion or extension changes limb tone |
| **Plantar grasp** | Pressure on the sole; toes curl |
| **Babinski** | Stroke the sole; big toe extends and toes fan (normal in infancy) |
| **Palmar-mandibular (Babkin)** | Pressure on the palms; mouth opens and head flexes |
| **Palmar-mental** | Palm scratch; contraction of the chin muscle |

### Postural reflexes
Stepping, crawling, swimming, head and body righting, parachuting, labyrinthine, pull-up.

> **Remember:** an asymmetrical Moro reflex suggests a fractured clavicle, brachial plexus injury (Erb palsy) or hemiparesis. A Moro that is still present at 6 months suggests neurological abnormality.

## 8. Assessment of gestational age and newborn screening

- **Gestational age** is assessed by clinical scoring (for example the New Ballard score, using physical and neuromuscular maturity), to classify the baby as preterm, term or post-term and appropriate, small or large for gestational age.
- **Newborn screening** (where available) covers:
  - Phenylketonuria
  - Sickle cell disease
  - Hypothyroidism
  - Cystic fibrosis
  - Congenital adrenal hyperplasia (written as "hypoplasia" in the slides; the screened condition is hyperplasia)

## Key points to revise

1. Examine all babies within 24 hours and again before discharge, after taking a full history and checking the records.
2. Use a systematic checklist: Apgar, four-limb saturations, measurements, skin, head, eyes, mouth, neck, chest, abdomen, spine, genitalia, anus, limbs, hips, neurology.
3. Meconium within 24 hours, urine within 48 hours; confirm anal patency.
4. Cord has 2 arteries and 1 vein; caput crosses sutures, cephalohaematoma does not.
5. Term resting posture is flexed; preterm babies are more extended.
6. Most primitive reflexes disappear by 4 months; persistence suggests delay, absence suggests lesion.
7. Screen hips with Barlow and Ortolani; treat with a Pavlik harness.
8. Newborn screening: PKU, sickle cell, hypothyroidism, cystic fibrosis, CAH.
