# Cholera and Bacillary Dysentery

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Gastroenterology and Infectious Diseases**

> **In one line:** cholera is a toxin-mediated infection of the small bowel by *Vibrio cholerae*; cholera toxin locks adenylyl cyclase on, causing massive painless watery (secretory) diarrhoea and death from dehydration unless fluid is replaced. Bacillary dysentery (shigellosis) is an invasive infection of the rectum and sigmoid colon by *Shigella*, causing bloody mucoid stools, fever and tenesmus.

## What you must know

- Cholera: **Gram-negative, comma-shaped, aerobic or facultative anaerobic bacillus**; serogroups **O1** (biotypes classical and El Tor; serotypes Inaba and Ogawa) and **O139** cause epidemics. **El Tor** is now commoner, causes milder illness and more carriers.
- Cholera is spread **faeco-orally**; humans are the only reservoir. Stools are **painless, profuse, "rice-water" with a fishy odour** (up to 10 to 20 litres a day).
- **Cholera toxin** (one A subunit, five B subunits) raises cAMP, so chloride and water pour into the gut lumen. The bowel is not damaged, so there is no blood and few neutrophils.
- Treatment is mainly **rehydration** (ORS, or IV **Ringer's lactate** for severe disease) within 4 hours; antibiotics shorten illness (single-dose **doxycycline 300 mg, ciprofloxacin 1 g or azithromycin 1 g**).
- Mortality: under 1% if treated, about 60% if untreated.
- Shigellosis: *S. dysenteriae* is the most serious (**Shiga toxin**: haemorrhagic colitis and **HUS**); *S. sonnei* is commonest and mildest. *S. flexneri* is linked to **Reiter's syndrome** (HLA-B27).

# PART A: CHOLERA

## 1. Definition and organism

- Acute infection of the **small intestine** by *Vibrio cholerae*.
- Gram-negative, comma-shaped, motile bacillus (aerobic or facultative anaerobe). *V. cholerae* is a **saltwater organism**; it lives in brackish water and associates with plankton and shellfish.
- Antigens: **flagellar H antigen** and **somatic O antigen** (the O antigen defines the serogroup).
- Pathogenic serogroups: **O1** and **O139**.
  - O1 has two biotypes, **classical** and **El Tor**, and each is further typed as **Inaba** or **Ogawa**.
  - El Tor has largely replaced classical; it is more often mild or asymptomatic, so there are more carriers.
- Other vibrios exist, for example *V. parahaemolyticus* (gastroenteritis from seafood).

## 2. Epidemiology and history

- Affects about 3 to 5 million people worldwide each year, with 100,000 to 150,000 deaths. It remains both **epidemic and endemic** in many developing regions.
- Originated in the Indian subcontinent (Ganges delta). It reached Russia in 1817, then Europe, and Africa with traders and colonists.
- Seven pandemics so far. The **seventh** began in 1961, reached Africa in 1971 and the Americas in 1991.
- In Kenya, outbreaks follow floods, drought, refugee camps and informal settlements with poor water and sanitation.

## 3. Risk factors and transmission

**Host factors**

- A large inoculum is needed in normal adults (more than 100 million organisms) because gastric acid kills most vibrios. So **low gastric acid (hypochlorhydria)** raises risk: children, older adults, antacids and acid-suppressing drugs, previous gastric surgery.
- **Children under 5 years.**
- **Blood group O** carries the highest risk of severe disease (O much greater than B, then A, then AB; AB is relatively protected).
- Low immunity: **HIV**, malnourished children.
- Selective advantage: heterozygous carriers of the **cystic fibrosis gene** may be relatively protected, which may explain the gene's persistence.

**Environmental factors**

- Poor sanitation and unsafe water, overcrowding (refugee camps, slums, pilgrimages), though it can occur anywhere.
- Raw or undercooked contaminated seafood, especially **shellfish**.

**Transmission**

- **Faeco-oral**, through contaminated water or food. Humans are the only reservoir.
- Infectious dose: **1,000 to 1,000,000 organisms in water**; fewer (**100 to 10,000**) when ingested with food, because food buffers gastric acid.
- **Incubation:** a few hours to 5 days, average 1 to 3 days. It is shorter with high gastric pH (antacids) or a large inoculum.
- For every person who develops disease, 3 to 100 are infected without symptoms (carriers who still spread infection).
- **Communicability:** during the acute stage and a few days after recovery. By the end of week 1 about 70% are non-infectious, and by the end of week 3 about 98%.

## 4. Pathogenesis

1. Ingested vibrios are mostly killed by gastric acid; survivors pass through the stomach and reach the small bowel.
2. They penetrate the mucus layer and colonise the intestinal wall (they do **not** invade the mucosa).
3. They secrete **cholera toxin**, made of **one A subunit (A1 and A2 segments) and five B subunits**.
4. The **B subunits bind GM1 ganglioside receptors** on enterocytes, and the A subunit is internalised.
5. A1 separates from A2. **A1 ADP-ribosylates the Gs protein**, which switches adenylyl cyclase permanently on and raises **cAMP**.
6. High cAMP opens the **chloride channels (CFTR)** and blocks sodium absorption. Chloride, bicarbonate and water flow out of the cell into the lumen.
7. The result is **secretory diarrhoea** with massive loss of fluid, sodium, potassium and bicarbonate. Without treatment, death follows within 18 hours to several days.

> **Exam tip:** the slide says A1 "stimulates ADP breakdown"; the correct mechanism is ADP-ribosylation of the Gs alpha subunit, leaving adenylyl cyclase permanently active. Cholera is the classic example of a **non-inflammatory, non-invasive** diarrhoea.

> **Remember (quiz questions):** (1) *V. cholerae* survives gastric acid only in large numbers, or when acid is low or buffered by food; (2) few neutrophils are in the stool because the bacteria do not invade or inflame the mucosa; the toxin acts on intact epithelium.

## 5. Clinical features

- **Sudden onset of profuse, painless watery diarrhoea and vomiting**, up to 10 to 20 litres of fluid a day.
- **Rice-water stools** (flecks of mucus in clear fluid) with a **fishy odour**.
- Vomiting, muscle cramps (from electrolyte loss).
- Signs of **severe dehydration**: thirst, sunken eyes, dry mucosae, reduced skin turgor, tachycardia, hypotension, weak pulse, oliguria, altered consciousness.
- Without treatment: **acute kidney injury** (prerenal then acute tubular necrosis), other organ failure, shock and death. Hypoglycaemia and seizures occur in children.

## 6. Investigations

- **Diagnosis is mainly clinical** during an outbreak; confirm with laboratory tests.
- **Stool**: fresh stool or a rectal swab. A **rapid dipstick test** detects *V. cholerae* antigen. **Culture and sensitivity** confirms the diagnosis and antibiotic susceptibility. Dark-field microscopy shows darting motility.
- **Enrichment media:** alkaline peptone water (pH 8.6); Monsur's taurocholate tellurite peptone water (pH 9.2).
- **Plating media:** alkaline bile salt agar; Monsur's gelatin taurocholate trypticase tellurite agar (GTTA); **TCBS agar** (most widely used; contains thiosulphate, citrate, bile salts and sucrose). Cholera vibrios ferment sucrose and give **flat, yellow colonies 2 to 3 mm across**. Serotyping is by agglutination with specific antisera.
- **Blood tests:**
  - Raised urea and creatinine (deranged renal function).
  - **Raised haematocrit and white count** from haemoconcentration.
  - **Metabolic acidosis** (bicarbonate loss) with a wide anion gap (lactate from poor perfusion).
  - **Total body potassium is depleted** but serum potassium may appear normal at first because acidosis moves potassium out of cells; it falls as acidosis is corrected.

## 7. Management

**Principle:** replace losses quickly and keep replacing them. Rehydration saves lives; antibiotics are an adjunct.

**Rehydration**

- Mild to moderate dehydration: **oral rehydration solution (ORS)**, with added potassium where available.
- Severe dehydration or inability to drink: **intravenous Ringer's lactate** (with potassium). The goal of the rehydration phase is to restore normal hydration within **4 hours**.
- **Ringer's lactate is preferred over normal saline** because saline does not correct the metabolic acidosis.
- After rehydration, give maintenance fluids matching ongoing stool loss (measure losses; ORS 1 cup per stool in the cholera bed), and add zinc in children.
- Monitor pulse, blood pressure, urine output, stool volume, glucose in children, and potassium.

**Antibiotics** (shorten diarrhoea and reduce volume and excretion)

| Group | Single-dose options |
|---|---|
| Adults | Doxycycline 300 mg orally, or ciprofloxacin 1 g orally, or azithromycin 1 g orally |
| Alternatives | Co-trimoxazole, erythromycin (resistant strains are emerging; follow local sensitivity) |
| Pregnancy | Erythromycin 500 mg every 6 hours for 3 days, or azithromycin 1 g as a single dose |

> **Check:** for children, use weight-based azithromycin or erythromycin per the national guideline; doxycycline and quinolones are avoided in young children and pregnancy where alternatives exist.

**Criteria for discharge**

- Adequate oral intake.
- Normal urine flow (about 40 to 50 mL per hour).
- Stool output no more than 400 mL per hour.

## 8. Prognosis

- Early diagnosis and treatment: mortality **below 1%**.
- Untreated: mortality about **60%**.

## 9. Prevention and control

- Safe water (purification, chlorination), adequate water supplies.
- Proper sanitation; safe disposal and disinfection of infected faeces and waste.
- Simple hygiene measures (handwashing, food safety).
- Health education, especially for pilgrims and travellers.
- Mass chemoprophylaxis is used rarely; **antibiotic prophylaxis is now not recommended** routinely.
- **Notify** suspected cases immediately; it is a notifiable disease.

**Vaccines**

- Mainly **oral**: three in use, **Dukoral, Shanchol and Euvichol**. All need **2 doses** for full protection.
- Efficacy is variable, up to about 60%, short and long term.
- An injectable vaccine exists but is less effective under 5 years and is not WHO recommended.
- Used for high-risk groups and in outbreaks.
- Dose (for the vaccine described): children aged 1 year and over and adults, **2 doses of 1.5 mL at least 14 days apart**. Immunity develops about 1 week after the dose, lasting up to 6 months after one dose and at least 3 years after two.
- **Storage:** 2 to 8 °C; do not freeze (discard if frozen). Shanchol used in the **controlled temperature chain (CTC)** can be kept at up to 40 °C for a maximum of 14 days. Any vaccine out of the cold chain and unused after 14 days, or exposed above 40 °C, must be discarded.

# PART B: BACILLARY DYSENTERY (SHIGELLOSIS)

## 10. Definition and organisms

- Acute bacterial infection of the intestine that is **contagious and occurs in epidemics**.
- The lesion is in the **sigmoid colon and rectum**, producing frequent blood-stained mucoid stools.

| Species | Features |
|---|---|
| *Shigella sonnei* | Commonest; mild disease |
| *S. flexneri* | Moderately severe disease; linked to Reiter's syndrome |
| *S. boydii* | Mainly tropical |
| *S. dysenteriae* (type 1) | Most serious and infectious; produces **Shiga toxin** |

## 11. Pathogenesis and transmission

- **Faeco-oral**; a **very small number of organisms** (about 10 to 100) can cause disease.
- Disease depends on the number of bacteria, toxin production, **invasiveness** (attachment, penetration, multiplication) and host immunity.
- *Shigella* invades the colonic mucosal cells but only as far as the **lamina propria**. Damage comes from intracellular multiplication, cell-to-cell spread and **toxin production**, causing inflammation, ulceration and bleeding.
- **Shiga toxin** causes **haemorrhagic colitis** and **haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS)**.

## 12. Clinical features

- Incubation **1 to 2 days** (hours to 7 days).
- Diarrhoea and vomiting; stools are initially watery, then become **mucoid and blood-stained**.
- **Fever, tenesmus** (painful urge to pass stool), abdominal cramps, constitutional symptoms, and sometimes severe dehydration.

## 13. Differential diagnosis

| Acute dysentery | Distinguishing features |
|---|---|
| **Amoebic dysentery** (*Entamoeba histolytica*) | Reddish-brown "anchovy sauce" or jam-like stool; **flask-shaped ulcers**; amoebic trophozoites with ingested red cells on microscopy; liver abscess |
| Enteritis from *E. coli* (EIEC, EHEC), *Salmonella*, *Campylobacter*, viruses | Culture, stool PCR |

| Chronic dysentery | |
|---|---|
| Rectal and colonic carcinoma | Weight loss, change in bowel habit; colonoscopy |
| Non-specific ulcerative colitis | Relapsing course; colonoscopy and biopsy |
| Chronic schistosomiasis (*S. japonicum*) | Eggs in stool or rectal snip |

## 14. Reactive arthritis (Reiter's syndrome)

- Associated with ***S. flexneri***.
- Chronic **seronegative arthritis** (with urethritis and conjunctivitis in the full triad), caused by circulating bacterial antigen.
- Genetically linked to **HLA-B27**.

## 15. Investigations

- **Fresh stool**: microscopy (pus cells, red cells) and **culture** on selective media such as **MacConkey agar** and **Selenite F broth** (0.4%). Sensitivity testing guides therapy.
- Full blood count, urea, electrolytes and creatinine, especially if HUS is suspected.

## 16. Management

- **Oral fluids**; **IV fluids** if dehydrated.
- **Antibiotics when necessary**: **ciprofloxacin** or **co-trimoxazole**, then according to culture and sensitivity (resistance is common).
- Avoid antimotility drugs (loperamide), which risk toxic megacolon.
- Treat cerebral oedema with **20% mannitol**.
- Treat complications (HUS needs renal support).

## 17. Complications

- Dehydration, **acute renal failure**, intestinal perforation, rectal prolapse, metabolic problems (for example hypoglycaemia), CNS complications (seizures, encephalopathy).
- Rare, mainly in severe disease or low immunity: **toxic megacolon** and **haemolytic uraemic syndrome**.
- Reactive arthritis (Reiter's syndrome).

## 18. Prevention

- Personal hygiene (handwashing), proper sanitation, adequate safe water.
- **No vaccine is available.**

> **Exam tip:** compare the two. Cholera: non-invasive, toxin-only, watery painless stool, no fever, no blood, dehydration. Shigella: invasive, bloody mucoid stool with fever and tenesmus, distal colon.

## Key points to revise

1. *V. cholerae* O1 (El Tor, classical) and O139 cause epidemics; humans are the only reservoir, spread faeco-orally.
2. Cholera toxin (A1 plus five B subunits) ADP-ribosylates Gs, raising cAMP and driving chloride and water secretion.
3. Painless rice-water stool with fishy odour and up to 10 to 20 L per day causes dehydration, acidosis and kidney failure.
4. Diagnose by stool culture on TCBS (yellow colonies) or dipstick; treat on clinical grounds in an outbreak.
5. Rehydrate within 4 hours with ORS or Ringer's lactate; give a single-dose antibiotic (doxycycline, ciprofloxacin or azithromycin).
6. Oral cholera vaccines (Dukoral, Shanchol, Euvichol) need 2 doses at least 14 days apart; untreated mortality is about 60%.
7. Shigella invades the sigmoid and rectum, giving bloody mucoid stool, fever and tenesmus; *S. dysenteriae* (Shiga toxin) causes HUS.
8. Treat shigellosis with fluids and ciprofloxacin or co-trimoxazole per culture; there is no vaccine.
