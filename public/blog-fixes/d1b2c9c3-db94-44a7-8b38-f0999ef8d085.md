# Poisoning by Common Drugs and Agents

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Toxicology**

> **In one line:** most drug overdoses are managed by the same plan (airway, breathing, circulation, ECG monitoring, decontamination, supportive care), but a few drugs have a specific danger and antidote to remember: tricyclics (sodium channel block, treat with bicarbonate), beta blockers (glucagon), calcium channel blockers (calcium, high-dose insulin), iron (desferrioxamine), lithium (hydration, dialysis), and SSRIs (serotonin syndrome, cyproheptadine).

## What you must know

- **Tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs)** cause 20 to 25% of fatal drug poisonings in the UK and US; death is from **cardiac and CNS toxicity**, usually before reaching hospital. Over **15 to 20 mg/kg** is potentially fatal.
- **Sodium bicarbonate** is the key treatment for TCA conduction defects, ventricular arrhythmias and hypotension. **Avoid phenytoin and neuroleptics.**
- **SSRIs** are relatively safe in overdose; the danger is **serotonin syndrome** (mental status change, autonomic instability, neuromuscular hyperactivity). Treat with benzodiazepines and **cyproheptadine**.
- **Lithium** has a narrow therapeutic index; toxicity usually follows therapeutic dosing plus dehydration, diuretics or NSAIDs.
- **Beta blockers:** bradycardia and hypotension, treated with atropine, **glucagon**, isoprenaline, and calcium. **Calcium channel blockers:** calcium, then **high-dose insulin with glucose**.
- **Iron:** GI injury, then liver failure at 12 to 24 hours; treat with **desferrioxamine** when serum iron is over 500 µg/dL.
- **Quinine:** cardiotoxicity, deafness and **blindness**; multiple-dose charcoal.

## 1. General principles

- **ABC**, continuous ECG monitoring.
- **GI decontamination** (when indicated): activated charcoal if the patient is alert, cooperative and has taken a significant dose; if unconscious and requiring intubation, protect the airway first, then pass an orogastric tube, aspirate gastric contents and give charcoal.
- Treat specific complications (seizures, arrhythmias, hypotension, coma) and monitor electrolytes, renal function and blood gases.
- Call the poison centre or use toxicology references where available.

## 2. Tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs)

### Background

- **Examples:** amitriptyline, imipramine, amoxapine, doxepin (dosulepin/dothiepin), nortriptyline, trimipramine.
- Common cause of death from poisoning (20 to 25% of fatal drug poisonings in UK and US). Deaths usually happen **outside hospital**. Ingestion of **15 to 20 mg/kg or more** is potentially fatal.

### Mechanism of action and toxicity

- Therapeutic action: block the **serotonin transporter (SERT)** and **noradrenaline transporter (NET)**, raising synaptic levels of these neurotransmitters.
- Toxic effects come from action at many other receptors:

| Target | Effect |
|---|---|
| **H1 and H2** receptors | Sedation |
| **Alpha 1 and 2** receptors | Vasodilatation, hypotension |
| **GABA-A** | Seizures |
| **Muscarinic** receptors | Anticholinergic effects |
| **Sodium (and other) channels** | Slowed conduction. Sodium influx causes phase 0 depolarisation in cardiac muscle and Purkinje fibres, so blockade slows conduction |

### Clinical effects

- Depend on dose and time since ingestion. Patients **asymptomatic 3 hours after a normal-release preparation** do not normally develop major toxicity.
- Three major toxic syndromes: **anticholinergic, cardiac and CNS**. Death is usually from CNS and/or cardiac toxicity.
- **Anticholinergic:** dry mouth, dilated pupils, urinary retention, hot dry skin, tachycardia, delirium.
- **Cardiac:** sinus tachycardia, **wide QRS**, prolonged PR and QT, right bundle branch block pattern, terminal R wave in aVR, ventricular arrhythmias, hypotension. (A QRS over 100 ms predicts seizures and over 160 ms predicts ventricular arrhythmias.)
- **CNS:** rapid-onset decreased consciousness and coma (rapid absorption); look for **myoclonic jerks, hypereflexia and seizures**. Seizures are especially associated with **desipramine, amoxapine and dothiepin**. Convulsions can cause haemodynamic compromise.

### Treatment

- ABC, ECG monitoring.
- GI decontamination: **oral activated charcoal** if the patient is alert and cooperative and has ingested **over 5 mg/kg**; otherwise intubate, aspirate gastric contents via orogastric tube, then give charcoal.
- **Seizures:** diazepam 5 to 20 mg IV; phenobarbitone 15 to 18 mg/kg IV. **Avoid phenytoin** (it also blocks sodium channels and worsens conduction).
- **Anticholinergic delirium:** mild cases need reassurance, with or without a benzodiazepine. **Avoid neuroleptics** (most have significant anticholinergic activity).
- **Sodium bicarbonate:** gives a sodium load and alkalinisation. It is effective in reversing TCA-induced conduction defects and hypotension and is the **drug of choice for ventricular dysrhythmias and/or hypotension** from TCA poisoning.

> **Exam tip:** TCA overdose with a wide QRS, arrhythmia or hypotension means **IV sodium bicarbonate**. Avoid phenytoin, class Ia and Ic antiarrhythmics, and neuroleptics.

## 3. NSAIDs

- Usually minor: abdominal discomfort, vomiting and diarrhoea. **Convulsions** occur occasionally, especially with **mefenamic acid**.
- After substantial overdose (rare): coma, prolonged seizures, apnoea, liver dysfunction and renal failure.
- **Phenylbutazone and mefenamic acid** are the most toxic, able to cause all the above plus seizures.
- **Management:** activated charcoal if the patient presents early; symptomatic treatment of nausea and GI irritation (a PPI for gastric protection).

## 4. SSRIs and serotonin syndrome

### SSRI toxicity

- **Examples:** escitalopram, sertraline, fluoxetine, paroxetine, citalopram, fluvoxamine.
- Serotonergic neurons in the brainstem regulate mood, personality, temperature and wakefulness. **98% of body serotonin is peripheral**, regulating vascular tone, peristalsis and platelet activation.
- Compared with other antidepressants SSRIs **rarely cause death or serious sequelae**. Most fatalities follow very high doses (for example 150 times the dose) or co-ingestants.
- Overdose: nausea and vomiting, tremor, insomnia, sinus tachycardia. Agitation, drowsiness and convulsions are infrequent and may be delayed for several hours. Arrhythmias are infrequent; most patients need supportive care only.
- Unlikely to cause CNS depression, seizure, or significant cardiotoxicity (the exception is **citalopram**, with **QTc prolongation**).
- Can cause **hyponatraemia** (even at therapeutic doses). Do not typically cause anticholinergic symptoms, sedation or hypotension.

### Serotonin syndrome

- **Classical triad:** **mental status change, autonomic instability, increased neuromuscular tone** (hyperreflexia, clonus, rigidity). Seen with therapeutic use, drug interactions and deliberate self-poisoning.
- Drugs that precipitate it:

| Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|
| Increased serotonin formation | L-tryptophan |
| Impaired reuptake | Cocaine, ecstasy, SSRIs, SNRIs, TCAs, St John's wort |
| Increased release | Amphetamines, cocaine |
| Inhibited metabolism | **MAOIs**, linezolid |
| Direct serotonin agonists | Triptans, LSD, fentanyl |
| Increased receptor sensitivity | Lithium |

- **Differential diagnosis:** neuroleptic malignant syndrome, anticholinergic toxicity, malignant hyperthermia, sympathomimetic toxicity, meningitis and encephalitis.

> **Exam tip:** serotonin syndrome has **clonus and hyperreflexia with rapid onset** (hours); neuroleptic malignant syndrome has **lead-pipe rigidity and bradyreflexia** over days.

### Management

- **Stop all serotonergic agents.**
- **Sedate with benzodiazepines**; supportive care, cooling for hyperthermia.
- **Serotonin antagonist: cyproheptadine** 12 mg orally initially, then 2 mg every 2 hours if symptoms continue, or 4 to 8 mg orally until clinical response. (The slides also list trazodone and nefazodone, which are not routinely used for this; cyproheptadine is the standard.)

## 5. Lithium

### Background

- Used since **1870**; first for gout, then depression. Fell out of favour because of side effects; now **standard of care for bipolar disorder**.
- Taken orally (liquid or capsule). Excreted by the kidney, but **85% is reabsorbed**. It has a **narrow therapeutic index**.
- Lithium behaves like sodium in the body and its levels depend on sodium. **Salt intake, hydration, caffeine** and drug interactions all alter levels.

### Toxicity

- Occurs after intentional overdose, but **more often in patients on therapeutic doses**, because of interactions with **diuretics or NSAIDs** (dehydration, renal impairment) or an excessive prescribed dose.
- **Mild:** nausea, diarrhoea, polyuria (nephrogenic diabetes insipidus), dizziness, tremor.
- **Progressing to:** muscular weakness, drowsiness, confusion, myoclonus.
- **Severe:** coma, convulsions, ataxia, cardiac dysrhythmias such as heart block, blood pressure disturbances and renal failure.

### Management

- Check **serum lithium** concentration (repeat, since levels can rise with sustained-release forms).
- Ensure **adequate hydration** (IV saline). Stop lithium and interacting drugs.
- **Haemodialysis** for severe toxicity (neurological signs, renal failure or very high levels). Activated charcoal does **not** bind lithium.

## 6. Beta blockers

- Major toxicity: **bradycardia and hypotension**. Severe: heart block, pulmonary oedema, cardiogenic shock. May cause seizures, confusion and coma. **Sotalol** causes repolarisation abnormalities (QTc prolongation) and **torsades de pointes**.
- **Management:**
  - IV fluids (caution with pulmonary oedema)
  - **Atropine** for bradycardia and hypotension (high doses, up to 3 mg in an adult)
  - **Isoprenaline** (adrenoceptor agonist)
  - **Glucagon:** 50 µg/kg IV loading dose, then continuous infusion of 1 to 15 mg/hour. It increases inotropy by activating adenylate cyclase through a mechanism separate from catecholamines, **bypassing the beta blockade**
  - **Calcium salts** in hypotensive patients who overdose on beta blockers alone or with calcium channel blockers

## 7. Calcium channel blockers (CCBs)

- Highly toxic in overdose because they inhibit **L-type calcium channels**.
  - **Dihydropyridines** (nifedipine, amlodipine): mainly vascular smooth muscle, so **vasodilatation**.
  - **Diltiazem and verapamil:** predominantly cardiac, with **bradycardia and reduced contractility**.
- **Management:**
  - Hypotension: IV fluids
  - Persistent hypotension: **IV calcium gluconate** repeated as needed
  - Isoprenaline and glucagon
  - **High-dose insulin euglycaemic therapy (hyperinsulinaemia):** IV insulin with 10 to 20% dextrose, starting at 0.5 to 2.0 U/kg/hour and increasing to 5 to 10 U/kg/hour according to response, for patients not responding to other measures
- CCB poisoning is also associated with **hypoinsulinaemia and insulin resistance**, which impede glucose uptake by myocytes; this is why insulin helps.

> **Check:** the slide gives calcium gluconate as "10 mg IV over 5 minutes". The usual adult dose is **10 mL of 10% calcium gluconate** (about 1 g) over about 5 minutes, repeated as required.

## 8. Quinine

- Deaths reported with as little as **1.5 g in an adult and 900 mg in a child**.
- **Toxicity (cinchonism):** nausea, vomiting, tremor, tinnitus and deafness.
- **Serious poisoning:** hypotension, haemolysis, renal failure, ataxia, convulsions, coma; ventricular tachycardia (including torsades de pointes), ventricular fibrillation and sudden death.
- **Eye:** retinal photoreceptor toxicity causes blurred vision and impaired colour perception. Visual loss can be permanent, though some recovery, especially of central vision, often occurs over weeks.
- **Management:** **multiple-dose activated charcoal** for ingestions over 15 mg/kg; gastric lavage; 12-lead ECG, cardiac monitoring, urea and electrolytes; treat arrhythmias, hypotension, seizures and coma. **There is no effective treatment for the visual effects.**

## 9. Iron

### Clinical presentation

- Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain; **grey or black stools**, rectal bleeding.
- **Hyperglycaemia and leucocytosis**.
- Drowsiness, convulsions, coma, metabolic acidosis and cardiovascular collapse.
- **Hepatocellular necrosis at 12 to 24 hours** after overdose may progress to hepatic failure.
- **Gastrointestinal strictures** (pyloric stenosis) are a late complication.

### Management

- **Gastric lavage** (charcoal does not bind iron).
- **Desferrioxamine**, especially if **serum iron over 500 µg/dL**. Adverse effects: hypotension, allergic reactions, occasionally pulmonary oedema.
- Dose as given: **15 mg/kg/hour infusion**, for no longer than 24 hours, maximum 30 mg/kg/hour.

> **Check:** the standard maximum is lower in many references (infusion up to 15 mg/kg/hour, total not above about 80 mg/kg in 24 hours). Follow the national poison guideline.

## 10. Antidiabetic agents

- **Sulfonylureas:** chlorpropamide, glibenclamide, gliclazide, glipizide, tolbutamide. Cause prolonged **hypoglycaemia**.
- **Metformin:** rarely causes hypoglycaemia; the danger is **lactic acidosis**, especially in older patients, renal or hepatic impairment, or co-ingested ethanol.

### Management

- Activated charcoal.
- Correct **hypoglycaemia** with oral or IV glucose (50 mL of 50% dextrose); an infusion of **10 to 20% dextrose** may be needed to prevent recurrence. Octreotide is used for sulfonylurea-induced recurrent hypoglycaemia.
- IM **glucagon**.
- Urea and electrolytes; **arterial blood gases** to look for lactic acidosis (metformin).
- Treat acidosis with **sodium bicarbonate** (250 mL of 1.26% solution or 50 mL of 8.4% solution, repeated as necessary).

## 11. Cannabis

- **Smoked:** onset within 10 to 30 minutes. **Ingested:** onset 1 to 3 hours later. **Duration** 4 to 8 hours.
- **Clinical:** euphoria, perceptual alterations, conjunctival injection, then increased appetite and relaxation; occasionally hypertension, tachycardia, slurred speech, ataxia, anxiety, confusion, hallucinations and psychosis.
- Long-term use is thought to increase the lifetime risk of **schizophrenia**.
- Serious poisoning is rare; **supportive treatment** is all that is required.

## Key points to revise

1. TCA poisoning kills by cardiac and CNS toxicity; treat arrhythmia and hypotension with sodium bicarbonate; avoid phenytoin and neuroleptics.
2. Patients asymptomatic 3 hours after normal-release TCA ingestion rarely develop major toxicity.
3. SSRIs are relatively safe; watch for serotonin syndrome (triad), hyponatraemia, and citalopram QTc prolongation. Treat with benzodiazepines and cyproheptadine.
4. Lithium toxicity is mostly from therapeutic dosing with dehydration or NSAIDs and diuretics; hydrate and dialyse if severe.
5. Beta blocker overdose: atropine, glucagon, isoprenaline, calcium. CCB overdose: calcium, glucagon, high-dose insulin with glucose.
6. Iron: GI, then liver failure at 12 to 24 hours, strictures later; desferrioxamine for serum iron above 500 µg/dL.
7. Quinine: cardiotoxicity, deafness, irreversible visual loss; multiple-dose charcoal.
8. Sulfonylureas cause hypoglycaemia; metformin causes lactic acidosis.
