## Summary
This document provides a comprehensive overview of lung pathology, covering normal lung structure, common conditions like atelectasis and ARDS, and detailed explanations of obstructive and restrictive lung diseases. It delves into specific obstructive conditions such as emphysema, chronic bronchitis, asthma, and bronchiectasis, and examines various chronic interstitial lung diseases including idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, pneumoconioses (coal dust, silica, asbestos), sarcoidosis, hypersensitivity pneumonitis, pulmonary eosinophilia, and smoking-related interstitial diseases. The text also addresses pulmonary vascular diseases, focusing on pulmonary embolism, pulmonary hypertension, and diffuse alveolar haemorrhage syndromes. Finally, it details a wide range of pulmonary infections, from community-acquired bacterial and viral pneumonias to hospital-acquired, aspiration, and abscess formations, with specific sections on tuberculosis, nontuberculous mycobacteria, fungal infections, pneumonia in immunocompromised hosts, and pulmonary disease in HIV.

## Key Points
- **Acinus**: The functional unit of the lung distal to the terminal bronchiole, responsible for gas exchange.
- **Atelectasis**: Loss of lung volume due to inadequate expansion of air spaces, potentially reversible except for contraction atelectasis.
- **ARDS (Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome)**: Severe lung injury characterized by diffuse alveolar damage, hypoxemia, and non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema.
- **Obstructive Lung Disease**: Characterized by increased resistance to airflow, leading to a decreased FEV1/FVC ratio (e.g., emphysema, asthma).
- **Restrictive Lung Disease**: Characterized by reduced lung expansion, leading to a reduced FVC with a near-normal FEV1/FVC ratio (e.g., IPF, sarcoidosis).
- **Emphysema**: Permanent enlargement of air spaces distal to terminal bronchioles with alveolar wall destruction.
- **Chronic Bronchitis**: Defined clinically by a persistent productive cough for at least 3 months in 2 consecutive years, marked by mucus hypersecretion.
- **Asthma**: Chronic inflammatory airway disorder with reversible obstruction, bronchial inflammation, and hyperreactivity, driven by TH2 cells and eosinophils.
- **Bronchiectasis**: Permanent dilation of bronchi and bronchioles due to destruction of smooth muscle and elastic tissue, often secondary to persistent infection or obstruction.
- **Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (IPF)**: A chronic, progressive interstitial lung disease of unknown etiology characterized by Usual Interstitial Pneumonia (UIP) pattern.
- **Pneumoconioses**: Lung disorders caused by inhalation of mineral dusts, most commonly coal dust, silica, and asbestos.
- **Sarcoidosis**: A multisystem disease characterized by noncaseating granulomatous inflammation in various organs.
- **Hypersensitivity Pneumonitis**: An immunologically mediated inflammatory lung disease affecting alveoli, triggered by inhaled antigens.
- **Pulmonary Embolism**: Blockage of pulmonary arteries, most commonly by thrombi from deep leg veins.
- **Pulmonary Hypertension**: Elevated pulmonary arterial pressure, classified into five groups based on underlying causes.
- **Diffuse Alveolar Haemorrhage Syndromes**: Conditions involving bleeding into the alveolar spaces, such as Goodpasture syndrome and Granulomatosis with Polyangiitis (GPA).
- **Tuberculosis**: A communicable chronic granulomatous disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, characterized by granuloma formation and potential for cavitation.
- **Pneumonia in Immunocompromised Host**: Increased susceptibility to opportunistic infections like Pneumocystis jiroveci, CMV, fungi, and atypical bacteria.

## Detailed Notes

### 1. Normal Lung Structure

**Key concepts:** Airway anatomy · Acinus · Alveolar wall components · Cell types

- The respiratory system develops as an outgrowth from the ventral wall of the foregut.
- The right lung has 3 lobes and 3 main bronchi; the left lung has 2 lobes and 2 main bronchi.
- Airways progress from bronchi to bronchioles (lacking cartilage and submucosal glands) to terminal bronchioles, and finally to the acinus.
- The **acinus** is the part of the lung distal to the terminal bronchiole, consisting of respiratory bronchioles, alveolar ducts, alveolar sacs, and alveoli, which are the primary sites of gas exchange.

**Alveolar Wall Components (Blood to Air):**
- Capillary endothelium and its basement membrane.
- Pulmonary interstitium, containing elastic fibers, collagen, fibroblasts, smooth muscle cells, mast cells, and mononuclear cells.
- Alveolar epithelium:
    - **Type I pneumocytes**: Flat, plate-like cells covering 95% of the alveolar surface.
    - **Type II pneumocytes**: Rounded cells that synthesize surfactant and are crucial for repair after type I pneumocyte injury.
- Alveolar macrophages: These cells reside freely in the alveolar space and can contain carbon particles in urban dwellers.

### 2. Atelectasis (Collapse)

**Conditions covered:** Resorption · Compression · Contraction atelectasis

- Atelectasis is the loss of lung volume caused by the inadequate expansion of air spaces.
- It leads to ventilation-perfusion (V/Q) imbalance and hypoxia.
- Except for contraction atelectasis, other forms are potentially reversible.

**Three Forms:**

**Resorption Atelectasis**
- Occurs when an obstruction prevents air from reaching distal airways. The air distal to the obstruction is gradually absorbed, leading to alveolar collapse.
- The most common cause is obstruction of a bronchus.
- Causes include: postoperative mucous plugs, foreign body aspiration (especially in children), bronchial asthma, bronchiectasis, chronic bronchitis, and intrabronchial tumors (which may be the first sign of malignancy).

**Compression Atelectasis**
- Occurs due to the accumulation of fluid, blood, or air in the pleural cavity, which compresses the lung.
- Causes include: pleural effusion (e.g., in congestive heart failure) and pneumothorax (air leakage into the pleural cavity).
- Basal atelectasis can result from failure to breathe deeply, commonly seen in bedridden patients, those with ascites, or post-surgery.

**Contraction Atelectasis (Cicatrization)**
- Caused by local or diffuse fibrosis of the lung or pleura, which hampers lung expansion.
- This form of atelectasis is irreversible.

### 3. Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS)

**Key concepts:** DAD · Neutrophil-mediated injury · Hyaline membranes · Clinical course

- ARDS is defined as respiratory failure occurring within one week of a known clinical insult, characterized by bilateral opacities on chest imaging that are not fully explained by effusions, atelectasis, cardiac failure, or fluid overload.
- Severity is graded based on changes in arterial blood oxygenation.
- **Most frequent triggers** include pneumonia (35–45%) and sepsis (30–35%), followed by aspiration, trauma, pancreatitis, and transfusion reactions.
- **It is distinct from respiratory distress syndrome of the newborn**, which is caused by surfactant deficiency due to prematurity.

**Pathogenesis**
- The integrity of the alveolar-capillary membrane is compromised by endothelial and epithelial injury.
- Within 30 minutes of an acute insult, pulmonary macrophages synthesize **IL-8**, a potent neutrophil chemotactic agent.
- IL-8, IL-1, and TNF contribute to endothelial activation, leading to neutrophil sequestration and activation within pulmonary capillaries.
- Activated neutrophils release **reactive oxygen species and proteases**, damaging the alveolar epithelium and endothelium.
- This results in vascular leakiness and loss of surfactant, rendering the alveolar units unable to expand.
- The outcome is determined by the balance between destructive forces and endogenous anti-proteases and anti-oxidants.

**Morphology — Acute Phase**
- Lungs appear dark red, firm, airless, and heavy.
- Characterized by capillary congestion, necrosis of alveolar epithelial cells, and interstitial and intraalveolar edema and hemorrhage.
- **Hyaline membranes** are the most characteristic finding, lining distended alveolar ducts and composed of fibrin-rich edema fluid and remnants of necrotic epithelial cells.
- Collections of neutrophils are often seen in capillaries, especially in cases of sepsis.

**Morphology — Organizing Stage**
- Type II pneumocytes proliferate vigorously, attempting to regenerate the alveolar lining.
- Fibrin-rich exudates organize into intraalveolar fibrosis.
- There is marked thickening of the alveolar septa due to interstitial cell proliferation and collagen deposition.

**Clinical Features**
- Affects approximately 190,000 patients per year in the USA.
- 85% of cases develop within 72 hours of the initial insult.
- Overall hospital mortality is 38.5% (27% for mild, 32% for moderate, and 45% for severe ARDS).
- Poor prognostic factors include advanced age, bacteremia, and multi-organ failure.
- Survivors typically recover normal respiratory function within 6–12 months, though some may develop diffuse interstitial fibrosis.

### 4. Obstructive vs Restrictive Lung Disease

**Key distinction — Pulmonary Function Tests:**

| Feature           | Obstructive                      | Restrictive                        |
| :---------------- | :------------------------------- | :--------------------------------- |
| **FVC**           | Normal or slightly decreased     | Reduced                            |
| **FEV1**          | Significantly decreased          | Normal or reduced proportionately  |
| **FEV1/FVC ratio**| **Decreased**                    | **Near normal**                    |
| **Mechanism**     | Increased resistance to airflow  | Reduced lung expansion             |

**Obstructive diseases:** Emphysema, Chronic bronchitis, Asthma, Bronchiectasis.
**Restrictive diseases:** ARDS (acute), Pneumoconioses, IPF, Sarcoidosis (chronic); also chest wall disorders (obesity, pleural disease, neuromuscular disorders).

### 5. Obstructive Lung Diseases

**Conditions covered:** Emphysema · Chronic Bronchitis · Asthma · Bronchiectasis

#### Emphysema

- Permanent enlargement of air spaces distal to the terminal bronchioles with destruction of their walls, occurring **without significant fibrosis**.
- Classified based on its anatomic distribution within the acinus.

**Four Types:**

| Type                      | Location Affected                                       | Association                                       |
| :------------------------ | :------------------------------------------------------ | :------------------------------------------------ |
| Centriacinar (centrilobular) | Central/proximal acinus (respiratory bronchioles); distal alveoli spared | Cigarette smoking; upper lobes; most common       |
| Panacinar (panlobular)    | Entire acinus uniformly enlarged                        | α1-antitrypsin deficiency; lower lobes            |
| Distal acinar (paraseptal) | Proximal acinus normal; distal part involved; adjacent to pleura and septa | Young adults; spontaneous pneumothorax; may form bullae |
| Irregular                 | Acinus irregularly involved; associated with scarring    | Healed inflammatory disease; clinically asymptomatic; possibly most common form |

- Only **centriacinar and panacinar** emphysema cause significant airway obstruction.
- Centriacinar is approximately 20 times more common than panacinar.

**Pathogenesis — Key Mechanisms:**
- Smoking and noxious particles trigger lung damage and inflammation, leading to parenchymal destruction.
- **Protease-antiprotease imbalance**: Neutrophil and macrophage-derived proteases (like elastase) destroy connective tissue; deficiency of anti-proteases exacerbates this destruction.
- **α1-antitrypsin deficiency**: Encoded by the Pi locus on chromosome 14; homozygous Z allele results in markedly decreased serum levels. Over 80% of affected individuals develop symptomatic panacinar emphysema, worsened by smoking. It accounts for approximately 1% of all emphysema cases.
- **Oxidative stress**: Reactive oxygen species from cigarette smoke and activated macrophages/neutrophils cause additional tissue damage.
- **Inflammatory mediators**: Leukotriene B4, IL-8, and TNF recruit neutrophils, macrophages, and CD4+/CD8+ T cells.
- Airway infection is not an initiating factor but can cause acute exacerbations.
- Loss of elastic tissue in alveolar walls reduces radial traction on small airways, leading to **airway collapse during expiration**, resulting in functional airflow obstruction.

**Morphology:**
- Panacinar emphysema: Lungs appear pale, voluminous, often obscuring the heart at autopsy.
- Centriacinar emphysema: Lungs are deeper pink, less voluminous, with more severe involvement in the upper two-thirds.
- Histology shows destruction of alveolar walls without fibrosis, leading to enlarged air spaces, diminished alveolar capillaries, and deformed terminal and respiratory bronchioles.
- **Bullous emphysema**: Characterized by large subpleural blebs or bullae greater than 1 cm, which may rupture and cause pneumothorax.

**Clinical Features:**
- Dyspnea is the first and most prominent symptom, with insidious onset and steady progression.
- Reduced FEV1 and normal/near-normal FVC result in a reduced FEV1/FVC ratio.
- **"Pink puffers"**: Patients with pure emphysema, often barrel-chested, dyspneic, with prolonged expiration and a hunched-forward posture. They maintain adequate oxygenation until late stages and exhibit prominent hyperventilation.
- **"Blue bloaters"**: Patients with emphysema plus chronic bronchitis. They experience less dyspnea, have CO2 retention, are hypoxic and cyanotic, and are often obese. They are prone to recurrent infections.
- Secondary pulmonary hypertension can develop, leading to cor pulmonale (right-sided heart failure) in 20–30% of patients.
- Death typically results from respiratory failure or right-sided heart failure.

**Conditions Related to Emphysema:**
- **Compensatory emphysema**: Dilation of residual alveoli after surgical removal of diseased lung or lobe.
- **Obstructive overinflation**: Expansion of lung due to air trapping from subtotal airway obstruction, which can be life-threatening.
- **Mediastinal (interstitial) emphysema**: Air enters the lung interstitium and tracks to the mediastinum and subcutaneous tissue. It can follow sudden increases in intraalveolar pressure (e.g., vomiting, violent coughing), whooping cough, or respirator use. Subcutaneous emphysema results in the patient "blowing up like a balloon" with crackling crepitation.

#### Chronic Bronchitis

- **Defined clinically**: Persistent productive cough for at least **3 consecutive months in at least 2 consecutive years**.
- Common in cigarette smokers and urban dwellers, affecting 20–25% of men aged 40–65.

**Pathogenesis:**
- A distinctive feature is **hypersecretion of mucus beginning in the large airways**.
- Cigarette smoking is the most important cause, along with irritants like sulfur dioxide and nitrogen dioxide.
- Irritants lead to hypertrophy of mucous glands in the trachea and bronchi, and an increase in goblet cells in smaller bronchi and bronchioles.
- Inflammation involves macrophages, neutrophils, and lymphocytes, but **not eosinophils** (unlike asthma).
- Airflow obstruction arises from:
    1. **Small airway disease (chronic bronchiolitis)**: Characterized by mucous plugging, inflammation, and bronchiolar wall fibrosis.
    2. **Coexistent emphysema**.
- IL-13 from T cells and innate lymphoid cells plays a significant role in many effects, increasing mucin gene transcription and neutrophil elastase production in response to tobacco smoke.
- Microbial infection plays a secondary role, perpetuating inflammation and exacerbating symptoms.

**Morphology:**
- The mucosal lining of larger airways is hyperemic, swollen, and covered by mucinous or mucopurulent secretions.
- **Enlargement of mucus-secreting glands** is a diagnostic feature, assessed by the **Reid index** (ratio of submucosal gland thickness to bronchial wall thickness; normally ≤0.4, increased in chronic bronchitis).
- Chronic bronchiolitis involves goblet cell metaplasia, mucous plugging, inflammation, and fibrosis.
- In severe cases, complete obliteration of the lumen occurs, termed **bronchiolitis obliterans**.
- Emphysematous changes often coexist.

**Clinical Features:**
- Some patients have persistent cough and sputum production without significant ventilatory dysfunction.
- Others develop COPD with hypercapnia, hypoxemia, and cyanosis.
- Frequent exacerbations lead to more rapid progression and poorer outcomes than emphysema alone.
- Progressive disease can lead to pulmonary hypertension, cardiac failure, recurrent infections, and respiratory failure.

#### Asthma

- A chronic inflammatory disorder of the airways causing **recurrent episodes of wheezing, breathlessness, chest tightness, and cough** (particularly at night or early morning).
- **Hallmarks**: Intermittent reversible airway obstruction, chronic bronchial inflammation with eosinophils, bronchial smooth muscle hypertrophy and hyperreactivity, and increased mucus secretion.
- The incidence has increased significantly in Western countries over the past four decades.
- The **hygiene hypothesis** suggests that reduced exposure to infectious organisms in early childhood may lead to defects in immune tolerance, predisposing to later hyperreactivity.

**Types of Asthma:**

**1. Atopic Asthma (most common)**
- A classic Type I IgE-mediated hypersensitivity reaction.
- Usually begins in childhood and is associated with a positive family history of atopy.
- Triggered by allergens (dust, pollen, animal dander, food) or infections.
- Skin testing with the offending antigen elicits an immediate wheal-and-flare reaction.
- Driven by **TH2 cells**, with key cytokines including:
    - **IL-4 and IL-13**: Stimulate IgE production.
    - **IL-5**: Activates eosinophils.
    - **IL-13**: Stimulates mucus production.
- IgE coats submucosal mast cells. Allergen exposure leads to mast cell degranulation, causing a **two-phase reaction**:
    - **Early (immediate) phase**: Bronchoconstriction, increased mucus, vasodilation; mediated by histamine, PGD2, leukotrienes (LTC4/D4/E4), and reflex neural pathways.
    - **Late phase**: Inflammatory response; chemokines (including eotaxin) recruit TH2 cells, eosinophils, and leukocytes, amplifying the inflammatory reaction.

**2. Non-Atopic Asthma**
- No evidence of allergen sensitization; skin tests are negative.
- Triggered by viral infections (rhinovirus, parainfluenza) and inhaled pollutants (SO2, ozone, NO2).
- Virus-induced inflammation lowers the threshold of subepithelial vagal receptors to irritants.
- It shares the same ultimate mediators as atopic asthma and is treated similarly.

**3. Drug-Induced Asthma**
- **Aspirin** is a prominent example, inhibiting cyclo-oxygenase and leading to an abnormality in prostaglandin metabolism.
- Presents with rhinitis, nasal polyps, urticaria, and bronchospasm.

**4. Occupational Asthma**
- Triggered by fumes (epoxy resins, plastics), organic/chemical dusts (wood, cotton, platinum), or gases (toluene).
- Attacks develop after repeated exposure to the inciting antigen.

**Airway Remodeling**: Structural changes occur from repeated bouts of inflammation, including:
- Hypertrophy of bronchial smooth muscle and mucous glands.
- Increased vascularity.
- Subepithelial collagen deposition.
- These changes can occur years before symptoms begin.

**Morphology (fatal/biopsy cases):**
- Lungs are distended due to air trapping, with small areas of atelectasis.
- **Curschmann spirals**: Mucous plugs containing whorls of shed epithelium occluding bronchi/bronchioles.
- **Charcot-Leyden crystals**: Crystalloids formed from eosinophil protein galectin-10.
- Airway remodeling changes include: thickening of the airway wall, **sub-basement membrane fibrosis** (a characteristic finding), increased submucosal vascularity, enlarged submucosal glands with goblet cell metaplasia, and hypertrophy/hyperplasia of bronchial muscle.

**Clinical Features:**
- Severe dyspnea and wheezing result from bronchoconstriction and mucus plugging, leading to air trapping and progressive hyperinflation.
- Attacks last from one to several hours and subside spontaneously or with therapy.
- Intervals between attacks are typically free from overt respiratory difficulties.
- **Status asthmaticus**: A severe paroxysm that does not respond to therapy, persisting for days to weeks. It is associated with hypercapnia, acidosis, and severe hypoxia, posing a potentially fatal risk.
- Treatment involves glucocorticoids (anti-inflammatory), bronchodilators (beta-adrenergic drugs, leukotriene inhibitors), and agents blocking IL-4/IL-5.
- **Bronchial thermoplasty** is a procedure that uses controlled thermal energy during bronchoscopy to reduce bronchial smooth muscle mass.

#### Bronchiectasis

- **Permanent dilation of bronchi and bronchioles** caused by the destruction of smooth muscle and elastic tissue. It typically results from or is associated with chronic necrotizing infections.
- Bronchiectasis is **not a primary disorder** but is always secondary to persistent infection or obstruction.

**Predisposing Conditions:**
- **Bronchial obstruction**: Caused by tumors, foreign bodies, or mucus impaction. It also complicates asthma and chronic bronchitis.
- **Cystic fibrosis**: Leads to widespread severe bronchiectasis due to obstruction by viscid mucus and secondary infections.
- **Immunodeficiency states** (especially immunoglobulin deficiencies): Result in recurrent bacterial infections.
- **Primary ciliary dyskinesia (immotile cilia syndrome)**: A rare autosomal recessive disorder characterized by inherited abnormalities of cilia, impairing mucociliary clearance and leading to persistent infections. It is also associated with infertility in males.
- **Necrotizing/suppurative pneumonia**: Caused by organisms like *S. aureus* and *Klebsiella* spp. Post-tuberculosis bronchiectasis is significant in endemic areas.

**Pathogenesis:**
- Involves two intertwined processes: **obstruction and chronic infection**. Either can be the initiator.
- Obstruction leads to impaired secretion clearance and subsequent infection. The resulting inflammatory damage causes airway dilation, which becomes irreversible.
- Persistent necrotizing infection leads to poor clearance, obstruction, peribronchial fibrosis, and traction on the bronchi, ultimately causing bronchiectasis.

**Morphology:**
- Typically affects the lower lobes bilaterally due to the more vertical air passages.
- Bronchiectasis caused by tumors or aspiration is sharply localized to a single segment.
- Airways can be dilated up to **four times** their usual diameter and are visible almost to the pleural surface (normal bronchioles are not visible beyond 2–3 cm from the pleura).
- Histology varies with activity and chronicity:
    - Active cases show intense acute and chronic inflammatory exudate, leading to desquamation of the lining epithelium and ulceration.
    - Organisms commonly found include staphylococci, streptococci, pneumococci, *Haemophilus influenzae*, and *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*.
    - During healing, the epithelium may regenerate, but dilation and scarring persist. Fibrosis of the bronchial and bronchiolar walls can occur, and abscess cavities may form.

**Clinical Features:**
- Severe persistent cough with expectoration of mucopurulent, sometimes fetid sputum.
- Dyspnea, rhinosinusitis, and hemoptysis are common.
- Episodes are often precipitated by upper respiratory tract infections or new pathogens.
- Severe, widespread disease can lead to obstructive ventilatory defects, hypoxemia, hypercapnia, pulmonary hypertension, and cor pulmonale.
- Complications, now less frequent, include brain abscess, amyloidosis, and cor pulmonale.

### 6. Chronic Interstitial (Restrictive, Infiltrative) Lung Diseases

**Key shared features:**
- Bilateral, often patchy, pulmonary fibrosis primarily affecting the alveolar walls.
- Reduced lung compliance, requiring increased effort to breathe (dyspnea).
- Damage to the alveolar epithelium and interstitial vasculature, leading to abnormal ventilation-perfusion (V/Q) ratios and hypoxia.
- Chest radiographs show small nodules, irregular lines, or ground-glass shadows.
- In advanced stages, patients develop respiratory failure, pulmonary hypertension, and cor pulmonale, with end-stage disease appearing as "honeycomb lung."
- Pulmonary function tests typically show a reduced FVC with a near-normal FEV1/FVC ratio (restrictive pattern).

#### Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (IPF)

- A pulmonary disorder of **unknown etiology** characterized by patchy, progressive bilateral interstitial fibrosis.
- Also known as **cryptogenic fibrosing alveolitis**.
- Affects males more than females and is a disease of aging, rarely occurring before age 50 (and very rarely before 55).
- The histologic and radiologic pattern required for diagnosis is **Usual Interstitial Pneumonia (UIP)**.
- It is a **diagnosis of exclusion**, as similar changes can be seen in asbestosis and collagen vascular diseases.

**Pathogenesis:**
- Believed to result from repeated injury and defective repair of the alveolar epithelium in genetically predisposed individuals.
- **Genetic associations**:
    - Germline mutations in **telomerase genes** lead to cellular senescence and a profibrotic phenotype.
    - A **MUC5B gene variant** (present in ~35% of affected individuals) alters mucin production.
    - Germline mutations in surfactant genes.
    - These genes are primarily expressed in lung epithelial cells, suggesting an epithelial cell abnormality is the primary initiator.
- Abnormal epithelial repair leads to exuberant fibroblastic/myofibroblastic proliferation, forming **fibroblastic foci**.
- Excessive activation of **TGF-β** (a profibrotic factor) drives the fibrotic process.

**Morphology:**
- Pleural surfaces may appear cobblestoned due to retraction of scars along interlobular septa.
- The cut surface reveals firm, rubbery white areas of fibrosis, preferentially located in the lower lobes, subpleural regions, and along interlobular septa.
- Histology shows **patchy interstitial fibrosis** of varying intensity, which is the hallmark.
- The earliest lesions are **fibroblastic foci**, representing exuberant fibroblastic proliferation.
- Over time, these areas become more collagenous and less cellular.
- **Honeycomb fibrosis** develops as dense fibrosis leads to the collapse of alveolar walls and the formation of cystic spaces lined by hyperplastic type II pneumocytes or bronchiolar epithelium.
- Interstitial inflammation is patchy, predominantly composed of lymphocytes, with occasional plasma cells, mast cells, and eosinophils.
- Secondary pulmonary hypertensive changes are often present.

**Clinical Features:**
- Gradual onset of non-productive cough and progressive dyspnea.
- Physical examination characteristically reveals **"dry" or "Velcro-like" crackles** during inspiration.
- Late stages may present with cyanosis, cor pulmonale, and peripheral edema.
- Anti-inflammatory therapies have limited efficacy as inflammation is secondary.
- **Anti-fibrotic therapies** include:
    - **Nintedanib** (a tyrosine kinase inhibitor).
    - **Pirfenidone** (an inhibitor of TGF-β).
    - Both are approved for IPF and have shown positive clinical trial outcomes.
- The **overall prognosis is poor**, with a survival of 3–5 years. Lung transplantation is the only definitive treatment.

**Other Fibrosing Diseases:**
- **NSIP (Non-specific Interstitial Pneumonia)**: A chronic bilateral interstitial lung disease frequently associated with collagen vascular disorders (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis). It has a **much better prognosis than IPF**. Histologically, it shows mild to moderate interstitial chronic inflammation and fibrosis that is patchy but uniform.
- **Cryptogenic organizing pneumonia**: Presents with cough and dyspnea. Radiographically, it shows subpleural or peribronchial patchy airspace consolidation from intraalveolar plugs of loose organizing connective tissue. Some patients recover spontaneously, while most are treated with oral steroids.

#### Pneumoconioses

- Lung disorders caused by the inhalation of mineral dusts. The term has been broadened to include inorganic particulates and chemical fumes.
- The three most common types are those caused by **coal dust, silica, and asbestos**.
- Tobacco smoking exacerbates the effects of all inhaled mineral dusts, particularly with asbestos exposure.

**Key Pathogenic Principle:**
- Particles between 1–5 µm in diameter are considered most dangerous as they lodge at the bifurcation of distal airways.
- Particles larger than 5–10 µm are unlikely to reach distal airways.
- Particles smaller than 0.5 µm can move in and out of alveoli without substantial deposition.
- Pulmonary alveolar macrophages are central to the pathogenic process; they phagocytose particles, activate the inflammasome, and produce IL-1, initiating an inflammatory response that leads to fibroblast proliferation and collagen deposition.

##### Coal Worker's Pneumoconiosis (CWP)

**Spectrum of Disease:**
1.  **Anthracosis**: The most innocuous form, characterized by carbon pigment engulfed by macrophages and accumulated in connective tissue along pulmonary and pleural lymphatics and draining lymph nodes. It is seen in coal miners, urban dwellers, and tobacco smokers and causes no cellular reaction.
2.  **Simple CWP**: Manifests as coal macules (dust-laden macrophages with a small amount of collagen in a delicate network) and larger coal nodules. These are most heavily involved in the upper lobes and upper zones of the lower lobes. Centrilobular emphysema may also occur.
3.  **Complicated CWP (PMF — Progressive Massive Fibrosis)**: Develops over many years from the coalescence of coal nodules. It presents as multiple dark black scars, greater than 2 cm (sometimes up to 10 cm), composed of dense collagen and pigment. Less than 10% of simple CWP cases progress to PMF.

**Key Points:**
- CWP is usually benign with minimal lung function decrement.
- PMF can lead to increasing pulmonary dysfunction, pulmonary hypertension, and cor pulmonale.
- There is **no increased frequency of lung carcinoma** in coal miners, distinguishing CWP from silicosis and asbestosis.
- PMF tends to progress even without further exposure.

##### Silicosis

- **The most prevalent chronic occupational disease globally**.
- Caused by the inhalation of crystalline silica, with sandblasting and hard-rock mining posing the highest risk.
- The most toxic forms are crystalline silica (quartz, cristobalite, tridymite), with **quartz being the most commonly implicated**.
- Silica particles activate the inflammasome, leading macrophages to release IL-1, TNF, fibronectin, oxygen free radicals, and fibrogenic cytokines.
- When mixed with other minerals (e.g., hematite), the fibrogenic effect of quartz is reduced.

**Morphology:**
- Early lesions appear as tiny, barely palpable, discrete pale-to-black nodules in the upper zones.
- The **silicotic nodule** is distinctive, characterized by concentrically arranged hyalinized collagen fibers surrounding an amorphous center, giving a **"whorled" appearance of collagen**.
- Polarized microscopy can reveal weakly birefringent silica particles in the center of the nodules.
- As the disease progresses, nodules coalesce to form hard collagenous scars and PMF. A honeycomb pattern may develop.
- Fibrotic lesions are also found in hilar lymph nodes and the pleura.

**Clinical Features:**
- Silicosis is often detected incidentally on routine chest radiographs in asymptomatic workers, showing fine nodularity in the upper zones.
- Shortness of breath typically appears late in the course, only after PMF is present.
- PMF can lead to pulmonary hypertension and cor pulmonale.
- **Increased susceptibility to tuberculosis**: Silicosis impairs cell-mediated immunity and inhibits the macrophage's ability to kill phagocytosed mycobacteria. Nodules of silicotuberculosis often have central caseation.
- The relationship between silica and lung cancer is **unsettled**, but most studies suggest some increased risk.

##### Asbestosis and Asbestos-Related Diseases

- Asbestos refers to a family of crystalline hydrated silicates with a fibrous geometry.
- **Six disease processes are linked to asbestos exposure**:
    1.  Parenchymal interstitial fibrosis (asbestosis).
    2.  Localized fibrous pleural plaques or diffuse pleural fibrosis.
    3.  Pleural effusions.
    4.  Lung carcinoma.
    5.  Malignant pleural and peritoneal mesothelioma.
    6.  Laryngeal carcinoma.
- The risk extends to **family members** of asbestos workers and individuals exposed outside the workplace.

**Pathogenesis:**
- Asbestos fibers are phagocytosed by macrophages, which activate the inflammasome, damaging phagolysosomal membranes and releasing proinflammatory and fibrogenic mediators.
- Asbestos acts as both a **tumor initiator and promoter**.
- Reactive free radicals are preferentially generated in the distal lung near the mesothelial layer, exhibiting oncogenic effects on the mesothelium.
- **Tobacco smoking combined with asbestos exposure** shows a remarkable synergy for lung carcinoma, as carcinogens in smoke are adsorbed onto asbestos fibers.

**Morphology:**
- **Asbestos bodies**: Golden-brown, fusiform or beaded rods with a translucent center. These are asbestos fibers coated with iron-containing proteinaceous material derived from macrophages, serving as a hallmark of exposure.
- Asbestosis primarily affects the **lower lobes and subpleurally**, spreading to the middle and upper lobes (contrasting with the upper lobe predilection of CWP and silicosis).
- Honeycombing can be seen in advanced cases. Visceral pleural fibrosis can lead to adhesions between the lungs and the chest wall.
- **Pleural plaques**: The most common manifestation of asbestos exposure. These are well-circumscribed plaques of dense collagen, often containing calcium, found on the anterior and posterolateral parietal pleura and over the diaphragm. They are usually asymptomatic.

**Clinical Features:**
- Progressively worsening dyspnea and cough with sputum production typically appear 10–20 years after exposure.
- The disease may remain static or progress to congestive heart failure, cor pulmonale, and death.
- **Lung carcinoma risk**: Increased 5-fold for asbestos workers.
- **Mesothelioma risk**: Increased over 1000-fold compared to the general population (which has 2–17 cases per million).
- Concomitant cigarette smoking significantly increases the risk of lung carcinoma but **not** mesothelioma.

#### Sarcoidosis

- A multisystem disease of **unknown etiology** characterized by **noncaseating granulomatous inflammation** in many tissues and organs.
- It is a **diagnosis of exclusion**, requiring the exclusion of other causes of noncaseating granulomas (e.g., mycobacterial/fungal infections, berylliosis).
- **Bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy or lung involvement** is a major finding at presentation in most cases.

**Epidemiology:**
- Predominantly affects adults younger than 40 years.
- High incidence in Danish and Swedish populations. In the USA, it is found in African Americans at a 10-fold higher rate than in whites.
- **Higher prevalence among non-smokers**, a unique characteristic among pulmonary diseases.

**Pathogenesis — Immunologic Abnormalities:**
- The disease is driven by a cell-mediated immune response to an unidentified antigen, primarily mediated by **CD4+ TH1 cells**.
- There is intraalveolar and interstitial accumulation of CD4+ TH1 cells, leading to peripheral T cell cytopenia.
- Increased levels of TH1 cytokines, such as **IL-2 and IFN-γ**, promote T cell proliferation and macrophage activation.
- Elevated levels of IL-8, TNF, and macrophage inflammatory protein-1α recruit T cells and monocytes, contributing to granuloma formation.
- Patients often exhibit **anergy** to common skin test antigens (e.g., Candida, PPD).
- Polyclonal hypergammaglobulinaemia is also common.
- Sarcoidosis can recur in new lungs following lung transplantation in at least one-third of patients.

**Morphology:**
- The cardinal feature is the **nonnecrotizing epithelioid granuloma**, a discrete collection of epithelioid cells rimmed by CD4+ T cells and interspersed multinucleate giant cells.
- Over time, fibroblasts proliferate, leading to collagen deposition and **hyalinized scar** formation.
- Two microscopic features may be seen (though not required for diagnosis):
    - **Schaumann bodies**: Laminated concretions of calcium and proteins.
    - **Asteroid bodies**: Stellate inclusions within giant cells.
- Foci of central necrosis are rarely seen in nodular forms.
- **Lungs**: Involved in **90%** of patients. Granulomas are found in the interstitium, often localized around bronchioles and pulmonary venules ("lymphangitic" distribution). Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid contains abundant CD4+ T cells. 5–15% of patients develop honeycomb lung.
- **Hilar and paratracheal lymph nodes**: Enlarged in 75–90% of cases. They are typically painless, firm, rubbery, and **non-matted** (not adherent), distinguishing them from tuberculous lymph nodes.
- **Skin (~25%)**: **Erythema nodosum** is a hallmark of acute sarcoidosis, presenting as raised, red, tender nodules on the anterior legs. Discrete painless subcutaneous nodules also occur.
- **Eye and lacrimal glands (20–50%)**: Iritis or iridocyclitis can lead to corneal opacities, glaucoma, and vision loss. Posterior uveal tract involvement can cause choroiditis and retinitis. Lacrimal gland involvement may lead to sicca syndrome (dry eyes).
- **Parotid glands (<10%)**: May present with painful enlargement and xerostomia (dry mouth).
- **Mikulicz syndrome**: Combined uveoparotid involvement.
- **Spleen**: Granulomas are present in ~75% of cases; clinical splenomegaly occurs in ~10%.
- **Liver**: Granulomas are typically found in portal triads in ~75% of cases; hepatomegaly occurs in ~33%.
- **Hypercalcaemia and hypercalciuria**: Increased calcium absorption is secondary to active vitamin D production by granuloma macrophages, **not** bone destruction.

**Clinical Features:**
- Many patients are asymptomatic and diagnosed incidentally on chest radiographs (bilateral hilar adenopathy) or at autopsy.
- Symptomatic cases (~2/3) typically present with gradual respiratory symptoms (dyspnea, dry cough, substernal discomfort) or constitutional symptoms (fever, fatigue, weight loss, night sweats).
- There is **no definitive diagnostic test**. Diagnosis requires compatible clinical and radiologic findings, exclusion of other disorders, identification of noncaseating granulomas in tissue, and exclusion of tuberculosis.
- The course is unpredictable, with periods of activity and remission.
- **65–70%** of patients recover with minimal or no residual manifestations.
- **20%** develop permanent lung dysfunction or visual impairment.
- **10–15%** succumb to progressive pulmonary fibrosis and cor pulmonale.

#### Hypersensitivity Pneumonitis

- An immunologically mediated inflammatory lung disease primarily affecting the alveoli, also known as **allergic alveolitis**.
- Usually occupational, triggered by heightened sensitivity to inhaled antigens such as moldy hay, contaminated water, bird feathers, or isocyanates.
- Unlike asthma (which affects the bronchi), damage occurs at the **alveolar level**, manifesting as **restrictive lung disease** with decreased diffusion capacity, compliance, and total lung volume.

**Evidence of Immunologic Mediation:**
- Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) reveals increased CD4+ and CD8+ T lymphocytes.
- Most patients have specific antibodies against the offending antigen in their serum.
- Complement and immunoglobulins can be demonstrated in vessel walls by immunofluorescence.
- Noncaseating granulomas are found in the lungs of two-thirds of patients.

**Morphology:**
- Patchy mononuclear cell infiltrates in the pulmonary interstitium, with **peribronchiolar accentuation**.
- Lymphocytes predominate, accompanied by plasma cells and epithelioid macrophages. Neutrophils may be present in the acute form.
- **Loose, poorly formed granulomas without necrosis** are seen in over two-thirds of cases, typically located peribronchiolarly.
- In advanced chronic cases, bilateral upper-lobe predominant interstitial fibrosis (UIP pattern) develops.

**Clinical Features:**
- **Acute form**: Fever, cough, dyspnea, and constitutional symptoms arise **4–8 hours after exposure** to the antigen. The diagnosis is usually obvious due to the temporal relationship with antigen exposure.
- **Chronic form**: Insidious onset of cough, dyspnea, malaise, and weight loss.
- Termination of antigen exposure after acute attacks leads to complete resolution within days.
- Failure to remove the inciting agent can lead to **irreversible chronic interstitial pulmonary disease**.

#### Pulmonary Eosinophilia

- Disorders characterized by pulmonary infiltrates rich in eosinophils, generally of immunologic origin.

**Categories:**
- **Acute eosinophilic pneumonia**: Rapid onset of fever, dyspnea, hypoxia, and diffuse infiltrates. BAL shows >25% eosinophils. Prompt response to corticosteroids is typical.
- **Simple pulmonary eosinophilia (Loeffler syndrome)**: Transient pulmonary lesions, blood eosinophilia, and a benign clinical course. Alveolar septa are thickened by eosinophils and occasional giant cells.
- **Tropical eosinophilia**: Caused by microfilariae and helminthic parasites.
- **Secondary eosinophilia**: Associated with asthma, drug allergies, and certain vasculitides.
- **Idiopathic chronic eosinophilic pneumonia**: Characterized by aggregates of lymphocytes and eosinophils in septal walls and alveolar spaces, typically at the lung periphery. Presents with high fever, night sweats, and dyspnea. It is a diagnosis of exclusion.

#### Smoking-Related Interstitial Diseases

**Desquamative Interstitial Pneumonia (DIP)**
- The most striking feature is the accumulation of numerous macrophages containing **dusty-brown pigment (smoker's macrophages)** within the air spaces.
- Alveolar septa are thickened by a sparse inflammatory infiltrate (usually lymphocytes), with mild interstitial fibrosis.
- Pulmonary function tests typically show a mild restrictive abnormality.
- The prognosis is **good**, with excellent response to steroids and smoking cessation. However, some patients may progress despite therapy.

**Respiratory Bronchiolitis**
- A common lesion found in smokers.
- Characterized by pigmented intraluminal macrophages in a bronchiocentric distribution within the first and second-order respiratory bronchioles.
- Mild peribronchiolar fibrosis may be present.
- Symptoms include a gradual onset of dyspnea and dry cough, which typically **recede with smoking cessation**.

### 7. Pulmonary Vascular Diseases

**Conditions covered:** Pulmonary Embolism · Pulmonary Hypertension · Diffuse Alveolar Haemorrhage Syndromes

#### Pulmonary Embolism, Haemorrhage, and Infarction

- Pulmonary embolism (PE) causes approximately 50,000 deaths per year in the USA.
- **Over 95% of all pulmonary emboli arise from thrombi in the large deep veins of the legs**, particularly those propagated to involve the popliteal vein and larger veins above it.

**Risk Factors:**
- Prolonged bed rest (especially with leg immobilization).
- Surgery, particularly orthopedic surgery on the knee or hip.
- Severe trauma (burns, multiple fractures).
- Congestive heart failure.
- The period around parturition or use of oral contraceptive pills (high estrogen content).
- Disseminated cancer.
- Primary disorders of hypercoagulability (e.g., Factor V Leiden mutation).

**Pathophysiologic Consequences (depend on embolus size and cardiopulmonary status):**
- Large vessel occlusion leads to an abrupt increase in pulmonary artery pressure, diminished cardiac output, and right-sided heart failure (acute cor pulmonale), sometimes resulting in sudden death.
- Two important consequences of arterial occlusion are:
    1. An increase in pulmonary artery pressure due to blockage and vasospasm (mediated by neurogenic factors, thromboxane A2, and serotonin).
    2. Ischemia of the downstream pulmonary parenchyma.
- **Infarction is the exception**, occurring in only about 10% of patients. It is more likely when cardiac function or bronchial circulation is compromised, or when the affected region is underventilated.

**Mechanisms of Hypoxemia:**
- Perfusion of atelectatic lung zones (due to surfactant reduction and pain leading to reduced chest wall movement).
- Decreased cardiac output, widening the arteriovenous oxygen saturation difference.
- Right-to-left shunting through a patent foramen ovale (present in 30% of normal individuals).

**Morphology:**
- **Saddle embolus**: A large embolus lodged at the bifurcation of the main pulmonary artery, potentially causing sudden death from hypoxia or acute cor pulmonale.
- **Pulmonary infarcts**:
    - Occur in approximately 75% of cases, most commonly in the lower lobes; over 50% are multiple.
    - They are typically **wedge-shaped**, with the base at the pleural surface and the apex pointing toward the hilum.
    - They are initially hemorrhagic, appearing as raised, red-blue areas of coagulative necrosis.
    - Adjacent pleura is covered by fibrinous exudate.
    - Over 48 hours, red blood cells lyse, and the infarct becomes red-brown due to hemosiderin.
    - Eventually, a gray-white peripheral zone of fibrosis develops, forming a scar.

**Clinical Consequences:**
- **60–80%** are clinically silent; small emboli are often cleared by fibrinolysis, and the bronchial circulation sustains the lung.
- **5%** result in sudden death, acute right-sided heart failure, or cardiovascular collapse (shock), occurring when over 60% of the pulmonary vasculature is obstructed.
- **10–15%** cause pulmonary infarction, requiring some degree of circulatory insufficiency.
- **<3%** lead to recurrent showers of emboli, causing pulmonary hypertension, chronic right-sided heart failure, and pulmonary vascular sclerosis.

**Management:**
- **Prophylaxis**: Anticoagulation, early ambulation, elastic stockings, intermittent pneumatic calf compression, and isometric leg exercises.
- **Treatment**: Anticoagulation therapy. Thrombolytic therapy is indicated for massive embolism with hemodynamic instability (shock, acute right heart failure).
- Patients who have experienced one PE have a **30% chance of developing a second** event.

#### Pulmonary Hypertension

- Defined as pulmonary pressures of **≥25 mmHg at rest**. Normally, pulmonary blood pressures are one-eighth of systemic pressures.

**WHO Classification (5 Groups):**
1.  **Pulmonary arterial hypertension**: Includes heritable forms, connective tissue diseases, HIV infection, and congenital heart disease with left-to-right shunts.
2.  **Left-sided heart disease**: Including systolic/diastolic dysfunction and valvular disease.
3.  **Lung diseases and/or hypoxia**: Such as COPD, interstitial lung disease, and obstructive sleep apnea.
4.  **Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension**.
5.  **Unclear or multifactorial mechanisms**.

**Common Causes:**
- Chronic obstructive or interstitial lung diseases can obliterate alveolar capillaries, increasing pulmonary resistance.
- Mitral stenosis leads to increased left atrial pressure, which is transmitted to the pulmonary arterial side.
- Recurrent thromboemboli reduce the functional cross-sectional area of the pulmonary vascular bed.
- Autoimmune diseases (e.g., systemic sclerosis) can affect the pulmonary vasculature or interstitium.
- Obstructive sleep apnea, associated with obesity and hypoxemia, is a significant contributor.

**Idiopathic Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension (IPAH):**
- Up to 80% of cases have a genetic basis, sometimes inherited as autosomal dominant with incomplete penetrance.
- Inactivating germline mutations in the **BMPR2** (bone morphogenetic protein receptor 2) gene are found in 75% of familial cases and 25% of sporadic cases.
- BMPR2 defects lead to endothelial dysfunction and proliferation of vascular smooth muscle cells in the pulmonary vasculature.
- Only 10–20% of BMPR2 mutation carriers develop overt disease, suggesting that modifier genes and/or environmental triggers also contribute.
- It is most common in **women aged 20–40 years**.

**Morphology:**
- In all forms, **medial hypertrophy of pulmonary muscular and elastic arteries**, pulmonary arterial atherosclerosis, and right ventricular hypertrophy are present.
- Organizing or recanalized thrombi suggest recurrent pulmonary emboli.
- Coexisting diffuse pulmonary fibrosis or severe emphysema/chronic bronchitis points to chronic hypoxia.
- Arterioles and small arteries show medial hypertrophy and intimal fibrosis, leading to pinpoint lumens.
- **Plexiform lesion**: A tuft of capillary formations that creates a network spanning the lumens of dilated, thin-walled small arteries. This is a characteristic finding in severe/advanced disease.

**Clinical Features:**
- Symptoms typically appear when the disease is advanced: dyspnea and fatigue. Some patients experience anginal chest pain.
- Over time, patients develop respiratory distress and cyanosis, along with right ventricular hypertrophy.
- Death usually occurs from decompensated cor pulmonale (often with superimposed thromboembolism and pneumonia) within **2–5 years in 80% of patients**.
- Treatment includes vasodilators for group 1 PAH and refractory cases, and lung transplantation is the definitive treatment.

#### Diffuse Alveolar Haemorrhage Syndromes

**Three main syndromes:**

##### Goodpasture Syndrome
- An uncommon **autoimmune disease** characterized by circulating autoantibodies against specific domains of **type IV collagen** found in the basement membranes of renal glomeruli and pulmonary alveoli.
- These antibodies trigger destruction and inflammation, leading to **necrotizing hemorrhagic interstitial pneumonitis and rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis**.
- Most cases occur in teens or twenties, with a **male preponderance**. A majority of affected individuals are active smokers.
- **Morphology**: Lungs are heavy with diffuse alveolar hemorrhage. Focal necrosis of alveolar walls, intraalveolar hemorrhage, fibrous thickening of septa, and hypertrophic type II pneumocytes are seen. Abundant hemosiderin is present. A **linear pattern of IgG deposition** along alveolar septa is the hallmark diagnostic finding, also observed in renal biopsies.
- **Treatment**: **Plasmapheresis** (to remove antibodies) combined with immunosuppressive drugs (to inhibit antibody production). Severe renal disease may necessitate renal transplantation.

##### Granulomatosis with Polyangiitis (GPA — formerly Wegener Granulomatosis)
- Over 80% of patients develop upper respiratory tract or pulmonary manifestations.
- Lung lesions include **necrotizing vasculitis ("angiitis") and parenchymal necrotizing granulomatous inflammation**.
- Symptoms can include chronic sinusitis, epistaxis, nasal perforation, cough, hemoptysis, and chest pain.
- **PR3-ANCAs are present in ~95% of cases**.

### 8. Pulmonary Infections

**Conditions covered:** Community-Acquired Bacterial and Viral Pneumonias · Hospital-Acquired Pneumonias · Aspiration Pneumonia · Lung Abscess · Tuberculosis · Nontuberculous Mycobacteria · Fungal Infections · Pneumonia in Immunocompromised Host · HIV Pulmonary Disease

#### Community-Acquired Bacterial Pneumonias

**Key Defense Mechanisms:**
- Mucociliary elevator, alveolar macrophages, complement system, IgA (upper airways), IgG/IgM (lower airways), and immune T cells.
- Cigarette smoke impairs mucociliary clearance and macrophage activity.
- Alcohol suppresses neutrophil function and blunts cough and epiglottic reflexes, increasing aspiration risk.

**Patterns of Anatomic Distribution:**
- **Bronchopneumonia**: Characterized by patchy consolidation, often multilobar and bilateral, typically affecting the bases. It involves neutrophil-rich exudate filling bronchi, bronchioles, and adjacent alveolar spaces.
- **Lobar pneumonia**: Involves consolidation of a large portion or an entire lobe. It classically progresses through four stages:
    1.  **Congestion**: Lung is heavy, boggy, and red; vascular engorgement; intraalveolar fluid with few neutrophils; numerous bacteria present.
    2.  **Red hepatization**: Massive confluent exudation; neutrophils, red blood cells, and fibrin fill alveolar spaces; lung becomes red, firm, and airless, resembling liver.
    3.  **Grey hepatization**: Progressive disintegration of red blood cells; fibrinosuppurative exudate persists; color changes to grayish-brown.
    4.  **Resolution**: Exudate is broken down by enzymatic digestion and then resorbed, ingested by macrophages, expectorated, or organized by fibroblasts.

**Complications of Pneumonia:**
- Tissue destruction and necrosis can lead to abscess formation.
- Spread to the pleural cavity results in **empyema** (fibrinosuppurative reaction).
- Bacteraemic dissemination can cause metastatic abscesses, endocarditis, meningitis, and suppurative arthritis.

**Key Organisms:**

| :----------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **_S. pneumoniae_**      | Most common community-acquired bacterial pneumonia; often presents as lobar pneumonia; increased risk in CHF, COPD, diabetes, asplenia, immunoglobulin deficiency; gram-positive lancet-shaped diplococci in sputum. |
| **_H. influenzae_**      | Community-acquired; particularly in children (type b, now vaccine-preventable); most common bacterial cause of acute exacerbations of COPD.                                                                       |

#### Community-Acquired Viral Pneumonias

- Most common causes include influenza A and B, RSV, human metapneumovirus, adenovirus, rhinovirus, rubeola, and varicella.
- **Morphology**: The inflammatory reaction is largely confined to the alveolar walls (interstitial). Septa are widened and edematous, with a mononuclear infiltrate (lymphocytes, macrophages, occasional plasma cells). Alveolar spaces are generally free of cellular exudate in classic cases. Severe cases can progress to diffuse alveolar damage with hyaline membranes.
- It is not possible to distinguish bacterial from viral pneumonia based solely on radiologic appearance.
- Damage to the respiratory epithelium can inhibit mucociliary clearance, **predisposing to secondary bacterial infections**.

**Influenza Infections:**
- Influenza is a single-stranded RNA virus. The nucleoprotein determines the type (A, B, or C).
- The surface glycoproteins, **hemagglutinin (HA)** and **neuraminidase (NA)**, determine the subtype (e.g., H1N1, H3N2). Host antibodies to HA prevent infection, while antibodies to NA ameliorate infection.
- **Type A** influenza infects humans, pigs, horses, and birds and is the primary cause of pandemic and epidemic infections.
- **Antigenic drift**: Minor mutations in HA/NA genes allow the virus to escape host antibodies, leading to epidemics.
- **Antigenic shift**: Major genetic reassessments where both HA and NA genes are replaced through reassortment with animal virus RNA segments. This renders the human population susceptible to the new virus, leading to **pandemics**.
- The 1918 pandemic was caused by a virus with swine influenza sequences and resulted in 20–40 million deaths worldwide.
- The avian H5N1 strain is a concern for a future pandemic if it recombines with a highly infectious human influenza strain.

#### Hospital-Acquired (Nosocomial) Pneumonias

- Defined as pulmonary infections acquired during a hospital stay.
- Most common in severely ill patients, the immunosuppressed, and those on prolonged antibiotics or mechanical ventilation.
- **Ventilator-associated pneumonia** is a high-risk designation.
- The most common isolates are **gram-negative rods** (e.g., Enterobacteriaceae, *Pseudomonas* spp.) and **_S. aureus_** (often methicillin-resistant, MRSA).
- **_S. pneumoniae_ is NOT a common nosocomial pathogen**, unlike in community-acquired pneumonia.

#### Aspiration Pneumonia

- Occurs in debilitated patients or those who aspirate gastric contents (e.g., post-stroke, unconscious, or experiencing repeated vomiting).
- Abnormal gag and swallowing reflexes facilitate aspiration.
- The pneumonia has both a **chemical component** (due to gastric acid irritation) and a **bacterial component**.
- Typically, more than one organism is recovered, with aerobes being more common than anaerobes.
- It is often necrotizing, with a fulminant clinical course and is a frequent cause of death in predisposed individuals.
- **Abscess formation** is a common complication in survivors.
- Microaspiration, especially in patients with gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), may exacerbate other lung diseases but does not typically cause pneumonia itself.

#### Lung Abscess

- A localized area of suppurative necrosis within the pulmonary parenchyma, forming one or more cavities.

**Causes:**
- Aspiration of infective material from carious teeth, infected sinuses, or tonsils (during oral surgery, anesthesia, coma, or in cases of alcoholic intoxication).
- Aspiration of gastric contents along with oropharyngeal organisms.
- Complication of necrotizing bacterial pneumonias caused by organisms like _S. aureus_, _S. pyogenes_, _K. pneumoniae_, _Pseudomonas_ spp., and rarely type 3 pneumococci. Mycotic infections and bronchiectasis can also lead to abscesses.
- **Bronchial obstruction**, particularly by bronchogenic carcinoma, can lead to abscess formation within excavated necrotic tumor.
- Septic embolism from right-sided infective endocarditis.
- Hematogenous spread in disseminated pyogenic infections (e.g., staphylococcal bacteremia), leading to multiple lung abscesses.
- **Anaerobic bacteria are present in almost all lung abscesses**, being the exclusive isolates in one-third to two-thirds of cases. Common species include *Prevotella*, *Fusobacterium*, *Bacteroides*, *Peptostreptococcus*, and microaerophilic streptococci.

**Morphology:**
- Abscesses can range from a few millimeters to large cavities up to 5–6 cm in diameter.
- **Aspiration abscesses** are more common on the **right side** due to the more vertical airways, usually occurring as a single lesion in the posterior segment of the upper lobe or apical segments of the lower lobe.
- Abscesses related to pneumonia or bronchiectasis are often multiple, basal, and diffusely scattered.
- Septic emboli result in multiple abscesses in any region.
- Abscesses almost invariably rupture into the airways, allowing for partial drainage and often creating an **air-fluid level visible on radiographs**.
- Rupture into the pleural cavity can cause a **bronchopleural fistula**, leading to pneumothorax or empyema.
- Embolization of septic material can spread to the brain, causing meningitis or a brain abscess.

**Clinical Features:**
- Prominent cough producing copious foul-smelling purulent or sanguineous sputum, sometimes with hemoptysis.
- Spiking fever, malaise, clubbing of fingers, weight loss, and anemia are common.
- **Lung abscesses are found in 10–15% of patients with bronchogenic carcinoma**, so an underlying carcinoma must be considered in older adults with lung abscess.
- Secondary amyloidosis may develop in chronic cases.
- Treatment involves antibiotics and surgical drainage if necessary; mortality is approximately 10%.

#### Tuberculosis

- A communicable chronic granulomatous disease caused by **_Mycobacterium tuberculosis_**.
- The World Health Organization (WHO) considers TB the **most common cause of death from a single infectious agent**, with approximately 1.7 billion infected globally, 8–10 million new cases, and 1.5 million deaths annually.
- The reservoir is individuals with active pulmonary disease.
- Transmission occurs via inhalation of airborne organisms in aerosols produced during expectoration or from contaminated secretions.
- _M. tuberculosis_ are slender rods that are **acid-fast** (resistant to decolorization with acid-alcohol after staining with carbolfuchsin using the Ziehl-Neelsen or fluorescent auramine-rhodamine stains).

**Risk Factors:**
- Poverty, crowding, chronic debilitating illnesses.
- Diabetes mellitus, Hodgkin lymphoma, chronic lung disease (especially silicosis), chronic renal failure, malnutrition, alcoholism, and immunosuppression.
- **HIV infection is the dominant risk factor** in areas where HIV is prevalent.

**Key Distinction: Infection vs. Disease**
- **Infection**: Seeding of a focus with organisms, which may or may not lead to disease.
- In most individuals, infection results in an asymptomatic state with a tiny fibrocalcific nodule. Viable organisms can remain dormant for decades.
- If immune defenses are lowered, the infection may reactivate, leading to communicable disease.

**Tuberculin (Mantoux) Test:**
- An intracutaneous injection of 0.1 mL of purified protein derivative (PPD). A visible, palpable induration of ≥5 mm at 48–72 hours indicates a positive test.
- A positive test signifies cell-mediated hypersensitivity to tubercular antigens but **does not differentiate between latent infection and active disease**.
- **False negatives** can occur due to viral infections, sarcoidosis, malnutrition, Hodgkin lymphoma, immunosuppression, or overwhelming active TB.
- **False positives** can occur from infection by atypical mycobacteria.

**Pathogenesis — Primary TB:**
- **Entry into macrophages**: _M. tuberculosis_ enters macrophages via mannose receptor and complement receptors.
- **Replication in macrophages**: Mycobacteria prevent the fusion of lysosomes with phagocytic vacuoles, allowing them to proliferate within macrophages. This can lead to bacteremia within the first three weeks, seeding multiple sites, usually asymptomatically.
- **Development of Cell-Mediated Immunity (~3 weeks after exposure)**: Mycobacterial antigens are presented in draining lymph nodes to CD4+ T cells by dendritic cells or macrophages. This, along with IL-12 from macrophages, stimulates the generation of **CD4+ TH1 cells** that secrete **IFN-γ**.
- **TH1-Mediated Macrophage Activation**: IFN-γ activates macrophages, leading to the release of:
    - **TNF**: Recruits monocytes, which differentiate into epithelioid histiocytes, initiating the granulomatous response.
    - **iNOS**: Increases nitric oxide (NO) levels, which generates reactive nitrogen intermediates that can kill mycobacteria.
    - Antimicrobial peptides (defensins).
- **Granuloma Formation and Tissue Damage**: The TH1 response orchestrates granuloma formation. Caseous necrosis occurs in individuals with immune deficits.
- **Key Principle**: Immunity and hypersensitivity develop in parallel. Tuberculin negativity in an _M. tuberculosis_-infected patient is an ominous sign of waning resistance. Defects in any step of the TH1 pathway (IL-12, IFN-γ, TNF, NO production) result in poorly formed granulomas and disease progression. TNF antagonist therapy (used for rheumatoid arthritis) significantly **increases the risk of TB reactivation**.

**Primary Tuberculosis — Morphology:**
- Almost always begins in the lungs, with inhaled bacilli implanting in the distal air spaces of the lower upper lobe or upper lower lobe, near the pleura.
- **Ghon focus**: A 1–1.5 cm area of gray-white inflammatory consolidation, often with central caseous necrosis.
- **Ghon complex**: A Ghon focus along with regional caseating lymph nodes and lymphatic involvement.
- In approximately 95% of individuals, cell-mediated immunity controls the infection, leading to progressive fibrosis and calcification of the Ghon complex, forming the **Ranke complex** (detectable on radiographs).
- **Progressive primary tuberculosis**: Occurs in immunocompromised or malnourished individuals, especially in HIV patients with CD4+ T cells below 200 cells/µL. These individuals cannot mount a typical CD4+ T cell-mediated response, resulting in the absence of characteristic caseating granulomas (**nonreactive tuberculosis**).

**Secondary (Reactivation) Tuberculosis:**
- Arises in a previously sensitized host, most commonly from reactivation of dormant primary lesions decades later, or less commonly, from reinfection.
- Only less than 5% of patients with primary disease develop secondary TB.
- **Classically localized to the apex of one or both upper lobes**, possibly related to the high oxygen tension in these areas.
- Preexisting hypersensitivity leads to a prompt, marked tissue response that tends to wall off the focus.
- Regional lymph nodes are less prominently involved than in primary TB.
- **Cavitation occurs readily**, with erosion into a bronchus. This facilitates dissemination along airways, making it a significant source of infectivity (sputum contains bacilli).
- **Morphology**: An initial lesion less than 2 cm, located within 1–2 cm of the apical pleura. It is sharply circumscribed, firm, gray-white to yellow, with variable caseation and peripheral fibrosis. Characteristic coalescent tubercles with central caseation are seen.
- Progressive pulmonary TB: An apical lesion enlarges, caseation expands, and erosion into a bronchus creates an irregular cavity lined by caseous material. Hemoptysis can occur from erosion into blood vessels.
- **Miliary pulmonary disease**: Organisms reach the bloodstream via lymphatics, recirculate to the lungs via the pulmonary arteries, and form small (2 mm) yellow-white foci scattered throughout the parenchyma ("millet seeds").
- **Systemic miliary TB**: Hematogenous dissemination to organs like the liver, bone marrow, spleen, adrenal glands, meninges, kidneys, fallopian tubes, and epididymis.
- **Isolated-organ TB**: Can affect the meninges (tuberculosis meningitis), kidneys, adrenal glands, and bones (**Pott disease** — vertebral TB, often with paraspinal "cold" abscesses).
- **Lymphadenitis**: The most frequent form of extrapulmonary TB, usually affecting the cervical lymph nodes (**scrofula**). It is typically unifocal and non-matted.
- Pleural complications: Serous pleural effusions, tuberculous empyema, and obliterative fibrous pleuritis.

**HIV and TB:**
- **CD4+ T cell count >300 cells/µL**: Typical secondary TB (apical disease with cavitation).
- **CD4+ T cell count <200 cells/µL**: Resembles progressive primary TB (lower/middle lobe consolidation, hilar lymphadenopathy, noncavitary disease).
- Extrapulmonary involvement increases from 10–15% with mild immunosuppression to over 50% with severe immunosuppression.

**Diagnosis:**
- Acid-fast staining (Ziehl-Neelsen or fluorescent auramine-rhodamine).
- Conventional cultures can take up to 10 weeks; liquid media radiometric assays provide results in approximately 2 weeks.
- PCR amplification can be performed on liquid media or tissue sections.
- Culture remains the gold standard, as it allows for identification of PCR-negative cases and drug susceptibility testing.
- **MDR-TB (Multidrug-Resistant TB)**: Resistance to at least two primary drugs. Approximately 500,000 new cases occur annually (about 3% of new cases, 20% of previously treated cases). Eastern Europe and Russia are epicenters.
- Approximately 5–10% exhibit **Extensive Multidrug Resistance (XDR-TB)**, defined as resistance to a broad spectrum of antibiotics currently in use.

#### Nontuberculous Mycobacterial Disease

- Most commonly cause chronic localized pulmonary disease in immunocompetent individuals.
- The most frequently implicated species are **_Mycobacterium avium-intracellulare_ (MAC), _M. kansasii_, and _M. abscessus_**.
- Disease may manifest as upper-lobe cavitary disease mimicking TB, especially in patients with a long history of smoking or alcohol abuse, or concomitant COPD, cystic fibrosis, or pneumoconiosis.
- In **immunosuppressed individuals (especially HIV patients)**, MAC infection can lead to disseminated disease with symptoms like fever, night sweats, weight loss, hepatosplenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, and gastrointestinal symptoms. This typically occurs late in the course of HIV infection (CD4+ count <100 cells/µL) and is characterized by tissue examination showing **foamy macrophages filled with atypical mycobacteria**, often without granulomas.

#### Fungal Infections — Histoplasmosis, Coccidioidomycosis, Blastomycosis

**Three dimorphic fungi with distinct geographic distributions:**
- **_H. capsulatum_**: Found in the Ohio and central Mississippi River valleys and Appalachian Mountains, thriving in warm, moist soil contaminated with bat or bird droppings.
- **_C. immitis_**: Prevalent in the southwestern and far western USA, particularly California's San Joaquin Valley ("valley fever").
- **_B. dermatitidis_**: Its distribution overlaps with histoplasmosis.

**Histologic Identification of Yeast Forms:**
- **_H. capsulatum_**: Round to oval, small yeast forms (2–5 µm in diameter).
- **_C. immitis_**: Characterized by thick-walled, nonbudding spherules (20–60 µm), often filled with numerous small endospores.
- **_B. dermatitidis_**: Round to oval, larger yeast forms (5–25 µm) that reproduce by **characteristic broad-based budding**.

**Clinical Manifestations (common to all three):**
1.  **Acute (primary) pulmonary infection**: Presents as a "flu-like" syndrome. Pulmonary nodules (aggregates of macrophages containing the organisms) and regional lymph node lesions evolve into granulomas. These may undergo necrosis, leading to fibrosis and calcification, resembling primary TB. Differentiation requires identifying the yeast forms, best visualized with silver stains. This form is self-limited in most cases.
2.  **Chronic cavitary pulmonary disease**: Predominantly affects the upper lobes and resembles secondary TB. It can manifest as perihilar masses that mimic bronchogenic carcinoma. Symptoms include cough, hemoptysis, dyspnea, and chest pain.
3.  **Disseminated miliary disease**: Occurs in infants or immunocompromised individuals (especially those with HIV). Well-formed granulomas are typically absent. The disease involves focal collections of phagocytes containing yeast forms in the liver, spleen, lymph nodes, GI lymphoid tissue, bone marrow, adrenal glands, and meninges. It presents as a febrile illness with hepatosplenomegaly, anemia, leukopenia, and thrombocytopenia.
- **_B. dermatitidis_ specific**: Cutaneous infections frequently induce striking epithelial hyperplasia that can be mistaken for squamous cell carcinoma.

#### Pneumonia in the Immunocompromised Host

**Common Opportunistic Pathogens:**
- Bacteria: _P. aeruginosa_, _Mycobacterium_ spp., _L. pneumophila_, _Listeria monocytogenes_.
- Viruses: Cytomegalovirus (CMV), herpesvirus.
- Fungi: _Pneumocystis jiroveci_, _Candida_ spp., _Aspergillus_ spp., _Cryptococcus neoformans_.

##### Cytomegalovirus (CMV)
- A member of the herpesvirus family.
- **Hallmark**: Gigantism of both the cytoplasm and nucleus of infected cells, with a large intranuclear inclusion surrounded by a clear halo, often described as an **"owl's eye"** inclusion.
- **Cytomegalic inclusion disease**: The classic form in neonates, affecting multiple organs.
- In immunosuppressed adults (e.g., AIDS, allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplant recipients), CMV pneumonitis is a serious condition.
- **Transmission routes**: Transplacental (congenital), cervical/vaginal secretions or breast milk (perinatal), saliva (preschool children), venereal route (>15 years), and iatrogenic transmission via organ transplantation or blood transfusion.
- Serious disseminated CMV disease affects the lungs (pneumonitis), GI tract (colitis), and retina (retinitis). The CNS is usually spared.
- **CMV retinitis** is the most common form of opportunistic CMV disease.
- **Morphology**: Infected cells are enlarged (up to ~40 µm) with prominent intranuclear basophilic inclusions that occupy about half the nuclear diameter and are separated from the nuclear membrane by a clear halo. Smaller cytoplasmic inclusions may also be present.
- **CMV mononucleosis** (in immunocompetent individuals) is usually asymptomatic but can manifest as an infectious mononucleosis-like illness (fever, atypical lymphocytosis, lymphadenopathy, hepatomegaly, mild hepatitis).
- **Diagnosis**: Characteristic inclusions in tissue sections, viral culture, rising antiviral antibody titers, and PCR detection of CMV DNA (which has revolutionized post-transplant monitoring).

##### _Pneumocystis jiroveci_ (formerly _P. carinii_)
- Now classified as a **fungus** (previously considered a protozoan).
- Nearly all individuals are exposed in the first few years of life, with latent infection. Reactivation occurs almost exclusively in the immunocompromised.
- In **HIV patients**, the risk increases inversely with the CD4+ T cell count, with **CD4+ counts <200 cells/µL** being a strong predictive factor.
- Infection is largely confined to the lung, causing **interstitial pneumonitis**.
- **Morphology**: Characterized by the presence of intraalveolar **foamy, pink-staining exudate ("cotton candy" exudate)** on H&E staining. Septa are thickened by edema and a minimal mononuclear infiltrate. Silver stains reveal **round to cup-shaped cysts (4–10 µm)** within the alveolar exudates.
- **Clinical features**: Fever, dry cough, and dyspnea are present in 90–95% of patients. Radiographs typically show bilateral perihilar and basilar infiltrates. Hypoxia and a restrictive lung defect on pulmonary function studies are common.
- **Diagnosis**: Identification of the organism in induced sputum or bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid using immunofluorescence is the most sensitive and effective method. Relapses are common in AIDS patients unless the underlying immunodeficiency is corrected or prophylactic therapy is administered.

##### Candidiasis
- **_C. albicans_** is the most common disease-causing fungus and a normal inhabitant of the oral cavity, GI tract, and vagina.
- **Morphology**: Presents as yeast-like forms (blastoconidia), pseudohyphae, and true hyphae. **Pseudohyphae** are important diagnostic clues, appearing as budding yeast cells joined end-to-end at constrictions, mimicking true hyphae. They are visible with routine H&E but special stains (GMS, PAS) are commonly used.
- **Presentations**:
    - **Thrush** (oral): Most common presentation, characterized by gray-white dirty pseudomembranes. Seen in newborns, debilitated patients, children on inhaled corticosteroids, post-antibiotic use, and HIV patients.
    - **Vaginitis**: Common in diabetics, pregnant women, and those using oral contraceptives.
    - **Esophagitis**: Occurs in AIDS patients and those with hematologic malignancies, causing dysphagia and retrosternal pain.
    - **Skin infections**: Onychomycosis, paronychia, folliculitis, intertrigo, balanitis, and diaper rash.
    - **Chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis**: Associated with T cell defects, including **Job syndrome** (an inherited condition with a defect in Th17 T cell responses).
    - **Invasive candidiasis**: Hematogenous dissemination can lead to renal abscess, myocardial abscess, endocarditis, brain abscesses/meningitis, endophthalmitis, hepatic abscesses, and Candida pneumonia. **Candida endocarditis** is the most common fungal endocarditis, occurring in patients with prosthetic heart valves or IV drug abusers.

##### Cryptococcosis
- Caused by **_C. neoformans_**, which is almost exclusively opportunistic in immunocompromised individuals (AIDS, hematologic malignancies).
- Acquired by inhalation of aerosolized material, often contaminated soil or **bird droppings**.
- The fungus initially localizes in the lungs and can then disseminate to the meninges.
- **Morphology**: The yeast form is 5–10 µm with a **thick gelatinous capsule**, reproducing by budding. It lacks pseudohyphae or true hyphae. The capsule is not directly visible on routine H&E but creates a clear **"halo"** around the yeast cells. India ink or PAS staining can highlight the fungus. The **capsular polysaccharide antigen** is the target for a latex agglutination assay, which is positive in over 95% of cases.
- CNS involvement: Fungi can grow in gelatinous masses within the meninges or expand perivascular Virchow-Robin spaces, forming characteristic **"soap-bubble lesions"**. The tissue response ranges from florid proliferation with minimal inflammation in the immunodeficient host to a granulomatous reaction in more immunocompetent individuals.

##### Opportunistic Moulds — _Aspergillus_ and Mucormycosis

**Aspergillosis:**
- **Invasive aspergillosis**: Most commonly localizes to the lungs, manifesting as necrotizing pneumonia. Systemic dissemination, especially to the brain, is often fatal and almost exclusively occurs in the immunocompromised.
- **Allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis**: Occurs in patients with asthma, involving a type I hypersensitivity reaction to the fungus colonizing the bronchi. Patients typically have circulating IgE antibodies against _Aspergillus_ and peripheral eosinophilia.
- **Aspergilloma ("fungus ball")**: Fungal colonization of pre-existing pulmonary cavities (e.g., dilated bronchi, lung cysts, post-TB cavitary lesions). This can act like a ball valve, leading to infection and hemoptysis.
- **Morphology**: Hyphae are **septate and branch at acute angles**, a key distinguishing feature from Mucor. Both _Aspergillus_ and Mucor can cause suppurative or granulomatous reactions and have a propensity to invade blood vessel walls, leading to hemorrhage, vascular necrosis, and infarction.

**Mucormycosis:**
- Caused by fungi in the Zygomycetes order (e.g., *Rhizopus*, *Mucor*).
- Hyphae are **nonseptate and branch at right angles**, distinguishing them from _Aspergillus_.
- **Diabetic ketoacidosis** is the most common predisposing condition for fulminant invasive rhinocerebral mucormycosis.
- Rhinocerebral form: The fungus colonizes the nasal cavity and sinuses and can spread by direct extension into the brain, orbit, and head and neck structures.
- Pulmonary disease: Can manifest as localized cavitary lesions or diffuse miliary involvement.

#### Pulmonary Disease in HIV Infection

- Pulmonary disease is the **leading contributor to morbidity and mortality** in HIV-infected individuals.
- Anti-retroviral therapy and chemoprophylaxis have significantly reduced the incidence and improved outcomes.

**CD4+ T Cell Count as a Guide to Differential Diagnosis:**
- **CD4+ >200 cells/µL**: Bacterial and tubercular infections are common.
- **CD4+ <200 cells/µL**: Increased risk of _Pneumocystis_ pneumonia.
- **CD4+ <50 cells/µL**: Increased risk of CMV and _M. avium_ complex infections.

**Key Points:**
- Bacterial lower respiratory tract infections by "usual" pathogens (_S. pneumoniae_, _S. aureus_, _H. influenzae_, gram-negative rods) are exceedingly common in HIV patients. These infections are often more frequent, more severe, and more frequently associated with bacteremia than in non-HIV individuals.
- Not all pulmonary infiltrates in HIV patients are infectious. Conditions like Kaposi sarcoma, pulmonary non-Hodgkin lymphoma, and primary lung cancer occur with increased frequency.
- Pulmonary disease in HIV may result from **more than one cause simultaneously**.

    → FEV1/FVC ratio.

    → Persistent productive cough for at least 3 consecutive months in at least 2 consecutive years.

    → Type I IgE-mediated hypersensitivity.
