# Hepatic Amoebiasis (Amoebic Liver Abscess)

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Hepatology and Infectious Diseases**

> **In one line:** hepatic amoebiasis is the commonest extra-intestinal form of *Entamoeba histolytica* infection: trophozoites invade the colon, travel up the portal vein and destroy liver tissue to form an abscess, usually in the posterosuperior right lobe. It presents with fever and right upper quadrant pain, is diagnosed by imaging plus serology, and is cured by metronidazole followed by a luminal agent.

## What you must know

- *Entamoeba histolytica* is the only pathogenic intestinal amoeba; the **cyst** is the infective form and the **trophozoite** causes invasive disease.
- Transmission is **faeco-oral** (contaminated food and water); a single cyst can cause disease. About **90% of infections are asymptomatic**.
- Liver abscess is **7 to 10 times commoner in adult males**, usually a single abscess in the **posterosuperior right lobe**, and often appears **1 to 3 months after** an attack of dysentery (many patients have no recent diarrhoea).
- Features: fever, right upper quadrant pain, tender hepatomegaly, **leucocytosis without eosinophilia**, raised alkaline phosphatase, raised right hemidiaphragm.
- **Ultrasound or CT** shows the abscess but **cannot distinguish amoebic from pyogenic abscess**; **serology** (positive in 92 to 97%) helps; the aspirate is sterile "anchovy paste" pus.
- Treatment: **metronidazole 800 mg tds for 7 to 10 days** (cure above 90%), then a **luminal agent** (diloxanide furoate, paromomycin) to clear gut cysts. Aspiration only in selected cases.

## 1. Epidemiology

- Amoebiasis affects **40 to 50 million people a year**, mostly in developing countries with poor socioeconomic and sanitation levels (India, Africa, Central and South America).
- In developed countries: **migrants, travellers, institutionalised patients and men who have sex with men** (venereal faeco-oral transmission).
- Hepatic amoebiasis is the **commonest extra-intestinal manifestation**.
- **Sex:** equal in infected children and in amoebic colitis, yet **7 to 10 times more common in adult males**. Proposed reasons: hormonal effect (post-menopausal women have higher rates) and alcohol.

## 2. The organism and life cycle

Three intestinal species look alike:

| Species | Role |
|---|---|
| ***Entamoeba histolytica*** | The cause of **all symptomatic disease** |
| *E. dispar*, *E. moshkovskii* | Look the same on microscopy but are non-pathogenic |

Other amoebae in stool: *E. coli*, *E. hartmanni*, *E. polecki*, *Iodamoeba bütschlii*, *Endolimax nana*, *Dientamoeba fragilis*. Apart from *E. histolytica*, *E. polecki*, *D. fragilis* and *I. bütschlii* (which can cause some disease), the others are commensals.

- **Cyst:** the infective form; survives for **weeks to months** in the environment.
- **Trophozoite:** the invasive form.
- Infection follows ingestion of cysts in contaminated food or water (or faeco-oral sexual contact). Cysts survive stomach acid, excyst to **trophozoites** in the small intestine, and colonise the colon.

## 3. Pathogenesis

1. Trophozoites penetrate the mucus barrier and adhere to colonic cells through a surface **galactose/N-acetyl-galactosamine lectin**.
2. They kill epithelial and inflammatory cells by **proteinases, contact-dependent lysis, apoptosis and amoebapores** (small peptides forming pores in lipid bilayers), causing cytolysis. This produces the flask-shaped colonic ulcers of amoebic dysentery.
3. Amoebae **ascend the portal venous system** to the liver, block small portal radicles and release proteases, producing acute focal inflammation.
4. The **abscess** results from tissue destruction by contact-dependent cytolysis. It lies mostly in the **posterosuperior surface of the right lobe** (streaming of portal blood from the caecum and ascending colon goes to the right lobe).

> **Remember:** the liver abscess pus is not true pus: it contains liquefied necrotic hepatocytes, and the trophozoites sit in the **wall** of the abscess, not in the centre.

### Risk factors for invasive disease

- Extremes of age, **pregnancy**, **corticosteroids** and immunosuppressants, malignancy, malnutrition, **alcoholism** (and HIV/immune compromise).

## 4. Clinical features

### Acute presentation (most common)

- **1 to 2 weeks of fever** and **right upper quadrant pain**.
- **Jaundice is uncommon.**
- **Diarrhoea at the same time in less than one-third**; a history of dysentery in the previous months is not infrequent. The abscess can occur after exposure of only 4 days.
- Pain may be referred to the **right shoulder (acromial region)**; dry cough; occasionally pain in the lower abdomen or right iliac fossa. The patient can be emaciated.

### Chronic presentation (rare)

- Months of fever, weight loss, abdominal pain, hepatomegaly and anaemia. Important in the differential diagnosis of other liver pathologies (it mimics tumour or TB).

### Examination

- **Tender liver** (point tenderness over the intercostal spaces), diminished movement of the right side of the chest, signs of right basal collapse or effusion.
- The left lobe may undergo compensatory hypertrophy in a very large abscess.

## 5. Investigations

| Test | Findings |
|---|---|
| **Blood count** | **Leucocytosis without eosinophilia**; anaemia of chronic disease |
| **LFTs** | **Raised alkaline phosphatase**; transaminases may be raised; low albumin in chronic disease |
| **Stool microscopy** | Amoebae in only **about 18%** |
| **Stool culture** | Positive in about 75% (slide figure) |
| **Imaging (chest and abdomen)** | At least one of **ultrasound, CT, MRI** in all suspected cases. CXR: **raised, immobile right hemidiaphragm**, basal atelectasis or effusion. Imaging shows a single (right or left lobe) or multiple lesions. |
| **Serology** | Antibodies in **92 to 97%**; negative in the first 7 days; **indirect haemagglutination (IHA) is the most sensitive (83 to 100%)**; agar gel diffusion, counter-immunoelectrophoresis. Positive in 25% of people in endemic areas, so a positive result is not helpful there; **a negative result helps exclude the disease**. Remains positive 6 to 12 months only. |
| **Newer tests** | Recombinant antigen EIA, serum antigen detection, PCR |

> **Exam tip:** none of the imaging modalities can differentiate amoebic abscess from pyogenic or malignant abscess; the diagnosis can usually be presumed while awaiting other results (right-sided abscess + endemic area + positive serology + response to metronidazole).

### Aspiration

- Not conventionally required or usually recommended. Consider when:
  - no response after **3 to 5 days** of therapy,
  - the abscess is at immediate risk of rupture (large, left lobe, near the pericardium),
  - rapid exclusion of the diagnosis is needed (suspected pyogenic abscess).
- In refractory cases it enhances recovery.
- The aspirate is an **acellular proteinaceous debris, "chocolate-coloured" or "anchovy paste"** fluid of necrotic hepatocytes. Trophozoites are seen in under 20% (they lie at the periphery).
- Antigen tests and PCR on the aspirate help. Culture for bacteria: organisms are obvious on Gram stain in pyogenic abscess but not in amoebic abscess.

## 6. Complications

Mostly in malnourished or chronic alcoholic patients; patients are very ill with a high mortality.

- **Bacterial superinfection.**
- **Rupture and extension** into:
  - the subphrenic space (subphrenic abscess),
  - the peritoneal sac (peritonitis),
  - bile ducts (cholangitis, jaundice),
  - abdominal wall and flanks,
  - the pleural cavity (sympathetic effusion, empyema; lung abscess or hepatobronchial fistula with "anchovy sauce" sputum),
  - the pericardial sac and mediastinum (**left lobe abscess**; high mortality).
- Haematogenous spread (brain, lung).

## 7. Differential diagnosis of a focal liver lesion

Ask: is the lesion symptomatic or found by chance? Consider age, sex, oral contraceptive use, risk factors for chronic liver disease or cirrhosis, travel history and any known extrahepatic cancer. Tumour markers, multiple imaging methods and sometimes histology are needed. **Fine needle aspiration is controversial** (often non-diagnostic, bleeding and seeding of malignant tissue).

| Lesion | Key points |
|---|---|
| **Pyogenic abscess** | Portal infection, spread from local structures, biliary obstruction and infection, haematogenous seeding (often in the young), immunosuppression, AIDS, transplants; increasingly in older people. Fever with rigors, non-specific infection markers, imaging shows abscesses and biliary obstruction; portal vein thrombosis can occur. Exclude infection in a tumour ("malignant abscess"). |
| **Tuberculous abscess** | Rare; usually other focus of TB or immune compromise; AFB in pus or necrotic tissue |
| ***Fasciola hepatica*** | Liver necrosis and abscess; multiple echogenic areas or a complex mass; most specific test is counter-immunoelectrophoresis |
| **Hydatid and simple cysts** | Cysts are the commonest focal hepatic lesion; large cysts cause mass effect; infection mimics an abscess; imaging plus hydatid serology is specific |
| **Cavernous haemangioma** | Commonest benign liver tumour; women; large lesions compress organs or thrombose; imaging is diagnostic |
| **Hepatic adenoma** | Non-cirrhotic liver, premenopausal women, usually over 2 years of oral contraceptive use; risk of **rupture and malignant change**; avoid biopsy; diagnosis after resection |
| **Hepatocellular carcinoma** | History of chronic liver disease (viral hepatitis, alcohol, metabolic); stigmata of cirrhosis; MRI is diagnostic |
| **Cholangiocarcinoma** | Chronic cholestatic disease: primary sclerosing cholangitis, long-standing choledochal cyst, intrahepatic stones, biliary parasites; MRI and ERCP with brush cytology |
| **Liver metastasis** | Look for the primary; often diagnosed without biopsy; CT-guided biopsy helpful |

## 8. Treatment

Both **tissue (invasive-stage) agents** and **luminal agents** are used.

| Type | Drug | Details |
|---|---|---|
| **Tissue agent (first line)** | **Metronidazole** | **800 mg orally tds for 7 to 10 days**; cure above 90%; well absorbed, so IV gives no real advantage unless oral intake is impossible or absorption is poor; no resistance reported |
| Tissue agent (alternatives) | **Tinidazole**, **ornidazole** | Few days; a single 2 g dose gives about 94% success; tinidazole 2 to 3 days gave 100% in one series |
| **Luminal agent** | **Paromomycin** | 30 mg/kg/day in 3 divided doses for 10 days |
| Luminal agent | **Diloxanide furoate** | 500 mg tds for 10 days |
| Luminal agent | Diiodohydroxyquin | 650 mg tds for 20 days |

- Give a luminal agent **after** the tissue agent to eradicate asymptomatic gut colonisation, even if stools are negative.
- **Slow response or relapse:** consider aspiration and/or a prolonged course.
- **Secondary bacterial infection:** add an appropriate antibiotic.
- Other care: analgesia, nutrition, stop alcohol. Surgery is rarely needed (rupture with peritonitis).

> **Remember:** metronidazole kills trophozoites in tissue but not the cysts in the lumen. That is why a luminal agent must follow.

## 9. Prognosis and prevention

- The disease is treatable with oral medicines; **cure is the rule**. Mortality is high with rupture, delayed diagnosis and malnutrition.
- Prevention: safe water, sanitation and food hygiene, handwashing, avoiding raw salads in endemic areas.

## Key points to revise

1. *E. histolytica*: cyst (infective), trophozoite (invasive); faeco-oral; 90% asymptomatic.
2. Amoebic liver abscess is the commonest extra-intestinal form; adult males; right lobe; follows dysentery by 1 to 3 months.
3. Fever, RUQ pain, tender liver, leucocytosis without eosinophilia, raised ALP.
4. Imaging cannot separate amoebic from pyogenic; serology (IHA) is positive in most; stool microscopy is positive in only about 18%.
5. Aspirate is "anchovy paste"; aspirate if no response in 3 to 5 days or risk of rupture.
6. Metronidazole 800 mg tds for 7 to 10 days, then diloxanide or paromomycin.
7. Complications: rupture into pleura, peritoneum or pericardium; left lobe abscess is the most dangerous.
