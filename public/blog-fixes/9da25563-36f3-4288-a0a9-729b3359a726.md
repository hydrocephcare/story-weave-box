# Rhinitis and Cough: Drug Treatment

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Respiratory and ENT pharmacology**

> **In one line:** rhinitis is irritation or inflammation of the nasal mucosa (allergic or non-allergic) treated with antihistamines, mast cell stabilisers, leukotriene antagonists, nasal steroids, decongestants and anticholinergics, plus allergen avoidance. Productive cough is helped by expectorants and mucolytics, while dry irritant cough is suppressed with peripheral or central antitussives.

## What you must know

- **Rhinitis** is inflammation or irritation of the nasal mucosa. Allergic rhinitis may be **seasonal (hay fever)** or **perennial**; non-allergic types include infectious rhinitis.
- Symptoms: **rhinorrhoea, nasal blockage, sneezing, congestion, post-nasal drip, itching, catarrh**, with or without systemic features.
- **Second- and third-generation antihistamines** are non-sedating; first-generation drugs cross the blood-brain barrier and sedate.
- Antihistamines control rhinitis, conjunctivitis, urticaria; they **do not control asthma, anaphylaxis, sinusitis, otitis or atopic dermatitis**.
- **Topical nasal corticosteroids** are the most effective single group for moderate to severe persistent rhinitis.
- **Topical decongestants** cause **rebound congestion** after 10 days to 2 weeks.
- **Ipratropium** nasal drops control watery rhinorrhoea (vasomotor rhinitis).
- **Productive cough**: expectorants (guaifenesin) and mucolytics (N-acetylcysteine, bromhexine, ambroxol, rhDNase). **Dry cough**: benzonatate (peripheral), codeine or dextromethorphan (central).

## 1. Rhinitis: definition and classification

- **Definition:** irritation and/or inflammation of the mucous membranes inside the nose.

| Group | Types |
|---|---|
| **Inflammatory** | **Allergic**: seasonal (hay fever) and perennial. **Infectious** (viral, bacterial) |
| **Non-inflammatory** | Non-allergic, e.g. vasomotor rhinitis (watery hypersecretion) |

- **Presentation:** runny nose, blocked nose, sneezing, nasal congestion, post-nasal drip, itching, and catarrh (involvement of other mucous membranes such as the eyes and throat), plus systemic features in some.

## 2. Treatment strategy

| Approach | Details |
|---|---|
| **Prevention (non-drug)** | **Environmental control** (avoid allergen, dust mite measures); **allergen immunotherapy** |
| **Drugs** | H1 antihistamines; anti-allergics (mast cell stabiliser **cromolyn**, leukotriene receptor antagonist **montelukast**); corticosteroids; decongestants (adrenergic agonists); anticholinergics; antibiotics (infective); mucolytics |

## 3. Antihistamines (H1 receptor blockers)

### Classification

| Generation | Chemical groups and examples |
|---|---|
| **First** | Alkylamines: chlorpheniramine. Ethanolamines: dimenhydrinate, diphenhydramine. Ethylenediamines: antazoline. Phenothiazines: promethazine. Piperazines: cyclizine. Piperidines: azatadine. Miscellaneous: cyproheptadine |
| **Second** | Cetirizine, loratadine, ketotifen |
| **Third** | Levocetirizine, fexofenadine, desloratadine |

### Comparison

| Feature | First generation | Second generation | Third generation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Receptor action | Non-selective | Selective | More selective |
| Lipophilicity | Lipophilic | Non-lipophilic | Non-lipophilic |
| Blood-brain barrier | Crosses | Poorly crosses | Does not cross |
| Sedation | **Sedating** | Non-sedating | Non-sedating |
| Efficacy | Higher antihistaminic efficacy | Antihistaminic plus antiallergic | Antihistaminic and antiallergic |
| Side effects | Major | Little | Rare |
| Interactions | Additive pharmacodynamic adverse effects | With enzyme inhibitors (macrolides, antifungals, calcium antagonists) | **No interactions, minimal adverse effects** |

- All have a relatively **short duration**, and all can be used systemically or topically.
- They are **"drying agents"**: they reduce secretions and local inflammation, and act more on the upper than the lower airways.
- Sedation is either used therapeutically (insomnia, sleep aid, anxiety, vertigo, cough) or avoided as a side effect.
- **In children** first-generation drugs can cause excitation, agitation and convulsions.

### Indications

| Linked to H1 blockade | Not linked to H1 blockade |
|---|---|
| **Good control:** rhinitis, conjunctivitis, urticaria, "flu" (sneezing and cough), itching | Vertigo and motion sickness (dimenhydrinate, diphenhydramine, promethazine; reduce firing from inner ear to vomiting centre) |
| **Poor control:** asthma, otitis, anaphylaxis, sinusitis, atopic dermatitis | Anti-emetic (promethazine: acts on the vomiting centre and is anticholinergic) |
| | Anti-parkinsonism (chlorpheniramine, dimenhydrinate, promethazine; anticholinergic action on extrapyramidal effects) |
| | **Appetite stimulation** (cyproheptadine, by 5-HT modulation) |
| | Anti-arrhythmic action (promethazine, antazoline; sodium channel and local anaesthetic effects) |

> **Exam tip:** antihistamines do not replace adrenaline in anaphylaxis and are not effective for asthma.

## 4. Anti-allergics

### Mast cell stabilisers: cromolyn and nedocromil

- **Prevent release of histamine** from mast cells (by inhibiting chloride channels). They act **only as prophylaxis**; they do not antagonise histamine already released.
- Used more in **children** for prophylaxis of perennial allergic rhinitis (nasal drops), and for allergic or exercise-induced asthma (inhaled powder or nebulised solution).
- Must be given **daily** and never stopped abruptly.
- Adverse effects: cough, wheeze, headache, rash.

### Leukotriene receptor antagonists (montelukast)

- **Block leukotriene actions.**
- More used for prophylaxis of lower airway disease (perennial allergen-, exercise- or **aspirin-induced asthma**) than for upper airway allergy (chronic rhinosinusitis). Adverse effects as in asthma.

## 5. Corticosteroids

- **Anti-inflammatory**: block phospholipase A2, reducing arachidonic acid, prostaglandin and leukotriene synthesis.
- **Topical nasal sprays**: beclomethasone, budesonide, fluticasone.
- Adverse effects: nasal irritation, fungal infection, hoarseness.
- Given for **severe intermittent or moderate persistent** symptoms.

## 6. Decongestants (alpha-adrenergic agonists)

They treat nasal stuffiness by vasoconstriction of nasal mucosal vessels.

| Route | Examples | Problems |
|---|---|---|
| **Topical** | Imidazolines: naphazoline, oxymetazoline, xylometazoline. Phenylethylamines: phenylephrine, methoxamine | **Rebound nasal stuffiness** with repeated use (10 days to 2 weeks) |
| **Systemic** | Pseudoephedrine | Nervousness, insomnia, tremor, palpitations, hypertension |

- Avoid systemic decongestants in **hypertension, heart failure, angina, hyperthyroidism and glaucoma**.

## 7. Anticholinergics

- **Ipratropium** nasal drops control **rhinorrhoea** (excess nasal secretion), so are very effective in **vasomotor rhinitis** (watery hypersecretion). Also a bronchodilator in asthma; adverse effects as for asthma.

## 8. Cough

### Physiology

1. **Mucociliary clearance** keeps the tracheobronchial tree clean by forming sputum of optimum quantity and viscosity that cilia move upward.
2. **Cough reflex** expels sputum when mucociliary clearance is inadequate.

| Type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Productive ("wet")** | Useful; clears secretions. Treat with expectorants and mucolytics |
| **Dry ("irritant")** | Not useful, annoying; caused by irritant vapours, gases, infection, cancer. Treat with antitussives |

## 9. Expectorants

Remove mucus by increasing and loosening secretions; cough is reduced indirectly.

| Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Reflex stimulation:** irritate the gut, stimulating a gastropulmonary vagal reflex that loosens and thins secretions | **Guaifenesin** |
| **Direct stimulation:** stimulate secretory glands to produce more respiratory fluid | Iodinated glycerol, sodium or potassium iodide / acetate, ammonium chloride, ipecacuanha |

**Indications:** common cold, bronchitis, laryngitis, pharyngitis, influenza, measles, chronic paranasal sinusitis, pertussis.

## 10. Mucolytics

They change the biophysical quality of sputum so it is cleared more easily by cilia or by less intense coughing.

| Mechanism | Example |
|---|---|
| Reduce viscoelasticity by increasing water content | Hypertonic saline, sodium bicarbonate |
| Reduce adhesiveness | Steam inhalation |
| Break **disulphide (S-S) bonds** in mucus glycoproteins with a reducing SH group | **N-acetylcysteine** |
| Produce serous mucus (smaller sialomucins); secretolytic; activate ciliary clearance | **Bromhexine, ambroxol** |
| Cleave extracellular bacterial DNA that raises sputum viscosity | **rhDNase (dornase alfa, Pulmozyme)** |

- Most are useful as **adjuvants** in COPD, asthma and bronchitis when mucus is excessive or thick; partial benefit in bronchiectasis, pneumonia and TB; hardly any benefit in cystic fibrosis and severe infections (**rhDNase** is used in CF).
- **N-acetylcysteine:** adverse effects bronchospasm, stomatitis, rhinorrhoea, rash, nausea and vomiting. It is a **free radical scavenger** and the antidote in **paracetamol (acetaminophen) overdose**.

## 11. Antitussives (dry cough)

Stop or reduce cough by acting on the peripheral or central part of the reflex.

### 11.1 Local measures

| Site | Agent |
|---|---|
| Pharynx | **Demulcents** (lozenges, gargles) form a protective coat |
| Larynx | **Emollients** (menthol, eucalyptus) |
| Tracheobronchial tree | Aerosols or hot steam inhalation (tincture of benzoin compound, eucalyptol) |
| During bronchoscopy or bronchography | Local anaesthetic aerosols: lidocaine, benzocaine, tetracaine |

### 11.2 Peripherally acting

- Inhibit airway stretch receptors and pulmonary stretch receptors in the alveoli.
- **Benzonatate**: reduces receptor sensitivity by local anaesthetic action. Adverse effects: drowsiness, dizziness, dysphagia, allergy. Overdose: confusion, hallucination, restlessness, tremor.

### 11.3 Centrally acting

| Class | Drug | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Opioids** | **Codeine, pholcodine** | Act on **mu opioid receptors** |
| **Non-opioid** | **Dextromethorphan** | Raises the threshold of the cough centre. As potent as codeine, **but** no drowsiness, less constipation, **no respiratory depression, no inhibition of mucociliary clearance, no addiction**. Adverse effects: nausea, vomiting, dizziness, rash, itch; at high doses hallucinations and opioid-like effects on respiration and gut |
| Sedating antihistamines | e.g. diphenhydramine | Used for cough at night |

> **Remember:** do not give antitussives in productive cough; suppressing it retains secretions.

## Key points to revise

1. Rhinitis may be allergic (seasonal or perennial) or non-allergic; treat with avoidance plus drugs.
2. Newer antihistamines (cetirizine, loratadine, fexofenadine) do not sedate; first-generation drugs sedate and interact.
3. Antihistamines work in rhinitis, urticaria and conjunctivitis but not asthma or anaphylaxis.
4. Nasal steroids are used for severe intermittent or moderate persistent rhinitis; topical decongestants cause rebound after 10 days to 2 weeks.
5. Cromolyn is only prophylactic; ipratropium dries watery rhinorrhoea.
6. Expectorants (guaifenesin) loosen mucus; mucolytics (N-acetylcysteine, ambroxol, bromhexine, rhDNase) thin it.
7. Dry cough: benzonatate (peripheral), codeine or dextromethorphan (central; dextromethorphan has fewer side effects).
