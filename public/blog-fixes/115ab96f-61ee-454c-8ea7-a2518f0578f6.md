## 1. Fine Needle Aspiration Cytology (FNAC) (×2)
**Q: What is FNAC?**
- A minimally invasive procedure used to aspirate cells from a solid mass or cystic lesion
- Purpose: determine if lesion is benign, malignant, or inflammatory through cytological examination
**Q: What instrument holds the syringe?**
- Cameco handle (syringe pistol)
- Allows one-handed aspiration while the other hand immobilizes the mass
**Q: What needle size is used?**
- 22–27 gauge
**Q: Common sites for FNAC?**
- Breast masses
- Thyroid nodules
- Lymph nodes (cervical, axillary, inguinal)
- Salivary glands
- Subcutaneous soft tissue masses
**Q: Why is negative pressure applied?**
- To shear cells off the stroma and draw them into the needle hub
**Q: Critical step before withdrawing the needle?**
- Release negative pressure first
- If withdrawn under suction, cells get sucked into the barrel — poor quality smear
**Q: What happens to the sample after extraction?**
- Expelled onto a glass slide
- Smeared into a thin layer
- Either air-dried (for Romanowsky stain) or alcohol-fixed (for Papanicolaou or H&E stain)
**Q: Advantages of FNAC?**
- Minimally invasive, often no local anaesthesia needed
- Rapid turnaround time
- Cost-effective
- Outpatient/bedside procedure
- Reduces unnecessary surgical biopsies
**Q: Limitations of FNAC?**
- Only provides cytological detail — no tissue architecture
- Cannot reliably distinguish in situ from invasive carcinoma
**Q: Complications?**
- Local pain
- Bruising/haematoma
- Infection
- Rare: pneumothorax (if near chest wall without ultrasound guidance)

## 2. Otitis Externa (Swimmer's Ear)
**Q: What is otitis externa?**
- Inflammation/infection of the external auditory canal
- Also called "swimmer's ear"
**Q: Common causes?**
- Bacterial: Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Staphylococcus aureus (most common)
- Fungal: Aspergillus, Candida
- Irritants: water exposure, trauma (cotton swabs), eczema
**Q: Clinical features?**
- Ear pain (otalgia)
- Itching of the ear canal
- Redness and swelling of canal
- Discharge from ear
- Possible hearing loss due to canal swelling
**Q: Gross pathological findings?**
- Erythema and swelling of external auditory canal
- Possible scaling and white discharge
- Thickening of ear canal skin
**Q: Complications if untreated?**
- Spread to surrounding tissues (perichondritis, cellulitis)
- Malignant otitis externa — especially in diabetics and immunosuppressed
- Chronic otitis externa
**Q: Management?**
- Topical antibiotics (e.g. ciprofloxacin ear drops)
- Topical steroids to reduce inflammation
- Aural toilet (cleaning the canal)
- Analgesics for pain
- Avoid water exposure until healed
**Q: At-risk populations?**
- Swimmers
- Diabetics
- Chronic skin conditions (eczema, psoriasis)
- Hearing aid users
- Frequent ear cleaning habit
**Q: Prevention?**
- Keep ears dry
- Avoid insertion of objects into ears
- Use earplugs while swimming if prone
- Maintain proper ear hygiene without trauma

## 3. Sickle Cell Disease (×2)
**Q: What is shown on the blood smear?**
- Sickle Cell Disease (sickle cell anaemia)
**Q: Describe the abnormal findings?**
- Numerous RBCs with crescent/sickle shape
- Contrast to normal biconcave disc-shaped RBCs
**Q: What genetic abnormality causes it?**
- Point mutation in HBB gene (chromosome 11)
- Valine substituted for glutamic acid at position 6 of the beta-globin chain
- Forms haemoglobin S (HbS)
**Q: Why do RBCs take on a sickle shape?**
- HbS polymerizes under low oxygen conditions
- Distorts RBCs into rigid sickle shape
**Q: Clinical manifestations?**
- Chronic anaemia
- Vaso-occlusive crises (painful episodes)
- Increased risk of infections
- Dactylitis (swelling of hands/feet in children)
- Aplastic crises
- Acute chest syndrome
- Splenic sequestration
**Q: Complications of sickle-shaped RBCs?**
- Obstruction of microvasculature → ischaemic organ damage (spleen, bone, brain)
- Haemolysis (shortened RBC lifespan)
- Increased risk of stroke and organ dysfunction
**Q: How is it inherited?**
- Autosomal recessive
**Q: Laboratory tests to confirm diagnosis?**
- Haemoglobin electrophoresis (detects HbS)
- Sickle cell screening test (sodium metabisulfite test)
- Peripheral blood smear
**Q: Treatment?**
- Hydroxyurea (increases fetal haemoglobin)
- Regular blood transfusions
- Pain management
- Infection prophylaxis/antibiotics
- Curative: haematopoietic stem cell transplant (select cases)
**Q: Normal RBC vs sickle cell appearance?**
- Normal RBC: round, biconcave disc
- Sickle RBC: elongated, crescent-shaped

## 4. Xanthomas / Familial Hypercholesterolaemia
**Q: What findings are seen?**
- Multiple subcutaneous nodules over fingers, foot, and eyelids (xanthelasma)
- Corneal arcus in the eye
- Characteristic of xanthomas and arcus senilis
**Q: Clinical significance?**
- Reflect severe hyperlipidaemia
- Major risk factor for premature atherosclerosis and cardiovascular disease
- External markers indicating need for aggressive lipid management
**Q: Types of xanthomas?**
- Tendon xanthomas — on Achilles tendon and knuckles
- Xanthelasma — yellowish plaques on eyelids
- Eruptive xanthomas — small yellowish papules on buttocks and extensor surfaces
- Tuberous xanthomas — firm, painless red/yellow nodules over joints
- Plane xanthomas — flat or slightly elevated macules/plaques on eyelids
**Q: What causes the pale ring around the cornea?**
- Corneal arcus (arcus senilis)
- Caused by cholesterol deposits in the peripheral cornea
**Q: Associated systemic disorder?**
- Familial hypercholesterolaemia — genetic disorder with high blood cholesterol
**Q: How is diagnosis confirmed histologically?**
- Excise nodule, embed in paraffin, section with microtome
- Stain and examine under microscope
- Shows lipid-laden macrophages (foam cells)

## 5. Microtome
**Q: What is a microtome?**
- A laboratory instrument used to cut extremely thin sections of biological tissue for microscopic examination
**Q: How does it work?**
- Tissue embedded in paraffin is secured in the block holder
- Moved across a very sharp blade
- Produces thin sections typically 3–5 µm thick
- Sections mounted on slides for staining and examination

## 6. Acute Promyelocytic Leukaemia (APL) / Auer Rods (×2)
**Q: What cells are seen on the blood smear?**
- Blasts, promyelocytes, and abnormal promyelocytes
- Suggestive of Acute Promyelocytic Leukaemia (APL / AML-M3)
**Q: What are Auer rods?**
- Needle-like or rod-shaped cytoplasmic inclusions
- Formed by fusion of primary (azurophilic) granules
**Q: Clinical significance of Auer rods?**
- Pathognomonic for Acute Myeloid Leukaemia (AML)
- Never found in healthy individuals or lymphoid leukaemias (ALL)
**Q: Features of abnormal promyelocytes?**
- Numerous Auer rods
- Large azurophilic granules (marked +++)
- Irregular or bilobed nuclei
- More intense granulation than normal promyelocytes
**Q: What are faggot cells?**
- Cells in APL containing multiple bundles/clusters of Auer rods
**Q: Why is APL a medical emergency?**
- High risk of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)
**Q: Differential diagnoses for abnormal WBCs?**
- Acute Myeloid Leukaemia (AML)
- Acute Lymphoblastic Leukaemia (ALL) — but no Auer rods
- Chronic Myeloid Leukaemia (CML) in blast crisis
**Q: Treatment of AML?**
- Induction chemotherapy to achieve remission
- Consolidation therapy (post-remission)
- Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation
**Q: Further tests to confirm subtype?**
- Bone marrow aspiration and biopsy
- Flow cytometry for immunophenotyping
- Cytogenetic analysis for chromosomal abnormalities

## 7. Pap Smear Brush / Cervical Cytology (×2)
**Q: What is a Pap smear brush?**
- A cervical cytology brush used to collect cells from the cervix
- Used for Papanicolaou (Pap) smear — screening for cervical cancer and precursors
**Q: How is it used?**
- Inserted into cervical os
- Rotated 360°
- Bristles collect exfoliated cells from endocervix and transformation zone
**Q: Indications?**
- Routine cervical cancer screening
- Detection of precancerous and cancerous lesions
- Diagnosing cervical infections
**Q: Common findings on Pap smear?**
- Normal squamous cells
- Inflammatory cells
- Low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL)
- High-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL)
- Malignant cells suggestive of cervical cancer

## 8. Tissue Cassette and Paraffin Wax
**Q: What are these items used for?**
- Tissue cassette: holds tissue samples during processing
- Paraffin wax: embeds tissue to allow thin sectioning for microscopy
**Q: Purpose of paraffin wax in tissue processing?**
- Infiltrates tissue after dehydration and clearing
- Provides firm consistency for thin sectioning (3–5 microns)
- Preserves cellular details for pathological examination
**Q: Ideal melting point of tissue wax?**
- 56–58°C — optimal for routine histopathological embedding
**Q: Why embed tissue in wax?**
- Maintains tissue structure
- Supports tissue for sectioning
- Preserves cellular details for microscopic analysis

## 9. Full Haemogram / Complete Blood Count (CBC)
**Q: What is a full haemogram?**
- Complete blood count with differentials
- Measures WBC, RBC, haemoglobin, PCV, platelets, and differential white cell count
**Q: Abnormal findings suggesting bacterial infection?**
- WBC elevated (e.g. 20.84 × 10³/µL; ref: 4.0–10.0)
- Neutrophils elevated (e.g. 87.2%; ref: 30–70%)
- Lymphocytes low (e.g. 8.3%; ref: 20–50%)
- Low PCV suggesting anaemia
**Q: Interpretation of neutrophilia + leukocytosis?**
- Suggests acute bacterial infection or severe inflammation
**Q: Additional tests recommended?**
- Blood culture (if sepsis suspected)
- Reticulocyte count (for anaemia type)
- Peripheral blood film
- Urinalysis

## 10. Plasma Cells
**Q: How do you recognize a plasma cell microscopically?**
- Eccentric nucleus
- Clumped "clock-face" or "cartwheel" chromatin pattern
- Abundant basophilic cytoplasm
- Perinuclear hof (clear area next to nucleus)
**Q: Function of plasma cells?**
- Synthesis and secretion of antibodies (immunoglobulins)
**Q: Origin of plasma cells?**
- Differentiated from B lymphocytes
**Q: Diseases where plasma cells are increased?**
- Multiple myeloma
- Chronic inflammation
**Q: What is a Mott cell?**
- Plasma cell packed with immunoglobulin-containing Russell bodies
**Q: Where are plasma cells normally found?**
- Bone marrow
- Spleen
- Mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT)
- Lymph nodes
**Q: Tumours of plasma cells?**
- Multiple myeloma
- Plasmacytoma

## 11. Coagulation / Haematological Report
**Q: Normal platelet count?**
- 130,000–400,000/µL
**Q: Normal bleeding time?**
- 1–4 minutes
**Q: What does prolonged prothrombin time (PT) indicate?**
- Extrinsic pathway defect (Factor VII)
- Liver disease
- Vitamin K deficiency
- Warfarin therapy
- DIC
**Q: Significance of aPTT?**
- Measures intrinsic pathway
- Prolonged in haemophilia, heparin therapy, DIC
**Q: Normal fibrinogen range?**
- 219–516 mg/dL
**Q: Increased bleeding time with normal platelet count suggests?**
- Platelet dysfunction (e.g. Glanzmann thrombasthenia, Bernard-Soulier syndrome)
**Q: Which test is affected in Haemophilia A?**
- aPTT will be prolonged
**Q: Low platelet count is seen in?**
- Thrombocytopenia: ITP, DIC, bone marrow failure, drug reactions

## 12. Hydrocephalus (×2)
**Q: What is hydrocephalus?**
- Abnormal accumulation of CSF in cerebral ventricles causing their dilation
**Q: Types of hydrocephalus?**
- Communicating
- Non-communicating (obstructive)
- Ex vacuo
**Q: Causes of congenital hydrocephalus?**
- Aqueductal stenosis
- Dandy-Walker malformation
- Arnold-Chiari malformation
- Intrauterine infections
**Q: Clinical features in a child?**
- Enlarged head circumference
- Bulging fontanelle
- Scalp vein distension
- "Setting sun" sign of eyes
**Q: What is "setting sun" sign?**
- Downward deviation of eyes due to increased intracranial pressure
**Q: What is hydrocephalus ex vacuo?**
- Ventricular enlargement due to brain atrophy — not increased CSF production
**Q: Treatment?**
- Surgical shunt (ventriculoperitoneal shunt)
- Correct underlying cause
**Q: Complications if untreated?**
- Increased intracranial pressure
- Brain damage
- Developmental delay
- Death
