# Fibromyalgia

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Rheumatology**

> **In one line:** fibromyalgia is a chronic widespread pain syndrome with fatigue, unrefreshing sleep and cognitive problems, caused by **central sensitisation** (the brain and spinal cord amplify normal sensory signals) rather than by tissue inflammation. Tests are normal; diagnosis is clinical, and management is education, graded aerobic exercise, sleep and mood care, with drugs as add-ons.

## What you must know

- Core features: **chronic widespread musculoskeletal pain**, **fatigue**, **non-restorative sleep** and cognitive disturbance ("**fibro fog**").
- Aetiology is unknown. Key mechanisms are **central pain sensitisation** and **disturbed delta-wave (stage 4 non-REM) sleep**.
- There is **no tissue inflammation**: ESR, CRP, full blood count, and imaging are normal. Tests are done only to exclude other disease.
- Strong **female predominance (about 10:1)**; prevalence in the UK and USA is about 2 to 3%, rising with age.
- **1990 ACR criteria:** widespread pain plus **11 of 18 tender points** (4 kg/cm² pressure). **2010 criteria** use the **WPI and SS score** and need no tender-point examination.
- First-line management is **education, aerobic exercise (30 minutes, 3 times a week), sleep hygiene and stress reduction**. Drugs: amitriptyline, duloxetine, milnacipran, pregabalin, gabapentin, cyclobenzaprine.
- Opioids and NSAIDs are not helpful (the pain is not nociceptive or inflammatory).

## 1. Definition

- **Fibromyalgia (FM)** is a condition of generalised pain and consequent disability, characterised by **chronic widespread musculoskeletal pain**.
- Fatigue, cognitive disturbance, psychiatric symptoms and multiple somatic symptoms often accompany it.
- The aetiology is unknown and the pathophysiology uncertain.
- There is **no evidence of tissue inflammation** despite soft-tissue pain.
- It is frequently associated with medically unexplained symptoms in other systems (irritable bowel, headache, bladder symptoms).

## 2. Epidemiology and risk factors

- Prevalence in the UK and USA is about **2 to 3%** in some series; US figures of **6.4%** overall (7.7% in women, 4.9% in men) have also been reported. Europe and South America range from **3.3 to 8.3%**.
- It occurs in all races and cultures, at any age including adolescence, and **increases with age**, peaking at about **7% in women over 70**.
- **Female to male ratio is about 10:1.**
- Between ages 20 and 55, fibromyalgia is the commonest cause of generalised musculoskeletal pain in women.
- Prevalence in adolescents is similar to that in adults in many studies.
- More than **40%** of patients referred to a tertiary pain clinic meet the criteria.
- The risk is higher if there is an existing rheumatic disease. FM may co-exist with **axial spondyloarthritis and SLE**.

### Risk factors

- Unresolved **psychosocial distress**: previous abuse, marital disharmony, alcoholism or illness in the family
- Poor sleep
- Previous injury or assault
- Low income
- Triggers: physical, emotional or environmental stressors such as road traffic accidents, repetitive injury and some illnesses

## 3. Pathophysiology

The cause is poorly understood, but two interrelated abnormalities are consistently reported: **disturbed, non-restorative sleep** and **pain sensitisation** from abnormal central pain processing.

- **Central sensitisation:** blunted descending (inhibitory) pain pathways and altered neurotransmitters lead to abnormal processing of sensory signals in the CNS. The pain threshold falls and normal signals are amplified, so pain is constant and out of proportion (**allodynia and hyperalgesia**).
- **Neuroendocrine changes:** altered **serotonin, substance P, growth hormone and cortisol** point to dysregulation of the autonomic and neuroendocrine systems. Thyroid hormone and pituitary growth hormone imbalance have also been proposed.
- **Disturbed stress response:** frequent comorbidity with mood disorders suggests a major role for the stress (HPA axis) response.
- **Sleep disruption:** loss of **delta-wave (non-REM stage 4) sleep** is linked to daytime fatigue and FM.
- **Proposed sequence (Duna and Wilke, 1993):** disordered sleep leads to reduced serotonin, which reduces the pain-modulating effect of endorphins and increases **substance P**. Together with sympathetic nervous system changes, this causes muscle ischaemia and greater pain sensitivity.
- Constant hypervigilance to pain is itself linked with psychological problems, which feed back into the cycle.

> **Remember:** FM is a disorder of pain **processing**, not of muscle or joint damage. That is why biopsies, scans, and inflammatory markers are normal.

## 4. Clinical features

### Main symptoms

| Symptom | Details |
|---|---|
| **Widespread pain** | Chief complaint; bilateral, above and below the waist, may begin locally (often neck and shoulders) |
| **Fatigue** | Worst on waking and again in mid-afternoon |
| **Unrefreshing sleep** | Wakes tired despite adequate hours |
| **Cognitive disturbance ("fibro fog")** | Poor attention, difficulty multitasking, memory problems |

### Other symptoms

- Headache
- Dysmenorrhoea
- Extreme sensitivity to cold
- Restless legs
- Odd patterns of numbness and tingling, paraesthesia
- Exercise intolerance
- Stiffness
- Palpitations
- Impaired concentration, memory and attention span

Associated syndromes: irritable bowel syndrome, interstitial cystitis, chronic fatigue syndrome, temporomandibular disorder, depression and anxiety.

### Examination

- Usually normal apart from **diffuse tenderness**. There is no synovitis, no muscle wasting, and no neurological deficit.
- Always look for signs of an alternative or co-existing condition (synovitis, rash, proximal weakness, goitre).

## 5. Diagnostic criteria

### 1990 ACR classification criteria

Both must be present:

1. **Widespread pain** on both sides of the body, above and below the waist
2. **At least 11 of 18 tender points** (9 bilateral sites)

| Site (bilateral) | Location |
|---|---|
| 1 | Suboccipital muscle insertions |
| 2 | Anterior C5 to C7 intertransverse spaces |
| 3 | Mid upper border of trapezius |
| 4 | Origin of supraspinatus |
| 5 | Second costochondral junctions |
| 6 | 2 cm distal to the lateral epicondyles |
| 7 | Upper outer quadrants of the buttocks |
| 8 | Greater trochanteric prominence |
| 9 | Medial fat pad of the knees |

- Pressure should be about **4 kg/cm²**, enough to whiten the examiner's nail bed.
- Used widely in trials but **not useful for diagnosis in practice**.

**Limitations of the 1990 criteria:**

- Doctors did not know how to examine tender points, did them wrongly, or refused to do so.
- Key symptoms (fatigue, sleep, cognition) were not captured.
- The threshold left little room for variation, and patients whose symptoms improved no longer met the criteria.

### 2010 ACR preliminary criteria (modified 2011, then 2016)

A patient has FM if **all three** are met:

1. **WPI 7 or more and SS score 5 or more, OR WPI 3 to 6 and SS score 9 or more**
2. Symptoms present at a similar level for **at least 3 months**
3. No other disorder that would explain the pain

| Measure | How it is scored |
|---|---|
| **Widespread Pain Index (WPI)** | Number of body areas (out of 19) with pain in the past week; score 0 to 19 |
| **Symptom Severity (SS) scale** | Severity over the past week of **fatigue, waking unrefreshed and cognitive symptoms**; each scored 0 to 3, total 0 to 9 (plus a somatic symptom component in the full scale) |

> **Check:** the slides state the SS total as 0 to 12 and quote "SS of at least 7 and WPI of at least 15". The standard thresholds are those in point 1 above (WPI 7 or more with SS 5 or more, or WPI 3 to 6 with SS 9 or more). The 2016 revision requires WPI 7 or more with SS 5 or more (or WPI 4 to 6 with SS 9 or more), pain in at least 4 of 5 regions, and symptoms for at least 3 months.

## 6. Investigations

- **No abnormality** on routine blood tests or imaging.
- Tests are done to **exclude mimics**: FBC, ESR/CRP, TSH, calcium, vitamin D, CK, and antinuclear antibody or rheumatoid factor only if clinical features suggest autoimmune disease.

## 7. Differential diagnosis

| Condition | Points to separate it |
|---|---|
| **Polymyalgia rheumatica** | Age over 50, morning stiffness of shoulders and hips, very high ESR/CRP, dramatic steroid response |
| **Spondyloarthritis** | Inflammatory back pain, enthesitis, HLA-B27, sacroiliitis on imaging |
| **Inflammatory myopathy** | Proximal **weakness** (not just pain), raised CK, abnormal EMG and biopsy |
| **Systemic inflammatory arthropathies** (RA, SLE) | Synovitis, rash, raised inflammatory markers, autoantibodies |
| **Hypothyroidism** | Fatigue, myalgia, raised TSH |
| Others | Depression, sleep apnoea, vitamin D deficiency, HIV (relevant locally), chronic fatigue syndrome |

## 8. Management

Approach: **educate first**, then exercise, then drugs for those who do not respond to non-drug measures.

### Patient education

- **Reassure** that fibromyalgia is a **real disease**, not damage or deformity, and does not shorten life.
- Explain the role of stress and mood; teach **relaxation** and offer formal **stress-reduction programmes**.
- Stress **good sleep hygiene** and treat any contributing sleep disorder (e.g. sleep apnoea, restless legs).
- Cognitive behavioural therapy helps coping and mood.

### Exercise

- **Cardiovascular fitness training** reduces pain and improves sleep.
- Minimum of **30 minutes of aerobic exercise, three times a week**, with heart rate near target range.
- Start low and increase slowly (graded exercise) to avoid flares.

### Drug therapy

| Class | Examples | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Tricyclic antidepressants** | **Amitriptyline** (low dose at night) | Helps pain and sleep |
| **Muscle relaxant (tricyclic structure)** | **Cyclobenzaprine** | Effective in FM |
| **SNRIs** | **Duloxetine**, **milnacipran** | FDA-approved |
| **SSRIs** | Fluoxetine and others | Help mood; weaker for pain |
| **Anticonvulsants (gabapentinoids)** | **Pregabalin** (FDA-approved), **gabapentin** | Reduce central sensitisation |

- The three FDA-approved drugs are **pregabalin, duloxetine and milnacipran**.
- Antidepressants and anticonvulsants are the best-studied and most consistently effective.
- Simple analgesics may be used for flares; **strong opioids are discouraged**.

> **Exam tip:** NSAIDs, steroids and opioids do not work well in FM because there is no inflammation. Steroids are used only if PMR is the diagnosis instead.

## 9. Complications

- **"Fibro fog":** persistent cognitive problems and memory difficulty that interfere with concentration.
- Patients are **more likely to be hospitalised** for any reason than the general population.
- Depression, anxiety, disability, and reduced work capacity.

## 10. Prognosis

- Most patients in **tertiary referral** studies continue to have chronic pain and fatigue.
- Patients managed by **primary care** in the community have a **much better** prognosis.
- Poor prognostic factors: **female sex, low socioeconomic status, unemployment, obesity, depression, and history of abuse**.

## Key points to revise

1. FM = chronic widespread pain + fatigue + unrefreshing sleep + fibro fog, with **normal tests**.
2. Mechanism: **central sensitisation** and loss of delta-wave sleep; low serotonin, high substance P.
3. Female to male about 10:1; prevalence rises with age.
4. 1990 ACR: widespread pain plus **11 of 18 tender points**; 2010 ACR: **WPI and SS**, symptoms for 3 months, no other cause.
5. Exclude PMR, spondyloarthritis, myositis, SLE/RA and hypothyroidism.
6. Management: education, **aerobic exercise 30 minutes three times a week**, sleep hygiene, CBT.
7. Drugs: amitriptyline, cyclobenzaprine, duloxetine, milnacipran, pregabalin, gabapentin; avoid opioids.
8. Prognosis is better in community care than in tertiary centres.
