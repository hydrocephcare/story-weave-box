## SEMESTER TWO — COMPLETE NOTES**1. Introduction to Haematology**
- Study of blood, blood-forming organs (marrow, lymphoid tissue, spleen), and their diseases.
- Blood = plasma (~55%) + cells (~45%): RBCs, WBCs, platelets.
- **Sites of haemopoiesis across life (must-know sequence):**
Mesoblastic stage — yolk sac (weeks 2–10 gestation)
- Hepatic stage — liver/spleen (6 weeks–7 months gestation)
- Medullary stage — bone marrow (from ~5 months gestation; sole site by birth)
- Adult red (active) marrow confined to: vertebrae, sternum, ribs, skull, pelvis, proximal femur/humerus.
- Yellow marrow (fatty, inactive) can revert to red marrow under stress (e.g. severe chronic anaemia).
- **Essay angle**: "Describe the sites of haemopoiesis from foetal life to adulthood" is a classic short essay — use the 3-stage sequence as your skeleton.
## 2. Haemopoiesis
- Process forming/maturing all blood cells from one ancestor: **pluripotent haemopoietic stem cell (HSC)**, capable of self-renewal and differentiation.
- Splits into two lineages:
**Myeloid** → erythrocytes, granulocytes (neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils), monocytes, megakaryocytes/platelets
- **Lymphoid** → T cells, B cells, NK cells
- **Growth factors (must-know matching for MCQs):**
Erythropoietin (EPO) → RBCs (renal origin, hypoxia-triggered)
- Thrombopoietin → platelets (mainly hepatic origin)
- G-CSF / GM-CSF → granulocytes
- Interleukins (IL-3, IL-7…) → early progenitors
- Bone marrow stroma (stromal cells, fat, sinusoids) physically/chemically supports developing cells — "the soil the seed grows in."
## 3. Erythrocytes
- Biconcave, anucleate discs, ~7–8 µm diameter, lifespan **~120 days**.
- Function: O2/CO2 transport via haemoglobin; biconcave shape maximises surface area and flexibility for capillary passage.
- **Maturation sequence (must-know, often asked to list in order):** Proerythroblast → Basophilic erythroblast → Polychromatic erythroblast → Orthochromatic erythroblast → Reticulocyte → Mature erythrocyte
Nucleus extruded at the orthochromatic stage — why mature RBCs are anucleate.
- Reticulocytes retain residual RNA (bluish/polychromatic tint on stain), circulate ~1–2 days before full maturity — reticulocyte count is a marker of marrow erythropoietic activity.
- Production driven by EPO; destruction mainly extravascular in the spleen — Hb broken down to globin (recycled to amino acids) and heme (→ iron recycled, protoporphyrin → bilirubin → excreted, hence jaundice if haemolysis is excessive).
- **Red cell indices (must-know definitions — common MCQ stem):**
MCV — mean cell volume (average size)
- MCH — mean cell haemoglobin (average Hb mass per cell)
- MCHC — mean cell Hb concentration (Hb relative to cell volume)
- Haematocrit/PCV — % of blood volume occupied by RBCs
## 4. Haemoglobin
- Tetramer of 4 globin chains (adult: 2 alpha + 2 beta), each carrying one heme group with central Fe²⁺ that binds O2 reversibly.
- Loads O2 in lungs (high pO2), unloads in tissues (lower pO2, higher CO2/H⁺ — **Bohr effect** shifts the O2-Hb dissociation curve right, favouring unloading).
- **Haemoglobin types (must-know chain composition — classic matching question):**
HbA (α2β2) — major adult Hb (~97%)
- HbA2 (α2δ2) — minor adult component (~2%)
- HbF (α2γ2) — dominant foetal Hb, falls sharply after birth (residual <1% in adults)
- Synthesis requires: adequate iron supply, normal globin genes (alpha cluster — chromosome 16; beta cluster — chromosome 11), and protoporphyrin (heme synthesis pathway).
- **Abnormal haemoglobins (high-yield for both MCQ and essay):**
**HbS (sickle cell disease)** — single point mutation, beta chain: glutamic acid → valine at position 6; causes Hb polymerisation under deoxygenation → sickling, vaso-occlusion, haemolysis
- **Thalassaemias** — reduced/absent globin chain synthesis (alpha-thalassaemia = alpha gene defect; beta-thalassaemia = beta gene defect) → chain imbalance → ineffective erythropoiesis + haemolysis
## 5. Anaemia
- Definition: Hb/red cell mass below normal reference range for age and sex → reduced O2-carrying capacity.
- **Classify by mechanism (essay skeleton #1):**
Decreased production — iron deficiency, megaloblastic anaemia (B12/folate deficiency), aplastic anaemia, anaemia of chronic disease, marrow infiltration (leukaemia, metastases)
- Increased destruction (haemolytic) — hereditary (sickle cell disease, thalassaemia, hereditary spherocytosis, G6PD deficiency) or acquired (autoimmune haemolytic anaemia, malaria, microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia/DIC)
- Blood loss — acute (trauma/haemorrhage) or chronic (GI bleed, menorrhagia)
- **Classify by morphology using MCV (essay skeleton #2 — the one examiners love most):**
Microcytic (MCV <80fl) — iron deficiency, thalassaemia, anaemia of chronic disease, sideroblastic anaemia
- Normocytic (MCV 80–100fl) — acute blood loss, anaemia of chronic disease, early haemolytic anaemia, aplastic anaemia, renal disease
- Macrocytic (MCV >100fl) — B12/folate deficiency, liver disease, hypothyroidism, raised reticulocytes
- Clinical features: fatigue, pallor, dyspnoea, tachycardia, dizziness + specific clues — koilonychia/spoon nails (iron deficiency), jaundice (haemolytic), glossitis (B12/folate deficiency).
- Key investigations: FBC, peripheral blood film, reticulocyte count, iron studies (ferritin, TIBC, serum iron), B12/folate, Hb electrophoresis, marrow exam when indicated.
- **Must-know exam pattern**: vignette gives symptoms + film appearance + MCV → you must name BOTH the anaemia type AND its mechanism. This is the single most-tested clinical reasoning skill in this whole unit.
## 6. Leukocytes
- **Granulocytes**: neutrophils (most abundant, first responders to bacterial infection/acute inflammation), eosinophils (allergic reactions, parasitic infections), basophils (allergic/hypersensitivity reactions, contain histamine/heparin)
- **Mononuclear cells**: lymphocytes (B cells, T cells, NK cells — adaptive immunity) and monocytes (phagocytic; become tissue macrophages)
- Normal differential count (approx): neutrophils 40–75%, lymphocytes 20–45%, monocytes 2–10%, eosinophils 1–6%, basophils 0–1%.
- Origin: granulocytes/monocytes from myeloid lineage; lymphocytes from lymphoid lineage (T cells mature in thymus, B cells mature in bone marrow).
- **Must-know clinical pairings (favourite MCQ format):**
Neutrophilia — bacterial infection, stress, steroid use
- Eosinophilia — allergy, parasitic infection
- Lymphocytosis — viral infection
- Neutropenia — viral infection, certain drugs, marrow failure
## 7. White Blood Cell Malignancies 1 — Acute Leukaemias
- Malignant clonal proliferation of immature blasts in the marrow, suppressing normal haemopoiesis.
- **AML (Acute Myeloid Leukaemia)** — myeloid blasts; more common in adults; **Auer rods** on blood film = must-know diagnostic clue.
- **ALL (Acute Lymphoblastic Leukaemia)** — lymphoid blasts; the most common leukaemia of childhood.
- Pathogenesis: chromosomal translocation/mutation arrests differentiation at the blast stage → blasts accumulate and crowd out normal marrow elements → pancytopenia.
- Clinical features: anaemia (fatigue, pallor), neutropenia (recurrent infections), thrombocytopenia (bruising, bleeding) + infiltrative features — hepatosplenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, bone pain, gum hypertrophy (especially AML-M5), CNS involvement (especially ALL).
- Diagnosis: marrow blast count ≥20% (WHO criteria); immunophenotyping/flow cytometry distinguishes AML from ALL; cytogenetics guide prognosis.
- **Must-know cytogenetics**: t(15;17) — AML-M3 (acute promyelocytic leukaemia), strongly associated with DIC at presentation, treated with ATRA (all-trans retinoic acid).
## 8. White Blood Cell Malignancies 2 — Chronic Leukaemias & Lymphomas
- **CML (Chronic Myeloid Leukaemia):**
Clonal proliferation of mature/maturing myeloid cells
- Hallmark: **Philadelphia chromosome t(9;22) → BCR-ABL fusion gene** (constitutive tyrosine kinase activity) — the single most important fact in this topic
- Phases: chronic → accelerated → blast crisis
- Film: marked leukocytosis with the full spectrum of granulocyte maturation, splenomegaly common
- Treatment: tyrosine kinase inhibitors (imatinib) — a classic "mechanism of drug action" essay link
- **CLL (Chronic Lymphocytic Leukaemia):**
Clonal proliferation of mature but functionally incompetent B lymphocytes
- Commonest leukaemia in older adults, often picked up incidentally
- Film: small mature lymphocytes + **smear/smudge cells** — classic must-know film finding
- Lymphadenopathy/hepatosplenomegaly if advanced
- **Lymphomas (brief overview — detailed coverage usually a separate topic):**
Hodgkin lymphoma — **Reed-Sternberg cells** (binucleate, "owl-eye" nuclei) — must-know diagnostic cell
- Non-Hodgkin lymphoma — heterogeneous group, no Reed-Sternberg cells
## 9–10. Platelet Structure and Morphology
- Anucleate cytoplasmic fragments budded off **megakaryocytes** in the marrow; lifespan ~7–10 days; normal count 150,000–400,000/µL.
- **Granule contents (must-know for mechanism questions):**
Alpha granules — von Willebrand factor (vWF), fibrinogen, PDGF
- Dense granules — ADP, ATP, serotonin, calcium
- **Primary haemostasis — 3-step sequence (essay skeleton):**
Adhesion — platelet GpIb receptor binds subendothelial collagen via a vWF bridge
- Activation — shape change; releases ADP and thromboxane A2, recruiting further platelets
- Aggregation — GpIIb/IIIa receptors link platelets to each other via fibrinogen bridges
- Regulated by thrombopoietin (mainly hepatic).
- **Disorders (must-know classification):**
Thrombocytopenia — decreased production (marrow failure, aplastic anaemia, leukaemic infiltration) vs increased destruction (ITP, DIC, hypersplenism)
- Thrombocytosis — reactive (infection, inflammation, iron deficiency, post-splenectomy) vs primary/clonal (essential thrombocythaemia)
- Qualitative defects — Glanzmann thrombasthenia (GpIIb/IIIa deficiency), Bernard-Soulier syndrome (GpIb deficiency), von Willebrand disease (commonest inherited bleeding disorder overall)
- **Key clinical differentiator**: platelet/vascular bleeding is mucocutaneous — petechiae, purpura, epistaxis, gum bleeding — contrast this directly against coagulation factor bleeding below.
## 11. Coagulation System
- Secondary haemostasis — stabilises the platelet plug into a durable fibrin clot.
- **Classic cascade (most exam-relevant model, even though physiologically the cell-based model is now preferred):**
Intrinsic pathway — XII → XI → IX → VIII; measured by **aPTT**
- Extrinsic pathway — tissue factor + factor VII; measured by **PT**
- Common pathway — factor X → prothrombin (II) → thrombin → fibrinogen (I) → fibrin
- Factor numbering I–XIII; know especially I = fibrinogen, II = prothrombin.
- **Vitamin K-dependent factors (must-know list)**: II, VII, IX, X
- Natural anticoagulants (limit clot extension): antithrombin III, protein C, protein S.
- Fibrinolysis: plasmin degrades fibrin → **D-dimer**, a clinical marker of clot turnover (e.g. DVT/PE workup).
- **Must-know disorder/lab-pattern table — high-yield for MCQs:**
Haemophilia A — Factor VIII deficiency, X-linked, ↑aPTT, normal PT
- Haemophilia B — Factor IX deficiency, X-linked, same lab pattern as A
- Von Willebrand disease — ↓/dysfunctional vWF, ↑aPTT + abnormal platelet function tests
- Vitamin K deficiency / warfarin / liver disease — ↑PT (± ↑aPTT in severe cases)
- DIC — ↑PT AND ↑aPTT, ↓platelets, ↓fibrinogen, ↑D-dimer (consumption of everything at once — the giveaway pattern)
- Clinical bleeding pattern: factor deficiencies cause deep bleeding — haemarthroses (joint bleeds), muscle haematomas — directly contrast with the mucocutaneous bleeding of platelet disorders. This contrast is a near-guaranteed essay or MCQ differentiator.
