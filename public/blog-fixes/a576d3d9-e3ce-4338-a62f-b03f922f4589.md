## Why Genetics Is Clinically Important

**The scale of the problem**
- Approximately 5% of individuals under 25 years develop a serious disease with a significant genetic component — this is not a rare, footnote topic
- Genetic diagnosis and screening are generally **neither easy nor cheap**
- Only around 50% of early pregnancy miscarriages show demonstrable chromosomal abnormalities — not 100%, a common exam trap

**The three master categories**
Every genetic disorder falls into one of three buckets. Identify the bucket first, and the rest of the logic follows:
1. **Mendelian (single-gene) disorders** — one gene, one major effect, predictable inheritance pattern
2. **Chromosomal disorders** — whole chromosomes or large segments missing, extra, or rearranged
3. **Complex/multigenic disorders** — many genes interacting with environment (e.g., diabetes, hypertension, most common adult diseases)

## Mendelian Inheritance — The Underlying Logic

Understand *why* each pattern behaves the way it does — the disease lists become obvious afterward.

**Autosomal Dominant (AD)**
- One abnormal copy of the gene (from either parent) is sufficient to cause disease, because the gene sits on a non-sex chromosome
- **Pattern on a family tree:** disease appears in every generation, no skipping; affected parent has 50% chance of passing it to each child; both sexes equally affected
- **Molecular theme:** typically involves **structural proteins** (collagen, cytoskeletal proteins) or receptors — one bad copy disrupts structure even with a normal copy present
- **Onset:** often adulthood
- **Disorders to know as a set:** Marfan syndrome (fibrillin-1), Huntington disease (trinucleotide repeat), familial hypercholesterolemia (LDL receptor defect), polycystic kidney disease, hereditary spherocytosis (~75% of cases)

**Autosomal Recessive (AR)**
- Need TWO abnormal copies (one from each parent) to manifest disease; single copy = silent carrier
- **Pattern:** skips generations; both sexes equally affected; parents typically unaffected carriers; often only one sibling affected
- **Molecular theme:** typically involves **enzyme deficiencies** — one working copy usually produces enough enzyme to avoid disease
- **Onset:** often childhood
- **Disorders to know as a set:** phenylketonuria, galactosemia, glycogen storage diseases, mucopolysaccharidoses, sickle cell anaemia (structural protein exception — one normal beta-globin gene still makes enough normal haemoglobin), most lysosomal storage diseases

**X-linked Recessive**
- Gene is on the X chromosome. Males have only one X, so a single abnormal copy causes disease. Females have two X's, so need two abnormal copies to be affected — usually just carriers
- **Pattern:** almost exclusively affects males; affected males cannot pass it to sons; carrier mothers pass it to 50% of sons (affected) and 50% of daughters (carriers)
- **Key example:** Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) — the most tested X-linked recessive disorder in this course
- **Why carrier females are usually protected:** random X-inactivation (lyonization) means roughly half their cells express the normal X

**The trap option**
"All sex-linked disorders are X-linked recessive" is FALSE: Y-linked disorders exist (rare), and **X-linked dominant** disorders exist too (e.g., vitamin D–resistant rickets).

## The Molecular Basis — Mutations

**Types of mutation**
- **Missense:** one amino acid swapped for another; protein still made but altered. Sickle cell anaemia is the textbook example — Glu→Val substitution at codon 6 of beta-globin
- **Nonsense:** a codon becomes a "stop" signal, truncating the protein — usually far more damaging than missense
- **Silent:** DNA changes, but the resulting amino acid is the same (codon redundancy) — no effect on protein

**SNPs (Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms)**
- The most common form of human genetic variation
- Almost always **biallelic**
- Millions exist across the genome (not "less than 100")
- Majority lie **outside coding regions** (not "99% in coding regions")
- Used clinically for disease-risk stratification

**CNVs (Copy Number Variations)**
- Large contiguous stretches of DNA that vary in copy number between individuals
- Responsible for millions of base pairs of difference between any two people
- **DO involve coding sequences/genes** — a common false-statement trap says otherwise
- Immune system and nervous system genes disproportionately affected
- Can be biallelic or complex (multiple alleles)

**Epigenetics**
- Modulation of gene or protein expression **without any change to the underlying DNA sequence** — via DNA methylation, histone modification
- Not the same as a silent mutation (that's DNA-sequence-level)
- Becomes critical later in genomic imprinting (Prader-Willi, Angelman)

## Lysosomal Storage Diseases — A High-Yield Cluster

| Disease | Enzyme deficiency | Substance accumulated |
|---|---|---|
| **Tay-Sachs disease** | Hexosaminidase A | GM2 ganglioside |
| **Niemann-Pick A/B** | Sphingomyelinase | Sphingomyelin |
| **Niemann-Pick C** | Defect in intracellular cholesterol transport | Cholesterol and other lipids |
| **Gaucher disease** | Glucocerebrosidase | Glucocerebroside (NOT mucopolysaccharides) |
| **Mucopolysaccharidoses (MPS)** | Various lysosomal enzymes | Mucopolysaccharides (glycosaminoglycans) |

All lysosomal storage diseases are **autosomal recessive**. The single most common trap: confusing Gaucher disease's storage product with mucopolysaccharides — Gaucher = glucocerebroside, MPS = mucopolysaccharides.

## Complex (Multigenic) Disorders

- Caused by interaction of **multiple variant genes AND environment** together — neither alone is sufficient
- Environment **strongly** modifies phenotype expression
- Occur when many polymorphisms are co-inherited
- Some disease polymorphisms are shared across diseases; others are disease-specific
- Even **normal** phenotypic traits (height, skin colour, intelligence) are multigenic

## Single-Gene Disorders of Large Effect — General Properties

- Individually rare, but collectively account for roughly **1% of adult hospital admissions**
- Follow Mendelian inheritance patterns
- A single mutated gene can cause **multiple phenotypic effects** (pleiotropy)
- Different protein classes produce different severities when defective:
 - Enzyme defects → often mild if partial function remains (AR pattern)
 - Receptor defects → moderate, dose-dependent effects
 - Structural protein defects → often the most dramatic phenotypes (AD pattern)
 - These do **NOT** all present with similar magnitude of dysfunction

## Chromosomal Disorders — The Basics

**What counts as a chromosomal disorder**
- May result from **numerical** alterations (too many/too few chromosomes) or **structural** alterations (rearranged, deleted, duplicated segments)
- May affect **autosomes or sex chromosomes**
- Usually **NOT** inherited in a family pattern — most arise **de novo** during gametogenesis

**General rules**
- Loss of chromosomal material is generally **worse** than gain
- Sex chromosome imbalances are tolerated far **better** than autosomal imbalances
- Sex chromosome disorders often produce subtle abnormalities, sometimes undetected at birth
- Virtually all chromosomal syndromes have some congenital malformation component

**Numerical terminology**
- **Euploid** = any exact multiple of the haploid number (n)
- **Polyploid** = 3n, 4n, etc. — generally results in spontaneous abortion
- **Aneuploid** = any number NOT an exact multiple of n (trisomy = 2n+1, monosomy = 2n−1)

## Karyotyping and Cytogenetic Notation

**Karyotyping**
- Photographic representation of a stained metaphase spread, arranged by decreasing length
- G-banding produces alternating light/dark bands, allowing mapping of loci and identification of **large** structural abnormalities
- **Critical limitation:** resolution is in the megabase range — **cannot** detect point mutations

**Reading ISCN shorthand**
Example: **46,XX,t(2;5)(q31;p14)**
- 46,XX = total chromosome number and sex chromosomes
- t(2;5) = reciprocal translocation between chromosomes 2 and 5
- (q31;p14) = breakpoints listed in the **same order** as chromosomes named: chr2 breaks at **q31** (long arm), chr5 breaks at **p14** (short arm)
- Memory rule: **p = petit = short arm; q = the other one = long arm**

## The Autosomal Trisomies

| Syndrome | Chromosome | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| **Patau syndrome** | Trisomy 13 | Severe malformations, life expectancy usually <1 year |
| **Edward syndrome** | Trisomy 18 | Severe malformations, life expectancy usually <1 year |
| **Down syndrome** | Trisomy 21 | Milder phenotype, life expectancy often 50–60 years |

**Comparing Patau and Down syndrome**
- Similar: both trisomies, both cytogenetic disorders, both associated with increased maternal age, both associated with congenital malformations
- **Different: life expectancy** — Patau/Edward are drastically shorter-lived than Down syndrome. Any option claiming similar life expectancy (~40 years) in both is false.

**Robertsonian translocation trap (21q21q)**
If a mother carries a 21q21q Robertsonian translocation, every viable pregnancy results in Down syndrome — **100%**. Her gametes carry either both copies of 21 (→ trisomy 21) or neither (→ monosomy 21, non-viable). No "normal" outcome is possible.

## Structural Chromosomal Deletions

**Cri du chat syndrome**
- Partial deletion of the **short arm (p arm) of chromosome 5** (5p−)
- NOT a Robertsonian translocation, NOT a trisomy or monosomy

**22q11.2 deletion syndrome**
- Small interstitial deletion of the **long arm** of chromosome 22
- Includes **DiGeorge syndrome** and **velocardiofacial syndrome**
- Features: congenital heart disease, facial dysmorphism, thymic and parathyroid hypoplasia
- Diagnosed by FISH
- **Confers an INCREASED risk** of schizophrenia/bipolar disorder — one of the strongest known genetic risk factors for schizophrenia

## Trinucleotide Repeat Disorders

- **Fragile X syndrome** — CGG repeat expansion in FMR1. Clinical picture: large everted ears, long face, large mandible, macroorchidism, intellectual disability
- **Huntington's disease** — CAG repeat expansion
- **Myotonic dystrophy** — CTG repeat expansion
- **Spinal and bulbar muscular atrophy (Kennedy disease)** — CAG repeat expansion

Fraccaro syndrome (achondrogenesis type 1A) is **NOT** a trinucleotide repeat disorder — a common distractor placed among this list.

## Sex Chromosome Disorders

**Klinefelter syndrome**
- Karyotypes: 47,XXY (classic), 46,XY/47,XXY (mosaic), 47,XXY/48,XXXY (mosaic variant)
- 46,XY alone is simply a normal male karyotype
- Features: **tall stature** (not short), eunuchoid body habitus, gynecomastia, reduced facial/body/pubic hair, hypogonadism
- A coexisting normal 46,XY cell line in mosaic Klinefelter produces a **milder**, not more severe, phenotype

**Turner syndrome**
- Karyotype: 45,X (monosomy X)
- Features: short stature (below 3rd percentile), webbed neck, shield-like chest with widely spaced nipples, cubitus valgus, streak ovaries (not testicular atrophy — Turner affects females), primary amenorrhea, decreased estrogen, increased FSH/LH

**Extreme sex chromosome deviations**
- 49,XXXXY is compatible with life — sex chromosome aneuploidies are tolerated far better than autosomal ones
- 47,XYY individuals have essentially normal life expectancy
- **X-inactivation (lyonization)** explains tolerance of extra X's: only one X is active per cell. Lyonization occurs only on X, never autosomes.

**Disorders of sex development**
- **Androgen insensitivity syndrome:** 46,XY karyotype, tissues cannot respond to androgens → phenotypically female external appearance, primary amenorrhea, presents in adolescence. Classic vignette: "17-year-old phenotypic female, primary amenorrhea, XY karyotype."

## Genomic Imprinting

**The concept**
- Transcriptional silencing of one parent's copy of a gene (paternal or maternal, depending on the gene), established during gametogenesis
- Result: only ONE functional copy exists, even though two were inherited
- **Critical point:** loss of the one functional allele is NOT compensated — the imprinted (silenced) copy cannot rescue the loss

**The two classic imprinting diseases — same region, opposite parent of origin**

| Syndrome | Genetic defect | Clinical picture |
|---|---|---|
| **Prader-Willi syndrome** | Deletion of **paternal** 15q11-13 | Hyperphagia, obesity, hypotonia, intellectual disability |
| **Angelman syndrome** | Deletion of **maternal** 15q11-13 | Severe intellectual disability, seizures, ataxic/jerky gait, inappropriate laughter, fair complexion |

Memory anchor: fair complexion + severe mental retardation + seizures + ataxic gait + inappropriate laughter = **Angelman syndrome** (maternal deletion).

## Mitochondrial Inheritance

- Mitochondrial DNA is inherited **exclusively from the mother**
- **Fathers never transmit mitochondrial mutations to any child** — sons or daughters
- Only daughters of an affected woman can transmit the mutation onward
- Rare diseases; affect tissues with high energy demand: skeletal muscle, heart, brain
- **Leber hereditary optic neuropathy** is the prototypical mitochondrial disease

## Dysmorphology — Malformation, Deformation, Disruption, Sequence

Separate these four terms by **mechanism**:
- **Malformation** — primary error of morphogenesis; developmental process **intrinsically** abnormal from the start
- **Deformation** — a normally-formed structure physically distorted by **extrinsic mechanical forces** (e.g., oligohydramnios compression)
- **Disruption** — secondary destruction of a previously **normal** organ/region by an external event (e.g., amniotic bands). Disruptions are **not heritable**, no recurrence risk
- **Sequence** — a cascade of anomalies triggered by a single **primary defect**. Example: bilateral renal agenesis → oligohydramnios → pulmonary hypoplasia and limb deformities (Potter sequence)

Not all malformations are evident at birth; the majority overall have **unknown causes**.

## Teratogens and Congenital Anomaly Prevention

- Teratogen-sensitive window: roughly **weeks 3–9**, peaking at **weeks 4–5** (organogenesis)
- **Thalidomide** → classic cause of severe limb malformations
- **Maternal smoking** → linked to placental abnormalities and SIDS
- **Maternal alcohol** → prenatal and postnatal growth retardation (fetal alcohol syndrome)
- **Vitamin A:** both deficiency AND excess in early pregnancy are teratogenic — a U-shaped risk
- **Vaccination against viral teratogens** (e.g., rubella) is protective
- "Lack of morning sickness" is not a validated marker of teratogenesis — treat as a false/distractor statement

## Diagnostic Techniques

**What enabled the molecular diagnostics era**
Human genome sequencing plus public reference databases, tailor-made PCR kits, high-resolution DNA/RNA microarrays, and automated next-generation sequencing — together.

**FISH (Fluorescence In Situ Hybridization)**
- Fluorescent DNA probes specific to particular chromosomal regions
- Probes hybridize to metaphase spreads or interphase nuclei
- Requires a fluorescence microscope — **not** a cheap, ordinary-light-microscopy technique
- Resolution ~100 kilobases; requires knowing in advance what region to probe
- Uses: rapid diagnosis, detecting numeric chromosomal abnormalities, subtle microdeletions/complex translocations, gene amplification analysis
- Purely **diagnostic** — no role in genetic therapy

**Array-based Comparative Genomic Hybridization (array-CGH)**
- Test and reference DNA labelled with two different fluorescent dyes
- Amplifications → increased signal; deletions → decreased signal, relative to normal DNA
- **Key advantage over FISH:** detects abnormalities genome-wide **without needing prior knowledge** of what to look for

**PCR (Polymerase Chain Reaction)**
- Direct molecular detection method; exponential amplification of a target DNA sequence
- RNA starting material must first be reverse-transcribed into cDNA (RT-PCR)
- Requires the normal gene sequence known beforehand, to design primers
- **Does not involve microscopy** of any kind

**Indications for postnatal genetic testing**
Diagnosing a genetic disorder in a symptomatic child, recurrent miscarriage workup (ruling out parental balanced translocation), assessing genetic/mental illness risk in a child born to affected parents, resolving disputed paternity when a child's diagnosis is otherwise hard to pin down.
