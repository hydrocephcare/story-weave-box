# Percutaneous Transhepatic Cholangiography and Biliary Drainage in Obstructive Jaundice

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Hepatobiliary Medicine and Interventional Procedures**

> **In one line:** when bile flow from the liver to the duodenum is blocked, the biliary tree can be decompressed through the skin: a needle punctures a peripheral intrahepatic duct (PTC), contrast outlines the blockage, and a catheter or stent is then placed across or above it (PBD). It is used mainly when ERCP fails or cannot be done, or for high hilar blocks.

## What you must know

- **Obstructive (post-hepatic) jaundice** is caused by interrupted bile flow into the duodenum: gallstones in the common bile duct, benign strictures, pancreatic head cancer, cholangiocarcinoma, and nodal metastases.
- **ERCP is first line** for diagnosis and treatment; PTC/PBD is used when ERCP is **not feasible or fails**.
- **PTC** is the diagnostic step (contrast cholangiogram); today it is almost always the first step of a **therapeutic PBD**, because **MRCP** has replaced purely diagnostic PTC.
- Drainage types: **external, internal-external, internal (stent)**.
- **Contraindications:** uncorrected coagulopathy, massive ascites, no safe needle tract.
- **Main complications:** haemorrhage and haemobilia, biliary sepsis, bile leak, pneumothorax, catheter blockage or displacement.

## 1. Background

- Obstructive jaundice causes **conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia** with dark urine, pale stools and pruritus. Causes: **choledocholithiasis**, benign biliary strictures, **pancreatic head adenocarcinoma**, **cholangiocarcinoma**, and **metastatic lymphadenopathy** at the porta hepatis.
- **ERCP** is usually the preferred first-line method for both diagnosis and therapy. When it is not technically feasible or fails, **percutaneous transhepatic** options (PTC and PBD) take over.
- Imaging first: ultrasound shows dilated ducts, and **MRCP** maps the level and cause of obstruction without invasive access.

## 2. Indications and contraindications

### Indications

- **Failed or impossible ERCP:** altered anatomy (Roux-en-Y gastric bypass, Billroth II reconstruction, duodenal diverticula, severe gastric outlet obstruction) that stops the endoscope reaching the ampulla of Vater.
- **High biliary obstruction:** complex hilar lesions (for example **Bismuth-Corlette type III or IV cholangiocarcinoma**) where selective ERCP cannulation of intrahepatic ducts is difficult.
- **Symptom relief** in unresectable malignancy: severe **pruritus**, intractable jaundice, or **cholangitis**.
- **Preoperative biliary decompression** to lower bilirubin before major hepatobiliary surgery and reduce perioperative morbidity (routine use is controversial).

### Contraindications

- **Uncorrected coagulopathy:** raised INR, prolonged prothrombin time or severe thrombocytopenia greatly increase bleeding after liver puncture. Correct with vitamin K, plasma or platelets first.
- **Severe or massive ascites:** increases the distance to the liver capsule, hinders tract formation and carries a high risk of bile leaking into the peritoneum (drain the ascites first if the procedure must go ahead).
- **No safe needle tract:** intervening lung, bowel or tumour.

## 3. Percutaneous transhepatic cholangiography (PTC)

PTC is an image-guided technique to **visualise the intrahepatic and extrahepatic biliary tree**.

1. **Procedure:** under local anaesthesia and moderate sedation, with ultrasound or fluoroscopic guidance, a fine needle (for example a **Chiba needle**) is passed through the liver into a peripheral intrahepatic bile duct. Prophylactic antibiotics are given beforehand.
2. **Contrast:** injected slowly at **low pressure** to map the anatomy, show the site and extent of the block, and plan treatment.
3. **Transition to drainage:** purely diagnostic PTC has largely been replaced by **MRCP**. PTC is now almost always done as the first step of a therapeutic PBD.

## 4. Percutaneous biliary drainage (PBD)

Once access is secured, a drainage catheter is placed over a guidewire, past or above the obstruction.

| Type | Description | When used |
|---|---|---|
| **External drainage** | Side-hole catheter above the block; bile drains to an external bag | Temporary emergency measure in **acute suppurative cholangitis**, or when the block cannot be crossed |
| **Internal-external drainage** | Catheter crosses the stricture with side holes above and below it; bile flows into the gut or out to a bag if left open | Preserves **enterohepatic circulation** and fluid balance |
| **Internal drainage (stenting)** | **Self-expanding metal stents (SEMS)** or plastic stents placed across the stricture; the external drain is capped and then removed | Long-term palliation or definitive internal patency |

## 5. ERCP compared with PTC/PBD

| Feature | ERCP | PTC / PBD |
|---|---|---|
| Route | Endoscopic (through the mouth) | Percutaneous (transhepatic) |
| Preferred for | Distal biliary obstruction, bile duct stones | High or hilar obstruction, altered GI anatomy |
| Sedation | Conscious sedation or general anaesthesia | Local anaesthesia with moderate sedation |
| Main risks | Post-ERCP pancreatitis, duodenal perforation | Bleeding and haemobilia, bile leak, pneumothorax |
| Ascites | Minimal effect | Major contraindication |

> **Exam tip:** distal block or stones means ERCP first; hilar tumour or surgically altered anatomy means PTC/PBD.

## 6. Complications

- **Haemorrhage and haemobilia:** injury to intrahepatic portal or arterial branches along the needle track; bleeding into the peritoneum or into the biliary tree.
- **Biliary sepsis:** over-injection of contrast under high pressure into an infected, obstructed system can push bacteria into the bloodstream. Give antibiotics and avoid over-distending the ducts.
- **Bile leak and peritonitis:** bile leaking around the catheter tract, especially if the catheter slips out.
- **Pneumothorax or pleural effusion (biliothorax):** high intercostal routes can cross the pleura.
- **Catheter blockage or dislodgement:** needs regular flushing, care, and periodic upsizing or replacement.

## Key points to revise

1. PTC/PBD is used mainly when ERCP fails or is impossible, or for high hilar obstruction.
2. PTC is the diagnostic step (needle into an intrahepatic duct, low-pressure contrast); it is now mostly the first step of PBD because MRCP does the diagnosis.
3. PBD: external, internal-external, or internal (stent) drainage.
4. Internal drainage preserves enterohepatic circulation and avoids electrolyte loss and malabsorption.
5. Contraindications: uncorrected coagulopathy and severe ascites (and no safe tract).
6. Complications: haemorrhage and haemobilia, sepsis, bile leak, pneumothorax, catheter problems.
