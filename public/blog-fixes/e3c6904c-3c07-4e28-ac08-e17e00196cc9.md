## Q1 — FACIAL NERVE LESIONS**a) UMN vs LMN Lesion — Clinical Difference**
UMN lesion: contralateral lower face weakness; forehead spared (bilateral cortical input to upper face nucleus).
LMN lesion: ipsilateral complete facial paralysis — upper and lower face, including forehead.
**b) Anatomical Basis**
The facial motor nucleus lies in the pons. Its upper division (forehead) receives bilateral corticobulbar input; its lower division receives only contralateral input. A UMN lesion (e.g. stroke) destroys one corticobulbar tract — the lower face loses its only cortical input contralaterally, but the forehead remains functional via the intact opposite tract. An LMN lesion (e.g. Bell's palsy) destroys the nerve distal to the nucleus, cutting off all ipsilateral motor supply — full ipsilateral paralysis results.
**c) Posterior Column Sensory Modalities**
- Fine (discriminative) touch
- Proprioception (position sense)

## Q5 — MEDIAN NERVE**A. Course**

At elbow (e.g. supracondylar fracture): loss of pronation, weak wrist flexion with ulnar deviation, loss of lateral finger and thumb flexion, hand of benediction on attempted fist.

AION injury: inability to make OK sign (weak flexor pollicis longus and lateral FDP).

## Q14 — PLACENTA FORMATIONThe placenta is a temporary vascular organ formed from fetal (chorion frondosum) and maternal (decidua basalis) components. Functions: gaseous exchange, nutrient/waste transfer, hormone production (hCG, progesterone, oestrogen, hPL), and passive IgG transfer.
**Fetal Component**
Chorionic membrane: extraembryonic mesoderm + cytotrophoblast + syncytiotrophoblast.
Villous development:
- Primary villi (day 13): cytotrophoblast core covered by syncytiotrophoblast
- Secondary villi (day 16): mesodermal core invades
- Tertiary villi (day 21): fetal capillaries form → fetoplacental circulation established
-

Chorion frondosum: villi adjacent to decidua basalis proliferate. Chorion laeve: other villi degenerate.
Umbilical cord: two umbilical arteries (deoxygenated blood to placenta) + one umbilical vein (oxygenated blood to fetus) in Wharton's jelly.
**Maternal Component**
Decidua basalis: endometrium beneath the embryo; contains spiral arteries supplying intervillous spaces.
Intervillous spaces: lacunae within syncytiotrophoblast filled with maternal blood; fetal villi float here for exchange.
**Placental Barrier (early)**
Syncytiotrophoblast → cytotrophoblast → trophoblast basement membrane → villus mesenchyme → fetal capillary endothelium. Becomes progressively thinner with advancing gestation.

## Q20 — CLINICAL NEUROANATOMY CASE (MS)Case: 20-year-old female; diplopia, photophobia, progressive ataxia, medially fixed left eye, nystagmus, left facial droop, urinary frequency → Multiple Sclerosis (MS).
**a) Diplopia:** Internuclear ophthalmoplegia (INO) due to demyelinating lesion of the medial longitudinal fasciculus (MLF), disrupting coordination between CN VI and CN III nuclei → impaired horizontal conjugate gaze.
**b) Ataxia:** Lesion in cerebellar hemispheres/vermis or cerebellar peduncles (middle/inferior) or disruption of spinocerebellar or dorsal column pathways.
**c) Medially fixed left eye:** Left abducens (CN VI) palsy → left lateral rectus cannot abduct → unopposed medial rectus (CN III) pulls eye medially. CN VI nucleus in pons is vulnerable to pontine demyelination.
**d) Nystagmus:** Lesion in vestibular nuclei (pons/medulla), cerebellum (flocculonodular lobe), or MLF. INO-related nystagmus occurs in the abducting eye during attempted horizontal gaze.
**e) Facial palsy:** Left-sided complete facial weakness (including forehead) = LMN lesion of CN VII. Site: facial nucleus or nerve fascicle in the pons — commonly demyelinated in MS.
**f) Urinary frequency:** Neurogenic bladder from loss of supraspinal inhibition of the sacral micturition reflex. Likely lesion: pontine micturition centre (Barrington's nucleus) or descending spinal pathways → detrusor overactivity.
**g) Lesion localisation:** Left dorsal pons and adjacent structures.
Reasons: left CN VI palsy (left pontine tegmentum), left LMN CN VII lesion (left pontine facial nucleus), INO from MLF demyelination, ataxia from middle cerebellar peduncle disruption, urinary frequency from pontine micturition centre or descending spinal tract involvement.

## Q21 — TRACHEOESOPHAGEAL FISTULA**A. Likely Type**
Gross Type C (Oesophageal atresia with distal TEF) — accounts for ~85–90% of cases.
Evidence: NG tube coiling in upper oesophageal pouch (blind-ending proximal oesophagus); choking and cyanosis from overflow; air in stomach on X-ray (via distal fistula).
Type A (pure oesophageal atresia): coiling without gastric air; no fistula.
Type E (H-type): oesophagus patent → NG tube should pass; coiling would not be expected here.
**B. Embryological Basis**
Week 4–5: respiratory diverticulum (lung bud) arises from ventral foregut. Tracheoesophageal septum normally divides foregut → dorsal oesophagus and ventral trachea/lungs.
In TEF/EA: abnormal partitioning → oesophageal atresia (failed elongation/recanalisation of dorsal foregut) + persistent fistula (incomplete closure of tracheoesophageal septum) → blind upper oesophagus with distal tracheal communication.

- Outer plexiform layer: synapses between photoreceptors, bipolar cells, and horizontal cells
- Inner nuclear layer: bipolar, horizontal, amacrine, and Müller glial cells
- Inner plexiform layer: synapses between bipolar and ganglion cells; amacrine cell processes
- Ganglion cell layer: output neurons of the retina
- Nerve fibre layer: ganglion cell axons → converge to form optic nerve
- Internal limiting membrane: basal lamina of Müller cells
-

**b) Pancoast Tumour — Clinical Presentations and Anatomical Basis**
- Lower brachial plexus (C8–T1): pain radiating down medial arm and forearm, hand weakness and wasting, medial forearm and hand sensory loss
- Sympathetic chain (stellate ganglion): Horner's syndrome — ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis, enophthalmos
- Rib and vertebral invasion: localised chest wall/scapular/shoulder pain
- Recurrent laryngeal nerve (left side): hoarseness from vocal cord paralysis
- Subclavian vein compression: upper limb oedema, distended neck veins
- Phrenic nerve invasion: ipsilateral diaphragmatic paralysis → dyspnoea
- Spinal cord invasion (severe): paraparesis, bladder dysfunction

## Q23 — KIDNEY, HEART, SPLEEN & DEVELOPMENTAL ANATOMY**1. Kidney Relations**
Right kidney — anterior: liver, 2nd part duodenum, right colic flexure. Posterior: 12th rib, psoas major.
Left kidney — anterior: stomach, spleen, pancreas tail. Posterior: 11th and 12th ribs, quadratus lumborum.
**2. Autonomic Innervation of the Heart**
Sympathetic (T1–T5 via sympathetic chain): positive chronotropy, positive inotropy, mild coronary dilation.
Parasympathetic (vagus CN X): negative chronotropy, decreased atrial contractility, minor coronary constriction.
**3. Spleen Relations**
- Anterior: stomach (gastrosplenic ligament)
- Medial: left kidney (lienorenal ligament)
- Inferior: splenic flexure of colon
- Superior/posterior: diaphragm
-

**4. Jejunum vs Ileum** — see Q3a.
**5. Inguinal Canal Boundaries** — see Q3b.
**6. Spina Bifida**
Occulta vs cystica: occulta — vertebral arch fails to fuse, no spinal content protrusion, skin intact; cystica — protrusion of contents through spinal defect.
Cystica types: meningocele (meninges + CSF only); myelomeningocele (meninges + CSF + spinal cord).
**7. Hydrocephalus**
Communicating: CSF flows freely through ventricles but absorption is impaired (arachnoid granulations).
Non-communicating: intraventricular obstruction (e.g. aqueductal stenosis).
Ex-vacuo causes: Alzheimer's disease (cortical atrophy); cerebral infarction or trauma (tissue loss → ventricular dilation).
**8. Prosencephalon Anomalies**
Hydranencephaly vs holoprosencephaly: hydranencephaly — near-complete absence of cerebral hemispheres replaced by fluid (brainstem preserved); holoprosencephaly — failure of forebrain to divide into two hemispheres.
External features of holoprosencephaly: cyclopia (single midline eye); cleft lip/palate or hypotelorism.
**9. Posterior Fossa Anomalies**
Dandy–Walker: cystic 4th ventricle dilatation, absent/underdeveloped cerebellar vermis, enlarged posterior fossa.
Arnold–Chiari II: cerebellar tonsil and brainstem herniation through foramen magnum; associated with myelomeningocele.
Chiari II features: downward displacement of cerebellum and medulla; hydrocephalus.
**10. CSF Flow and Reabsorption**
Pathway: lateral ventricles → foramina of Monro → third ventricle → cerebral aqueduct → fourth ventricle → foramina of Magendie and Luschka → subarachnoid space.
Reabsorption: arachnoid villi/granulations in the superior sagittal sinus.
**11. Prosencephalon Derivatives**
- Telencephalon → cerebral hemispheres, basal ganglia, lateral ventricles
- Diencephalon → thalamus, hypothalamus, epithalamus, third ventricle, optic structures
-

**12. Mantle and Marginal Layer Derivatives (Spinal Cord)**
- Mantle layer → grey matter (neuronal cell bodies)
- Marginal layer → white matter (axonal tracts)
-

**13. Teratogens in Neural Tube Defects**
- Valproic acid
- Folate deficiency (nutritional or drug-induced, e.g. methotrexate)

## Q24 — HISTOLOGY AND CELL BIOLOGY**1. Kidney Cell Types and Functions**
- Podocytes: foot processes form filtration slits; selective glomerular filtration
- Mesangial cells: structural support, phagocytosis, regulate GFR
- Proximal tubule cells: tall cuboidal with brush border; reabsorption of glucose, amino acids, water, electrolytes
- Distal tubule cells: cuboidal, no brush border; sodium and water reabsorption regulated by aldosterone
- Juxtaglomerular (JG) cells: modified afferent arteriolar smooth muscle; secrete renin → RAAS activation

Components: tight junctions between Sertoli cells; seminiferous tubule basement membrane; Sertoli cell cytoplasm.

|---|---|---|
