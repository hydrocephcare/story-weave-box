## PART ONE: MYCOLOGY
## 1. True Pathogenic vs Opportunistic Mycoses
A comparison of the two categories of fungal disease based on virulence, host response, and organism behavior.
| Property | True Pathogenic Infections | Opportunistic Infections |
|---|---|---|
| Degree of virulence | Well developed | Limited |
| Host condition | Resistance high or low | Resistance low |
| Primary portal of entry | Respiratory | Respiratory, mucocutaneous |
| Nature of infection | Usually primary, pulmonary and systemic; usually asymptomatic | Varies from superficial skin to pulmonary and systemic; usually symptomatic |
| Nature of immunity | Well developed, specific | Weak, short-lived |
| Infecting form | Primarily conidia | Conidia or mycelia |
| Thermal dimorphism | Shows strongly | Not usually |
| Habitat of fungus | Soil | Varies from soil to flora of humans and animals |
| Geographical location | Restricted to endemic regions | Distributed worldwide |
| Example organisms | *Histoplasma capsulatum*, *Blastomyces dermatitidis* | *Candida*, *Aspergillus*, *Pneumocystis jirovecii* |

## 2. Common Name vs Clinical Name of Disease
Several fungal diseases are better known by informal or regional names than by their clinical terms.
- Darling's disease → Histoplasmosis
- Valley fever → Coccidioidomycosis
- South American/Brazilian blastomycosis → Paracoccidioidomycosis
- Mycotic mycetoma → Pseudoallescheriasis
- Balanitis → Candidiasis of the penis

## 3. Histoplasmosis (Darling's Disease)
A dimorphic fungal infection named after the physician who first described it.
**Background**
- First described by Samuel Darling
- "Histio" = within histiocytes
- "Plasma" = resembled *Plasmodium*
**Causative agent**
- *Histoplasma capsulatum*
- Small dimorphic fungus
- Facultative intracellular yeast
- Localizes in phagocytic cells, including circulating monocytes
- Despite the name, it has no capsule
- Filamentous form found in soil enriched with bat or bird guano
- Isolated worldwide; endemic in the USA
**Pathogenesis**
- Microconidia inhaled into lungs
- Microconidia convert to yeast form
- Neutrophils, macrophages, lymphocytes, and NK cells respond
- Organisms confined mainly to macrophages
- Less commonly seen in tissue spaces
- Occasionally seen in peripheral WBCs in disseminated infection
**Clinical manifestations**
- Acute pulmonary — self-limited; dry cough, fever, fatigue
- Chronic pulmonary — fatigue, fever, night sweats, chronic cough, weight loss
- Disseminated — common in children under 2 years and adolescents
- HIV-positive patients — higher risk of lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, skin and mucous membrane lesions
**Laboratory diagnosis**
- Microscopy of thick blood smear, bone marrow, or liver biopsy for intracellular yeast
- Serology (complement fixation) for antibodies/antigens
- Skin testing with histoplasmin
- Blood test for antibodies
- PCR for antigen detection in blood
- Bacterial culture to rule out other organisms
- Chest X-ray
- CT scan of chest
- Histopathology and cytology with staining
**Treatment**
- Oral itraconazole 200 mg/day for 6–12 weeks
- Amphotericin B 0.7–1.0 mg/kg daily

## 4. Cryptococcosis (Disease Associated with Pigeon Feces)
An opportunistic yeast infection strongly linked to bird droppings and immunocompromise.
**Causative agent**
- *Cryptococcus neoformans*
**Occurrence**
- Worldwide fungus, mostly in decaying matter
- Yeast with an antigenic polysaccharide capsule
- Isolated from fruits, milk, vegetation, soil, and pigeon feces
- Opportunistic fungus
**Pathogenesis**
- Common in AIDS/immunocompromised patients
- Acquired mainly by inhalation
- Occasionally acquired through skin or mucosa
- Most infections asymptomatic
- Pulmonary form may cause mild pneumonitis
- Disseminated infection leads to visceral, cutaneous, and meningeal disease
- Visceral form mimics tuberculosis and cancer
- Cryptococcal meningitis is the most serious form; can resemble tuberculous meningitis
**Clinical manifestations**
- Fever and headache
- Nausea, vomiting, malaise
**Laboratory diagnosis**
- Cryptococcal antigen latex particle agglutination test on CSF
- India ink or nigrosine wet mount — shows encapsulated yeast
- Confirmation by culture of CSF
**Treatment**
- Fluconazole 400 mg/day for 8–10 weeks
- Flucytosine 100 mg/kg/day for 2 weeks

## 5. Risk Factors for Fungal Infections
Conditions and practices that increase susceptibility to fungal disease.
- Humidity
- Immunosuppression
- Poor hygiene
- Cytotoxic chemotherapy
- Organ and bone marrow transplant
- Indwelling IV catheters
- Broad-spectrum antimicrobial use

## 6. Virulence Factors Used by Fungi
How different structural and physiological traits help fungi establish disease.
| Virulence Factor | Function | Associated Pathogens |
|---|---|---|
| Cell wall glycoproteins | Adherence to epithelial surfaces | *Candida* species |
| Melanin pigment | Shields against immune cells, hydrolyses tissue | *Cr. neoformans*, *W. dermatitidis* |
| Capsules, glucans | Anti-phagocytic | *Cr. neoformans* |
| Thermotolerance | Survive and replicate at 37°C | Human pathogens generally |
| Resistance to microbicidal products (e.g. H2O2) | Evasion of host defense by tissue phase (yeast/spherule) of virulent dimorphic fungi | *Blastomyces*, *Coccidioides*, *Histoplasma*, *Paracoccidioides*, *Sporothrix schenckii* |
| Epithelial cell and monocyte cytotoxic activity | Evasion of host defenses | *Candida albicans*, *Candida tropicalis* |

## 7. Eumycetoma vs Actinomycetoma
Comparing the two forms of mycetoma by cause, presentation, and treatment.
| Feature | Eumycetoma | Actinomycetoma |
|---|---|---|
| Causative agents | Fungi | Bacteria |
| Lesion characteristics | Well-encapsulated, clear margin | Diffuse, no clear margin |
| Sinuses | Few | Many |
| Colour of grains | Mostly white or black | Various colours, but not black |
| Course of infection | Slowly progressive | Inflammatory and rapid |
| Bone invasion | After a long time | Rapid |
| Cavities on radiograph | Few, large, clear margins | Numerous, small, unclear margins (except *Actinomadura madurae*) |
| Drug of choice | Ketoconazole, Itraconazole | Dapsone + streptomycin; rifampicin or sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine; amikacin + co-trimoxazole |
| Management | Both medical and surgical intervention required | Medical treatment alone usually sufficient |
| Surgery alone | May cure small, well-encapsulated lesions; up to 90% recurrence | Not indicated |
| Medical treatment alone | Partial cure or improvement | Useful in most cases |

## 8. Mycetoma — General
**Introduction**
- Chronic skin infection caused by bacteria (actinomycetoma) or fungi (eumycetoma)
- Classic triad: painless firm skin lumps, weeping sinuses, discharge containing grains
- 80% occur in the feet
- Most eumycetoma caused by *Madurella mycetomatis*
- Most actinomycetoma caused by *Nocardia brasiliensis*, *Streptomyces somaliensis*, *Actinomadura madurae*, *Actinomadura pelletieri*
- Associated with a weakened immune system
**Risk factors**
- Causative organisms live in soil and water
- Enter through wounds or small skin injuries (e.g. thorn pricks)
- Risk reduced by disinfecting wounds and wearing shoes
**Pathogenesis**
- Caused by saprotrophs in soil/thorny shrubs in semi-desert climates
- Localized skin trauma initiates infection (e.g. stepping on a splinter)
- First sign: painless subcutaneous swelling
- Over years, progresses to a nodule (lump)
- Massive swelling and hardening of the area
- Skin rupture and formation of sinus tracts
- Sinus tracts discharge pus and grains containing organisms
- Underlying bone frequently affected
- Some patients painless, others report itching or pain
**Laboratory diagnosis**
- Specimen: tissue biopsy, excised sinus, or serosanguinous fluid containing granules
- Macroscopic exam: yellow granules indicate actinomycetes; black/white granules indicate fungi
- Microscopic exam: crushed granules using 10% KOH and calcofluor white mounts
- Tissue sections stained with H&E
- True septate hyphae seen in eumycotic type
- Culture: granules cultured on SDA at room temperature to isolate molds

## 9. Systemic Mycoses — Characteristics
Core features shared by fungal infections of internal organs.
- Fungal infection of internal organs
- Primarily involves the respiratory system
- Infection occurs by inhalation of airborne conidia
- More than 95% are self-limiting and asymptomatic
- The rest become symptomatic and disseminate via the hematogenous route
- Caused by dimorphic fungi that infect healthy, immunocompetent individuals
- Systemic infections in immunocompromised patients are called opportunistic mycotic infections

## 10. Paracoccidioidomycosis — Diagnosis
- Usually diagnosed by examining sputum or pus from infected individuals
- Microscopy identifies *Paracoccidioides brasiliensis*
- May also use tissue biopsy from lungs, skin, and/or lymph nodes
- Confirmed by culturing infected tissue and testing positive for *P. brasiliensis*
- Blood tests useful but cannot distinguish active from past infection
- Chest X-ray may show patchy areas of fungal infiltration

## 11. Fungi Classified by Property
Grouping key medically important fungi by shared biological behavior.
**Multiply within macrophages**
- *Candida albicans*, *Cryptococcus neoformans*, *Aspergillus fumigatus*
**Produce melanin**
- *Paracoccidioides brasiliensis* (and related melanized fungi)
**Affect CNS**
- *Cryptococcus neoformans*, *Histoplasma capsulatum*
**Basidiomycete etiology**
- *Cryptococcus neoformans*
**Form yeast-like cells**
- *Candida albicans*, *Cryptococcus neoformans*

## 12. Similarities Between Pairs
- Flucytosine and fluconazole — both used to treat cryptococcosis
- *Sporothrix schenckii* and *Blastomyces dermatitidis* — both cause subcutaneous mycoses
- Epidermophyton and Microsporum — both affect skin, both produce macroconidia, both cause cutaneous mycoses

## 13. Identify the Fungal Infection
- Produces sclerotic bodies → *Phialophora verrucosa*
- Characterized by erythema nodosum → associated with systemic mycoses (notably coccidioidomycosis)
- Associated with pigeon droppings → *Cryptococcus neoformans*

## 14. Dermatophyte Conidial Shapes
Distinguishing dermatophyte genera by the shape of their macroconidia.
- Trichophyton → pencil-shaped macroconidia
- Microsporum → spindle-shaped macroconidia
- Epidermophyton → club-shaped macroconidia
**Epidermophyton on SDA**
- Slow-growing colonies
- Greenish-brown or khaki colour
- Suede-like surface
- Raised and folded in the centre
- Flat periphery with submerged fringe of growth
**Microsporum**
- Thick-walled
- Spindle-shaped
- Multicellular conidia

## 15. Tinea Nigra Palmaris
**Etiology**
- *Hortaea (Phaeoannellomyces) werneckii*
- Found in soil, compost, humus, and wood
- Occurs in humid tropical/subtropical regions
- Transmitted via injury
**Clinical manifestations**
- Lesions non-inflammatory and non-scaling
- Brown to black macules on the palmar aspect of the hands

## 16. Tinea Versicolor
**Overview**
- Also called dermatomycosis furfuracea, pityriasis versicolor, tinea flava
- Skin eruption on trunk and proximal extremities
- Mostly caused by *Malassezia globosa*
- *Malassezia furfur* responsible for a minority of cases
- Fungal infection of the stratum corneum
- Presents as hypo- or hyperpigmented skin patches
- Commonly affects back, underarms, upper arms, chest, lower legs, neck
- Occasionally affects the face
**Diagnosis**
- KOH mount of skin scales shows short, curved, septate hyphae with budding yeast cells ("spaghetti and meatballs" appearance)
- Culture media supplemented with fatty acids
- Fluoresces yellow under Wood's lamp
**Treatment**
- Topical imidazole

## 17. Mode of Action of Antifungals
Antifungal drug classes grouped by their mechanism of action.
- Fungal cell wall synthesis inhibition — Caspofungin
- Bind fungal cell membrane ergosterol — Amphotericin B, Nystatin
- Inhibit ergosterol + lanosterol synthesis — Terbinafine, Naftifine, Butenafine
- Inhibit ergosterol synthesis — Azoles
- Inhibit nucleic acid synthesis — 5-Flucytosine
- Disrupt mitotic spindle / inhibit fungal mitosis — Griseofulvin
- Miscellaneous — Ciclopirox, Tolnaftate, Haloprogin, Undecylenic acid, Topical azoles

## 18. Classes of Fungi Based on Reproduction
**Asexual spores (formed by mitosis)**
- Conidia/conidiospores — small single-celled microconidia or large multicellular macroconidia at hyphal tips
- Sporangiospores — single-celled, formed within a sporangium on a sporangiophore
- Arthrospores — single-celled, formed from fragmented hyphae
- Chlamydospores — single-celled, thick-walled
- Blastoconidia (blastospores) — formed via budding (e.g. yeasts)
**Sexual spores (formed less frequently)**
- Ascospores — single-celled, formed in a sac called an ascus (4–8 per ascus)
- Basidiospores — single-celled, formed in a club-shaped basidium (four meiospores)
- Zygospores — large, thick-walled spores formed when two sexually compatible hyphal tips fuse
**Terminology**
- Fungi with known sexual stages → "perfect fungi"
- Fungi without known sexual stages → "imperfect fungi"

## 19. Candidiasis
**Causative agent**
- *Candida albicans*
**Pathogenesis**
- Normal commensal inhabitant of human/animal mucosa
- Candida infections are therefore endogenous
- Develops when immunity is compromised, especially cellular immunity
- First step: epithelial colonization
- Colonization depends on adherence to epithelial cells and proteins
- Adherence allows withstanding fluid forces that expel particulates
- Invasion involves penetration and damage of the host cell envelope
- Transmigration likely mediated by physical and/or enzymatic processes
- Phospholipids and proteins are major components of the host cell membrane
- Phospholipases cleave phospholipids, inducing cell lysis
- Cell lysis facilitates tissue invasion
**Clinical manifestations**
- Oral candidiasis — sore, burning tongue, dysphagia
- Mucocutaneous candidiasis
- Vulvovaginal candidiasis
- Intestinal candidiasis
- Esophageal candidiasis
**Treatment**
- Nystatin
- Clotrimazole
- Amphotericin B
- Fluconazole
- Itraconazole

## 20. Types of Dermatophytoses
**Acute/inflammatory type**
- Associated with cell-mediated immunity (CMI) to the fungus
- Generally heals spontaneously or responds well to treatment
**Chronic/non-inflammatory type**
- Associated with failure to express CMI at the infection site
- Relapsing
- Responds poorly to treatment

## PART TWO: VIROLOGY
## 21. How Vaccines Work Against Viral Infection
- Work with the body's natural defenses to safely develop immunity
- Vaccine is introduced into the body, often by injection
- Minor symptoms (e.g. fever) after vaccination are normal and expected
- WBCs are triggered to produce specific antibodies
- The immune system remembers the germ
- On re-exposure, antibodies eliminate it before illness develops

## 22. Herpes Simplex Virus — Portal of Entry
- Skin
- Mucosal surfaces of oral cavity, genitals, eyes

## 24. Interferon — Mechanism
- Interferons are cytokines involved in intercellular signaling
- Secreted by cells in response to viral or foreign stimulation
- Do not directly inhibit viral replication
- Instead stimulate infected and nearby cells to produce protective proteins
- These proteins prevent the virus from replicating within cells
- Further viral production is inhibited, stemming the infection
- Also have immunoregulatory functions:
  - Inhibit B-lymphocyte (B-cell) activation
  - Enhance T-lymphocyte (T-cell) activity
  - Increase NK cell cytotoxic capability

## 25. Prions
- A prion is a proteinaceous infectious particle requiring no coding nucleic acid
- Causes fatal neurodegenerative disorders in humans and animals
- Two isoforms exist:
  - Pathological PrPSc — beta-pleated sheet conformation
  - Non-pathological cellular PrPC — alpha-helical conformation
- Key pathogenic mechanism: conversion of the alpha-helical form to the pathological beta-sheet form
**Human prion diseases**
- Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease
- Kuru
**Animal prion diseases**
- Scrapie (sheep)
- Bovine spongiform encephalopathy (cattle)
- Chronic wasting disease (deer)

## 26. Viral Encephalitis — Pathogenesis
- Inflammation of the brain parenchyma caused by a virus
- Viruses first infect and replicate outside the CNS
- Most reach the CNS via the circulatory system
- A minority travel from nerve endings toward the CNS
- Once in the brain, viral effects and host inflammatory response disrupt neural function
- Leads to illness and complications, often neurological (impaired motor skills, altered behavior)
**Viruses causing encephalitis**
- Rabies virus
- Poliovirus
- Zika virus
- Rubella virus
- Measles virus
- Mumps virus
- Herpes simplex virus
- Varicella zoster virus

## 27. Cytomegalovirus (CMV)
**Transmission**
- Person-to-person contact (kissing, sexual contact, saliva/urine on hands then touching eyes, nose, or mouth)
- Breast milk of an infected woman
- Transplacental (infected pregnant women to unborn babies)
- Blood transfusions and organ transplantation
**Congenital manifestations — temporary symptoms**
- Liver problems
- Spleen problems
- Jaundice (yellow skin and eyes)
- Purple skin splotches
- Lung problems
- Small size at birth
- Seizures
**Congenital manifestations — permanent symptoms/disabilities**
- Hearing loss
- Vision loss
- Mental disability
- Small head
- Lack of coordination
- Seizures
- Death
**Organ-specific manifestations**
- CNS: microcephaly, mental retardation, spasticity, epilepsy, periventricular calcification
- Eye: chorioretinitis, optic atrophy
- Ear: sensorineural deafness
- Liver: hepatosplenomegaly, jaundice (due to hepatitis)
- Lung: pneumonitis
- Heart: myocarditis
- Blood: thrombocytopenic purpura, hemolytic anemia
- Late sequelae in infants asymptomatic at birth: hearing defects, reduced intelligence
**Laboratory diagnosis**
- Direct detection: biopsy for CMV inclusions/antigens (sensitivity may be low)
- Virus isolation: conventional cell culture — gold standard, but takes up to 4 weeks
- Serology: IgG indicates past infection; IgM indicates primary infection (may also appear in reactivation)
- PCR: more sensitive than shell vial or antigenemia assays
- Other tests: complement fixation, fluorescence assays (pp65 antigenemia, IFA, ACIF), indirect hemagglutination
**Prevention**
- No licensed vaccine available
- Towne strain is a candidate live attenuated vaccine, but concerns exist about latency/reactivation
- Screening and matching CMV status of donor and recipient
- Use of CMV-negative blood for transfusions
- CMV immunoglobulin for seronegative recipients before transplant
- Prophylactic antivirals: acyclovir, ganciclovir

## 28. Adenovirus
**Overview**
- Infects lining of eyes, airways, lungs, intestines, urinary tract, and nervous system
- Common cause of fever, cough, sore throat, diarrhea, pink eye
- More common in children than adults; most kids have had one infection by age 10
- Usually mild, self-resolving in a few days
- Can be more serious in people with weak immune systems, especially children
**Mode of transmission**
- Ingestion/fecal-oral
- Respiration (droplets)
- Contact/hand to eye
- Venereal
**Prevention**
- Chlorination of pools and drinking water
- Personal hygiene
- Handwashing
**Associated disease sites**
- Ocular: conjunctivitis, keratoconjunctivitis
- Respiratory: pharyngitis, pertussis, pneumonia, acute respiratory disease
- Gastrointestinal: gastroenteritis, hepatitis, hemorrhagic cystitis
- Systemic spread: meningoencephalitis, myocarditis

## 29. Viral Structural Proteins and Functions
- Capsid — protects the viral genome
- Reverse transcriptase — viral replication
- Glycoprotein (Gp) receptors — attachment
- Hemagglutinin (HA) — binding/attachment; helps virus recognize and enter host; target for neutralizing antibodies
- Neuraminidase (NA) — cleaves sialic acid, aiding release of virus from host cell

## 30. Importance of Viral Receptors
- Aids attachment — the most important step in the viral life cycle
- Confers specificity — virus only affects cells with complementary receptor
- Enables entry into host cell
- Informs development of antiviral drugs
- Informs development of vaccines
- Involved in cell signaling
- Maintains cellular structure

## 31. Basic Principle of Vaccination
- Works with the body's natural defenses to safely develop immunity
- Vaccine introduced into the body, often by injection
- Minor symptoms (e.g. fever) after vaccination are normal
- WBCs triggered to produce specific antibodies
- Immune system remembers the germ
- Re-exposure triggers antibody response before illness develops

## 32. IPV vs OPV
**Why IPV (inactivated) is often preferred**
- Cannot replicate; not live
- Cannot cause disease, even in immunodeficient persons
- Less affected by circulating antibody than live antigens
- Can be given even when antibody is already present in the blood
**Why attenuated (live) vaccines are sometimes preferred**
- Provide longer-lasting immunity than inactivated vaccines
- Inactivated vaccines need multiple doses over time
- First dose of inactivated vaccine usually "primes" rather than protects
- Protective response develops after second or third dose
- Live vaccines mimic natural infection closely
- Live vaccines produce strong cellular immunity
- Inactivated vaccines mainly produce antibody response with little/no cellular immunity
- Antibody titers from inactivated vaccines diminish with time
- Some inactivated vaccines require periodic booster doses

## 34. Active vs Passive Immunization
**Active immunization**
- Induction of immunity after antigen exposure
- Antibodies created by the recipient
- Antibodies may be stored permanently
- Can occur naturally (unexposed person encounters microbe)
- Immune system eventually creates antibodies — a slow process
- If microbes are dead/inactive, there may not be enough time for antibodies to be useful in that exposure
**Adaptive immunity overview**
| Type | Naturally Acquired | Artificially Acquired |
|---|---|---|
| Active | Antigens enter the body naturally; body induces antibodies and specialized lymphocytes | Antigens introduced via vaccines; body produces antibodies and specialized lymphocytes |
| Passive | Antibodies pass from mother to fetus via placenta or to infant via mother's milk | Preformed antibodies in immune serum introduced by injection |

## 36. Antigenic Drift and Shift in Influenza
- Flu viruses constantly change and mutate
- Antigenic drift: changes that happen slowly over time
- Antigenic shift: results when two different flu strains combine and infect the same cell, and their genomes combine
- Antigenic shift causes a sudden change in the virus
- Antigenic shift can result in a pandemic strain

## 37. Cancer Cell vs Normal Cell
Contrasting the defining biological features of malignant and healthy cells.
| Feature | Cancer Cell | Normal Cell |
|---|---|---|
| Shape | Irregular | Regular |
| Nucleus | Larger, darker | Proportionate size |
| Growth | Out of control | Controlled, systematic |
| Death | Evades apoptosis (effectively immortal) | Mortal (undergoes apoptosis) |
| Maturation | Immature, doesn't mature | Mature (cell differentiation) |
| Communication | Doesn't communicate | Communicates |
| Visibility to immune system | Invisible to immune cells | Visible to immune cells, with ID |
| Blood supply | Tumor angiogenesis | Angiogenesis only during repair |
| Oxygen requirement | Doesn't like or require oxygen | Requires oxygen |
| Glucose preference | Loves, craves glucose | Requires some glucose |
| Energy efficiency | Very low (about 5%) | Very high (about 95%) |
| ATP yield | 2 units | 36 units |
| Cell environment | Acidic | Alkaline |
| Nutrient preference | Glucose | Fat, ketones, glucose |

## 38. Rabies Virus — Pathogenesis
- Virus enters through a bite
- Grows and multiplies at the trauma site for about a week
- Enters nerve endings
- Advances toward the ganglia, spinal cord, and brain
- Infection cycle completes when virus replicates in the salivary glands
**Clinical phases**
- Prodromal phase: fever, nausea, vomiting, headache, fatigue; pain, burning, tingling at wound site in some
- Furious phase: agitation, disorientation, seizures, twitching, hydrophobia
- Dumb phase: paralysis, disorientation, stupor
- Progresses to coma phase, resulting in death

## 39. Post-Exposure Rabies Management
- Combined passive and active post-exposure immunization
- Infuse the wound with human rabies immune globulin (HRIG)
- Vaccination with human diploid cell vaccine (HDCV) — an inactivated vaccine
- Given in 6 doses with 2 boosters
