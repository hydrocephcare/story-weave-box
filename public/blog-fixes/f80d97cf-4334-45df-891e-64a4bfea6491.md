## Blood Trans & Governance
- Blood transfusion = safe transfer of blood components from donor to recipient
- UK: all blood banks inspected by the **Medicines and Healthcare Regulatory Agency (MHRA)**
- Adverse events reported to **SABRE** scheme
- Errors reported to **SHOT** (Serious Hazards of Transfusion) scheme
- Donors are voluntary; blood is donated into plastic bags with anticoagulant (citrate, phosphate, dextrose — **CPD**)

## 2. Blood Donor Selection (Table 30.1)
- Age 17–70 years (max 65 at first donation)
- Weight >50 kg
- Hb >134 g/L men, >120 g/L women
- Max 3 donations/year; min 16 weeks between donations
- Apheresis for platelets/plasma: up to 24 times/year
**Exclusions:**
- Cardiovascular disease, epilepsy, CNS disorders, cancer, diabetes (insulin-dependent), renal disease, IV drug users
- Occupations where delayed faint is dangerous (pilots, drivers, crane operators)
- Defer 12 months after tattoo/piercing, paid sex, homosexual sex, acupuncture
- Defer 12 months if history of hepatitis; accepted if HBV/HCV markers negative
- Defer if travel suggests infection risk

## 3. Donor Testing (Table 30.2 — England & Wales)
1. Blood group, Rh status (D, C, E, c, e), K antigen
2. Screen for red cell alloantibodies
3. Microbiological tests:
   - HIV 1 & 2 (antibody + RNA)
   - HBV (antibody + RNA)
   - HCV (antibody + RNA)
   - HTLV (antibody)
   - CMV (antibody — for immunosuppressed recipients)
   - Malaria antibody screening (exposed donors)
   - Chagas' disease antibody (exposed donors)
   - Syphilis antibody (all donations)
   - **No reliable test for prions** currently available

## 4. Red Cell Antigens & Blood Groups
**Clinical significance:** individuals lacking a blood group antigen may produce antibodies against it, causing haemolytic transfusion reactions (~400 red cell antigens described)
### ABO System
- Encoded by a single gene with 3 alleles: A, B, O
- A and B alleles add carbohydrate residues to a basic glycoprotein (H antigen)
- A adds N-acetyl galactosamine; B adds d-galactose
- **Naturally occurring antibodies** (IgM, occasionally IgG) present in plasma of those lacking the antigen — even without prior transfusion
| Phenotype | Genotype | Antigens | Natural Antibodies | UK Frequency |
|-----------|----------|----------|--------------------|--------------|
| O | OO | O (H) | Anti-A, Anti-B | 46% |
| A | AA or AO | A | Anti-B | 42% |
| B | BB or BO | B | Anti-A | 9% |
| AB | AB | AB | None | 3% |
- A and B antigens present on most body cells including WBCs and platelets
- In 80% of population (secretors), antigens also found in saliva, plasma, semen, sweat
### Rh System
- Two closely linked genes: **RhD** and **RhCE**
- RhD encodes D antigen (present = Rh+, absent = Rh–)
- RhCE encodes C/c and E/e antigens via alternative splicing
- **Rh antibodies do NOT occur naturally** — result from transfusion or pregnancy
- Anti-D is clinically most important; also anti-C, anti-E, anti-c, anti-e
- Anti-d does not exist
- Most common Rh genotype in UK whites: CDe/cde (Rr) = 31%
### Other Blood Group Systems (Table 30.3)
- Kell, Duffy, Kidd, Lewis, MN, P, Lutheran, Li
- Less clinically important than ABO and Rh
- Immune antibodies detected infrequently
- Molecular techniques increasingly used for extended red cell genotyping

## 5. Blood Group Serology Techniques
### Antiglobulin (Coombs') Test — fundamental test in transfusion
- Antihuman globulin (AHG) is produced in animals after injection of human globulin
- When AHG added to red cells coated with immunoglobulin or complement → agglutination = positive
**Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT):**
- Detects antibody/complement already on red cells in vivo
- Positive in: haemolytic disease of newborn, autoimmune haemolytic anaemia, drug-induced haemolysis, haemolytic transfusion reactions
**Indirect Antiglobulin Test (IAT):**
- Detects antibodies in serum that have coated red cells in vitro
- Used for: routine antibody screening pre-transfusion, blood group antibodies in pregnancy
### Compatibility Testing (Table 30.8)
- Donor cells tested against recipient serum
- For IgM: saline at 37C
- For IgG: indirect antiglobulin test at 37C, low ionic strength saline, enzyme-treated red cells
### Cross-matching Steps
1. ABO and Rh group determined from patient
2. Serum screened for antibodies (IAT on group O red cells panel)
3. If alloantibody found → donor blood selected lacking that antigen
4. Electronic cross-match available if group + antibody screen done twice

## 6. Hazards of Allogeneic Transfusion
### Infectious Agents (Table 30.7)
**Viruses:**
- Hepatitis A, B, C, D (D requires co-infection with HBV)
- HIV 1+2
- HTLV I + II
- CMV, EBV, HHV-8
- Parvovirus B19
- West Nile virus, TTV, GBV-C
**Bacteria:**
- Endogenous: Treponema pallidum (syphilis), Borrelia burgdorferi (Lyme), Brucella, Yersinia/Salmonella
- Exogenous: Staphylococcal spp., Pseudomonas/Serratia
- Rickettsiae: Rickettsia rickettsii, Coxiella burnettii
- Bacterial infections most frequently transmitted by platelets stored >3 days
**Protozoa:** Malaria, Chagas' disease, Toxoplasmosis, Babesiosis, Leishmaniasis
**Prions:** nvCJD — plasma for fractionation and fresh frozen plasma for infants obtained from USA; blood components from people with nvCJD are excluded as donors in UK
### HIV Transmission
- Transmitted by cells or plasma
- Excluded donors: male homosexuals, bisexuals, IV drug users, prostitutes, their sexual partners, partners of haemophiliacs
- Sub-Saharan Africa and South-East Asia high-risk
- Window period transmission possible (donor incubating infection but antibody-negative)
### CMV
- Usually subclinical but can cause infectious mononucleosis
- Dangerous in: premature babies <1500g, stem cell/organ transplant recipients, anti-CD52 recipients, pregnant women (fetal risk)
- CMV-negative blood required for these patients
### Measures to Protect Recipient (Table 30.6)
- Donor selection and deferral
- Stringent arm cleaning
- Microbiological + immunohaematological testing
- Discarding first 20–30 mL of blood
- Leucodepletion of cellular products
- Post-collection viral inactivation of FFP
- Monitoring and testing for bacterial contamination
- Pathogen inactivation of cellular components

### Haemolytic Reactions
- Massive: usually IgM antibodies activating complement (ABO incompatibility)
- Extravascular: IgG immune antibodies (Rh system) — less severe but life-threatening
**Haemolytic Shock Phase:**
- Urticaria, pain in lumbar region, flushing, headache, precordial pain, SOB, vomiting, rigors, pyrexia, fall in BP
- Haemoglobinaemia, jaundice, DIC
- Moderate leucocytosis (15–20 x10^9/L)
- If anaesthetised — shock phase masked
**Oliguric Phase:** renal tubular necrosis, acute renal failure
**Diuretic Phase:** fluid and electrolyte imbalance during recovery
### Investigation of Immediate Transfusion Reaction
1. Stop transfusion; check patient identity matches compatibility label
2. Send donor blood + post-transfusion patient blood to lab:
   - Repeat grouping pre and post transfusion
   - Direct antiglobulin test on post-transfusion sample
   - Check plasma for haemoglobinaemia
   - Test for DIC
   - Examine donor sample for bacterial contamination; blood cultures at 20°C and 37°C
3. Urine for haemoglobinuria
4. Blood samples at 6h and 24h: bilirubin, Hb, methaemalbumen
5. If positive: serum examined 5–10 days later for red cell/white cell antibodies
### Management of Major Haemolysis
- Maintain BP and renal perfusion
- IV dextran, plasma, or saline; frusemide sometimes needed
- Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV + antihistamine for shock
- Adrenaline 1:10,000 IV in small doses for severe shock
- Dialysis if acute renal failure occurs
### Other Transfusion Reactions
**Hyperhaemlysis syndrome:** sickle cell patients; haemolyse donor blood even without alloantibodies; treat with gammaglobulin and corticosteroids
**Febrile reactions (HLA antibodies):** sensitisation from pregnancy or prior transfusion; rigors, pyrexia, pulmonary infiltrates; minimised by leucodepletion
**Allergic (non-haemolytic):** hypersensitivity to donor plasma proteins; urticaria, pyrexia, dyspnoea, facial oedema, rigors; treat with antihistamines/hydrocortisone; adrenaline useful
**Circulatory overload:** slow transfusion + diuretic therapy
**Bacterial contamination:** very rare; circulatory collapse; particular problem with platelet packs stored at 20–24°C
**GVHD (Graft-versus-host disease):** live lymphocytes transfused to immunocompromised patient; fatal; prevented by irradiation of blood products (Table 30.10)
**TRALI (Transfusion-Related Acute Lung Injury):** within 6 hours; pulmonary infiltrates with chest symptoms; caused by HLA antibodies in donor plasma (multiparous women); management supportive; male donors preferred to reduce risk
**Post-transfusion purpura:** severe thrombocytopenia 7–10 days post-transfusion; caused by anti-HPA-1a (PlA1) antibody; self-limiting; treat with immunoglobulin or plasma exchange
**Viral transmission:** hepatitis viruses most common; CMV, EBV also; HIV, HTLV very rare due to screening
**Iron overload:** repeated transfusions → iron deposits in reticuloendothelial tissue, then liver, myocardium, endocrine glands; major problem in thalassaemia major and chronic refractory anaemias

## 8. Irradiated Blood Products (Table 30.10)
Indications:
- Recipients of allogeneic or autologous stem cell transplantation
- Bone marrow/stem cell donors (7 days pre-harvest and during harvest)
- Solid organ transplants on immunosuppression
- Hodgkin lymphoma
- Acute leukaemia
- Fludarabine, bendamustine, other purine antagonists
- Anti-CD52 treatment
- Inherited immunodeficiency
- Intrauterine and neonatal transfusions

## 9. Blood Components
### Preparation (Fig. 30.1)
Whole blood → centrifuge → red cell concentrate + buffy coat + fresh plasma
- Fresh plasma → FFP (clinical use or fractionation) or cryoprecipitate + cryosupernatant
- Buffy coat (pooled from 4 donors) → platelets
- Red cells → optimal additive solution (SAGM) → stored at 4°C
### Red Cell Storage
- Stored 4–6°C for up to 35 days
- First 48h: slow progressive K+ loss from red cells into plasma (use fresh blood for exchange transfusion in neonates)
- Fall in 2,3-DPG during storage → returns to normal within 24h post-transfusion
- Additive solutions (ATP, 2,3-DPG) maintain shelf life
### Leucodepletion
- Routine in many countries including UK
- Removes majority of white cells
- Done soon after collection (more effective than bedside filtration)
- Component = leucodepleted if <5 x10^6/L white cells
- Reduces: febrile reactions, HLA alloimmunisation, CMV transmission, theoretical nvCJD risk
### Red Cells (Packed)
- Plasma-depleted packed red cells — treatment of choice for most transfusions
- Diuretic often given simultaneously in elderly
- Infusion should be slow to avoid circulatory overload
- Iron chelation therapy for patients on regular transfusion programmes
### Granulocyte Concentrates
- From buffy coats of normal donors or patients with chronic myeloid leukaemia
- Used in severe neutropenia (<0.5 x10^9/L) not responding to antibiotics
- Transmit CMV; must be irradiated (GVHD risk)
### Platelet Concentrates
- Harvested by cell separators or from individual donor units
- Stored at room temperature
- Used in: thrombocytopenic patients, disordered platelet function, active/at-risk bleeding
**Platelet count thresholds:**
- Prophylaxis: keep >5–10 x10^9/L (higher if sepsis, drug use, coagulation disorders)
- Invasive procedures (liver biopsy, lumbar puncture): raise to >50 x10^9/L
- Brain or eye surgery: raise to >100 x10^9/L
- Massive haemorrhage: keep >50 x10^9/L (Chapter 26)
**Avoid platelet transfusion in:**
- Autoimmune thrombocytopenic purpura (unless serious haemorrhage)
- Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia
- Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura
- Haemolytic uraemic syndrome
**Refractoriness to platelets:**
- Poor increment post-transfusion: <7.5 x10^9/L at 1h or <4.5 x10^9/L at 24h
- Causes: immunological (HLA alloimmunisation) or non-immunological (sepsis, hypersplenism, DIC, drugs)
- HLA-matched or cross-match-compatible platelets needed for HLA antibody patients
- Direct stimulators of platelet production (romiplostim, eltrombopag) may reduce need

## 10. Plasma Preparations
### Fresh Frozen Plasma (FFP)
- Rapidly frozen from fresh blood; stored <−30°C
- Usually from single donors; pooled also available
- **Uses:** replacement of coagulation factors when specific concentrates unavailable; massive transfusion; liver disease; DIC; after cardiopulmonary bypass; reverse warfarin; thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura
- Virally inactivated forms now available
- Male donors preferred (reduce TRALI risk)
### Human Albumin Solution 4.5%
- Useful plasma volume expander when sustained osmotic effect required
- Do not give in excess
- Used in: plasmapheresis, fluid replacement in hypoalbuminaemia
### Human Albumin Solution 20% (salt-poor albumin)
- Used in severe hypoalbuminaemia when minimal electrolyte content needed
- Indications: nephrotic syndrome, liver failure
### Cryoprecipitate
- Obtained by thawing FFP at 4°C
- Contains concentrated factor VIII and fibrinogen
- Stored <−30°C or lyophilized at 4–6°C
- Was widely used for haemophilia A and von Willebrand disease before purified factor VIII became available
- Main use now: **fibrinogen replacement in DIC, massive transfusion, hepatic failure**
### Freeze-Dried Factor VIII Concentrates
- Used for haemophilia A and von Willebrand disease
- Small volume — ideal for children, surgical cases, home treatment
- Use declining as recombinant factor VIII becomes widely available
### Freeze-Dried Factor IX–Prothrombin Complex Concentrates
- Contain variable amounts of factors II, VII, IX, X
- Mainly used for **factor IX deficiency (Christmas disease)**
- Also used in liver disease, haemorrhage from oral anticoagulant overdose, or factor VIII inhibitors
- Risk of thrombosis
### Protein C Concentrate
- Used in severe sepsis with DIC (e.g. meningococcal septicaemia)
- Reduces thrombosis from depletion of protein C
### Immunoglobulin (pooled)
- Valuable source of antibodies against common viruses
- Used in: hypogammaglobulinaemia (passive protection against viral/bacterial disease)
- Repeated doses every 3–4 weeks
- Also used in: immune thrombocytopenia, post-transfusion purpura, alloimmune neonatal thrombocytopenia
### Specific Immunoglobulin
- From donors with high titres of specific antibody
- Examples: anti-RhD, anti-hepatitis B, anti-herpes zoster, anti-rubella

## 11. Autologous Donation & Transfusion
Three methods:
1. **Predeposit** — blood taken from patient weeks before elective surgery
2. **Haemodilution** — blood removed immediately before surgery once anaesthetised; reinfused at end
3. **Salvage** — blood lost during operation collected and reinfused
**Advantages:** avoids transfusion-transmitted viruses; increased by HIV anxiety
**Risks:** higher risk of bacterial contamination and clerical errors vs viral transmission risk; must be fit to donate; predicted operative replacement should be 2–4 units (larger volumes require longer storage, costlier)

## 12. Reduction of Blood Product Use
- Preoperative correction of anaemia (especially iron deficiency)
- Stop anti-platelet therapies (e.g. aspirin) where possible
- Lower trigger levels for transfusion (Hb 70–80 g/L sufficient in most surgical patients)
- In surgery: alternative fluid replacement, intraoperative/postoperative cell salvage
- Biological alternatives: erythropoietin, recombinant clotting factors, recombinant activated clotting factor VII (VIIa), fibrin glue
- Recombinant erythropoietin widely used in renal failure patients, dialysis, cancer, myelodysplasia
- Factor VIIa reduces transfusion need in major haemorrhage (surgery or trauma)
- Red cell substitutes (fluorinated hydrocarbons, stromal-free haemoglobin solutions) under development — not yet clinically proven

## 13. Acute Blood Loss & Massive Haemorrhage
- After single episode of blood loss: initial vasoconstriction; plasma volume rapidly expands; Hb and packed cell volume fall; rise in neutrophils and platelets
- Reticulocyte response: day 2–3, lasts 8–10 days
- Hb begins to rise by day 7
- If iron stores depleted → Hb may not subsequently rise to normal
- Clinical assessment needed to gauge transfusion requirement
- Transfusion usually unnecessary if losses <500 mL
- May not be needed with losses up to 1.5 L
- Blood transfusion is not without risks — should not be undertaken lightly

## Summary Points
- Blood transfusion = safe transfer of blood components; red cells must be matched between donor and recipient
- Careful donor selection and microbiological testing protect both donor and recipient
- Red cells have >400 antigens; ABO and Rh most important
- Group O or B subjects develop natural IgM antibodies; these may haemolyse or opsonise incompatible donor red cells
- Antibodies also develop from transfusion or pregnancy; cross-matching ensures compatibility
- Complications: haemolytic reactions, febrile reactions, circulatory overload, infection transmission (especially viral), iron overload
- Components other than red cells include: platelets, FFP, albumin, coagulation factor concentrates, immunoglobulin
