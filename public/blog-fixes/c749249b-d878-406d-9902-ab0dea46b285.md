# Urinary Tract Infections

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Nephrology and urology**

> **In one line:** a urinary tract infection (UTI) is infection anywhere from the urethra to the kidney, usually caused by gut-derived Gram-negative bacteria (mainly *E. coli*) ascending the urethra. It is far commoner in women because of a short urethra, and ranges from asymptomatic bacteriuria and cystitis to pyelonephritis and Gram-negative septicaemia. Diagnose with urinalysis and culture; treat cystitis briefly and pyelonephritis more aggressively.

## What you must know

- In health only the lower urethra is colonised; the rest of the tract is **sterile**.
- About **half of all women** have a UTI in their lifetime; UTI in men and children suggests an **abnormal urinary tract**.
- **Lower UTI:** cystitis, urethritis. **Upper UTI:** acute pyelonephritis.
- Significant bacteriuria: **more than 10^5 organisms/mL** in a mid-stream clean-catch sample (a lower count can be significant in a symptomatic patient).
- *E. coli* from the gut causes about **75%** of infections.
- **Asymptomatic bacteriuria** is treated only in **infants, pregnant women and those with urinary tract abnormalities** (and before invasive urological procedures).
- Uncomplicated lower UTI: treat for **3 days**.
- **Sterile pyuria** should make you think of **renal tuberculosis**.

## 1. Definitions and spectrum

| Category | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Uncomplicated UTI** | cystitis or pyelonephritis in a **premenopausal, non-pregnant adult woman** with no comorbidity, no functional or structural anomaly of the tract, and no history of instrumentation |
| **Complicated UTI** | patient is a **child or pregnant**; structural or functional anomaly of the tract; comorbidity predisposing to UTI (e.g. diabetes, immunosuppression); recent instrumentation or surgery |

**Spectrum of presentation:** asymptomatic bacteriuria, symptomatic acute urethritis and cystitis, acute pyelonephritis, acute prostatitis, and septicaemia (usually Gram-negative bacteria).

## 2. Aetiology

| Group | Organisms |
|---|---|
| **Non-specific bacteria (commonest)** | *E. coli* (about 75%, from the gut), *Klebsiella*, *Proteus*, *Enterobacter*, *Pseudomonas*, streptococci, *Staphylococcus epidermidis* (*Klebsiella* more in hospitalised patients), *Enterococcus*, *Staphylococcus saprophyticus* in young women |
| **Granuloma-forming** | tuberculosis, fungal infection, syphilis |
| **Parasitic** | *Echinococcus granulosus*, **schistosomiasis** (*S. haematobium*, important in Kenya) |
| **Helminths** | *Enterobius* (pinworms) |
| **Protozoa** | *Trichomonas vaginalis* |

> **Remember:** *Proteus* produces urease, alkalinises urine and causes **struvite (staghorn) stones**.

## 3. Routes of infection

- **Ascending** (commonest): from the urethra to bladder to kidney.
- **Haematogenous** (mostly the route in pyelonephritis in this lecture, and for TB).
- **Lymphogenous.**
- **Direct extension** from adjacent organs.
- **Iatrogenic** (catheters, instrumentation).
- Foci in the prostate or kidney.

## 4. Pathophysiology and risk factors

### Why women are more susceptible

The female urethra is **short**, and there are no bactericidal prostatic secretions. Sexual intercourse and instrumentation push organisms upward.

### Innate host defences

| Level | Defence |
|---|---|
| **Mucosal** | mucopolysaccharide lining, **IgA**, urothelial secretion of cytokines; in men, bactericidal **zinc-containing prostatic secretions** and a long urethra |
| **Urine** | **acidic pH**, mechanical flushing by urine flow, urinary inhibitors of bacterial adherence |

### Risk factors

- **Behavioural:** voiding dysfunction, recent sexual intercourse.
- **Genetic:** family history, vaginal mucus properties.
- **Anatomical or physiological:** female sex, pregnancy.
- Others: obstruction (stones, prostatic enlargement), vesico-ureteric reflux, catheters, diabetes, immunosuppression, post-menopausal atrophic changes.

## 5. Clinical syndromes

### Urethritis

- Organisms access the numerous periurethral glands (bulbar and penile urethra in men; the whole female urethra) and colonise acutely or chronically.
- Pathogens: *Chlamydia*, HSV, *Neisseria gonorrhoeae*.

### Cystitis

- Common in women; "**honeymoon cystitis**" follows sexual intercourse.
- In men it is **complicated** (ascending infection, prostatitis, instrumentation). **Chronic bacterial prostatitis** is the commonest cause of recurrent cystitis in men.

**Typical features of cystitis and urethritis**
- Frequency, urgency, dysuria.
- Suprapubic pain during and after voiding.
- **Strangury:** intense desire to pass more urine after micturition because of spasm of the inflamed bladder wall.
- Cloudy, offensive urine; microscopic or visible haematuria.

### Acute urethral syndrome (AUS)

- Women with **dysuria, frequency and pyuria**, but routine urine cultures are negative or show low colony counts.
- Causes: urethritis (*Ureaplasma urealyticum*, *Chlamydia trachomatis*, negative on routine culture), intermittent or low-count bacteriuria, reaction to toiletries or disinfectants, symptoms related to intercourse, post-menopausal atrophic vaginitis.

### Asymptomatic bacteriuria

- More than 10^5 organisms/mL in an apparently healthy, asymptomatic patient.
- **Treat** in infants, pregnant women and those with urinary tract abnormalities.
- **Screen** only those likely to develop complications if left untreated:
  - pregnant women at **12 to 16 weeks**
  - kidney transplant in the last 6 months
  - before certain invasive genitourinary procedures
  - children with vesico-ureteric reflux (VUR)
- Persistent asymptomatic bacteriuria is common in post-menopausal women, well-controlled diabetics and patients with stents or indwelling catheters; if there are no symptoms, **do not screen or treat routinely**.

### Acute pyelonephritis

- Suggested by prominent **systemic symptoms**: fever, rigors, vomiting, hypotension, loin pain, guarding or tenderness. May need admission.
- Only about **30%** have symptoms of cystitis or urethritis.
- Uncommon in men with a normal urinary tract; commoner in **pregnancy** and in young girls after catheterisation.
- **Papillary necrosis** is associated with diabetes, analgesic nephropathy, *Candida* pyelonephritis, sickle cell disease and obstruction (loin pain, haematuria, sloughed papillae in urine).

### Acute prostatitis

Suggested by **perineal or suprapubic pain, pain on ejaculation and a tender prostate on rectal examination**, usually with fever and dysuria.

> **Remember:** do not massage an acutely inflamed prostate (risk of bacteraemia).

### Renal tuberculosis

- Secondary to TB elsewhere, spread by the **blood**; lesions start in the **renal cortex**.
- Urine shows white cells (neutrophils) but **routine culture is sterile: sterile pyuria**. Send early-morning urine (three samples) for AFB/GeneXpert and culture.

## 6. Examination

- Fever, chills, rigors.
- **Renal angle tenderness** (renal punch).
- Distended bladder (retention).
- Urethral discharge.
- Tender prostate on DRE.
- Abdominal (suprapubic) pain.

## 7. Investigations

| Test | Role |
|---|---|
| **Urine dipstick** | **leucocyte esterase** (pyuria), **nitrite** (Gram-negative reduction of nitrate), blood |
| **Urine microscopy** | WBCs, RBCs; WBC casts point to the kidney |
| **Urine culture** | positive if **over 10^5 CFU/mL** (*E. coli*, *Staphylococcus*, *Proteus*, *Klebsiella*, *Enterococcus*); gives sensitivities |
| **U&Es** | renal function in pyelonephritis, sepsis, obstruction |
| **Imaging** | KUB radiograph, ultrasound, MCUG, CT/CT urography, MRI, IV pyelography (excretory urography), transrectal ultrasound and biopsy for prostate |

- Imaging is indicated in **men, children, recurrent infections, no response to treatment in 48 to 72 hours, haematuria or suspected obstruction or stones**.
- Blood cultures if febrile or septic.

> **Exam question from the slides:** in a boy with acute pyelonephritis the most specific urinary finding is **WBC casts** (they show the infection is in the kidney). The leucocyte esterase, nitrite and Gram stain findings are not specific to the upper tract.

## 8. Differential diagnosis

- **Lower UTI:** urethritis from a sexually transmitted infection (notably chlamydia), reactive arthritis (Reiter's syndrome), vaginitis, interstitial cystitis.
- **Acute pyelonephritis:** pyelonephrosis, acute appendicitis, diverticulitis, cholecystitis, salpingitis, ruptured ovarian cyst, ectopic pregnancy.

## 9. Management

> **Check:** the slides do not list drugs. Choose the antibiotic according to local sensitivities and national guidelines; resistance to amoxicillin and co-trimoxazole is now common among *E. coli* in Kenya.

### Lower UTI (cystitis)

- **3-day course** is the norm; it is less likely to disturb bowel flora than long courses. Typical options: nitrofurantoin, co-trimoxazole (if locally sensitive), a quinolone (ciprofloxacin), or amoxicillin-clavulanate.
- Increase fluids and treat dysuria with simple analgesia.

### Acute pyelonephritis

- Mild: oral antibiotics (e.g. a quinolone or amoxicillin-clavulanate) for 7 to 14 days.
- Severe, vomiting, septic, pregnant or complicated: **admit**, IV fluids, **IV antibiotics** (ceftriaxone, an aminoglycoside such as gentamicin, or a quinolone), then switch to oral by sensitivity.
- Look for obstruction (ultrasound); an obstructed infected system needs urgent drainage.

### Special groups

- **Pregnancy:** treat every bacteriuria, including asymptomatic; use agents safe in pregnancy (cefalexin, amoxicillin-clavulanate); avoid quinolones, tetracyclines and, near term, nitrofurantoin and co-trimoxazole.
- **Men:** longer courses (7 to 14 days); look for prostatitis.
- **Prostatitis:** prolonged antibiotics with good prostatic penetration (quinolone or co-trimoxazole) for several weeks.

### Follow-up and prophylaxis for recurrent UTI in women

- Urine culture **1 to 2 weeks after treatment** and thereafter as required.
- Fluid intake of **at least 2 L/day**.
- **Regular complete emptying** of the bladder.
- Good personal hygiene.
- Cranberry juice.
- **Empty the bladder before and after sexual intercourse.**
- Low-dose or post-coital antibiotic prophylaxis may be used in frequent recurrences.

## 10. Complications

- **Sepsis** (Gram-negative septicaemia).
- Recurrent infections.
- **Permanent renal damage** (scarring) with untreated infection.
- **Low birth weight** delivery in pregnancy.
- Papillary necrosis, perinephric abscess, emphysematous pyelonephritis in diabetics.

## Key points to revise

1. UTI is infection of any part of the tract; the normal urinary tract above the lower urethra is sterile.
2. Women are affected more because of a short urethra and no prostatic secretions; men and children with UTI need investigation for an anomaly.
3. Diagnosis: over 10^5 organisms/mL on a mid-stream sample; leucocyte esterase and nitrite on dipstick support it.
4. *E. coli* from the gut causes about 75%; *Proteus* causes struvite stones.
5. Treat asymptomatic bacteriuria only in infants, pregnancy and urinary tract abnormalities; screen at 12 to 16 weeks in pregnancy.
6. Cystitis: 3-day course; pyelonephritis: systemic illness, loin pain, WBC casts; admit if septic or pregnant.
7. Sterile pyuria suggests renal TB (or recent antibiotics, or chlamydia).
8. Prevent recurrence with fluids (2 L/day), complete voiding, hygiene and post-coital voiding.
