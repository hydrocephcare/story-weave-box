# Approach to Anaemia

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Haematology**

> **In one line:** anaemia is almost always a **secondary** problem, so the task is to find the cause. Work it up in a fixed order: **reticulocyte count first** (is the marrow responding?), then **MCV, RDW and blood film** to sort the cause into blood loss/haemolysis, hypoproliferation or maturation defects.

## What you must know

- Anaemia is the **commonest haematological disorder** and nearly always secondary to another disease.
- Four mechanisms: **blood loss, decreased production (marrow failure/hypoproliferative), maturation defects (ineffective erythropoiesis), increased destruction (haemolysis)**.
- **Reticulocyte count is the first test.** A corrected reticulocyte (production) index above 2.5 means haemolysis or bleeding; below 2 means hypoproliferation or a maturation defect.
- At least **75% of anaemias are hypoproliferative**, mostly iron deficiency or chronic inflammation.
- **MCV** divides anaemia into microcytic (below 80 fl), normocytic (80 to 100) and macrocytic (above 100).
- **Ferritin** separates iron deficiency (below 15) from chronic disease (above 120); the blood film is essential for combined deficiencies.

## 1. Definition and causes

- Decrease in the number of circulating red blood cells (reduced haemoglobin).
- Causes: blood loss; decreased red cell production (marrow failure); red cell maturation defects; increased destruction (haemolysis).

## 2. The work-up

### 2.1 Basic first-line tests

- **History and examination**, reticulocyte count, blood film, MCV, ferritin, WBC with differential, platelets.

### 2.2 Over-investigation (avoid)

The "exaggerated" work-up (iron/TIBC/ferritin, folate/B12, LDH/bilirubin, haptoglobin/urine haemosiderin, direct and indirect Coombs, haemoglobin electrophoresis, acid haemolysis test for PNH, osmotic fragility, empirical iron/folate/B12, crossmatch and 2 to 4 units transfusion, GI and haematology referral with bone marrow) wastes time and money. The reticulocyte count tells you which of these are needed.

- **If the reticulocyte count is high**, do not send iron studies, folate/B12, acid haemolysis, GI consult or marrow.

### 2.3 Reticulocyte count

- **Absolute count = reticulocyte % x RBC count** (for example 0.01 x 5,000,000 = 50,000/microlitre).
- Normal: 1 to 4% of circulating red cells in adults; absolute count up to about 120,000/microlitre.
- It is the most accurate way to assess the marrow's response.
- **Reticulocyte production index above 2.5 = haemolysis most likely**; **below 2 = hypoproliferative or maturation disorder**.
- Acute blood loss does not change the reticulocyte count immediately (reticulocytosis begins at 3 to 5 days).

> **Remember:** polychromasia and polychromatophilic macrocytes on the film suggest reticulocytosis.

### 2.4 Blood film

- Size and shape of red cells (anisocytosis, poikilocytosis), polychromasia (reticulocytosis), **dimorphic population** (transfusion, sideroblastic anaemia), and platelet and white cell abnormalities.

## 3. Classification by mechanism

| Group | Features |
|---|---|
| **Hypoproliferative** | No erythroid hyperplasia in marrow; at least 75% of anaemias; mostly iron deficiency or inflammation; from marrow damage, iron deficiency or inadequate erythropoietin; may be microcytic and hypochromic in mild iron deficiency or chronic inflammation |
| **Maturation disorders** | Anaemia with inappropriately low reticulocyte index; macro- or microcytosis; **nuclear** defects (macrocytosis) and **cytoplasmic** defects (microcytosis and hypochromia) |
| **Haemolysis / blood loss** | Production index above 2.5; polychromatophilic macrocytes; acute bleeding has no early retic change |

## 4. Approach by MCV

### 4.1 Normocytic (MCV 80 to 100 fl)

- Commonest cause: **anaemia of chronic disease**; also **early iron deficiency** and combined deficiencies. Marrow failure (aplastic anaemia, infiltration) gives normocytic anaemia too.

**Anaemia of chronic disease (ACD)**
- Common; develops over 1 to 2 months; non-progressive; usually mild to moderate (haematocrit below 0.20 occasionally).
- About 30% are mildly microcytic. Iron low, **TIBC low**, transferrin saturation low, **ferritin normal or high**; WBC and platelets normal or raised.
- Particular hazard of ICU patients.
- Pathophysiology: cytokines (IL-1, TNF) reduce erythropoietin response and production, decrease transferrin synthesis, reduce iron mobilisation from macrophages (**hepcidin-mediated block**) and re-use in erythropoiesis, so serum iron falls despite adequate stores, ferritin rises, and reticulocytes are low.
- **IL-1** also stimulates fever, granulopoiesis, thrombopoiesis, ferritin, immunoglobulins, fibrinogen, factor VIII, CRP, IL-2 and IL-6; it inhibits erythropoiesis and synthesis of transferrin and albumin.
- Causes: thyroid disease, collagen vascular disease (RA, SLE, polymyositis, polyarteritis nodosa), inflammatory bowel disease (ulcerative colitis, Crohn's), malignancy, chronic infections, familial Mediterranean fever, renal failure.

### 4.2 Microcytic (MCV below 80 fl)

| Cause | RDW and other clues |
|---|---|
| **Iron deficiency** | **High RDW** (normal 11.5 to 14.5%) |
| **Thalassaemia minor** | **Normal RDW**, high red cell count, Mentzer index below 13 |
| Sideroblastic anaemia | Rare; dimorphic film, ringed sideroblasts |
| Metal poisoning | Lead, aluminium |
| Haemoglobinopathies | Occasional; thalassaemia major |

**Ferritin pathway**
- Ferritin **below 15**: iron deficiency anaemia.
- Ferritin **120 or more**: iron deficiency excluded.
- Ferritin **15 to 120**: check TIBC (high suggests iron deficiency; normal or low suggests ACD); then a **trial of iron** (if anaemia corrects, iron deficiency) or examine marrow iron stores if it does not.

### 4.3 Macrocytic (MCV above 100 fl)

- MCV **above 110**: almost always **folate or cobalamin (B12) deficiency**. MCV 100 to 110: look for other causes (alcohol, liver disease, hypothyroidism, reticulocytosis, drugs, myelodysplasia).

**Macrocytosis of alcoholism**
- Seen in 25 to 96% of alcoholics; direct marrow toxicity.
- MCV rise slight (100 to 110), minimal or no anaemia, **round macrocytes (not oval)**, no neutrophil hypersegmentation, normal folate stores.

**Megaloblastic haematopoiesis**
- Disrupted DNA synthesis and ineffective haematopoiesis with giant precursors and nuclear-cytoplasmic asynchrony in marrow.
- Blood: **neutrophil hypersegmentation and macro-ovalocytes**; anaemia, often with leucopenia and thrombocytopenia.
- Almost always B12 or folate deficiency.

**Evolving cobalamin deficiency (usual sequence)**
1. Serum cobalamin falls.
2. **Methylmalonic acid and homocysteine rise.**
3. MCV rises within the normal range, with neutrophil hypersegmentation.
4. MCV rises above normal.
5. Anaemia and/or neuropathy and symptoms.

### 4.4 Dimorphic anaemias (two deficiencies)

- Folate and iron deficiency (pregnancy, alcoholism).
- B12 and iron deficiency (pernicious anaemia with atrophic gastritis).
- Thalassaemia minor with B12 or folate deficiency.
- Iron deficiency with haemolysis (prosthetic valve).
- Folate deficiency with haemolysis (sickle cell disease).
- The blood film is critical; the MCV may look normal because the two effects cancel.

## 5. Haemolytic anaemia

- Anaemia of increased destruction: normochromic normocytic (macrocytic from reticulocytes), **shortened red cell survival**, **reticulocytosis**.
- **Tests that show haemolysis:** reticulocyte count with serial Hb, **haptoglobin (low)**, **urine haemosiderin**; also helpful: serum bilirubin (unconjugated, raised), LDH (raised), haemoglobinuria. These have sensitivity and specificity problems and none define the cause.
- **A normal film does not exclude haemolysis.**
- **Tests to define the cause (they do not prove haemolysis):**

| Test | Detects |
|---|---|
| Haemoglobin electrophoresis, Hb A2 | Haemoglobinopathies; **beta-thalassaemia trait (raised A2)** |
| Red cell enzymes (G6PD, pyruvate kinase) | Enzyme deficiencies |
| Direct and indirect antiglobulin (Coombs) tests | Immune haemolysis |
| Cold agglutinins | Usually post-infection |
| Osmotic fragility | Hereditary spherocytosis (resistance to lysis in thalassaemia) |
| Acid haemolysis (Ham) test | **PNH** |
| Clotting profile | DIC |

## 6. Summary algorithm

1. **Check reticulocyte count first.**
2. If **raised**: look for haemolysis or bleeding.
3. If **normal or low**: marrow failure; tailor the work-up to MCV, RDW and film.
   - MCV low: iron or globin problem.
   - MCV high: megaloblastic or DNA synthesis problem.
   - MCV normal: look for combined anaemias or chronic disease.

> **Exam tip:** never start with the "shotgun" panel. Retic count, MCV and film are the three cheap tests that direct everything else.

## Key points to revise

1. Anaemia is nearly always secondary; always search for the cause.
2. The reticulocyte count comes first: index above 2.5 means haemolysis/bleeding, below 2 means marrow or maturation problem.
3. At least 75% of anaemias are hypoproliferative (iron deficiency or chronic inflammation).
4. Microcytic: iron deficiency (high RDW, ferritin below 15) versus thalassaemia trait (normal RDW).
5. Normocytic: think anaemia of chronic disease, early iron deficiency, combined deficiencies, marrow failure.
6. Macrocytic: MCV above 110 is B12/folate deficiency until proven otherwise; alcohol gives round macrocytes.
7. Haemolysis is shown by raised retics, LDH and bilirubin and low haptoglobin; specific tests identify the cause.
8. Use the blood film to detect dimorphic (combined) anaemias.
