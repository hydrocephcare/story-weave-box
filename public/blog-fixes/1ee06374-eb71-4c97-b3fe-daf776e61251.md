## MEDICAL VIROLOGY — SECTION 2 (Comprehensive Notes)
*Covers: Hepatitis Viruses → Oncogenic Viruses → Arboviruses → HIV (everything after the Mid-Term CAT)*

## 1. Hepatitis Viruses

**General concept**: "Hepatitis" = liver inflammation. Several unrelated viruses cause it — they differ completely in genome, transmission, and chronicity potential. This comparison table is one of the most commonly tested areas.

| Virus | Genome/Family | Transmission | Incubation | Chronicity? | Key exam points |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Hep A** | (+)ssRNA, Picornavirus | Faecal-oral (contaminated food/water) | 2–6 weeks | **No** chronic state | Self-limiting; vaccine available; common in poor sanitation areas |
| **Hep B** | dsDNA-RT (Baltimore class VII), Hepadnavirus | Blood, sexual contact, vertical (mother-to-child) | 1–6 months | **Yes** — can become chronic, especially if acquired at birth | Has surface antigen (HBsAg) used for screening; vaccine available; risk of cirrhosis & hepatocellular carcinoma |
| **Hep C** | (+)ssRNA, Flavivirus | Blood (transfusion, needles, IV drug use) | 2 weeks–6 months | **Yes** — majority (~75-85%) become chronic | NO vaccine available; treated with direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) now, very high cure rates; major cause of liver cirrhosis/HCC globally |
| **Hep D (Delta)** | ssRNA, defective/satellite virus | Blood, sexual | Variable | Yes, worsens disease | **Requires Hep B co-infection to replicate** (uses HBsAg as its own coat) — cannot infect alone |
| **Hep E** | (+)ssRNA, Hepevirus | Faecal-oral (contaminated water) | 2–8 weeks | No (except in immunocompromised) | Similar to Hep A clinically, BUT **high mortality in pregnant women** (can exceed 20% in 3rd trimester) — important exam point |
| **Non-A Non-B** | Historical term | — | — | — | Used before Hep C/E were identified as distinct agents; now mostly replaced by these specific diagnoses |

**Clinical course pattern to remember for essays**: Acute hepatitis (any cause) → fever, malaise, nausea, jaundice, dark urine, pale stool, raised liver enzymes (ALT/AST) → either resolves (Hep A, E) or can progress to chronic carrier state/cirrhosis/hepatocellular carcinoma (Hep B, C, D).

**Serological markers — Hep B (commonly examined)**:
- **HBsAg** – surface antigen; marker of current infection (acute or chronic)
- **Anti-HBs** – antibody to surface antigen; marker of immunity (from vaccine OR past cleared infection)
- **HBeAg** – marker of active viral replication/high infectivity
- **Anti-HBc IgM** – marker of recent/acute infection
- **Anti-HBc IgG** – marker of past infection (with or without clearance)

## 2. Oncogenic Viruses (Viral Carcinogenesis)

**Key terms — distinguish clearly for exam**:

- **Carcinogenesis** – the overall multi-step process by which normal cells become cancerous
- **Oncogenesis** – specifically the development of a tumor through activation of oncogenes/inactivation of tumor suppressor genes
- **Tumorigenesis** – the formation and progression of a tumor mass

**How viruses cause cancer (general mechanisms — important for essay answers)**:
1. **Insertional mutagenesis** – viral genome integrates near a host proto-oncogene, disrupting normal control and activating it
2. **Viral oncogenes** – virus carries its own cancer-causing genes (e.g. HPV's E6 and E7 proteins)
3. **Inactivation of tumor suppressor genes** – e.g. HPV E6 degrades p53; E7 inactivates Rb (retinoblastoma protein) — both are critical "brakes" on cell division
4. **Chronic inflammation** – persistent infection/inflammation (e.g. chronic Hep B/C) damages tissue and promotes cell turnover, increasing mutation risk
5. **Immunosuppression** – some oncogenic viruses act mainly by suppressing host immunity, allowing other oncogenic processes to proceed unchecked (e.g. EBV in immunocompromised patients)

**Major human oncogenic viruses — table form**:

| Virus | Associated Cancer(s) | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **HPV (Human Papillomavirus)** types 16, 18 | Cervical cancer, also anal/oropharyngeal cancers | E6 degrades p53; E7 inactivates Rb |
| **HSV / VZV** (mentioned in outline re: latent infection) | Not strongly oncogenic themselves, but their latency mechanism is the model studied alongside oncogenic herpesviruses | Establish lifelong latency in neurons; reactivate under stress/immunosuppression |
| **CMV** | Implicated in some cancers (e.g. glioblastoma) — less definitive than others; well known for congenital infection | Possible oncomodulation (alters tumor microenvironment) rather than direct transformation |
| **EBV (Epstein-Barr Virus)** | Burkitt lymphoma, Nasopharyngeal carcinoma, Hodgkin lymphoma | Infects B cells; drives them to proliferate continuously (immortalization); associated with c-myc translocation in Burkitt lymphoma |
| **Hepatitis B & C** | Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) | Chronic inflammation + (for HBV) insertional mutagenesis into host genome |
| **HHV-8** | Kaposi's sarcoma | Drives proliferation of endothelial cells, especially in immunosuppressed (HIV/AIDS) patients |
| **HTLV-1** (retrovirus, often grouped here) | Adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma | Tax protein interferes with cell cycle control |

**Exam tip**: If asked "discuss oncogenic viruses," structure your answer as: (1) define carcinogenesis/oncogenesis/tumorigenesis, (2) explain general mechanisms, (3) go through 3–4 specific viruses with their mechanism + cancer, (4) mention prevention (HPV vaccine, HBV vaccine = cancer prevention vaccines).

## 3. Arboviruses (Arthropod-Borne Viruses)

**Definition**: Viruses transmitted to humans by arthropod vectors (mosquitoes, ticks). Not a taxonomic group — a transmission-based grouping. Most are zoonotic (animal reservoir, humans often incidental/"dead-end" hosts).

| Virus | Vector | Family | Key clinical features |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Chikungunya** | *Aedes* mosquito | Togavirus | Severe joint pain (arthralgia) that can persist for months; fever, rash |
| **Dengue** | *Aedes* mosquito | Flavivirus | Fever, severe headache, retro-orbital pain, rash; **Dengue Hemorrhagic Fever/Shock Syndrome** on repeat infection with different serotype (antibody-dependent enhancement) |
| **Eastern Equine Encephalitis (EEE)** | Mosquito | Togavirus (Alphavirus) | Severe encephalitis, high mortality, mainly in horses & humans in the Americas |
| **St. Louis Encephalitis** | *Culex* mosquito | Flavivirus | Encephalitis, especially in older adults; USA |
| **West Nile Virus** | *Culex* mosquito | Flavivirus | Most infections mild/asymptomatic; can cause encephalitis/meningitis especially in elderly/immunocompromised; birds are reservoir |
| **Yellow Fever** | *Aedes* mosquito | Flavivirus | Jaundice (hence "yellow"), hemorrhagic fever, can be severe with liver failure; vaccine available (live attenuated) |
| **Zika** | *Aedes* mosquito | Flavivirus | Usually mild in adults; **major concern is congenital Zika syndrome** — microcephaly when mother infected during pregnancy |

**Exam tip**: Group by vector and by family — many arboviruses are Flaviviruses (Dengue, Zika, West Nile, Yellow Fever, St. Louis encephalitis), so know what they share: (+)ssRNA, enveloped, mosquito-borne, can cause hemorrhagic fever or encephalitis depending on the specific virus and host factors.

## 4. HIV (Human Immunodeficiency Virus) — High-Yield Section

**Classification**: Retrovirus → genus **Lentivirus** ("slow virus" — long incubation before AIDS develops). Baltimore Class VI (ssRNA-RT). Enveloped, icosahedral capsid containing two copies of (+)ssRNA genome plus reverse transcriptase enzyme.

**History (brief, for context in essays)**:
- First clinically recognized in 1981 (USA) — clusters of unusual *Pneumocystis* pneumonia and Kaposi's sarcoma in young men
- HIV identified as the causative agent by 1983-84 (Montagnier, Gallo)
- Believed to have originated from zoonotic transmission of SIV (Simian Immunodeficiency Virus) from primates in Africa

**HIV Structure**:
- Two glycoproteins on envelope: **gp120** (binds CD4 receptor) and **gp41** (mediates membrane fusion)
- Core enzymes carried in virion: **Reverse transcriptase**, **Integrase**, **Protease**

**HIV Replication Cycle (Life Cycle) — special/distinguishing features**:
1. **Attachment** – gp120 binds CD4 receptor on host cell (T-helper cells, macrophages, dendritic cells) PLUS a co-receptor (CCR5 or CXCR4)
2. **Fusion/Penetration** – gp41 mediates fusion of viral envelope with host membrane
3. **Reverse transcription** – viral RNA genome converted into dsDNA by reverse transcriptase (error-prone → high mutation rate, drives drug resistance and immune evasion)
4. **Integration** – viral DNA integrates permanently into host genome via integrase, forming a **provirus** — this is why HIV can never be fully eradicated from an infected person, only suppressed
5. **Transcription & translation** – host machinery transcribes provirus, viral proteins synthesized
6. **Assembly** – immature virions bud from cell surface
7. **Maturation** – viral protease cleaves precursor proteins into functional ones, creating an infectious mature virion (this step is OUTSIDE the cell, after budding — key fact, distinguishes maturation from assembly)

**Natural history of untreated HIV infection (clinical course — common essay)**:
- **Acute/seroconversion phase** – flu-like illness 2–4 weeks post-infection, high viral load, CD4 count dips transiently
- **Clinical latency (chronic phase)** – can last years; virus still replicating but immune system keeps pace; CD4 count slowly declines
- **AIDS (Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome)** – CD4 count drops below 200 cells/µL (or development of AIDS-defining opportunistic infections/cancers, e.g. *Pneumocystis* pneumonia, Kaposi's sarcoma, candidiasis, TB, CMV retinitis)

**Epidemiology** (general points to mention): Sub-Saharan Africa carries the highest global burden; transmission via sexual contact, blood/blood products, mother-to-child (vertical), needle-sharing; prevention via ART for prevention of mother-to-child transmission (PMTCT), condom use, PrEP (pre-exposure prophylaxis), male circumcision (reduces female-to-male transmission risk).

**Antiretroviral Therapy (ART) — drug classes and mechanism**:

| Drug Class | Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **NRTIs** (Nucleoside Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors) | Chain-terminate viral DNA synthesis during reverse transcription | Zidovudine (AZT), Lamivudine, Tenofovir |
| **NNRTIs** (Non-Nucleoside RT Inhibitors) | Bind directly to reverse transcriptase enzyme, block its activity (different binding site from NRTIs) | Efavirenz, Nevirapine |
| **Protease Inhibitors (PIs)** | Block viral protease → prevents cleavage of precursor proteins → produces immature, non-infectious virions | Ritonavir, Lopinavir, Atazanavir |
| **Integrase Inhibitors** | Block integrase → prevents viral DNA from integrating into host genome | Raltegravir, Dolutegravir |
| **Entry/Fusion Inhibitors** | Block gp41-mediated fusion, or block CCR5 co-receptor | Enfuvirtide (fusion), Maraviroc (CCR5 antagonist) |

**Why combination therapy (HAART/cART) is used**: HIV mutates extremely fast (error-prone reverse transcriptase, no proofreading) — using a single drug rapidly selects for resistant mutants. Combining 3+ drugs from different classes makes it statistically very unlikely the virus develops resistance to all simultaneously.

**Challenges for HIV vaccine production** (classic essay question — know this well):
1. **Extremely high mutation/antigenic variability** — reverse transcriptase has no proofreading, so the virus constantly changes its surface proteins, making it a "moving target" for antibodies
2. **Integration into host genome** — once integrated as a provirus, it becomes part of the host's own DNA and hides from immune surveillance (latent reservoir)
3. **Destroys the very cells needed to fight it** — HIV infects and kills CD4+ helper T cells, which are essential for coordinating an effective immune/vaccine response
4. **Lack of a clear correlate of protection** — unlike other diseases, no natural human has ever fully cleared HIV infection, so scientists don't have a known "successful immune response" blueprint to replicate with a vaccine
5. **Glycan shield** — gp120 is heavily coated in sugar molecules that physically hide it from antibodies
6. **Latent reservoirs in resting cells** — virus can remain dormant in long-lived memory T cells, invisible to both immune system and most antivirals, for the patient's lifetime
7. **Genetic diversity across global subtypes (clades)** — different HIV subtypes dominate in different regions, complicating a single universal vaccine
