Q37. Discuss Ovarian Carcinoma: Risk Factors, Pathogenesis, Classification, Morphology, Staging
**Risk factors:**
- Nulliparity / low parity
- Early menarche, late menopause (prolonged uninterrupted ovulation — "incessant ovulation" theory)
- Family history, BRCA1/2 mutations, Lynch syndrome (HNPCC)
- Infertility, endometriosis (→ endometrioid/clear cell subtype)
- Increasing age (peak 60s)
- Protective: OCP use, multiparity, breastfeeding, tubal ligation (interrupt ovulation cycle)

**Pathogenesis:**
- "Incessant ovulation" theory — repeated ovulation → repeated epithelial trauma/repair at ovarian surface → accumulated mutations
- BRCA1/2 mutations → defective homologous recombination DNA repair → genomic instability
- Two-pathway model:
 - **Type I (low-grade)** — slow, stepwise progression from borderline tumours; KRAS, BRAF mutations; indolent
 - **Type II (high-grade serous)** — most common and aggressive; arises from fallopian tube fimbrial epithelium (STIC lesions); early, near-universal **TP53** mutation; rapid, aggressive spread
- Endometrioid/clear cell subtypes arise from endometriosis-associated malignant transformation

**Classification (with examples):**
- **Surface epithelial tumours** (~65-70% of all, majority of malignant) — serous, mucinous, endometrioid, clear cell, Brenner
- **Germ cell tumours** (young women) — mature teratoma (benign), dysgerminoma, yolk sac tumour, choriocarcinoma
- **Sex cord-stromal tumours** — granulosa cell (oestrogen-secreting), Sertoli-Leydig (androgen-secreting), fibroma/thecoma
- **Metastatic** — Krukenberg tumour (bilateral, signet-ring cells, usually gastric primary)

**Morphology:**
- Serous cystadenocarcinoma — cystic, papillary excrescences, **psammoma bodies** (concentric calcifications) on histology, often bilateral
- Mucinous — multiloculated, mucin-filled cysts, tall columnar mucin-secreting epithelium
- Clear cell — hobnail cells, clear cytoplasm, "hobnail" pattern
- Malignant features generally: nuclear atypia, high mitotic rate, stromal invasion, necrosis

**Staging (FIGO, simplified):**
- Stage I — confined to ovary/ovaries
- Stage II — pelvic extension (uterus, tubes, other pelvic tissue)
- Stage III — peritoneal spread beyond pelvis and/or retroperitoneal/inguinal lymph nodes
- Stage IV — distant metastasis (liver parenchyma, lung, etc.)

**Spread pattern:** transcoelomic (peritoneal seeding) is the dominant/classic route, unlike most other cancers where haematogenous/lymphatic spread dominates early

**Tumour markers:** CA-125 (epithelial, monitoring not screening — poor specificity), AFP (yolk sac), β-hCG (choriocarcinoma), inhibin (granulosa cell)

**Clinical features:** vague — bloating, pelvic pain, early satiety, urinary frequency ("silent killer," usually presents late, Stage III at diagnosis)

Q38. Discuss Colorectal Carcinoma: Risk Factors, Pathogenesis, Morphology, Staging
**Risk factors:**
- Age >50
- Low-fibre, high-fat/red meat diet
- IBD (ulcerative colitis > Crohn's, risk rises with disease duration/extent)
- Family history — FAP, HNPCC/Lynch syndrome
- Smoking, alcohol, obesity, sedentary lifestyle
- Type 2 diabetes

**Pathogenesis (two distinct molecular pathways):**
- **Chromosomal instability pathway (adenoma-carcinoma sequence, ~80%)**
 - **APC** gene mutation (tumour suppressor) → initiates adenoma formation (loss of both alleles — "two-hit")
 - **KRAS** mutation → adenoma growth/progression
 - **p53** mutation → progression to invasive carcinoma
 - Sequence: normal mucosa → small adenoma → large/villous adenoma with high-grade dysplasia → invasive carcinoma
- **Microsatellite instability (MSI) pathway (~15-20%)**
 - Defective DNA mismatch repair genes (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2)
 - Underlies **Lynch syndrome (HNPCC)** — autosomal dominant, right-sided predominance, earlier onset, less step-wise polyp progression
- FAP — germline APC mutation → hundreds to thousands of colonic polyps from adolescence → near-100% progression to cancer if colon not removed

**Morphology:**
- Right-sided (caecum/ascending colon) — exophytic, polypoid, fungating masses; wider lumen tolerates growth without obstruction → presents late with occult bleeding/anaemia
- Left-sided (descending/sigmoid) — annular, "napkin-ring" constricting lesions; narrower lumen → presents earlier with obstruction, altered bowel habit, blood-streaked stool
- Microscopically: gland-forming adenocarcinoma (majority), mucinous subtype (worse prognosis), signet-ring subtype (worse prognosis)

**Staging (TNM, correlating with old Dukes' staging):**
- Stage I — invades submucosa/muscularis propria, no nodal spread (Dukes
A)
- Stage II — invades through muscularis into serosa/pericolic tissue, no nodes (Dukes
B)
- Stage III — any depth with regional lymph node involvement (Dukes
C)
- Stage IV — distant metastasis, classically liver (via portal venous drainage) (Dukes
D)

**Tumour marker:** CEA — used for monitoring/recurrence, not screening (low sensitivity/specificity early)

**Screening:** colonoscopy, faecal occult blood test/FIT, starting age 45-50 (earlier if family history)

Q39. Discuss Polycystic Ovarian Syndrome (PCOS): Aetiology, Pathogenesis, Diagnostic Criteria, Complications
**Aetiology:**
- Not fully understood — multifactorial, genetic predisposition + environmental factors (obesity)
- Strong association with insulin resistance (present in both obese and lean PCOS patients)

**Pathogenesis:**
- Central defect: **insulin resistance** → compensatory hyperinsulinaemia
- Hyperinsulinaemia acts on ovarian theca cells → increased androgen production
- Hyperinsulinaemia also suppresses hepatic **SHBG (sex hormone-binding globulin)** production → increases free (bioactive) testosterone
- Disrupted GnRH pulsatility → increased **LH** relative to FSH (elevated **LH:FSH ratio**, classically >2:1)
- Elevated LH → further stimulates theca cell androgen production
- Relatively low FSH → impairs follicular maturation → follicles arrest at various stages → multiple small subcapsular cysts ("string of pearls" on ultrasound)
- Peripheral aromatization of excess androgens to oestrone (in adipose tissue) → chronic unopposed oestrogenic state → disrupts normal feedback, perpetuates anovulation

**Diagnostic criteria (Rotterdam — 2 of 3 required):**
1. Oligo/anovulation
2. Clinical and/or biochemical hyperandrogenism (hirsutism, acne, elevated free testosterone)
3. Polycystic ovaries on ultrasound (≥12 follicles per ovary or increased ovarian volume)

**Clinical features:**
- Oligomenorrhea/amenorrhea, infertility
- Hirsutism, acne, androgenic alopecia
- Obesity (central), acanthosis nigricans (marker of insulin resistance)

**Complications:**
- Infertility (anovulation)
- **Endometrial hyperplasia and endometrial carcinoma** — from chronic unopposed oestrogen exposure (unbalanced by progesterone due to anovulation)
- Type 2 diabetes mellitus, metabolic syndrome
- Dyslipidaemia, cardiovascular disease risk
- Obstructive sleep apnoea
- Psychological — depression, anxiety, body image concerns

**Management principles:** weight loss (first-line, improves insulin sensitivity), metformin (insulin sensitizer), OCPs (regulate cycle, reduce endometrial risk), anti-androgens (hirsutism), clomiphene/letrozole (fertility)

Q40. Discuss Cushing Syndrome: Aetiology, Pathogenesis, Clinical Features, Complications
**Aetiology (causes of excess cortisol):**
- **Exogenous (iatrogenic) — most common cause overall** — long-term corticosteroid therapy
- **Endogenous:**
 - **Cushing's disease** — ACTH-secreting pituitary adenoma (most common endogenous cause, ~70%)
 - **Adrenal Cushing's** — cortisol-secreting adrenal adenoma or carcinoma (ACTH-independent)
 - **Ectopic ACTH syndrome** — paraneoplastic, classically **small cell lung carcinoma**, also bronchial carcinoids

**Pathogenesis:**
- Chronic cortisol excess → catabolic effects on protein/fat metabolism
- Protein catabolism → muscle wasting, thinning of skin, poor wound healing, striae (rupture of dermal collagen)
- Redistribution of fat → central/truncal obesity, "moon face," "buffalo hump" (dorsocervical fat pad), sparing of limbs
- Cortisol antagonizes insulin action → hyperglycaemia, insulin resistance → steroid-induced diabetes
- Mineralocorticoid cross-reactivity (at very high cortisol levels) → sodium/water retention → hypertension, hypokalaemia
- Suppression of immune function → increased infection risk
- Osteoblast inhibition + increased bone resorption → osteoporosis
- In pituitary/ectopic ACTH-driven cases: excess ACTH → bilateral adrenal cortical hyperplasia

**Clinical features:**
- Central obesity, moon face, buffalo hump
- Purple abdominal striae
- Proximal muscle weakness/wasting
- Thin skin, easy bruising, poor wound healing
- Hypertension
- Hyperglycaemia/diabetes
- Hirsutism, menstrual irregularity (androgen co-secretion)
- Osteoporosis, psychiatric disturbance (depression, mood lability)

**Diagnosis:** low-dose dexamethasone suppression test (screening), 24-hour urinary free cortisol, late-night salivary cortisol, then ACTH level ± high-dose dexamethasone suppression to localize source

**Complications:** diabetes, hypertension, osteoporotic fractures, increased cardiovascular risk, increased infection susceptibility, adrenal crisis if steroids abruptly withdrawn

Q41. Discuss Diabetes Mellitus: Classification, Pathogenesis, Complications
**Classification:**
- **Type 1** — autoimmune β-cell destruction, absolute insulin deficiency
- **Type 2** — insulin resistance + relative/progressive insulin deficiency
- **Gestational** — insulin resistance from placental hormones during pregnancy
- **Secondary/other** — pancreatitis, Cushing's, drug-induced (steroids), genetic syndromes (MODY)

**Pathogenesis — Type 1:**
- Genetic susceptibility (HLA-DR3/DR4) + environmental trigger (viral infection proposed)
- Autoimmune T-cell mediated destruction of pancreatic β-cells (islet cell antibodies, anti-GAD antibodies present)
- Progressive β-cell loss → absolute insulin deficiency once ~80-90% destroyed
- Onset typically childhood/young adulthood, prone to **diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)** — absolute insulin lack → unrestrained lipolysis → free fatty acids → hepatic ketogenesis → ketoacidosis

**Pathogenesis — Type 2:**
- Peripheral insulin resistance (skeletal muscle, liver, adipose tissue) — obesity-driven, adipokines (e.g. ↓ adiponectin, ↑ resistin/TNF-α) impair insulin signalling
- Compensatory hyperinsulinaemia initially maintains euglycaemia
- Progressive β-cell exhaustion/failure over time (amyloid deposition in islets — amylin) → declining insulin secretion
- Relative insulin deficiency in the setting of ongoing resistance → hyperglycaemia
- Onset typically adult/older, obesity-associated; prone to **hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state (HHS)** rather than DKA (residual insulin prevents significant ketogenesis)

**Complications:**
- **Microvascular** (hyperglycaemia → non-enzymatic glycation, polyol pathway activation, protein kinase C activation → small vessel damage):
 - Retinopathy (leading cause of adult blindness)
 - Nephropathy (diabetic glomerulosclerosis — Kimmelstiel-Wilson nodules, progresses to CK
D)
 - Neuropathy (peripheral — glove-and-stocking sensory loss; autonomic — gastroparesis, erectile dysfunction)
- **Macrovascular** (accelerated atherosclerosis):
 - Coronary artery disease (leading cause of death in diabetics)
 - Peripheral vascular disease (→ diabetic foot, risk of amputation)
 - Cerebrovascular disease/stroke
- **Acute:** DKA (Type 1), HHS (Type 2), hypoglycaemia (treatment-related)
- Increased infection susceptibility (impaired neutrophil function, vascular compromise)

## Q42. Lung Carcinoma: Risk Factors, Classification, Pathogenesis, Morphology, Staging
**Risk factors:**
- Smoking — by far the strongest (dose and duration dependent), also passive smoke exposure
- Occupational: asbestos (synergistic with smoking), radon, heavy metals
- Chronic lung disease/fibrosis, family history, air pollution
**Classification (major types):**
- **Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC, ~85%)**
 - Adenocarcinoma — most common overall now, peripheral location, includes never-smokers
 - Squamous cell carcinoma — central location, strongly smoking-linked, cavitation common
 - Large cell carcinoma — undifferentiated, poor prognosis
- **Small cell lung carcinoma (SCLC, ~15%)** — central, neuroendocrine origin, highly aggressive, early metastasis
**Pathogenesis:**
- Field cancerization — repeated carcinogen exposure (smoke) → sequential mutations across bronchial epithelium
- Key driver mutations: KRAS (adenocarcinoma), EGFR (adenocarcinoma, especially non-smokers), TP53 (SCLC, near-universal)
- SCLC — neuroendocrine cell origin → paraneoplastic hormone secretion
**Morphology:**
- Squamous — central, cavitating mass, keratin pearls, intercellular bridges on histology
- Adenocarcinoma — peripheral, gland-forming, mucin production
- SCLC — central, small blue cells, high mitotic rate, extensive necrosis
**Paraneoplastic syndromes:**
- Squamous cell — PTHrP → hypercalcaemia
- SCLC — ectopic ACTH (Cushing's), ADH (SIADH), Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome (anti-voltage-gated calcium channel antibodies)
**Staging:** TNM — tumour size/local invasion, nodal spread (hilar → mediastinal), metastasis (brain, bone, liver, adrenal — classic sites); SCLC often alternatively staged as limited vs extensive disease given near-universal metastasis at diagnosis
**Clinical features:** cough, haemoptysis, weight loss, Pancoast tumour (apical — brachial plexus/Horner's syndrome), SVC obstruction

## Q43. Lymphoma: Classification, Pathogenesis, Morphology, Staging
**Classification:**
- **Hodgkin lymphoma** — bimodal age distribution (young adult + >55), contiguous nodal spread
- **Non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL)** — majority B-cell origin, non-contiguous spread, more common overall
**Pathogenesis:**
- Hodgkin — **Reed-Sternberg cells** (binucleate, "owl-eye" nuclei, derived from germinal centre B-cells) drive an inflammatory reactive microenvironment; EBV implicated in some subtypes
- Burkitt lymphoma — **t(8;14)** → c-MYC overexpression, EBV-associated (endemic African form), "starry sky" pattern (macrophages engulfing apoptotic tumour cells amid sheets of lymphocytes)
- Follicular lymphoma — **t(14;18)** → BCL-2 overexpression → resistance to apoptosis
- Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) — most common aggressive NHL, sheets of large atypical lymphocytes
**Morphology:**
- Hodgkin — subtypes: nodular sclerosis (most common, lacunar cells, broad fibrous bands), mixed cellularity, lymphocyte-rich, lymphocyte-depleted (worst prognosis)
- NHL — variable, defined by cell of origin and architecture (nodular/follicular vs diffuse)
**Staging (Ann Arbor, used for both):**
- Stage I — single lymph node region
- Stage II — two or more regions, same side of diaphragm
- Stage III — regions on both sides of diaphragm
- Stage IV — disseminated, extranodal organ involvement (liver, bone marrow)
- Suffix: A (no systemic symptoms) vs B (B symptoms — fever, night sweats, weight loss — worse prognosis)
**Clinical features:** painless lymphadenopathy, B symptoms, hepatosplenomegaly (advanced)

## Q44. Leukemia: Classification, Pathogenesis, Morphology
**Classification:**
- Acute (blast proliferation, rapid course) vs chronic (mature cell proliferation, indolent)
- Lymphoid vs myeloid lineage
- Four main types: ALL, AML, CLL, CML
**Pathogenesis:**
- **ALL** — lymphoblast clonal proliferation, arrests maturation; most common childhood malignancy overall; TdT+ marker
- **AML** — myeloblast clonal proliferation; adults; associated with prior chemo/radiation, myelodysplasia; **Auer rods** (crystallized granules) diagnostic
- **CML** — **Philadelphia chromosome, t(9;22)** → BCR-ABL fusion gene → constitutively active tyrosine kinase → uncontrolled granulocyte proliferation; targeted therapy: imatinib (tyrosine kinase inhibitor)
- **CLL** — clonal proliferation of mature but dysfunctional B-lymphocytes; elderly; often indolent/incidental finding; **smudge cells** on blood film characteristic
**Morphology/lab findings:**
- Acute leukemias — bone marrow replaced by blasts (>20% blasts defines acute leukemia), pancytopenia from marrow crowding-out → anaemia, thrombocytopenia, neutropenia despite high WBC count
- CML — marked leukocytosis with full spectrum of granulocyte maturation stages, low leukocyte alkaline phosphatase (LAP score) distinguishes from leukemoid reaction
**Clinical features:** fatigue (anaemia), infections (neutropenia/dysfunctional WBCs), bleeding/bruising (thrombocytopenia), bone pain, lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly

## Q45. Stroke (Ischemic): Pathogenesis, Types, Complications
**Types:**
- Ischemic (~85%) — thrombotic, embolic, or hypoperfusion (watershed)
- Haemorrhagic (~15%) — intracerebral or subarachnoid
**Pathogenesis of ischemic injury (cascade):**
- Vessel occlusion → loss of blood flow → ATP depletion within minutes
- Failure of Na+/K+-ATPase pump → ionic gradient breakdown → cellular (cytotoxic) oedema
- Excess glutamate release → excitotoxicity via NMDA receptor overactivation
- Massive Ca²⁺ influx → activation of destructive enzymes (proteases, lipases, endonucleases)
- Free radical generation → membrane lipid peroxidation
- Result: **liquefactive necrosis** (unique to CNS infarcts vs coagulative necrosis elsewhere in the body — due to high lipid content and lack of structural collagen framework)
- Core (irreversibly infarcted) vs ischemic penumbra (hypoperfused, salvageable with timely reperfusion — the therapeutic window target)
**Risk factors:** hypertension (strongest), atrial fibrillation (embolic source), atherosclerosis, diabetes, smoking, hyperlipidaemia
**Complications:** cerebral oedema → raised ICP → herniation, haemorrhagic transformation, seizures, aspiration pneumonia, DVT/PE (immobility)

## Q46. Subdural vs Epidural Haematoma
**Subdural haematoma**
- Source: rupture of **bridging veins** (venous bleed, low pressure)
- Location: between dura and arachnoid
- CT appearance: crescent-shaped (concave), crosses suture lines
- Onset: slower, may be delayed days-weeks (chronic subdural in elderly/brain atrophy)
- Risk groups: elderly (brain atrophy stretches bridging veins), shaken baby syndrome, alcoholics
**Epidural haematoma**
- Source: rupture of **middle meningeal artery** (arterial bleed, high pressure)
- Location: between dura and skull
- CT appearance: biconvex/lens-shaped, does not cross suture lines (limited by dural attachments)
- Onset: rapid, classic **"lucid interval"** (brief consciousness after initial trauma, then rapid deterioration as haematoma expands)
- Associated: temporal bone fracture (overlies middle meningeal artery)
- Surgical emergency — rapid expansion can cause herniation quickly

## Q47. Alzheimer's vs Parkinson's Disease
**Alzheimer's disease**
- Cortical, diffuse — hippocampus and association cortices affected early
- Pathology: extracellular **amyloid-β plaques** + intracellular **tau neurofibrillary tangles**
- Cholinergic deficit (loss of basal forebrain cholinergic neurons) — basis of cholinesterase inhibitor treatment
- Clinical: progressive memory loss first, then language, visuospatial, executive dysfunction
- Risk factors: age (strongest), APOE ε4 allele, Down syndrome (amyloid precursor protein gene on chromosome 21)
**Parkinson's disease**
- Subcortical — **substantia nigra pars compacta** (basal ganglia)
- Pathology: loss of dopaminergic neurons, intracellular **Lewy bodies** (α-synuclein aggregates)
- Dopamine deficiency in nigrostriatal pathway → impaired movement initiation
- Clinical: motor features first — resting tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, postural instability (TRAP); later cognitive decline possible
- Treatment: levodopa/carbidopa (dopamine replacement)

## Q48. TB: Pathogenesis, Primary vs Secondary, Morphology
**Pathogenesis:**
- *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* inhaled → alveolar macrophages ingest but cannot kill (waxy mycolic acid cell wall resists lysosomal degradation)
- Cell-mediated immunity (Th1 response) develops over weeks → granuloma formation (macrophages, epithelioid cells, Langhans giant cells, surrounding lymphocytes) with central **caseous necrosis**
- Granuloma contains but doesn't always eliminate bacteria — can remain dormant (latent T
B)
**Primary TB:**
- First exposure, usually childhood
- **Ghon focus** — subpleural granuloma, typically lower/mid zone
- Combined with hilar lymph node involvement = **Ghon complex**
- Usually self-limited/contained by immune system in immunocompetent hosts
**Secondary (reactivation) TB:**
- Reactivation of latent infection (often with immunosuppression — HIV, malnutrition, ageing, diabetes) or reinfection
- Classic site: **apex of upper lobe** (highest oxygen tension favours this aerobic organism)
- More destructive — cavitation common, extensive caseous necrosis
**Miliary TB:** haematogenous dissemination → numerous small (millet-seed sized) granulomas across multiple organs — occurs with immunocompromise or overwhelming primary infection
**Clinical features:** chronic cough, haemoptysis, night sweats, weight loss, low-grade fever

## Q49. ARDS: Pathogenesis, Causes, Morphology
**Causes:**
- Direct lung injury — pneumonia, aspiration, near-drowning, inhalation injury
- Indirect (systemic) — sepsis (most common overall trigger), trauma, pancreatitis, massive transfusion
**Pathogenesis:**
- Initial insult → activation of alveolar macrophages and neutrophil recruitment
- Release of inflammatory mediators (TNF-α, IL-1, IL-8) → damage to alveolar capillary endothelium and epithelium
- **Diffuse alveolar damage** → increased capillary permeability → protein-rich exudate floods alveoli (non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema, distinguishing it from cardiogenic oedema)
- Exudate + cellular debris → **hyaline membrane formation** lining alveoli
- Surfactant dysfunction → alveolar collapse (atelectasis)
- Result: severe ventilation-perfusion mismatch → refractory hypoxaemia
**Clinical criteria (Berlin definition, conceptually):** acute onset (within 1 week), bilateral infiltrates on imaging, hypoxaemia not fully explained by cardiac failure/fluid overload
**Phases:** exudative (days 1-7, hyaline membranes) → proliferative (fibroblast proliferation, type II pneumocyte hyperplasia attempting repair) → fibrotic (in severe/prolonged cases)

## Q50. Acute Kidney Injury: Classification, Pathogenesis
**Classification:**
- **Prerenal (~most common)** — reduced renal perfusion (hypovolaemia, haemorrhage, heart failure, sepsis) — kidney parenchyma initially intact, reversible if perfusion restored promptly
- **Intrinsic/renal** — direct parenchymal damage:
 - Acute tubular necrosis (ATN) — ischemic (prolonged prerenal state) or toxic (aminoglycosides, contrast, myoglobin/rhabdomyolysis)
 - Acute glomerulonephritis
 - Acute interstitial nephritis (often drug-induced hypersensitivity — NSAIDs, penicillins)
- **Postrenal** — obstruction to urine outflow (stones, BPH, tumour, strictures) — bilateral or single functioning kidney needed for significant rise in creatinine
**Pathogenesis of ATN:**
- Ischemic — sustained hypoperfusion → tubular epithelial cell (especially proximal tubule and thick ascending limb) ATP depletion → cell death, sloughing of epithelium → tubular obstruction by casts + backleak of filtrate
- Toxic — direct tubular epithelial toxicity from nephrotoxins/pigments
**Clinical course of ATN:** oliguric phase → diuretic phase (recovering tubules can't yet concentrate urine) → recovery phase

## Q51. Chronic Kidney Disease: Causes, Pathogenesis, Complications
**Causes:** diabetes mellitus (leading cause), hypertension (second leading), chronic glomerulonephritis, polycystic kidney disease, recurrent obstruction/reflux nephropathy
**Pathogenesis:**
- Progressive irreversible nephron loss (from any cause) → remaining nephrons undergo compensatory hyperfiltration
- Hyperfiltration → increased intraglomerular pressure → glomerular hypertrophy and injury (maladaptive) → progressive glomerulosclerosis
- Self-perpetuating cycle: fewer functioning nephrons → more compensatory stress on survivors → further loss
- Staged by GFR (Stage 1: normal GFR with kidney damage → Stage 5: GFR <15, end-stage renal disease)
**Complications:**
- Anaemia — reduced erythropoietin production
- Renal osteodystrophy — impaired vitamin D activation → hypocalcaemia → secondary hyperparathyroidism → bone disease
- Metabolic acidosis — reduced acid excretion
- Hyperkalaemia — reduced excretion
- Fluid overload, hypertension
- Uraemia — pericarditis, encephalopathy, platelet dysfunction (bleeding tendency) at advanced stages

## Q52. Nephritic vs Nephrotic Syndrome
**Nephritic syndrome**
- Mechanism: inflammatory glomerular injury → disrupted glomerular barrier with leukocyte infiltration
- Features: haematuria (RBC casts, dysmorphic RBCs), mild-moderate proteinuria (<3.5g/day), hypertension, oliguria, mild oedema
- Examples: post-streptococcal GN, IgA nephropathy, rapidly progressive GN
**Nephrotic syndrome**
- Mechanism: podocyte/glomerular basement membrane damage → loss of size/charge selectivity without significant inflammation
- Features: heavy proteinuria (>3.5g/day), hypoalbuminaemia, generalized oedema, hyperlipidaemia (compensatory hepatic lipoprotein synthesis), increased infection/thrombosis risk (loss of immunoglobulins/antithrombin III in urine)
- Examples: minimal change disease (children, steroid-responsive), membranous nephropathy, FSGS, diabetic nephropathy

## Q53. Peptic Ulcer Disease: Aetiology, Pathogenesis, Complications
**Aetiology:**
- H. pylori infection — ~90% duodenal, ~70% gastric ulcers
- NSAIDs — inhibit COX-1 → reduced protective prostaglandins
- Zollinger-Ellison syndrome (gastrinoma) — rare, severe/refractory ulcers
**Pathogenesis:**
- H. pylori — urease production neutralizes local acid, allows colonization; induces chronic antral inflammation, increases gastrin release, damages mucosal defence
- NSAIDs — systemic COX-1 inhibition → decreased mucosal prostaglandin (PGE2) → reduced bicarbonate/mucus secretion, reduced mucosal blood flow → breakdown of protective barrier
- Net mechanism: imbalance between aggressive factors (acid, pepsin, H. pylori) and protective factors (mucus-bicarbonate barrier, blood flow, prostaglandins)
**Duodenal vs gastric ulcer:**
- Duodenal — pain relieved by eating, occurs 2-3 hours postprandial, always benign
- Gastric — pain worsened by eating, weight loss common, small malignant potential (must biopsy edges)
**Complications:** haemorrhage (most common), perforation (peritonitis, free air under diaphragm), gastric outlet obstruction (scarring/oedema), malignant transformation (gastric ulcers only)

## Q54. Gastritis: Autoimmune vs H. pylori-associated
**Autoimmune gastritis**
- Location: body and fundus
- Mechanism: autoantibodies against parietal cells and intrinsic factor
- Result: parietal cell destruction → achlorhydria → loss of intrinsic factor → **B12 malabsorption → pernicious anaemia**
- Achlorhydria → compensatory hypergastrinaemia (loss of negative feedback) → G-cell/ECL cell hyperplasia, increased risk of gastric carcinoid tumours
**H. pylori-associated gastritis**
- Location: antrum predominantly (can progress to pangastritis)
- Most common cause of chronic gastritis overall
- Mechanism: direct mucosal damage + chronic inflammatory response
- Complications: peptic ulcer disease, **MALT lymphoma** (chronic antigenic stimulation of mucosal lymphoid tissue), intestinal metaplasia → increased gastric adenocarcinoma risk

## Q55. Oesophageal and Gastric (Cardia) Carcinoma
**Oesophageal SCC**
- Location: upper/middle third
- Risk factors: smoking, alcohol (synergistic), hot beverages, achalasia, caustic strictures
**Oesophageal adenocarcinoma**
- Location: lower third
- Arises from **Barrett's oesophagus** — chronic GERD → squamous-to-columnar intestinal metaplasia → dysplasia → adenocarcinoma sequence
- Rising incidence in Western populations (linked to obesity/reflux)
**Gastric adenocarcinoma**
- **Intestinal type** — gland-forming, associated with H. pylori, intestinal metaplasia, chronic atrophic gastritis; often ulcerating mass, better prognosis
- **Diffuse type** — signet-ring cells (mucin pushes nucleus to periphery), infiltrative growth causing **linitis plastica** ("leather bottle" stomach), not clearly H. pylori-linked, worse prognosis, less differentiated
**Shared clinical features:** dysphagia (oesophageal), weight loss, epigastric pain, iron deficiency anaemia, late presentation typical for both

## Q56. Gallstones: Types, Pathogenesis, Complications
**Types:**
- **Cholesterol stones (majority in Western populations)** — yellow, form from bile supersaturated with cholesterol relative to bile salts/lecithin
- **Pigment stones:**
 - Black — associated with haemolysis (excess unconjugated bilirubin) or cirrhosis, form in sterile bile
 - Brown — associated with biliary tract infection/infestation (bacteria, parasites deconjugate bilirubin), form in infected bile
**Risk factors for cholesterol stones ("5 F's"):** Female, Fat (obesity), Forty (age), Fertile (multiparity), Fair (though genetic/ethnic factors matter more broadly); also rapid weight loss, OCPs/pregnancy (oestrogen increases cholesterol secretion), diabetes
**Pathogenesis:** cholesterol solubility in bile depends on ratio to bile salts and lecithin (Admirand's triangle) — supersaturation → cholesterol crystal nucleation → stone growth; also gallbladder stasis contributes
**Complications:**
- Biliary colic (stone transiently obstructing cystic duct)
- Acute cholecystitis (persistent obstruction → inflammation)
- Choledocholithiasis (stone in common bile duct → obstructive jaundice)
- Ascending cholangitis (infection behind obstruction — Charcot's triad: fever, jaundice, RUQ pain)
- Gallstone pancreatitis (stone obstructing pancreatic duct at ampulla)
- Gallstone ileus (fistula formation, stone causing bowel obstruction — rare)
