# Abnormal Liver Enzymes

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Hepatology**

> **In one line:** "liver function tests" are really **liver blood tests**: the aminotransferases (AST, ALT) mark hepatocyte injury, while ALP and GGT mark cholestasis; true liver function is judged from **albumin and prothrombin time**. The pattern (hepatocellular, cholestatic, mixed or infiltrative), the degree of elevation and the AST:ALT ratio point to the cause and guide the next test.

## What you must know

- Routine liver biochemistry: **bilirubin, AST, ALT, ALP, GGT and total protein**. These tests do not accurately reflect how well the liver works; **synthetic function = prothrombin time and albumin**.
- **ALT and AST** = hepatocellular injury. **ALP and GGT** = cholestasis (obstructive). Compare the rise in AST/ALT with the rise in ALP to name the pattern.
- **ALT is more specific to the liver**; AST is also found in heart, muscle, kidney and brain.
- **ALT greater than AST** in viral hepatitis (AST greater than ALT suggests cirrhosis); **AST greater than ALT** in alcoholic liver disease and steatohepatitis.
- Degree of rise: mild (low hundreds) suggests chronic viral hepatitis or acute alcoholic hepatitis; moderate (high hundreds to thousands) suggests acute viral hepatitis; **above 10,000** suggests shock liver, paracetamol toxicity or severe viral hepatitis.
- **Elevated ALP:** confirm it is hepatic by checking **GGT**; normal GGT with raised ALP points to bone disease or pregnancy.
- **Transaminases can be normal or low in cirrhosis** and metastatic liver disease because few healthy hepatocytes remain.
- Autoantibodies: **AMA** in PBC (over 95%), **SMA/ANA/LKM** in autoimmune hepatitis, **ANCA** in primary sclerosing cholangitis (which is linked to IBD).

## 1. Interpreting liver blood tests: the pattern

Upper limits of normal quoted in the lecture: ALT 30 IU/L, ALP 130 IU/L.

| Pattern | Biochemistry |
|---|---|
| **Hepatocellular injury** | predominant rise in AST and ALT; ALP and direct bilirubin may rise a little; indirect bilirubin may rise in advanced disease |
| **Obstruction / cholestasis** | predominant rise in **ALP and direct bilirubin**, with AST and ALT mildly raised at most |
| **Infiltrative disease** | predominant rise in **ALP**, with or without direct bilirubin |
| **Jaundice** | pre-hepatic, intra-hepatic or post-hepatic |

- If ALT and AST are raised out of proportion to ALP, the cause is **hepatocellular**.
  - Example 1: ALT and AST 200 (more than 7 times normal) with ALP 200 (about 1 times normal rise) is **hepatocellular**.
  - Example 2: ALP 500 with ALT 60 and AST 70 is **cholestatic**.
- Sometimes the pattern is **mixed**; go back to the clinical picture to decide.

> **Remember:** primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) is cholestatic, yet some aminotransferase rise occurs because of periportal and lobular necrosis.

## 2. Causes by pattern

| Hepatocellular | Cholestatic | Infiltrative |
|---|---|---|
| viral hepatitis (acute or chronic) | **Extrahepatic (obstructive):** biliary stones; strictures (malignant, peri-ampullary tumours, e.g. carcinoma in the head of the pancreas; primary sclerosing cholangitis; AIDS cholangiopathy) | granulomatous disease (TB, sarcoidosis, lymphoma) |
| alcoholic (ASH) and non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) | **Intrahepatic (non-obstructive):** PBC, small duct PSC, cystic fibrosis, sepsis, total parenteral nutrition, drugs, intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy | amyloidosis |
| drugs (paracetamol overdose, idiosyncratic reactions) | | |
| vascular injury (hypotension, outflow obstruction) | | |
| autoimmune hepatitis, metabolic disease, pregnancy-related causes | | |

### Approach to suspected liver disease

1. Take history and examine; check for risk factors (alcohol, drugs, viral exposure, family history).
2. Decide hepatocellular versus cholestatic from the enzyme pattern.
3. **Hepatocellular:** viral serology (HBsAg, anti-HCV), autoantibodies and immunoglobulins, iron studies, caeruloplasmin, alpha-1 antitrypsin, ultrasound; liver biopsy if unclear.
4. **Cholestatic:** **ultrasound** first to look for duct dilatation. If dilated, find the level and cause with **MRCP**, **endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)** or **ERCP**. If ducts are not dilated, think of intrahepatic cholestasis (AMA for PBC, drugs, ANCA/MRCP for PSC).

## 3. Individual tests

### Aminotransferases (AST and ALT)

- Enzymes within hepatocytes that leak into blood with cell damage.
- **AST:** about 80% mitochondrial, 20% cytoplasmic; also in heart, muscle, kidney and brain. High in hepatic necrosis, **myocardial infarction, muscle injury, congestive cardiac failure**.
- **ALT:** cytosolic and **more specific for the liver**, so a rise occurs only with liver disease.

**AST:ALT ratio**
- Viral hepatitis: ALT above AST, **unless cirrhosis is present, when AST exceeds ALT** (a ratio above 1 in viral hepatitis suggests cirrhosis).
- Alcoholic liver disease and steatohepatitis: AST often above ALT (classically AST:ALT above 2 in alcoholic hepatitis).

**Degree of elevation**

| Degree | Level | Think of |
|---|---|---|
| Mild | low hundreds | chronic viral hepatitis, acute alcoholic hepatitis |
| Moderate | high hundreds to thousands | acute viral hepatitis |
| Severe | above 10,000 | extensive necrosis: **ischaemic (shock) liver**, **paracetamol (acetaminophen) toxicity**, severe viral hepatitis |

**Causes in an asymptomatic patient (mnemonic):** autoimmune hepatitis; hepatitis B; hepatitis C; drugs or toxins; ethanol; fatty liver (hypertriglyceridaemia); growths (tumours); haemodynamic disorders (e.g. heart failure); iron (haemochromatosis), copper (Wilson's disease) and alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency.

### Alkaline phosphatase (ALP)

- Found in hepatic canalicular and sinusoidal membranes and also in **bone, intestine and placenta**, so it is not specific.
- Raised in **obstruction to bile flow** (cholestasis) anywhere in the biliary tree; a normal level makes cholestasis unlikely.
- A tenfold rise suggests extrahepatic biliary obstruction or intrahepatic cholestasis (e.g. PBC, drug-induced).
- **Check GGT:** raised GGT with raised ALP confirms a hepatic origin; normal GGT with raised ALP suggests **pregnancy or bone disease**.

### Gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT)

- Confirms that a raised ALP is hepatic.
- A useful guide to alcohol intake (raised in heavy drinkers and by enzyme-inducing drugs).

### Albumin

- A marker of synthetic function and of the severity of **chronic** liver disease; a falling level is a bad prognostic sign.
- Also low in nephrotic syndrome, malnutrition and inflammatory states (burns, sepsis, trauma).

### Prothrombin time (PT)

- A marker of synthetic function; its short half-life makes it sensitive in **acute and chronic** disease.
- The liver makes clotting factors I, II, V, VII, IX, X, XII and XIII.
- PT is not prolonged until most synthetic capacity has been lost (advanced disease). A PT that does not correct with vitamin K points to hepatocellular failure rather than cholestasis (vitamin K malabsorption).

### Serum autoantibodies

| Antibody | Disease |
|---|---|
| **Anti-mitochondrial (AMA)** | **PBC** (over 95%) |
| Anti-nuclear, **smooth muscle (SMA)**, liver/kidney microsomal | **autoimmune hepatitis** (often high titre) |
| **ANCA** | **primary sclerosing cholangitis** (associated with IBD) |

## 4. Cirrhosis: key points

- Pathology: loss of normal hepatic architecture, bridging fibrosis and nodular regeneration.
- **Causes:** chronic alcohol use, NASH, chronic hepatitis B and C, autoimmune disease (autoimmune hepatitis, PBC, PSC), genetic disorders (haemochromatosis, Wilson's disease), drugs (amiodarone, methyldopa, methotrexate), Budd-Chiari syndrome, cryptogenic (about 20%).
- **Signs of chronic liver disease:** leuconychia, clubbing, palmar erythema, hyperdynamic circulation, Dupuytren's contracture, spider naevi, xanthelasma, gynaecomastia, testicular atrophy, loss of body hair; hepatomegaly early, a shrunken liver late.
- **Investigations:** FBC, LFTs, clotting (raised INR), iron studies, hepatitis serology, immunoglobulins, autoantibodies, AFP, caeruloplasmin, alpha-1 antitrypsin; ultrasound with Doppler; MRI; **ascitic tap** (culture, protein, LDH, glucose, cell count for spontaneous bacterial peritonitis); **liver biopsy confirms**.
- **Complications:** (1) liver failure: coagulopathy, encephalopathy, hypoalbuminaemia, sepsis, hypoglycaemia; (2) portal hypertension: ascites, splenomegaly, varices, portosystemic shunts; (3) hepatocellular carcinoma.
- **Management:** remove the cause; treat symptoms (colestyramine for pruritus, antiviral therapy for hepatitis C, penicillamine for Wilson's disease); ascites: fluid restriction, low-salt diet, spironolactone; treat suspected SBP empirically with antibiotics. **Liver transplantation** is definitive (five-year survival from about 20% to 70% in end-stage disease).

> **Check:** the lecture mentions interferon-alpha for hepatitis C-related cirrhosis; this is obsolete, and direct-acting antivirals are now used.

## 5. Worked cases

1. **19-year-old with transient jaundice after exertion, bilirubin 37 µmol/L, normal FBC and reticulocytes:** **Gilbert's syndrome** (reduced UDP-glucuronosyltransferase activity, unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia, triggered by fasting, illness, exercise, stress). **Reassure**; normal reticulocytes exclude haemolysis, so no haematology referral, steroids or imaging.
2. **54-year-old woman with jaundice, RUQ pain, dark urine, pale stools; bilirubin 40, ALT 40, AST 50, ALP 350:** disproportionate ALP rise = **cholestasis; gallstones** are most likely (no weight loss argues against pancreatic head cancer; hepatitis would raise ALT and AST).
3. **Best imaging for suspected recurrent gallstones:** **abdominal ultrasound** (sensitive, fast, no radiation, cheap). Only about 10% of stones are radiopaque, CT is less sensitive, and ERCP is invasive and reserved for common bile duct stones.
4. **46-year-old with AF, hepatomegaly, AST 111, ALT 55, ALP 98:** hepatocellular injury from a drug; **amiodarone** is the culprit (with methyldopa and methotrexate).

## Key points to revise

1. "LFTs" are liver blood tests; true synthetic function is albumin and PT.
2. AST/ALT mean hepatocellular injury; ALP/GGT mean cholestasis; compare the two rises to name the pattern.
3. ALT is liver-specific; AST also comes from heart and muscle.
4. AST:ALT above 1 in viral hepatitis suggests cirrhosis; AST above ALT in alcohol; GGT also guides alcohol intake.
5. Very high transaminases (above 10,000) mean shock liver, paracetamol toxicity or severe viral hepatitis.
6. Raised ALP with normal GGT points to bone or pregnancy.
7. AMA = PBC; SMA/ANA/LKM = autoimmune hepatitis; ANCA = PSC.
8. In cholestasis, start with ultrasound, then MRCP/EUS/ERCP; for gallstones ultrasound is first-line.
