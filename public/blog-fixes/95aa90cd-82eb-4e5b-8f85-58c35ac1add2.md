# Nephrotic Syndrome in Children

**Year 4 · Paediatrics · Paediatric Nephrology**

> **In one line:** nephrotic syndrome is heavy proteinuria from a leaky glomerular filter (podocyte injury with foot process effacement), producing hypoalbuminaemia, oedema and hyperlipidaemia; in children it is usually **minimal change disease**, which responds to **corticosteroids**, but relapses are common and the complications (infection, thrombosis, steroid toxicity) cause most of the harm.

## What you must know

- **Definition**: proteinuria above **3.5 g/24 h** or **urine protein:creatinine ratio above 2**, with **albumin 2.5 g/dL or less**, **oedema** and **cholesterol above 200 mg/dL**.
- Incidence 1 to 3 per 100,000 children under 16 years; **90% idiopathic**, and **minimal change disease (MCNS)** is the commonest cause (about 85% of childhood cases; 85 to 90% of those under 6 years).
- MCNS: normal light microscopy, negative immunofluorescence, **foot process effacement** on EM, over 95% steroid-responsive.
- Typical child: boy aged 2 to 6 years, periorbital oedema, **no hypertension and no gross haematuria**.
- Start **prednisolone 60 mg/m2/day (2 mg/kg/day, maximum 60 mg)** for 4 to 6 weeks, then alternate-day for 8 weeks to 5 months. Biopsy is not needed in a typical child aged 1 to 8 years.
- Complications: **infection (pneumococcus, spontaneous bacterial peritonitis)**, **thrombosis**, hypovolaemia, steroid effects.
- **Steroid-resistant** disease is usually **FSGS** and carries about a 50% risk of end-stage kidney disease within 5 years if no remission.

## 1. Definition and epidemiology

- Nephrotic syndrome is the clinical picture of glomerular disease with heavy (**nephrotic-range**) proteinuria.
- Nephrotic range: **above 3.5 g/24 hours** or **urine protein:creatinine ratio (UPCR) above 2** (mg/mg).
- The triad that follows from urinary protein loss:
  1. **Hypoalbuminaemia** (2.5 g/dL or less).
  2. **Oedema**.
  3. **Hyperlipidaemia** (cholesterol above 200 mg/dL).
- Affects **1 to 3 per 100,000 children** under 16 years.
- Untreated, it carries a high risk of death, most commonly from infection.
- About **80% of children respond to corticosteroids**.

## 2. Aetiology

### 2.1 Primary (idiopathic): about 90% of childhood cases

No identifiable cause. Glomerular lesions:

- **Minimal change disease** (most common)
- **Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS)**
- **Mesangial proliferation**
- **Membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis**
- **C3 glomerulopathy**
- **Membranous nephropathy**

### 2.2 Secondary

- Systemic lupus erythematosus, Henoch-Schonlein purpura.
- Malignancy: lymphoma and leukaemia.
- Infections: hepatitis B and C, HIV, **malaria** (quartan malaria is the classic cause in Africa), also syphilis, schistosomiasis.
- Drugs (NSAIDs, gold, penicillamine).

### 2.3 Hereditary

- Mutations in genes that code for components of the glomerular filtration apparatus (for example nephrin, podocin). Think of this in infants under 1 year and in children with a family history.

> **Remember:** in Kenya, always consider secondary causes (HIV, hepatitis B, malaria) when the child is atypical, steroid-resistant, or has haematuria or hypertension.

## 3. Pathogenesis

### 3.1 The podocyte

- The basic defect is **increased permeability of the glomerular capillary wall**, causing massive proteinuria and hypoalbuminaemia.
- The **podocyte** is a highly differentiated epithelial cell on the outside of the capillary loop. Its **foot processes** end on the glomerular basement membrane (GBM) and interdigitate with those of neighbouring podocytes, linked by the **slit diaphragm**.
- Functions: structural support of the capillary loop, a major component of the **protein filtration barrier**, and synthesis and repair of the GBM.
- In all forms, immune and non-immune insults cause **foot process effacement**, loss of functional podocytes and altered slit diaphragm integrity. The end result is leak of protein into the urine.

### 3.2 Role of the immune system

- MCNS can follow **viral infections** and allergen exposure.
- It is also seen in children with **Hodgkin and T-cell lymphoma**.
- Response to immunosuppression (corticosteroids, cyclosporine) is indirect evidence that immune dysfunction (likely a circulating factor or T-cell abnormality) contributes.

## 4. Clinical consequences

### 4.1 Oedema

- The commonest presenting symptom. Exact mechanism is uncertain: low plasma oncotic pressure with underfilling (classic) and primary renal sodium retention (overfill) both contribute.
- Therapy aims at **gradual** reduction with judicious diuretics, sodium restriction, and cautious IV albumin if indicated.

### 4.2 Hyperlipidaemia

- Raised **cholesterol, triglycerides, LDL and VLDL**; HDL unchanged or low.
- Due to **increased hepatic synthesis** and **decreased catabolism** of lipids (and loss of lipoprotein lipase).

### 4.3 Infection

- Susceptible to **cellulitis, spontaneous bacterial peritonitis and bacteraemia**.
- Causes: **hypogammaglobulinaemia** (urinary IgG loss) and loss of complement factors (**C3, C5, factors B and D**), which impairs opsonisation.
- Special risk from **encapsulated bacteria**, especially **pneumococcus**.
- **Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis**: fever, abdominal pain, peritoneal signs. Pneumococcus is the commonest cause but Gram-negatives cause many cases. **Peritoneal fluid leucocytes above 250 cells/microlitre** strongly suggest SBP.
- Any child with nephrotic syndrome and fever must be evaluated aggressively, cultures drawn, and given prompt **empirical antibiotics**.

### 4.4 Hypercoagulability

- Causes: haemoconcentration and stasis from intravascular depletion, **increased platelet number and aggregability**, changes in clotting factors, raised hepatic **fibrinogen**, and urinary loss of **antithrombin III and protein S**.
- Venous thrombosis in any bed: **cerebral venous sinus, renal vein, pulmonary vessels**.

### 4.5 Others

- Hypovolaemia (acute kidney injury, shock), growth failure, malnutrition, hypocalcaemia (loss of vitamin D binding protein), hypothyroidism (loss of thyroid-binding globulin), and steroid toxicity.

## 5. Pathology of idiopathic nephrotic syndrome

| Lesion | Light microscopy | Immunofluorescence | Electron microscopy | Steroid response |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Minimal change (MCNS)** (about 85% of cases) | Normal or minimal mesangial increase | **Negative** | **Effacement of foot processes** only | **More than 95%** |
| **Mesangial proliferation** | Diffuse increase in mesangial cells and matrix | Trace to 1+ mesangial IgM and/or IgA | More mesangial cells and matrix plus effacement | About 50% |
| **FSGS** | **Focal** (some glomeruli) and **segmental** (part of a tuft): mesangial proliferation and scarring | **IgM and C3** in sclerosed areas | Segmental scarring with obliteration of capillary lumen | **About 20%** |

- FSGS may be secondary to **HIV**, **vesicoureteral reflux**, and IV drug use (heroin).
- FSGS is often progressive, involves all glomeruli over time, and leads to **end-stage renal disease** in most.

## 6. Clinical features

### 6.1 MCNS

- **Male:female 2:1**; usual age **2 to 6 years**.
- MCNS accounts for 85 to 90% of patients under 6 years, but only 20 to 30% of adolescents presenting for the first time. In adolescents **FSGS** is more common, and is the commonest cause of end-stage renal disease in this group.
- The first episode and relapses often follow **minor infections**, and rarely insect bites, bee stings or poison ivy.
- **Mild oedema** first around the **eyes** and lower limbs; later **generalised** with **ascites, pleural effusions, genital oedema**.
- Anorexia, irritability, abdominal pain, diarrhoea (gut oedema).
- **No hypertension and no gross haematuria** (no nephritic features).

### 6.2 Differential diagnosis of marked oedema

Protein-losing enteropathy, hepatic failure, heart failure, acute or chronic glomerulonephritis, protein malnutrition (kwashiorkor).

### 6.3 Features that make MCNS less likely (consider another diagnosis or biopsy)

- Age **under 1 year** (or over 12 years).
- **Family history** of nephrotic syndrome.
- **Extrarenal features**: arthritis, rash, anaemia.
- **Hypertension**, pulmonary oedema.
- Acute or chronic renal insufficiency.
- **Gross haematuria**; low complement.

## 7. Diagnosis and investigation

**Confirm nephrotic syndrome**

- **Urinalysis** with **first-morning UPCR**, serum electrolytes, urea, creatinine, **albumin** and **cholesterol**.

**Look for secondary causes (children aged 10 years and over, or atypical)**

- **C3 level**, **ANA**, **anti-dsDNA**; **hepatitis B, C and HIV** in high-risk groups.
- **Kidney biopsy** in children aged **12 years and over**, who are less likely to have MCNS.

**Typical MCNS findings**

- Dipstick **3+ or 4+ protein**; **microscopic haematuria in 20%**.
- Spot UPCR **above 2.0**.
- Creatinine usually normal (may rise with volume depletion).
- Albumin **below 2.5 g/dL**; cholesterol and triglycerides raised; **complement normal**.
- **Biopsy not routine** if the clinical picture is typical MCNS.

## 8. Management

### 8.1 Initial approach

- First episode with mild or moderate oedema can be managed as an **outpatient**; parents must recognise complications and may learn to use a **dipstick**.
- **Exclude tuberculosis** before starting corticosteroids.
- Age 1 to 8 years with uncomplicated disease: presume steroid-responsive MCNS and start steroids **without biopsy**.
- **Biopsy first** if gross haematuria, hypertension, renal insufficiency, low complement, or age under 1 or over 12 years.

### 8.2 Corticosteroids

- **Initial episode**: prednisone or prednisolone **60 mg/m2/day or 2 mg/kg/day (maximum 60 mg)**, single daily dose for **4 to 6 weeks**, then **alternate-day** (40 mg/m2 or 1.5 mg/kg on alternate days) for **8 weeks to 5 months** with tapering.
- KDIGO recommends at least **12 weeks** of steroid; always weigh the side effects of prolonged steroids.
- **80 to 90%** respond; most remit within the first **2 to 3 weeks**.

### 8.3 Definitions of response

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Remission** | UPCR below 0.2, or below 1+ dipstick, for 3 consecutive days |
| **Response** | Remission within the first 4 weeks of steroids |
| **Relapse** | First-morning UPCR above 0.2, or 2+ or more on dipstick (Albustix) for 3 consecutive days (many guidelines use UPCR above 2 or 3+ on dipstick) |
| **Frequent relapser** | 2 or more relapses within 6 months of initial therapy, or 4 relapses in 12 months |
| **Steroid dependent** | Relapse during taper, or within 2 weeks of stopping |
| **Steroid resistant** | Failure to remit with daily steroids (slide 27 says 4 weeks; slide 33 says 8 weeks) |

> **Check:** relapse thresholds differ across sources (UPCR above 0.2 versus above 2; dipstick 2+ versus 3+). The standard definition used by the International Study of Kidney Disease in Children is 3 or more consecutive days of 3+ protein, and steroid resistance is most often defined as no remission after 4 weeks of daily prednisolone (8 weeks in some guidelines). Use the definition your local guideline uses.

### 8.4 Relapses

- Common, especially in young children, and often triggered by **upper respiratory or GI infections**.
- Treat like the first episode but with a shorter daily course: **high-dose daily prednisone until remission**, then switch to **alternate-day** for a period that depends on relapse frequency.

### 8.5 Supportive care

- **Oedema**: severe oedema (large effusions, ascites, severe genital oedema) needs admission. **Sodium restriction (below 1,500 mg/day)**, fluid restriction if hyponatraemic, elevate a swollen scrotum, **loop diuretic (furosemide)** with extreme caution: over-diuresis causes hypovolaemia, acute kidney injury and thrombosis.
- **Severe oedema with intravascular volume depletion** (haemoconcentration, hypotension, tachycardia): **25% albumin 0.5 to 1.0 g/kg as slow infusion, followed by furosemide 1 to 2 mg/kg IV**. Use with a paediatric nephrologist and close monitoring: rapid infusion risks hypertension, heart failure and pulmonary oedema.
- **Dyslipidaemia**: low-fat diet, fat below 30% of calories, saturated fat below 10%, cholesterol below 300 mg/day.
- **Infection**: counsel families on signs of cellulitis, peritonitis, bacteraemia. **Take blood cultures before empirical antibiotics**. For suspected SBP, do paracentesis (cell count, Gram stain, culture) if enough fluid. Antibiotics must cover **pneumococcus and Gram-negatives**; a **third-generation cephalosporin** is a common choice.
- **Thromboembolism**: confirm with imaging, look for the underlying hypercoagulable state, anticoagulate with **heparin, LMWH or warfarin**.
- **Obesity and growth**: steroids may increase BMI, so give dietary counselling; long-term steroids affect growth; use steroid-sparing strategies.

### 8.6 Steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome (SRNS)

- Needs a **kidney biopsy**, assessment of renal function and quantified proteinuria.
- Usually **FSGS (about 80%)**, otherwise MCNS or membranoproliferative GN.
- About **50% risk of end-stage kidney disease within 5 years** if no partial or complete remission (especially FSGS).
- Persistent nephrotic syndrome brings poor quality of life, hypertension, serious infections and thromboembolism.
- **First-line**: **calcineurin inhibitors** (cyclosporine or tacrolimus). Side effects: hypertension, nephrotoxicity, hirsutism, gingival hyperplasia.
- **ACE inhibitors or ARBs** as adjuncts to reduce proteinuria.

### 8.7 Steroid-sparing agents (frequent relapsers, dependent, resistant)

| Agent | Role and key points |
|---|---|
| **Cyclophosphamide** 2 mg/kg single oral dose for 8 to 12 weeks | Prolongs remission and cuts relapses in frequently relapsing or steroid-dependent disease. Side effects: **neutropenia, disseminated varicella, haemorrhagic cystitis, alopecia, sterility, future malignancy**. Check WBC weekly; withhold if **below 5,000/mm3**. Alternate-day prednisone is often continued |
| **Calcineurin inhibitors** (cyclosporine, tacrolimus) | Initial therapy for SRNS; relapse is likely when stopped |
| **Mycophenolate mofetil** | Maintains remission in steroid-dependent or frequently relapsing disease |
| **Levamisole** | Antihelminthic with immunomodulating effect; reduces relapse risk compared with prednisone |
| **Rituximab** (anti-CD20) | Prolonged remission in steroid-dependent or resistant disease; up to **80% drug-free remission at 1 year** in steroid-dependent disease; less effective with calcineurin inhibitor and steroid-resistance or multidrug-resistant disease |

### 8.8 Immunisation

- Give **full pneumococcal vaccination (PCV13 and PPSV23)** and **annual influenza vaccine** to the child and household contacts.
- **Defer live vaccines** until prednisone is below 1 mg/kg daily or 2 mg/kg on alternate days; live vaccines are **contraindicated** while on cyclophosphamide or cyclosporine.
- After close contact with varicella, give **varicella-zoster immune globulin** if available to immunosuppressed children. Healthy household contacts can have live vaccines, but avoid direct contact with GI or respiratory secretions of vaccinated contacts for 3 to 6 weeks.

## 9. Prognosis

- Most steroid-responsive children relapse repeatedly, but **relapses become less frequent with age**.
- Those who respond rapidly to steroids and have **no relapse in the first 6 months** tend to be infrequent relapsers.
- Steroid-responsive disease rarely leads to chronic kidney disease, is rarely hereditary, and the child remains **fertile** (unless prolonged cyclophosphamide).
- Children should not be treated as chronically ill: full activity and **unrestricted diet in remission**.
- **SRNS (usually FSGS)** has a poorer prognosis: progressive renal insufficiency, then dialysis or transplant. **FSGS recurs in 30 to 50% of transplant recipients.**

> **Exam tip:** MCNS = normal light microscopy, foot process effacement on EM, no nephritic features, steroid-responsive. FSGS = steroid-resistant, progresses to ESKD, recurs after transplant.

## Key points to revise

1. Nephrotic syndrome = proteinuria above 3.5 g/24 h or UPCR above 2, albumin 2.5 g/dL or less, oedema, hyperlipidaemia.
2. Most childhood cases are idiopathic, and MCNS is the commonest; FSGS is the commonest cause in adolescents and in steroid resistance.
3. The podocyte and slit diaphragm are central: foot process effacement causes the leak.
4. MCNS has no hypertension or gross haematuria and a normal C3; biopsy only for atypical features (age under 1 or over 12, haematuria, hypertension, low complement, renal impairment).
5. Treat with prednisolone 60 mg/m2 (2 mg/kg, maximum 60 mg) daily for 4 to 6 weeks, then alternate days for 8 weeks to 5 months; exclude TB first.
6. Complications: infection (pneumococcus, SBP), thrombosis, hypovolaemia, steroid toxicity; use diuretics and albumin with caution.
7. Frequent relapsers and steroid-dependent patients need cyclophosphamide, mycophenolate, levamisole or rituximab; SRNS needs a calcineurin inhibitor and a biopsy.
8. Vaccinate against pneumococcus and influenza; defer live vaccines while on high-dose steroids.
