# HIV and the Gastrointestinal Tract

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · HIV and Gastroenterology**

> **In one line:** the gut is the main battlefield of HIV: it is a site of transmission, viral replication and massive CD4 T-cell (especially Th17) loss, which breaks down the mucosal barrier, lets bacterial products leak into the blood (microbial translocation) and drives chronic immune activation. Clinically this produces HIV enteropathy, opportunistic infections from mouth to anus, and liver, biliary and pancreatic disease; early HAART is the most important treatment.

## What you must know

- **Diarrhoea** is the commonest GI presentation of HIV; HIV enteropathy can occur from acute infection to advanced disease.
- Gut CD4 T-cell loss (especially **Th17**) happens early and causes **microbial translocation**, a major driver of progression.
- Oral **candidiasis** is the commonest oral lesion; **fluconazole** is first line for oesophageal candidiasis (14 to 21 days; no role for topical agents).
- **Cryptosporidium** and **microsporidia** cause chronic watery diarrhoea at low CD4; **Isospora** responds to co-trimoxazole; **CMV** colitis occurs at CD4 below 50.
- **AIDS cholangiopathy**: CD4 below 100, raised ALP and GGT, treated endoscopically (sphincterotomy, stenting).
- **HBV and HCV co-infection** speed liver disease, increase HAART hepatotoxicity and raise the risk of hepatocellular carcinoma.
- **Pancreatitis** risk: old NRTIs (didanosine, stavudine), pentamidine, CD4 below 200.

## 1. Introduction

- GI dysfunction is a **major manifestation of HIV**; the usual presentation is **diarrhoea**.
- New evidence shows **significant gut changes in acute HIV infection**, chiefly substantial **loss of CD4+ T cells in the GIT**.
- **Delay in starting HAART** increases the severity of HIV enteropathy and worsens outcome.
- The mucosal and intestinal immune systems are pivotal in AIDS pathogenesis. The most critical events happen in the gut: **transmission, viral amplification and CD4+ T-cell destruction**.
- Breakdown of the mucosal barrier with **microbial translocation** is a major driver of progression.
- The gut mucosa is a primary site of transmission and a major site of replication and CD4 destruction, **whatever the route of transmission**.

## 2. HIV enteropathy

Can occur from the acute phase to advanced disease.

| Functional features | Histological features |
|---|---|
| Diarrhoea | Lymphocytic infiltration |
| Raised GIT inflammation | Epithelial damage |
| Raised intestinal permeability (up to **fivefold** higher than healthy people) | Villous atrophy and **villous blunting** |
| Malabsorption of **bile acids and vitamin B12** | Crypt hyperplasia |

### Proposed mechanisms (still poorly understood)

- Direct **"virotoxic" effect** of HIV on the enterocyte.
- **Tat** (accessory protein) inhibits enterocyte glucose uptake.
- **gp120** raises intra-enterocyte calcium, with tubulin depolymerisation and reduced ability of epithelial cells to maintain ionic balance.
- HIV may induce **enterocytomegaly** (virus found near abnormally enlarged enterocytes).

### Th17 loss and loss of mucosal integrity

- HIV-mediated loss of **Th17 cells** from gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT) damages mucosal integrity and innate defence against gut microbes.
- Th17 cells maintain **intestinal homeostasis**: they help epithelial regeneration, stimulate **defensins and mucin**, and induce **claudins** (tight-junction proteins).
- The Th17 cytokine **IL-22** increases hepatic production of **lipopolysaccharide-binding protein (LBP)**.
- With massive CD4 depletion in the lamina propria, Th17 cells are probably depleted too, so gut integrity falls and microbial invasion rises.
- *Salmonella* has been shown to **translocate across the gut barrier** when Th17 function is impaired in people living with HIV.

### Lactose malabsorption

- Lactose malabsorption in people living with HIV is about **70%** versus **34%** in HIV-negative controls, and worsens with advanced disease.
- Structural and immune factors probably cause lactase deficiency.

### Impact of opportunistic infections

- OIs aggravate enteropathy through structural damage and immune sensitisation.
- It is hard to tell whether severe GI symptoms are due to HIV progression or to the OI itself.

> **Remember (summary):** Th17 depletion, structural lining defects and microbial translocation lead to immune activation and CD4 imbalance, with more viral replication and fibrosis.

## 3. Oral cavity

Oral lesions are early markers of immunosuppression. Candidiasis comes in forms: **pseudomembranous, erythematous, patchy, generalised, hyperkeratotic**.

### Summary of oral manifestations

| Type | Lesions |
|---|---|
| **White or yellowish-white, non-ulcerative** | Oral hairy leukoplakia (EBV), oral candidiasis, angular cheilitis, pseudomembranous candidiasis |
| **Red, non-ulcerative** | Erythematous candidiasis, Kaposi's sarcoma, non-Hodgkin lymphoma |
| **Ulcerative** | HSV, HZV, CMV, aphthous ulcers |
| **Abnormal structures** | Linear gingival erythema, necrotising gingivitis, periodontitis and stomatitis |

- **Necrotising ulcerative gingivitis/periodontitis:** painful, bleeds easily, may destroy tissue rapidly; caused by Gram-negative anaerobes, enteric organisms and yeast (*Candida albicans*).
- **Aphthous-type ulcers:** painful, interfere with swallowing; biopsy shows non-specific inflammation; consider HSV, cryptococcus and histoplasma as causes of ulcers. Topical steroids and thalidomide (acts on tissue-destructive cytokines) are used for major aphthae.
- **Oral hairy leukoplakia:** white corrugated, non-scrapable patch on the lateral tongue, associated with **EBV**, not painful and not premalignant.

### Oral candidiasis treatment

- **Topical agents** for a first episode of mild thrush: **clotrimazole troches** (10 mg, five times daily) or **nystatin suspension** (400,000 to 600,000 units four times daily). Studies: clotrimazole = fluconazole > nystatin.
- **Fluconazole** for moderate to severe oropharyngeal disease or recurrent disease: effective, palatable, easy to give, cheap.
- Use fluconazole rather than topical therapy in patients at risk of oesophageal candidiasis (**CD4 below 100 cells/µL**).

## 4. Oesophagus

Causes of oesophageal disease in HIV:

- **Candidiasis** (commonest)
- **Ulcers:** HSV, CMV, idiopathic (aphthous)
- Rarely: cryptococcosis, histoplasmosis, blastomycosis, aspergillosis, mycobacteria, *Nocardia*
- **Malignancies:** lymphoma, Kaposi's sarcoma

Patients present with **odynophagia, dysphagia and retrosternal pain**, which lowers intake and worsens **nutritional status** and weight loss.

### Oesophageal candidiasis

- **No role for local agents.**
- Oral versus parenteral route depends on severity of odynophagia.
- Duration **14 to 21 days**.
- **Fluconazole** remains first line; alternatives: amphotericin B, echinocandins; **voriconazole or posaconazole** for refractory disease.
- Diagnosis: endoscopy shows white plaques; a response to a trial of fluconazole is often used where endoscopy is unavailable.

### Oesophageal ulcers

- About **5%** of cases; usually multiple but may be single; may accompany herpes labialis or oropharyngeal ulcers (HSV).
- Treat HSV with **aciclovir 400 mg five times daily for 14 to 21 days** (famciclovir or valaciclovir as alternatives); **foscarnet** for unresponsive cases (suspect resistance or CMV).
- CMV ulcers are typically large, deep and linear; confirm by biopsy; treat with ganciclovir.

## 5. Stomach

- Presents with **dyspepsia and epigastric pain**.
- There is **no increase in *H. pylori*** in HIV.
- Causes: **CMV**, drug-induced dyspepsia, malignancies such as Kaposi's sarcoma.
- **Caution:** HAART containing a **protease inhibitor** interacts with **PPIs** (reduced absorption, especially of atazanavir and rilpivirine).

## 6. Small and large bowel

### Clinical presentations

Gastritis, enteritis, **intestinal perforation (CMV)**, peritonitis and ascites, intestinal obstruction, pancreatitis.

### Evaluation

History and examination, then: blood tests, **stool analysis**, upper or lower endoscopy with biopsy, paracentesis, abdominal ultrasound or CT.

### HIV-associated diarrhoea: causes

Infectious (bacterial, fungal, viral, protozoal), medication-induced, and HIV enteropathy. (* = commonest.)

| Group | Organisms |
|---|---|
| **Protozoal** | **Microsporidia\***, **Cryptosporidium\***, *Isospora belli*, *Giardia lamblia*, *Entamoeba histolytica*, *Leishmania donovani*, *Blastocystis hominis*, *Cyclospora* |
| **Viral** | **CMV\***, HSV, adenovirus, rotavirus, Norwalk virus, HIV |
| **Fungal** | Histoplasmosis, coccidioidomycosis, *Candida albicans* |
| **Bacterial** | ***Salmonella\****, ***Campylobacter\****, EAEC, *Mycobacterium avium* complex (MAC), *M. tuberculosis*, *C. difficile*, *Shigella*, small bowel bacterial overgrowth, *Yersinia*, *Vibrio* spp., *Brachyspira pilosicoli* |
| **Neoplasms** | Lymphoma, Kaposi's sarcoma |
| **Idiopathic** | "AIDS enteropathy" (diagnosis of exclusion) |

- Untreated patients have a roughly **20-fold higher** risk of *S. typhimurium* infection. *S. typhi*, *C. jejuni* and *Shigella* are also more frequent and cause **more severe disease** in HIV.

> **Exam tip:** approach to chronic diarrhoea in HIV: stool microscopy (including modified Ziehl-Neelsen for oocysts), culture, *C. difficile* toxin, then endoscopy with biopsy if still unexplained. Immunosuppression depth (CD4 count) guides the likely cause.

### Cryptosporidium

- Intestinal protozoan; *C. parvum* (and *C. hominis*). Incubation about **10 days**. Transmitted by the faecal-oral route; oocysts are resistant to chlorination. Its life cycle is unusual: it completes within a single host (autoinfection) and sits within the brush border but outside the cytoplasm.
- Pathogenesis: inflammatory (cytokines), **secretory** and malabsorptive.
- Mostly **watery diarrhoea**; can cause **cholangiopathy** (acalculous cholecystitis, sclerosing cholangitis).
- Diagnosis: stool **oocysts on modified Ziehl-Neelsen** stain; ELISA and PCR.
- Treatment: **supportive**, **nitazoxanide**; improves with **HAART** (immune restoration is the most effective therapy).

### Microsporidia

- **Obligate intracellular spore-forming** organisms. Commonest human species: ***Enterocytozoon bieneusi***.
- Extra-intestinal sites: eyes, muscles, liver, lung, brain, sinuses, kidney.
- Diagnosis: light microscopy with **modified trichrome or Weber stain** (sensitivity 100%, specificity 83%); indirect immunofluorescence (sensitivity 83%, specificity 96%); **gold standard: transmission electron microscopy**.
- Treatment: **albendazole 400 mg twice daily for 2 to 4 weeks** (*E. bieneusi* is resistant); **fumagillin** (antiangiogenic) for *E. bieneusi*; **HAART**.

### Isospora belli

- Unicellular protozoan, **faecal-oral** transmission; **oocysts** in stool; **blood eosinophilia** is a clue.
- Treatment: **co-trimoxazole (Septrin)**; ciprofloxacin is less effective.

### CMV colitis

- Risk factors: **CMV viraemia**, **CD4 below 50 cells/µL**. Common sites: oesophagus and colon. CMV colitis is the **second commonest CMV end-organ disease after retinitis**.
- Diagnosis: symptoms plus visualised **ulcers** plus histopathology. Viraemia can be detected by PCR and antigen assays; **antibodies are not useful**; blood cultures.
- Pathology: mucosal inflammation, necrosis, **cytomegalic cells** with eosinophilic intranuclear ("owl's eye") and basophilic intracytoplasmic inclusions.
- Patients with extra-ocular disease should be **screened for CMV retinitis at presentation and every 6 months** until CD4 is above 100 on HAART.
- Treatment: **ganciclovir or foscarnet for 3 to 6 weeks**, then **valganciclovir**; start **HAART**.
- Complications include haemorrhage and perforation.

## 7. Anorectal disease

More common in men who have sex with men (MSM). Includes:

- Perirectal abscess, anal fistulae, non-specific ulcers
- **Infectious proctitis:** *N. gonorrhoeae*, *Chlamydia*, HSV, syphilis
- Perirectal ulcers and erosions (**HSV reactivation**)
- Anal warts (**condylomata acuminata**), anal dysplasia
- **Kaposi's sarcoma**
- Less commonly ulcerative colitis, Crohn's
- **Anorectal carcinoma** is more frequent in homosexual men, with increasing incidence (HPV related).
- Evaluate with **anoscopy or sigmoidoscopy with mucosal biopsy**.

## 8. Hepatobiliary disease

Features: icterus, hepatomegaly, raised transaminases.

### Causes of hepatitis in HIV

- Infections: MAC, *M. tuberculosis*, disseminated mycoses (cryptococcus, histoplasma, sporothrix, coccidioides), candidaemia, PJP (38%)
- Tumours: hepatic or visceral Kaposi's sarcoma, extranodal NHL
- Co-infections: **HBV (with or without HDV), HCV, CMV**
- Drugs (ARVs, anti-TB drugs, co-trimoxazole)

### HBV-HIV co-infection

- Chronic HBV occurs in about **10%** of HIV-infected patients worldwide; higher in sub-Saharan Africa and Asia.
- Diagnosis same as in HIV-negative people. Some patients have **HBV mutants** with reduced or absent HBeAg, so **all need HBV DNA**.
- **HDV** speeds progression: in a Taiwan case-control study of 104 co-infected patients, 26 had triple infection with HDV and had more hepatic flares, cirrhosis, decompensation and death over 8 years.
- Immunosuppression is associated with higher viraemia and infectivity but **less liver injury** (less necro-inflammation, since damage is immune-mediated).
- **Significance:** more severe hepatitis; chronic HBV/HCV raise the risk of **HAART hepatotoxicity**; faster liver disease progression; more **HCC** and death.

**Treatment:**

- For patients not needing HAART: **pegylated interferon alfa-2a** (no RCT support in co-infection).
- Cirrhosis (compensated or decompensated): adefovir.
- **Monotherapy with 3TC, TDF or FTC is not recommended** (resistance).
- **Entecavir** induces the M184V mutation, so is not recommended first line.
- If these cannot be used, start HAART early (a regimen containing tenofovir plus lamivudine or emtricitabine is active against both viruses).

> **Check:** current guidance prefers a tenofovir-based dual-active HAART regimen for all co-infected patients regardless of CD4; the slide reflects older advice.

**Monitoring:** monthly aminotransferases, HBeAb every 6 months, HBV DNA quarterly; screen for **HCC** with serum alpha-fetoprotein and ultrasound.

### HCV-HIV co-infection

- Both are RNA viruses. HCV RNA rises after HIV seroconversion; **spontaneous clearance of HCV is less likely**.
- HIV accelerates HCV: faster progression to cirrhosis and higher liver-related morbidity and mortality (microbial translocation and **LPS** contribute).
- **HAART** reduces liver-related mortality and slows fibrosis.
- Chronic viral hepatitis raises the risk of **ARV hepatotoxicity**, possibly through immune reconstitution.

### AIDS cholangiopathy

- Biliary obstruction syndrome from infection with **strictures**.
- Organisms: ***C. parvum*** (commonest; affects large intrahepatic ducts), microsporidia, CMV, *Cyclospora cayetanensis*; **20 to 40% idiopathic**.
- Usually **CD4 below 100 cells/µL**.
- Presentation: **RUQ or epigastric pain**, diarrhoea; fever and jaundice are less common.
- Labs: raised **ALP** and **GGT** (GGT raised in about 90%), mild transaminase rise.
- Diagnosis: **ultrasound, ERCP, CT** (papillary stenosis, sclerosing cholangitis pattern, dilated ducts).
- Treatment is **primarily endoscopic**: **sphincterotomy and stenting**; ursodeoxycholic acid; medical treatment of the infecting agent does not change symptoms or cholangiographic findings.
- HIV is an independent risk factor for **cholangiocarcinoma**.

## 9. Pancreatitis

| Aetiology | Risk factors |
|---|---|
| Acute HIV infection, CMV, toxoplasmosis, *M. tuberculosis*, PJP, *C. parvum*, tumour invasion (Kaposi's sarcoma, lymphoma) | **Didanosine (mitochondrial toxicity)**, previous pancreatitis, CD4 below 200 |

- Drugs: didanosine, stavudine, tenofovir, ribavirin, hydroxyurea, pentamidine; less commonly lamivudine, isoniazid, co-trimoxazole, rifampicin, steroids, erythromycin, valproic acid.
- Managing: stop the offending drug, supportive care, treat the infection.

## Key points to revise

1. The gut is a primary site of HIV transmission, replication and CD4 (especially Th17) loss; microbial translocation drives immune activation.
2. HIV enteropathy: diarrhoea, villous blunting, crypt hyperplasia, increased permeability, bile acid and B12 malabsorption.
3. Oral candidiasis: topical for mild first episodes, fluconazole for severe, recurrent or CD4 below 100; oesophageal candidiasis needs systemic fluconazole for 14 to 21 days.
4. Chronic diarrhoea: think Cryptosporidium (modified ZN, nitazoxanide), microsporidia (albendazole), Isospora (co-trimoxazole), CMV (ganciclovir) and Salmonella.
5. CMV colitis: CD4 below 50; ulcers with cytomegalic inclusions; ganciclovir or foscarnet 3 to 6 weeks.
6. HBV and HCV co-infection accelerate liver disease and increase ARV hepatotoxicity and HCC; monitor ALT, HBV DNA and AFP with ultrasound.
7. AIDS cholangiopathy: CD4 below 100, raised ALP and GGT, endoscopic sphincterotomy and stenting.
8. Avoid pancreatitis-causing drugs (didanosine, stavudine, pentamidine) in at-risk patients.
