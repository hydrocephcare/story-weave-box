## Summary
This document provides comprehensive MBChB-level revision notes on endocrine pathology, covering disorders of the pituitary, thyroid, parathyroid, adrenal glands, and pancreas, as well as multiple endocrine neoplasia syndromes. It includes detailed explanations of anatomy, pathophysiology, clinical features, diagnosis, and treatment for a wide range of endocrine conditions.

## Key Points
- Pituitary disorders include hyperpituitarism (e.g., adenomas causing hormone excess or mass effects) and hypopituitarism (deficiency of pituitary hormones).
- Thyroid disorders encompass goiters, hypothyroidism (e.g., Hashimoto's), hyperthyroidism (e.g., Graves' disease), and various types of thyroiditis and tumors.
- Parathyroid disorders involve hyperparathyroidism (primary, secondary, tertiary) leading to hypercalcemia and hypoparathyroidism causing hypocalcemia.
- Adrenal disorders cover hyperfunction (Cushing syndrome, hyperaldosteronism, adrenogenital syndromes) and hypofunction (Addison's disease).
- Pancreatic endocrine disorders primarily focus on diabetes mellitus (T1DM, T2DM, MODY, gestational DM) and its acute and chronic complications, as well as pancreatic tumors.
- Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia (MEN) syndromes are inherited disorders characterized by tumors in multiple endocrine glands, with MEN1, MEN2A, and MEN2B being key examples.

## Detailed Notes
### TOPIC 1: PITUITARY DISORDERS

#### Anatomy & Overview
The pituitary gland (hypophysis) is located in the **sella turcica** and has two main lobes: the **anterior pituitary (adenohypophysis)**, responsible for producing GH, TSH, ACTH, FSH, LH, and Prolactin, and the **posterior pituitary (neurohypophysis)**, which stores and releases ADH (vasopressin) and Oxytocin.

### A. HYPERPITUITARISM

This condition is most commonly caused by a **functioning pituitary adenoma**.

#### Pituitary Adenomas
These are benign tumors of anterior pituitary cells, classified by size (<1 cm = **microadenoma**; >1 cm = **macroadenoma**) and the hormone they secrete.

| Adenoma Type | Hormone | Clinical Syndrome |
|---|---|---|
| Somatotroph | GH | Gigantism (children) / Acromegaly (adults) |
| Lactotroph (most common) | Prolactin | Hyperprolactinemia |
| Corticotroph | ACTH | Cushing disease |
| Thyrotroph | TSH | Hyperthyroidism |
| Gonadotroph | FSH/LH | Usually non-functioning |
| Null cell | None | Mass effects only |

#### Mass Effects of Pituitary Adenoma
Compression by a pituitary adenoma can lead to **bitemporal hemianopia** (due to compression of the optic chiasm), headache, hypopituitarism, and **cranial nerve palsies** (III, IV, VI) if the cavernous sinus is invaded.

#### 1. Hyperprolactinemia
The most common functioning pituitary adenoma is the prolactinoma.
- **Causes:** Prolactinoma, drugs (dopamine antagonists, opioids), hypothyroidism, stalk compression, and physiological states (pregnancy, breastfeeding).
- **Effects:** In women, it causes **galactorrhea, amenorrhea, and infertility**. In men, it can lead to impotence, decreased libido, infertility, and gynecomastia.
- **Treatment:** Dopamine agonists like bromocriptine and cabergoline.

#### 2. Growth Hormone Excess
- **Gigantism** results from excess GH before epiphyseal closure, leading to excessive linear growth.
- **Acromegaly** occurs when excess GH is present after epiphyseal closure. Features include enlarged hands and feet, coarse facial features, prognathism, macroglossia, and increased hat/shoe/ring size. Organomegaly (cardiomegaly, hepatomegaly), **carpal tunnel syndrome**, arthritis, **diabetes mellitus** (due to GH's anti-insulin effects), hypertension, and increased cancer risk are also associated.
- **Diagnosis:** Elevated IGF-1 levels and lack of GH suppression by an oral glucose tolerance test.

#### 3. ACTH Excess (Cushing Disease)
A pituitary ACTH-secreting adenoma causes bilateral adrenal hyperplasia and excess cortisol, leading to Cushing disease. This is discussed further under Adrenal section.

### B. HYPOPITUITARISM

#### Definition
This is the deficiency of one or more anterior pituitary hormones.

#### Causes
The most common cause is a **pituitary adenoma** compressing normal tissue. Other causes include **Sheehan syndrome** (postpartum pituitary necrosis), craniopharyngioma, traumatic brain injury, radiation, empty sella syndrome, granulomas, and hemochromatosis.

#### Clinical Effects (order of loss — GH lost first)
The hormones are typically lost in a specific order:
1. **GH deficiency**: Growth failure in children; fatigue and reduced muscle mass in adults.
2. **FSH/LH deficiency**: Hypogonadism, infertility, amenorrhea, and decreased libido.
3. **TSH deficiency**: Secondary hypothyroidism.
4. **ACTH deficiency**: Secondary adrenal insufficiency (without hyperpigmentation).
5. **Prolactin deficiency**: Failure to lactate postpartum.

#### Panhypopituitarism
This is the deficiency of all anterior pituitary hormones and can be a medical emergency if acute.

### C. POSTERIOR PITUITARY DISORDERS

#### Diabetes Insipidus (DI)
This condition is characterized by the **deficiency of ADH action**, leading to an inability to concentrate urine and resulting in **polyuria (large volumes of dilute urine) and polydipsia**.
- **Types:**
  - **Central DI**: Decreased ADH production (causes: head trauma, surgery, tumors, Sheehan syndrome, idiopathic). Treatment: **desmopressin (DDAVP)**.
  - **Nephrogenic DI**: Kidneys do not respond to ADH (causes: lithium, hypercalcemia, hypokalemia, genetic factors). Treatment: thiazides, low-salt diet.
- **Diagnosis:** Water deprivation test followed by a response to desmopressin.
- **Lab findings:** Low urine osmolality, high serum osmolality, and hypernatremia.

#### Syndrome of Inappropriate ADH (SIADH)
This involves **excess ADH**, leading to excessive water retention and **dilutional hyponatremia**.
- **Causes:** CNS disorders (meningitis, stroke, head trauma), pulmonary conditions (pneumonia, TB), **malignancy (Small cell carcinoma of the lung)**, drugs (carbamazepine, SSRIs), hypothyroidism, and Addison disease.
- **Lab findings:** Low serum sodium, low serum osmolality, **inappropriately concentrated urine** (urine osmolality > serum osmolality), and high urine sodium.
- **Symptoms:** Nausea, confusion, seizures, and coma due to cerebral edema.
- **Treatment:** Fluid restriction; hypertonic saline for severe cases; demeclocycline or tolvaptan.

## TOPIC 2: THYROID DISORDERS

### Normal Thyroid Function
The thyroid gland produces T3 (active) and T4 (prohormone), regulated by TSH from the pituitary, which is in turn regulated by TRH from the hypothalamus. T3 and T4 increase metabolic rate, protein synthesis, cardiac output, and gut motility.

### A. GOITER

#### Definition
A **goiter** is an **enlargement of the thyroid gland**, regardless of its cause.

#### Types
**1. Diffuse Non-toxic Goiter (Simple/Colloid Goiter)**
This is a generalized, smooth thyroid enlargement in euthyroid individuals.
- **Causes:** **Iodine deficiency** (most common worldwide) and goitrogens (e.g., cabbage, cassava).
- **Mechanism:** Low T3/T4 leads to increased TSH, causing thyroid hyperplasia and goiter.
- **Histology:** Follicles are distended with colloid, and the follicular epithelium is flattened.

**2. Multinodular Goiter**
This results from long-standing simple goiter with uneven hyperplasia leading to nodules. It can be toxic (causing hyperthyroidism) or non-toxic and may cause tracheal or esophageal compression. **Plummer syndrome** refers to toxic multinodular goiter, where autonomous nodules produce excess T3/T4.

### B. HYPOTHYROIDISM

#### Definition
A deficiency of thyroid hormone, leading to a **decreased metabolic rate**.

#### Primary vs Secondary
- **Primary hypothyroidism** originates in the thyroid gland (high TSH, low T4).
- **Secondary hypothyroidism** originates in the pituitary (low TSH, low T4).

#### Causes of Primary Hypothyroidism
The most common cause in iodine-sufficient areas is **Hashimoto thyroiditis**. Other causes include iodine deficiency, post-radioiodine therapy or thyroidectomy, certain drugs, and congenital hypothyroidism (cretinism).

#### Clinical Features
Hypothyroidism causes a slowing of all metabolic processes, including cold intolerance, weight gain, fatigue, constipation, bradycardia, and hypertension (diastolic). **Myxedema**—non-pitting edema due to glycosaminoglycan accumulation—is characterized by periorbital puffiness, a puffy face, and macroglossia. Other features include dry, coarse skin; coarse, brittle hair with hair loss (outer third of eyebrows); delayed reflexes; menorrhagia; infertility; hyperlipidemia; and cognitive slowing.
- **Cretinism** is hypothyroidism in infancy or childhood, leading to **irreversible intellectual disability**, short stature, coarse features, an umbilical hernia, and a protruding tongue.

#### Myxedema Coma
This is a severe, life-threatening form of hypothyroidism characterized by hypothermia, bradycardia, hypotension, and altered consciousness, often precipitated by infection, cold exposure, or surgery. Treatment includes IV T3/T4 and hydrocortisone.

### C. HYPERTHYROIDISM (THYROTOXICOSIS)

#### Definition
This condition involves excess thyroid hormone, leading to an **increased metabolic rate**.

#### Causes
The most common cause is **Graves disease** (60–80%). Other causes include toxic multinodular goiter, toxic adenoma, thyroiditis (subacute, postpartum), excess iodine, TSH-secreting pituitary adenomas, and struma ovarii.

#### Clinical Features
Symptoms reflect an acceleration of metabolic processes, including heat intolerance, weight loss despite increased appetite, tachycardia, palpitations, atrial fibrillation, tremor, anxiety, diarrhea, warm moist skin, fine hair, hair loss, oligomenorrhea, infertility, and proximal muscle weakness. **Lid lag and lid retraction** are common due to sympathetic stimulation.

#### Thyroid Storm (Thyrotoxic Crisis)
This is a life-threatening exacerbation of hyperthyroidism, presenting with high fever, extreme tachycardia, hypertension, agitation, delirium, and coma. Treatment involves antithyroid drugs, beta-blockers, iodine, hydrocortisone, and supportive care.

### D. GRAVES DISEASE

#### Definition
Graves disease is an autoimmune hyperthyroidism caused by **TSH receptor-stimulating antibodies (TRAb/TSI)**.

#### Pathogenesis
IgG autoantibodies (TSI) bind to the TSH receptor, mimicking TSH and causing continuous stimulation of the thyroid gland, leading to hyperplasia and excess T3/T4 production.

#### Classic Triad (Unique to Graves)
1. **Hyperthyroidism with diffuse goiter**.
2. **Exophthalmos (proptosis)**: Eyeball protrusion due to retroorbital tissue inflammation and glycosaminoglycan deposition, which is not reversed by treating hyperthyroidism.
3. **Pretibial myxedema (dermopathy)**: Localized, non-pitting skin thickening over the shins.

#### Additional Features
**Thyroid acropachy** (clubbing and periosteal new bone formation) is a rare feature. A diffuse smooth goiter and a bruit over the thyroid (due to increased vascularity) are also common.

#### Histology
Characterized by tall columnar follicular epithelium, scalloping of colloid, lymphocytic infiltration with germinal centers, and increased vascularity.

#### Lab
Low TSH, high T3/T4, positive TRAb/TSI, and diffuse uptake on radioiodine scan.

#### Treatment
Options include anti-thyroid drugs (carbimazole, propylthiouracil), **radioactive iodine (¹³¹I)** (most common definitive treatment), beta-blockers for symptom control, and surgery (thyroidectomy).

### E. THYROIDITIS

#### 1. Hashimoto Thyroiditis (Chronic Autoimmune Thyroiditis)
This is the **most common cause of hypothyroidism** in iodine-sufficient countries and the **most common thyroid disease overall**. It involves autoimmune destruction of the thyroid, with associated antibodies being anti-thyroid peroxidase (Anti-TPO) and anti-thyroglobulin (Anti-Tg).
- **Pathogenesis:** Primarily mediated by CD8+ cytotoxic T cells, with autoantibodies also playing a role.
- **Histology:** Dense lymphocytic infiltration with germinal centers, **Hürthle cell change** (large pink cells), follicular atrophy, and fibrosis.
- **Clinical course:** Can present with transient hyperthyroidism ("Hashitoxicosis") followed by hypothyroidism. The thyroid is typically firm and rubbery.
- **Complications:** Increased risk of thyroid lymphoma and papillary thyroid carcinoma.
- **Lab:** High TSH, low T4, positive anti-TPO antibodies.
- **Treatment:** Levothyroxine (T4 replacement).

#### 2. Subacute (De Quervain) Granulomatous Thyroiditis
This is the **most common cause of painful thyroid** and typically follows a viral infection. It is self-limiting and progresses through transient hyperthyroid, euthyroid, and hypothyroid phases before recovery.
- **Histology:** Granulomatous inflammation with giant cells.
- **Lab:** Elevated ESR and low radioiodine uptake.
- **Treatment:** NSAIDs, steroids for severe cases, and beta-blockers for hyperthyroid symptoms.

#### 3. Riedel Thyroiditis
A rare condition characterized by dense fibrous replacement of the thyroid, extending into surrounding structures, making the thyroid feel **"woody" or "iron"**. It can cause dysphagia, stridor, and hoarseness.

#### 4. Postpartum Thyroiditis
An autoimmune condition occurring within a year of delivery, similar to Hashimoto's, typically presenting with transient hyperthyroidism followed by hypothyroidism.

### F. THYROID TUMORS

#### Benign: Follicular Adenoma
This is the most common benign thyroid tumor. It is solitary, encapsulated, and well-defined, with no capsular or vascular invasion. It is usually non-functioning ("cold" nodule) and does not progress to carcinoma. Treatment is typically a lobectomy to exclude carcinoma.

#### Malignant Thyroid Tumors

**Overview:**

| Type | Frequency | Origin | Prognosis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Papillary | ~80% | Follicular cells | Excellent |
| Follicular | ~10% | Follicular cells | Good |
| Medullary | ~5% | Parafollicular C cells | Moderate |
| Anaplastic | ~1–2% | Follicular cells | Very poor |

**1. Papillary Thyroid Carcinoma — Most Common Thyroid Cancer**
This accounts for 80% of thyroid malignancies and has an excellent prognosis (>95% 10-year survival). It is associated with **radiation exposure** and RET/PTC rearrangements. Spread is primarily via **lymphatics** to cervical lymph nodes.
- **Histology:** Characterized by **Orphan Annie eye nuclei** (clear nuclei with dispersed chromatin), **nuclear grooves**, **psammoma bodies**, and a papillary architecture.

**2. Follicular Carcinoma**
This accounts for about 10% of thyroid cancers. Diagnosis requires evidence of **capsular or vascular invasion**. It spreads hematogenously, often to bone and lungs.

**3. Medullary Carcinoma**
Derived from parafollicular C cells, it secretes **calcitonin**. It is part of the MEN 2 syndromes and is characterized by amyloid deposition in the tumor stroma.

**4. Anaplastic Carcinoma**
This is a highly aggressive tumor with a very poor prognosis, typically affecting the elderly. It grows rapidly and invades locally, often presenting as a large, fixed mass with compressive symptoms.

### G. THYROID FUNCTION TESTS (TFTs)

- **Euthyroid Sick Syndrome:** Low TSH, low T3, low T4 in the context of severe systemic illness (e.g., sepsis, starvation). This is a physiological adaptation, not a true thyroid disorder.

## TOPIC 3: PARATHYROID DISORDERS

### Normal Parathyroid Function
The parathyroid glands secrete **parathyroid hormone (PTH)**, which is the primary regulator of **calcium and phosphate homeostasis**. PTH increases serum calcium by:
1. **Increasing bone resorption** (osteoclast activation).
2. **Increasing renal calcium reabsorption** (distal tubules).
3. **Increasing renal phosphate excretion**.
4. **Increasing 1α-hydroxylase activity** in the kidney, which converts calcidiol to calcitriol (active Vitamin D), thereby increasing intestinal calcium absorption.

### A. HYPERPARATHYROIDISM

#### Definition
Excess PTH production, leading to **hypercalcemia**.

#### Primary Hyperparathyroidism
This is due to **autonomous overproduction of PTH** by one or more parathyroid glands.
- **Causes:**
  - **Solitary adenoma** (80%) — most common
  - **Hyperplasia** of all four glands (15%)
  - Multiple adenomas (5%)
  - Parathyroid carcinoma (rare)
- **Associated conditions:** MEN1, MEN2A, radiation exposure to the neck.
- **Clinical Features:** "Stones, bones, abdominal groans, and psychic moans."
  - **Stones:** Renal calculi (calcium stones)
  - **Bones:** Osteitis fibrosa cystica (brown tumors), subperiosteal resorption (pathognomonic on X-ray), osteoporosis
  - **Abdominal groans:** Constipation, nausea, pancreatitis, peptic ulcers
  - **Psychic moans:** Depression, fatigue, confusion, memory loss
  - Hypertension, hyperuricemia, gout
- **Lab:** **Hypercalcemia**, **low/normal phosphate**, **high PTH**, high urine calcium, **elevated alkaline phosphatase** (bone formation)
- **Diagnosis:** Measuring serum calcium and PTH; imaging if needed (ultrasound, sestamibi scan).
- **Treatment:** Surgical removal of affected glands (parathyroidectomy). Indications include symptomatic hypercalcemia, serum calcium >1 mg/dL above normal, reduced bone mineral density (T-score < -2.5), or reduced renal function.

#### Secondary Hyperparathyroidism
This is **reactive PTH excess** in response to **chronic hypocalcemia**.
- Most common cause: **Chronic renal failure (CRF)**.
  - CRF leads to phosphate retention (hyperphosphatemia), which binds calcium, causing hypocalcemia.
  - CRF also reduces 1α-hydroxylase activity, impairing Vitamin D activation and thus calcium absorption.
  - Both factors stimulate PTH secretion, leading to hyperplasia of all four glands.
- Other causes: Vitamin D deficiency, malabsorption.
- **Lab:** **Low/normal calcium, high phosphate, high PTH**.
- **Treatment:** Correcting the underlying cause, Vitamin D supplements, phosphate binders, and calcimimetics.

#### Tertiary Hyperparathyroidism
This occurs when glands become **autonomous** after long-standing secondary hyperparathyroidism, leading to hypercalcemia even after the initial cause is corrected (e.g., post-kidney transplant). Parathyroidectomy may be required.

### B. HYPOPARATHYROIDISM

#### Causes
The most common cause is **post-surgical** removal or damage during thyroid or neck surgery. Other causes include autoimmune destruction, DiGeorge syndrome (22q11 deletion), hypomagnesemia, and infiltration by conditions like hemochromatosis.

#### Clinical Features of Hypocalcemia
These include **neuromuscular excitability** such as tetany, **Chvostek sign** (facial nerve tapping), **Trousseau sign** (carpal spasm), perioral numbness, tingling, and seizures. Cardiac arrhythmias due to a prolonged QT interval, dry skin, brittle nails, hair loss, and papilledema can also occur.

#### Pseudohypoparathyroidism
This is a condition of **end-organ resistance to PTH**, characterized by high PTH levels but low calcium. It is often associated with **Albright hereditary osteodystrophy**, which includes short stature, round face, short metacarpals/metatarsals, and subcutaneous calcifications.

## TOPIC 4: ADRENAL DISORDERS

### Anatomy & Zones
The adrenal cortex has three zones: **Zona Glomerulosa** (mineralocorticoids - Aldosterone), **Zona Fasciculata** (glucocorticoids - Cortisol), and **Zona Reticularis** (sex steroids - Androgens). The inner **Adrenal Medulla** produces catecholamines (Epinephrine, Norepinephrine).

### A. ADRENOCORTICAL HYPERFUNCTION

#### 1. Cushing Syndrome — Excess Cortisol

**Causes:**
- **Cushing Disease** (pituitary ACTH-secreting adenoma) — most common endogenous cause.
- **Ectopic ACTH** production by tumors (e.g., small cell lung cancer).
- **Primary adrenal tumor** (adenoma or carcinoma).
- **Iatrogenic** (exogenous corticosteroid therapy) — most common overall cause.

**Clinical Features:**
These include central obesity (buffalo hump, moon face), **purple striae**, proximal muscle weakness, skin thinning, easy bruising, osteoporosis, hypertension, hyperglycemia/diabetes, immunosuppression, hypokalemia (in ectopic ACTH), **hyperpigmentation** (in ACTH-dependent causes), acne, hirsutism, menstrual irregularities, and psychological changes.

**Lab:**
Elevated 24-hour urinary free cortisol, loss of diurnal cortisol rhythm, and abnormal responses to dexamethasone suppression tests (low-dose for screening, high-dose to differentiate pituitary from ectopic/adrenal causes).

#### 2. Hyperaldosteronism (Conn Syndrome)

**Primary Hyperaldosteronism (Conn Syndrome):** Autonomous overproduction of aldosterone, usually from an adenoma or bilateral adrenal hyperplasia.
- **Lab:** Hypokalemia, hypernatremia, metabolic alkalosis, **hypertension**, low renin, and high aldosterone.
- **Diagnosis:** Elevated aldosterone:renin ratio.
- **Treatment:** Surgical removal for adenoma, spironolactone for hyperplasia.

**Secondary Hyperaldosteronism:** Aldosterone excess due to high renin levels (e.g., renal artery stenosis, heart failure).

#### 3. Adrenogenital Syndromes (Congenital Adrenal Hyperplasia — CAH)
These are autosomal recessive enzyme deficiencies in cortisol synthesis, leading to low cortisol and high ACTH. This results in bilateral adrenal hyperplasia and shunting of precursors into androgen synthesis.

### B. ADRENOCORTICAL HYPOFUNCTION (Adrenal Insufficiency)

#### Primary Adrenal Insufficiency (Addison Disease)
This is a deficiency of both cortisol and aldosterone due to **adrenal gland destruction**.
- **Causes:**
  - **Autoimmune destruction** (most common in developed countries) — associated with other autoimmune diseases (e.g., Type 1 DM, Hashimoto's, hypoparathyroidism — APS Type 2).
  - **Infections** (TB, fungal infections, viral) — TB was historically common.
  - **Metastases**, hemorrhage (waterhouse-friderichsen syndrome in meningococcemia), drugs.
- **Clinical Features:**
  - **Cortisol deficiency:** Fatigue, weakness, weight loss, anorexia, nausea, vomiting, hypoglycemia.
  - **Aldosterone deficiency:** Salt craving, postural hypotension, dehydration.
  - **Hyperpigmentation** of skin and mucous membranes (due to high ACTH and MSH).
- **Lab:** **Low cortisol**, **high ACTH** (primary deficiency), **hyponatremia**, **hyperkalemia**, **hypotension**.
- **Diagnosis:** **ACTH stimulation test** (low baseline cortisol, no rise after ACTH administration).
- **Treatment:** **Hydrocortisone** (cortisol replacement) and **fludrocortisone** (aldosterone replacement). **Adrenal crisis** is a life-threatening emergency requiring IV fluids, IV hydrocortisone, and electrolyte correction.

#### Secondary Adrenal Insufficiency
This is deficiency of **cortisol** due to **pituitary ACTH deficiency**. Aldosterone production is usually preserved as it is primarily regulated by the renin-angiotensin system.
- **Causes:** Pituitary tumors, Sheehan syndrome, cranial irradiation, head trauma.
- **Clinical Features:** Similar to primary but **NO hyperpigmentation** and usually **NO hyperkalemia/hyponatremia**.
- **Lab:** **Low cortisol**, **low/normal ACTH**.
- **Diagnosis:** ACTH stimulation test shows no cortisol rise.
- **Treatment:** Hydrocortisone replacement.

### C. ADRENAL MEDULLA DISORDERS

#### Pheochromocytoma
A catecholamine-secreting tumor of chromaffin cells (medulla).
- **Clinical Features:** **Paroxysmal hypertension** (episodic severe headaches, palpitations, sweating), anxiety, tremor. Can also be sustained hypertension.
- **Lab:** Elevated urinary or plasma **free metanephrines** (metabolites of epinephrine and norepinephrine) — most sensitive test.
- **Diagnosis:** Imaging (CT/MRI).
- **Treatment:** **Alpha-blockade first** (e.g., phenoxybenzamine) to control blood pressure, followed by beta-blockade if needed for tachycardia, then surgical removal.

## TOPIC 5: DIABETES MELLITUS

### Definition
A group of metabolic disorders characterized by **hyperglycemia** resulting from defects in insulin secretion, insulin action, or both.

### Types of Diabetes Mellitus

#### Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus (T1DM)
- Autoimmune destruction of pancreatic **β-cells** → absolute insulin deficiency.
- Typically presents in childhood or adolescence but can occur at any age.
- **Insulitis** (inflammation of islets) and β-cell loss are characteristic histological findings.
- Associated with HLA-DR3, DR4.
- **Ketoacidosis** is common at diagnosis.
- Requires **insulin therapy** lifelong.

#### Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM)
- Heterogeneous disorder characterized by **insulin resistance** and **relative insulin deficiency**.
- Strong genetic predisposition, associated with obesity and physical inactivity.
- Histology shows **amyloid deposition** in islets.
- **Ketoacidosis** is rare at diagnosis but can occur with severe stress (e.g., infection).
- Treated with diet, exercise, oral hypoglycemic agents, and often insulin.

#### Other Specific Types of DM
- **MODY (Maturity Onset Diabetes of the Young)**: Monogenic, autosomal dominant forms with various mutations.
- **Gestational DM**: Occurs during pregnancy due to insulin resistance; increases risk of macrosomia, neonatal hypoglycemia, and future T2DM.
- **Secondary DM**: Caused by other conditions (pancreatitis, hemochromatosis, Cushing syndrome, acromegaly) or drugs (steroids).

### COMPLICATIONS OF DIABETES MELLITUS

#### Acute Complications

**1. Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA) — mainly T1DM**
Caused by severe insulin deficiency leading to hyperglycemia, metabolic acidosis, and ketonemia.
- **Features:** Hyperglycemia (>11 mmol/L), low pH (<7.3), ketonemia/ketonuria.
- **Symptoms:** Polyuria, polydipsia, nausea/vomiting, abdominal pain, **Kussmaul breathing**, **fruity breath**.
- **Treatment:** IV fluids, IV insulin infusion, potassium replacement.

**2. Hyperosmolar Hyperglycaemic State (HHS/HONK) — mainly T2DM**
Extreme hyperglycemia (>33 mmol/L) with severe dehydration and high serum osmolality, but without significant ketoacidosis.
- **Features:** Extreme dehydration, confusion, seizures, coma.
- **Treatment:** Gradual rehydration, low-dose insulin.

**3. Hypoglycemia**
Low blood glucose (<3.9 mmol/L), often caused by excess insulin or oral agents. Symptoms are adrenergic (sweating, tremor) and neuroglycopenic (confusion, seizures).

#### Chronic Complications

These arise from hyperglycemia leading to vascular and nerve damage.

**A. Microvascular Complications**

**1. Diabetic Nephropathy**
Damage to the glomerulus, characterized by diffuse or nodular glomerulosclerosis (**Kimmelstiel-Wilson lesion**), thickened GBM, and hyaline arteriolosclerosis in afferent and efferent arterioles. The earliest sign is **microalbuminuria**. Treatment involves ACE inhibitors/ARBs, glucose control, and BP control.

**2. Diabetic Retinopathy**
- **Non-proliferative:** Microaneurysms, hemorrhages, exudates, cotton wool spots.
- **Proliferative:** **Neovascularization**, leading to vitreous hemorrhage and retinal detachment. Requires laser photocoagulation or anti-VEGF therapy.

**3. Diabetic Neuropathy**
- **Peripheral symmetric polyneuropathy:** Loss of sensation in extremities ("stocking and glove"), leading to **diabetic foot** ulcers.
- **Autonomic neuropathy:** Affecting gut motility (gastroparesis), blood pressure regulation (orthostatic hypotension), and sexual function.
- **Mononeuropathy:** Affecting individual nerves (e.g., cranial nerve III palsy).

**B. Macrovascular Complications**
Accelerated atherosclerosis leading to **ischemic heart disease**, stroke, and peripheral arterial disease.

**C. Other Complications**
Including diabetic foot ulcers, recurrent infections, cataracts, and glaucoma.

## TOPIC 6: MULTIPLE ENDOCRINE NEOPLASIA (MEN)

### Definition
Rare **autosomal dominant** inherited syndromes characterized by tumors in two or more endocrine glands.

### MEN 1 (Wermer Syndrome)
Caused by mutation in the **MEN1 gene**. Characterized by the "3 Ps":
- **Parathyroid hyperplasia/adenoma** (most common, hypercalcemia).
- **Pituitary adenoma** (most commonly prolactinoma).
- **Pancreatic islet cell tumors** (e.g., gastrinoma causing Zollinger-Ellison syndrome, insulinoma).

### MEN 2A (Sipple Syndrome)
Caused by mutations in the **RET proto-oncogene**. Characterized by:
- **Medullary thyroid carcinoma** (virtually always present; calcitonin marker).
- **Pheochromocytoma** (often bilateral).
- **Parathyroid hyperplasia** (less common).
**Prophylactic thyroidectomy** is recommended.

### MEN 2B (MEN 3)
Also caused by **RET proto-oncogene** mutations. It is more aggressive and presents in childhood. Features include:
- **Medullary thyroid carcinoma** (most aggressive, earliest onset).
- **Pheochromocytoma**.
- **Mucosal neuromas** (lips, tongue).
- **Marfanoid habitus**.
- **NO parathyroid disease**.

### Summary Table — MEN Syndromes

| Feature | MEN 1 | MEN 2A | MEN 2B |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gene | MEN1 (11q13) | RET (10q11) | RET (10q11) |
| Parathyroid | Yes (90%) | Yes (20–30%) | No |
| Pituitary | Yes (30–40%) | No | No |
| Pancreas | Yes (60–70%) | No | No |
| MTC | No | Yes (90–100%) | Yes (most aggressive) |
| Pheo | No | Yes (50%) | Yes (50%) |
| Mucosal neuromas | No | No | Yes |
| Marfanoid habitus | No | No | Yes |

### Additional High-Yield Points for Exam

**Carcinoid Tumors (Neuroendocrine Tumors — NETs)**
These arise from enterochromaffin cells and most commonly occur in the appendix, ileum, and rectum. They secrete serotonin, and **carcinoid syndrome** occurs when liver metastases lead to systemic release of serotonin. Features include flushing, diarrhea, bronchospasm, and right-sided valvular heart disease. Diagnosis is via elevated urinary 5-HIAA.

2. What is the classic visual field defect associated with a pituitary macroadenoma compressing the optic chiasm? → Bitemporal hemianopia.

4. What are the three classic features unique to Graves disease? → Hyperthyroidism with diffuse goiter, exophthalmos, and pretibial myxedema.
