# Hyperthyroidism in Children

**Year 4 · Paediatrics · Paediatric Endocrinology**

> **In one line:** in children, hyperthyroidism is nearly always **Graves disease**, in which TSH-receptor stimulating antibodies (TRSAbs) drive the thyroid to make excess hormone. Diagnose with suppressed TSH and raised T4/T3 (plus TSHR antibodies), treat first with **methimazole** and a beta-blocker, and move to radioiodine or thyroidectomy if medical therapy fails. Neonatal Graves disease is a transient version caused by maternal antibodies crossing the placenta.

## What you must know

- **Hyperthyroidism** = excess hormone made and secreted by the thyroid. **Thyrotoxicosis** = any state of excess circulating thyroid hormone from any source; the distinction matters because treatment differs.
- **Graves disease** is the commonest cause in children: about 1 in 5000 children, peak **11 to 15 years**, **female:male 5:1**.
- Labs: **TSH suppressed, free T4 and T3 raised**; TSHR antibodies (TSI or TBII) are 96 to 97% sensitive and 99% specific.
- **Methimazole** is first-line antithyroid drug (ATD); **propylthiouracil (PTU) is not recommended in children** because of liver failure.
- Worst ATD side effect: **agranulocytosis** (0.1 to 0.5%): check a white count with any fever, sore throat or mouth ulcers.
- Remission is only about **25% after 2 years** of ATD in children, so many need **radioiodine (131I)** or **total/near-total thyroidectomy**.
- **Neonatal Graves** (about 2% of infants of mothers with Graves) is from transplacental TRSAbs, usually remits by 3 months; untreated, it can cause heart failure, death, craniosynostosis and developmental delay.

## 1. Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Hyperthyroidism** | Synthesis and secretion of excess thyroid hormone by the thyroid gland |
| **Thyrotoxicosis** | Any state of excess circulating thyroid hormone and its clinical effects, whatever the source |

## 2. Aetiology

| Group | Causes |
|---|---|
| **Autoimmune** | **Graves disease** (commonest in children): TRSAbs bind and activate the G protein-coupled TSH receptor |
| **Non-autoimmune hyperthyroidism** | Hyperfunctioning thyroid nodules; germline gain-of-function **TSHR mutations** (autosomal dominant or sporadic); **McCune-Albright syndrome** (activating mutation of the G-protein alpha-subunit) |
| **Rare** | Iodine-induced hyperthyroidism, TSH-secreting adenoma, toxic multinodular goitre, hyperfunctioning thyroid carcinoma |
| **Thyrotoxicosis without hyperthyroidism** | **Thyroiditis**, ingestion of exogenous thyroid hormone (**thyrotoxicosis factitia**) |
| **Inappropriate TSH secretion** | Dominant-negative mutation of thyroid hormone receptor beta (**THRB**), causing **resistance to thyroid hormone (RTH)**; TSH-secreting pituitary tumours (extremely rare in children) |
| **Mainly adult causes (rare in children)** | Choriocarcinoma, hydatidiform mole, struma ovarii, functional thyroid cancer |
| **Neonatal** | Transient disease in infants of mothers with Graves, resolving as maternal TRSAbs clear |

## 3. Graves disease

### Epidemiology

- About **0.02% of children (1 in 5000)**; only **5%** of all patients with hyperthyroidism are under 15 years.
- Peak incidence at **11 to 15 years**; **female:male 5:1**.
- Many have a **family history** of autoimmune thyroid disease.
- Rarely, Graves is seen from 6 weeks to 2 years in children of mothers with no history of hyperthyroidism.

### Pathogenesis

- Autoimmune. A postulated failure of T suppressor cells lets T helper cells sensitised to the TSH receptor antigen interact with B cells, which become **plasma cells making TRSAbs**.
- Thyroid infiltrate: dense lymphoid aggregates rich in **CD4+ helper cells**; cytotoxic **CD8+** cells predominate in less dense areas; activated B cells are more numerous than in blood.
- Systemic lymphoid hyperplasia: **thymic enlargement, splenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, lymphocytosis**, plus lymphocyte and plasma-cell infiltration of the thyroid and retro-orbital tissue.
- TRSAbs bind the TSH receptor, raise **cAMP**, and cause **thyroid hyperplasia and unregulated hormone synthesis** (a diffuse goitre).
- Some patients also make **TSH-receptor blocking antibodies (TRBAbs)** that bind without activating. The clinical course follows the **ratio of stimulating to blocking antibodies**.
- **Ophthalmopathy:** antibodies against antigens shared by thyroid and retro-orbital tissue. TSH receptors are present on retro-orbital adipocytes; TRSAbs bind extraocular muscle and orbital fibroblasts and stimulate glycosaminoglycan and cytokine production. 50 to 75% of children have some eye signs, but they are much milder than in adults.

### Genetic associations

- **HLA-B8** and **HLA-DR3** (DR3 carries a 7-fold relative risk).
- Associated HLA-D3-related autoimmune diseases: **Addison disease, type 1 diabetes, myasthenia gravis, coeliac disease**. Also SLE, rheumatoid arthritis, vitiligo, ITP and pernicious anaemia.
- In family clusters the commonest linked condition is **autoimmune thyroiditis**.

## 4. Clinical features

- The course is variable; children take **longer to remit** than adults.
- Onset is gradual: the interval from onset to diagnosis is typically **6 to 12 months**, and longer in prepubertal children.
- The earliest signs and the biggest differences from adults concern **growth and neuropsychological function**.

| System | Features |
|---|---|
| **Neuropsychiatric** | Tremulousness, headaches, mood disturbance and behavioural swings, poor sleep, short attention span, hyperactivity, fall in school performance. Many are first referred for suspected **ADHD**. |
| **Growth** | Accelerated linear growth and advanced bone age (weight loss despite good appetite is typical) |
| **Cardiovascular** | Raised cardiac output, tachycardia, palpitations, raised systolic BP, **wide pulse pressure**. Cardiac enlargement, failure and atrial fibrillation are rare. |
| **Skin** | Smooth, flushed, sweaty skin. Occasionally vitiligo or psoriasis. **Graves dermopathy** is rare in children and responds to steroids. |
| **Muscle** | **Proximal muscle weakness** is common |
| **Bone** | Increased bone resorption causes low bone density and higher fracture risk in chronic disease; density **normalises with treatment** |
| **Eye** | Stare, lid lag and retraction (from catecholamine potentiation), mild proptosis; milder than adults |
| **Neck** | Diffuse goitre, often with a bruit |

Thyroid hormones **potentiate catecholamines**, which explains tachycardia, tremor, sweating, lid lag and stare.

### Thyroid storm

- Extreme hyperthyroidism with severe biochemical derangement, **hyperthermia, tachycardia, heart failure and restlessness**, which may progress rapidly to delirium, coma and death.
- Precipitants: **trauma, infection, radioactive iodine treatment, surgery**.
- Treat as an emergency: beta-blocker, ATD, iodide (after the first ATD dose), steroids, cooling and supportive care in a high-dependency unit.

## 5. Investigations

### Biochemistry and antibodies

- **TSH suppressed**, **free T4 and T3 raised** (total T4 and T3 also raised).
- **TSHR antibodies** are present in most new cases and are measured two ways:
  - **TSI** (thyroid-stimulating immunoglobulin): a functional assay of cAMP-stimulating antibody (TRSAb).
  - **TBII** (thyrotropin-binding inhibitory immunoglobulin): a binding assay; cannot tell stimulating from blocking antibody.
  - In thyrotoxicosis both are **96 to 97% sensitive, 99% specific** for Graves.

### Radioiodine uptake and scintigraphy

- Used when history, examination and labs do not give the diagnosis.
- **123I** is the isotope of choice; uptake is assessed at 4 and 24 hours.
- **Graves:** uptake **high and diffuse**.
- **Hyperfunctioning nodule:** focal increased uptake.
- **Thyroiditis or exogenous hormone:** uptake **low**.

### Differential diagnosis

| Condition | Distinguishing findings |
|---|---|
| **Thyroid hormone resistance** | Raised free T4 and T3, but TSH inappropriately normal or raised |
| **TSH-secreting pituitary tumour** | Raised common **alpha-subunit** (shared by TSH, LH, FSH and hCG) |
| **Raised thyroxine-binding globulin or familial dysalbuminaemic hyperthyroxinaemia** | High total T4 but **normal free T4 and TSH**; clinically euthyroid |
| **Thyrotoxicosis factitia** | Free T4 and TSH like Graves, but **small thyroid, very low thyroglobulin, suppressed 123I uptake** |
| **Thyroiditis** | Low radioiodine uptake, self-limiting |

> **Exam tip:** low uptake thyrotoxicosis (thyroiditis, factitia) does not respond to ATDs. High uptake means true hyperthyroidism.

## 6. Management of Graves disease

### Antithyroid drugs (ATDs)

- **Methimazole (carbimazole's active metabolite)** is first line. It blocks **organification of iodide**, needed to make thyroid hormone. Half-life 6 to 8 hours allows once or twice daily dosing.
- **Propylthiouracil:** effective but **not recommended in children** because of liver failure.
- Response is seen at **3 to 4 weeks**, with good control by **3 to 4 months**. Reduce to the minimum dose that keeps the child euthyroid.
- **Remission:** about **25% after 2 years** of ATD; 30 to 50% after 4 to 10 years in some studies. Relapses usually occur **6 to 12 months** after stopping; then resume ATD or choose definitive treatment.

### Adverse effects of ATDs

| Reaction | Frequency and notes |
|---|---|
| Minor | 10 to 20%; usually in the first 3 months but can occur any time |
| Severe | 2 to 5% |
| **Urticarial rash** | Common and transient; antihistamine or short break and restart |
| **Agranulocytosis** | **0.1 to 0.5%**; can cause fatal infection. Check a white count in any episode of significant fever, pharyngitis or oral ulcers. |
| **Hepatitis** | 0.2 to 1.0% |
| Others | Lupus-like polyarthritis, glomerulonephritis, ANCA-positive vasculitis |
| **PTU** | Severe liver disease, including liver failure needing transplant; cholestatic jaundice is reversible on stopping |

- **Transient asymptomatic granulocytopenia (<2000/mm3)** is common in Graves disease, is **not** a warning of agranulocytosis and is not a reason to stop treatment.
- Severe adverse effects mean switching to **radioiodine or thyroidectomy**.
- Both methimazole and PTU are linked to **congenital malformations** with in-utero exposure: methimazole with **aplasia cutis, omphalocele, choanal atresia and urinary tract anomalies**; PTU with head, neck and urinary tract malformations.

### Symptom control

- A beta-blocker (**propranolol or atenolol**) controls tachycardia, tremor and sweating. It **does not change thyroid function or exophthalmos**.

### Definitive therapy

Indicated when medical management is not possible, there are severe ATD side effects, an adequate trial has not produced remission, or the family prefers it.

| Option | Key points |
|---|---|
| **Radioiodine ablation (131I)** | Effective in children. In severe thyrotoxicosis, restore euthyroidism with methimazole first to deplete stored hormone and reduce the risk of a **thyrotoxic flare from radiation thyroiditis**. Stop ATD **3 to 5 days before** so uptake is not inhibited. Failure rate 5 to 20%; retreat if still hyperthyroid after 6 months. Goal is **complete ablation**, so hypothyroidism follows and needs lifelong levothyroxine. |
| **Thyroidectomy** | Safe with an experienced surgeon. Render euthyroid with methimazole for 2 to 3 months first. Add **SSKI (1 to 3 drops 2 to 3 times daily) for 7 to 14 days** to reduce gland vascularity. Do **total or near-total** thyroidectomy rather than subtotal. Complications: **hypoparathyroidism** (transient or permanent) and **vocal cord paralysis**. Recurrence is low; patients become hypothyroid. |

### Graves ophthalmopathy

- Usually remits slowly and **independently** of the thyroid disease, but consistent euthyroidism helps.
- Severe cases need **high-dose glucocorticoids, orbital radiotherapy or orbital decompression**.

## 7. Neonatal (congenital) hyperthyroidism

### Cause

- **Transplacental passage of TRSAbs** from a mother with Graves disease that is active, in remission, or treated previously with radioiodine or surgery. Occasionally the mother has chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis with hypothyroidism.
- Occurs in about **2%** of infants of mothers with Graves.
- High TRSAb titre (more than 3 times the upper limit of normal) or a previous affected child raises the risk.
- If the mother took ATDs, onset may be delayed by **3 to 7 days** until the drug is cleared. If TRBAbs are also present, onset may be delayed by weeks, or the baby may be hypothyroid.
- In utero, **fetal tachycardia and goitre** suggest the diagnosis; use close ultrasound surveillance in mothers with uncontrolled disease, especially in the third trimester.

### Clinical features

- Prematurity, **intrauterine growth restriction**, low birth weight, **goitre** (tracheal compression if large).
- Stare, periorbital oedema, eyelid retraction, hyperthermia, irritability, diarrhoea, feeding difficulty, poor weight gain.
- Tachycardia, heart failure, hypertension, hepatosplenomegaly, cholestasis and jaundice, thrombocytopenia, hyperviscosity.
- Labs: suppressed TSH, raised T4, free T4 and T3; TRSAbs markedly raised at birth and clearing by about 3 months.
- Untreated: **cardiac failure and death**. Permanent sequelae include **craniosynostosis and developmental delay**.

### Treatment

- Start at onset of symptoms.
- **Methimazole 0.5 to 1.0 mg/kg/24 h in two doses (every 12 hours)** plus oral or IV **propranolol** (non-selective beta-blocker).
- Refractory: add **Lugol solution or potassium iodide (1 to 2 drops/day)**, the first iodide dose at least **1 hour after** the first ATD dose so it is not used for more hormone synthesis.
- Heart failure: IV fluids, corticosteroids, digitalisation.
- As hormone levels fall, taper the ATD to keep the baby euthyroid; occasionally use **block-and-replace** (ATD plus levothyroxine).

### Course

- Most cases remit by **3 months** (6 to 12 weeks typically), depending on antibody clearance.
- Persisting disease, often with a family history, suggests a **constitutively activating TSHR mutation** (autosomal dominant or sporadic) or **McCune-Albright syndrome**. These recur when ATDs stop and need radioiodine or surgery.

### Prognosis

- Delayed treatment: **advanced bone maturation, microcephaly, cognitive impairment**.
- Most treated infants have normal intellect, but some have neurocognitive problems from fetal hyperthyroidism.
- In-utero hyperthyroidism may suppress the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis, causing transient or permanent **central hypothyroidism** that needs replacement.
- Monitor neurocognitive development throughout childhood.

> **Remember:** mother with Graves post-ablation can still have high TRSAbs and a thyrotoxic baby, even though she herself is euthyroid or hypothyroid on levothyroxine.

## Key points to revise

1. Graves disease is the commonest paediatric hyperthyroidism: peak 11 to 15 years, F:M 5:1, HLA-DR3 and B8.
2. TRSAbs stimulate the TSH receptor via cAMP; ophthalmopathy is milder in children.
3. TSH suppressed with raised free T4 and T3; confirm with TSI or TBII; high diffuse 123I uptake.
4. First line is methimazole; avoid PTU in children (liver failure); watch for agranulocytosis.
5. Beta-blockers control symptoms only; remission is about 25% at 2 years.
6. Definitive therapy: 131I (stop ATD 3 to 5 days before) or total/near-total thyroidectomy (euthyroid first, SSKI beforehand; risks hypoparathyroidism and recurrent laryngeal nerve injury).
7. Thyroid storm is triggered by infection, surgery, trauma or radioiodine.
8. Neonatal Graves: maternal TRSAbs; treat with methimazole, propranolol and iodide; remits by about 3 months; risks heart failure, craniosynostosis and neurodevelopmental delay.
