## Summary
This document provides a comprehensive overview of white blood cell disorders, encompassing both non-neoplastic conditions like leukopenias and reactive proliferations, and a range of neoplastic conditions. It details the pathogenesis, morphology, clinical features, and prognosis of various lymphoid and myeloid malignancies, alongside plasma cell tumors. Key emphasis is placed on molecular mechanisms, classification, and diagnostic distinctions for each entity.

## Key Points
*   Neutropenia, particularly when severe (agranulocytosis) with counts below 500 cells/µL, significantly elevates the risk of fatal bacterial and fungal infections.
*   Reactive leukocytosis, an increase in circulating white cells in response to stimuli, must be carefully distinguished from true leukemias, especially in **leukemoid reactions**.
*   Infectious mononucleosis, caused by **Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)**, is characterized by lymphocytosis of activated CD8+ T cells (atypical lymphocytes) and can lead to complications such as splenic rupture.
*   Chronic nonspecific lymphadenitis manifests in patterns like follicular hyperplasia (B cell), paracortical hyperplasia (T cell), or sinus histiocytosis (macrophages), requiring differentiation from lymphoma.
*   Lymphoid neoplasms are clonal proliferations often arising from **germinal center or post-germinal center B cells**, with classification based on morphology, immunophenotype, genotype, and clinical features.
*   Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL) is the most common childhood leukemia, primarily of pre-B cell origin, with a high cure rate in children, though prognosis varies with age and specific genetic abnormalities.
*   Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia (CLL) and Small Lymphocytic Lymphoma (SLL) are indolent B cell tumours characterized by increased tumour cell survival due to **BCL2** and are the most common adult leukemias in the Western world, prone to immune dysregulation and transformation.
*   Follicular Lymphoma is a prevalent adult non-Hodgkin lymphoma defined by the **t(14;18) translocation** leading to **BCL2 overexpression** and a nodular proliferation of neoplastic germinal center B cells.
*   Multiple Myeloma, the most common plasma cell neoplasm, involves the bone marrow, causing **lytic lesions** throughout the skeleton and producing monoclonal immunoglobulins (**M proteins**) which lead to bone pain, hypercalcaemia, and renal dysfunction.
*   Hodgkin Lymphoma is distinct from non-Hodgkin lymphomas due to the presence of **Reed-Sternberg (RS) giant cells** and its predictable contiguous spread pattern, which allows for local therapy in early stages.

## Detailed Notes

### Non-Neoplastic Disorders of White Cells

**Conditions covered:** Leukopenia · Neutropenia · Reactive Leukocytosis · Infectious Mononucleosis · Reactive Lymphadenitis

### Leukopenia & Neutropenia

**Key concepts:** Mechanisms · Morphology · Clinical features · Treatment

**Leukopenia** results most commonly from a decrease in granulocytes. Lymphopenia is less common and is associated with congenital immunodeficiency, advanced HIV infection, and high-dose corticosteroid therapy.

**Neutropenia / Agranulocytosis** — a reduction in granulocyte count. When severe, it is called agranulocytosis. The risk of fatal bacterial and fungal infection rises sharply once the neutrophil count falls below **500 cells/µL.**

**Two mechanisms of neutropenia:**

**Decreased production** — caused by marrow failure (aplastic anaemia), extensive marrow replacement by tumour (e.g. leukaemia), cancer chemotherapy, or drugs that selectively impair differentiation of committed granulocytic precursors. Isolated neutropenias of this type are also caused by neoplastic proliferations of cytotoxic T cells and NK cells.

**Increased destruction** — caused by immune-mediated injury (sometimes drug-triggered), overwhelming bacterial, fungal, or rickettsial infections (increased peripheral utilisation), or splenomegaly (sequestration and accelerated removal of neutrophils).

**Morphology** — depends on the underlying cause. Marrow is hypercellular when there is excessive destruction or ineffective granulopoiesis (as in megaloblastic anaemia). Marrow is hypocellular when drugs suppress granulopoiesis. Most myelotoxic drugs reduce elements from all marrow lineages, not just granulocytes.

**Clinical features** — ulcerating, necrotizing lesions of the gingiva, floor of mouth, buccal mucosa, and pharynx (**agranulocytic angina**). Lesions contain large masses of organisms because of the absence of leukocytes.

**Treatment** — remove the offending drug, control infection, and administer **G-CSF** (granulocyte colony-stimulating factor) to stimulate neutrophil production by the bone marrow.

### Reactive Leukocytosis

**Key concept:** Types and their distinguishing causes

An increase in circulating white cells in response to microbial or non-microbial stimuli. Classified according to the white cell series affected. In some cases, reactive leukocytosis may mimic leukaemia — these **leukemoid reactions** must be distinguished from true malignancy.

| Type | Common Causes |
|------|--------------|
| Neutrophilic leukocytosis | Acute bacterial infections (pyogenic organisms); sterile inflammation (MI, burns) |
| Eosinophilia | Allergic disorders (asthma, hay fever, pemphigus); parasitic infestations; drug reactions; Hodgkin lymphoma; collagen-vascular disorders; atheroembolic disease |
| Basophilia | Rare; often indicates myeloproliferative disease (e.g. CML) |
| Monocytosis | Chronic infections (TB, endocarditis, rickettsiosis, malaria); SLE; inflammatory bowel disease |
| Lymphocytosis | Chronic immunologic stimulation (TB, brucellosis); viral infections (hepatitis A, CMV, EBV); Bordetella pertussis |

### Infectious Mononucleosis

**Key concepts:** EBV pathogenesis · Morphology · Clinical features · Complications · EBV and malignancy

Infectious mononucleosis is an acute, self-limited disease of adolescents and young adults caused by **Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)**, a herpesvirus. It is characterised by fever, sore throat, generalised lymphadenitis, and a lymphocytosis of activated CD8+ T cells. Cytomegalovirus (CMV) can produce an identical syndrome distinguishable only by serology.

**Epidemiology** — EBV is ubiquitous. In the developing world, primary infection occurs in early childhood and is nearly universal but mostly asymptomatic. In developed countries with better hygiene, infection is delayed to adolescence or young adulthood. Only ~20% of healthy seropositive individuals in developed countries shed virus, and only ~50% of those exposed acquire infection.

**Transmission** — direct oral contact (**"kissing disease"**). The virus initially infects oropharyngeal epithelial cells, then spreads to B cells in underlying lymphoid tissue.

**B cell infection takes two forms:**
- **Lytic infection** (minority of cells) — viral replication → cell lysis → release of virions
- **Latent infection** (majority of cells) — virus persists as an extrachromosomal episome; drives polyclonal B cell activation and proliferation via several EBV-encoded proteins

Latently infected B cells secrete antibodies including **heterophil anti-sheep red cell antibodies** — the basis of the Monospot diagnostic test.

**Immune response** — early IgM antibodies against viral capsid antigens, later shifting to persistent IgG. More important are **cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and NK cells**, which control EBV-positive B cell proliferation. These virus-specific CD8+ T cells appear in the circulation as **atypical lymphocytes** — the hallmark of mononucleosis. A small number of latently infected B cells escape immune control and persist for life.

**Morphology:**
- Peripheral blood leukocytosis with WBC typically 12,000–18,000 cells/µL; >50% are large **atypical lymphocytes** (12–16 µm) with oval, indented, or folded nuclei and abundant cytoplasm — mainly CD8+ T cells
- Lymphadenopathy most prominent in posterior cervical, axillary, and inguinal regions; nodes flooded by atypical lymphocytes occupying paracortical (T cell) areas; occasional Reed-Sternberg-like cells may cause confusion with Hodgkin lymphoma
- Spleen enlarged (300–500 g); infiltration of trabeculae and capsule makes it fragile and prone to rupture
- Liver: atypical lymphocytes infiltrate portal areas and sinusoids; scattered apoptotic cells and foci of necrosis — may resemble viral hepatitis

**Clinical features** — fever, sore throat, malaise, marked fatigability, generalised lymphadenopathy. Atypical presentations include fever of unknown origin, hepatitis, or febrile rash resembling rubella.

**Diagnosis** (in increasing order of specificity):
1. Lymphocytosis with characteristic atypical lymphocytes
2. Positive **heterophil reaction** (Monospot test)
3. Rising titres of antibodies specific for EBV antigens (viral capsid antigens, early antigens, EBNA)

Most patients recover within **4–6 weeks**, though fatigue may persist longer.

**Complications:**
- Hepatic dysfunction — jaundice, elevated liver enzymes, rarely liver failure
- **Splenic rupture** (potentially fatal)
- Neurologic, renal, cardiac, pulmonary, haematologic involvement
- In immunocompromised hosts (transplant recipients, HIV): unimpeded EBV-driven polyclonal B cell proliferation → transforms to monoclonal B cell lymphoma over time; reconstitution of immunity can cause regression

**X-linked lymphoproliferative syndrome** — rare inherited immunodeficiency; mutations in SH2D1A (encodes a signalling protein needed for T cell and NK cell activation). >50% of affected boys develop overwhelming fatal EBV infection; others develop lymphoma or hypogammaglobulinaemia.

### Reactive Lymphadenitis

**Key concepts:** Acute · Chronic (follicular hyperplasia, paracortical hyperplasia, sinus histiocytosis) · Cat-scratch disease

Infections and non-microbial stimuli activate immune cells in lymph nodes, causing lymphadenopathy. Histologic patterns are usually nonspecific.

**Acute Nonspecific Lymphadenitis** — may be localised (draining a local infection) or generalised (systemic infection). Nodes are swollen, grey-red, and engorged with large germinal centres and numerous mitotic figures. Pyogenic organisms cause a neutrophilic infiltrate; severe infections → follicle necrosis → abscess formation. With infection control, nodes may return to normal or undergo scarring.

**Chronic Nonspecific Lymphadenitis** — takes one of three patterns:

**Follicular hyperplasia** — B cell activation; reactive follicles contain activated B cells, scattered T cells, **tingible body macrophages** (containing nuclear debris), and follicular dendritic cells. Causes include rheumatoid arthritis, toxoplasmosis, and early HIV infection. Must be distinguished from follicular lymphoma — features favouring hyperplasia are: preserved nodal architecture, variation in germinal centre size and shape, mixed lymphocyte population, and prominent mitotic and phagocytic activity.

**Paracortical hyperplasia** — T cell zone reaction; parafollicular T cells transform into large proliferating immunoblasts that can efface B cell follicles. Seen in viral infections (especially EBV), after certain vaccinations (e.g. smallpox), and with certain drug reactions (especially phenytoin).

**Sinus histiocytosis** — distension of lymphatic sinusoids by hypertrophied endothelial cells and infiltrating macrophages (histiocytes). Often seen in nodes draining cancers; may represent an immune response to tumour products.

**Cat-Scratch Disease** — self-limited lymphadenitis caused by **Bartonella henselae**. Primarily affects children (<18 years in 90% of cases). Regional lymphadenopathy (most often axilla and neck) appears ~2 weeks after a cat scratch. Morphology: sarcoid-like granulomas that undergo central neutrophilic necrosis → **stellate necrotizing granulomas** — similar to those in lymphogranuloma venereum. The organism is extracellular and visible with silver stains. Diagnosis based on cat exposure history, serology for Bartonella antibodies, and characteristic morphology. Rarely complicated by encephalitis, osteomyelitis, or thrombocytopenia.

### Neoplastic Proliferations of White Cells

**Conditions covered:** Lymphoid neoplasms · Myeloid neoplasms · Histiocytic neoplasms

Tumours are the most important disorders of white cells. They fall into three broad categories:

*   **Lymphoid neoplasms** — non-Hodgkin lymphomas (NHLs), Hodgkin lymphoma, lymphocytic leukaemias, plasma cell neoplasms
*   **Myeloid neoplasms** — acute myeloid leukaemia (AML), myeloproliferative disorders, myelodysplastic syndromes
*   **Histiocytic neoplasms** — proliferations of macrophages and dendritic cells, including Langerhans cell histiocytoses

**Key principles of lymphoid neoplasm classification:**

B and T cell tumours are often composed of cells arrested at a specific stage of normal differentiation. Classification relies on immunohistochemistry or flow cytometry detecting lineage-specific antigens identified by their **CD (cluster of differentiation) number.**

The most common lymphomas are derived from **germinal centre or post-germinal centre B cells.** Germinal centre B cells undergo somatic hypermutation and immunoglobulin class switching — both are error-prone processes that place these cells at high risk for transforming mutations. Most recurrent chromosomal translocations in mature B cell malignancies involve immunoglobulin loci.

All lymphoid neoplasms are **clonal** — derived from a single transformed cell. Antigen receptor gene rearrangement precedes transformation, so all daughter cells share the same receptor gene configuration. This allows molecular distinction of clonal neoplasms from polyclonal reactive processes.

Lymphoid neoplasms often **disrupt normal immune function** — both immunodeficiency and autoimmunity may occur in the same patient. Patients with inherited or acquired immunodeficiency are themselves at high risk for EBV-associated lymphoid neoplasms.

NHLs are usually **widely disseminated at diagnosis** on sensitive molecular assays, even when clinically localised — systemic therapy is required. By contrast, **Hodgkin lymphoma** often arises at a single site and spreads predictably to contiguous lymph node groups — early-stage disease is sometimes treated with local therapy alone.

Classification is by the **WHO scheme**, incorporating morphology, immunophenotype, genotype, and clinical features. Three major categories: (1) B cell tumours, (2) T cell and NK cell tumours, (3) Hodgkin lymphoma.

### Acute Lymphoblastic Leukaemia / Lymphoblastic Lymphoma

**Key concepts:** Pathogenesis · Morphology · Genetics · Prognosis

ALL and lymphoblastic lymphoma are aggressive tumours of immature lymphocytes (lymphoblasts), occurring predominantly in children and young adults. They are morphologically and clinically indistinguishable and are treated similarly. ALL constitutes **80% of childhood leukaemia**, peaking at age 4, mostly of pre-B cell origin. Pre-T cell tumours are most common in males aged 15–20.

**Pathogenesis** — the principal defect is a **block in differentiation** caused by acquired mutations in lineage-specific transcription factors:
- B-ALL: TEL1, AML1, E2A, PAX5, EBF mutations
- T-ALL: TAL1 and NOTCH1 mutations

Complementary mutations allow **growth factor-independent proliferation.** In B-ALL, the most important is the **BCR-ABL fusion gene** created by the **(9;22) translocation** (Philadelphia chromosome).

**Clinical features** — symptoms relate to bone marrow suppression and tumour infiltration of other organs.
- Abrupt onset of symptom onset
- Fatigue (anaemia), fever (neutropenia-related infections), bleeding — petechiae, ecchymoses, epistaxis, gum bleeding (thrombocytopenia)
- Bone pain and tenderness from marrow expansion and subperiosteal infiltration
- Generalised lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, hepatomegaly — more pronounced in ALL than AML
- CNS manifestations (headache, vomiting, nerve palsies from meningeal spread) — more common in children and in ALL

**Laboratory findings** — WBC variable (may exceed 100,000/µL but is <10,000/µL in ~50%); anaemia almost always present; platelets usually <100,000/µL; neutropenia common. Diagnosis requires identification of blasts — peripheral blood may show none (aleukemic leukaemia), in which case marrow examination is essential.

**Morphology** — in ALL, blasts compose >25% of marrow cellularity. Lymphoblasts have coarse clumped chromatin, one or two nucleoli, and scant agranular cytoplasm. Cytoplasmic glycogen granules are PAS-positive. Distinguish from myeloblasts (finer chromatin, more cytoplasm, azurophilic granules, peroxidase-positive).

**Immunophenotype** — **TdT (terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase)** is present in >95% of ALL cases and is specific for pre-B and pre-T cells. Further subtyping uses CD19 (B cell) and CD3 (T cell).

**Genetic features** — ~90% of ALLs have non-random karyotypic abnormalities:
- Childhood pre-B ALL: hyperdiploidy (>50 chromosomes) and t(12;21) involving TEL1/AML1 — good prognosis
- Adult pre-B ALL: ~25% have **t(9;22) BCR-ABL** — poor prognosis
- Pre-T ALL: diverse chromosomal aberrations including T cell receptor locus translocations

**Prognosis** — treatment of childhood ALL is one of oncology's great success stories. Children aged 2–10 have the best outcomes; up to **80% are cured** with intensive chemotherapy. Poor prognostic variables: male gender, age <2 or >10, high WBC at diagnosis, molecular evidence of persistent disease at day 28 of treatment, MLL rearrangement, or BCR-ABL fusion.

### Chronic Lymphocytic Leukaemia / Small Lymphocytic Lymphoma

**Key concepts:** Pathogenesis · Morphology · Immunophenotype · Clinical features · Transformation

CLL and SLL are essentially identical, differing only in degree of peripheral blood involvement. If peripheral blood lymphocyte count exceeds **4,000 cells/µL** → CLL; if not → SLL. **CLL is the most common leukaemia of adults in the Western world.** Both are much less common in Asian populations.

**Pathogenesis** — an indolent, slowly growing tumour where **increased tumour cell survival** (not proliferation) is the key mechanism. Tumour cells contain high levels of **BCL2** (anti-apoptotic protein) — not due to gene rearrangement (as in follicular lymphoma) but likely from loss of regulatory micro-RNAs encoded on chromosome 13.

Immune dysregulation is prominent: tumour cell accumulation suppresses normal B cell function → **hypogammaglobulinaemia** in >50% of patients. Paradoxically, ~15% of patients develop **autoantibodies** against their own red cells or platelets (made by non-malignant bystander B cells). Over time, tumour cells displace normal marrow elements → anaemia, neutropenia, thrombocytopenia.

**Morphology** — sheets of small lymphocytes with scattered ill-defined foci of larger, actively dividing cells (**proliferation centres**) diffusely efface lymph nodes. **Proliferation centres are pathognomonic for CLL/SLL.** Circulating tumour cells are fragile and produce characteristic **smudge cells** on blood smears.

**Immunophenotype and genetics** — mature B cells expressing CD19, CD20, CD23, surface immunoglobulin, and **CD5** (a helpful clue — among B cell lymphomas, only CLL/SLL and mantle cell lymphoma commonly express CD5). ~50% have karyotypic abnormalities: trisomy 12 and deletions of chromosomes 11, 13, and 17. Activating NOTCH1 mutations predict worse outcome. Chromosomal translocations are rare (unlike other B cell neoplasms).

**Clinical features** — often asymptomatic at first detection. Common signs: easy fatigability, weight loss, anorexia, generalised lymphadenopathy, and hepatosplenomegaly (50–60% of patients). Hypogammaglobulinaemia → increased susceptibility to bacterial infections. Less commonly, autoimmune haemolytic anaemia and thrombocytopenia occur.

**Prognosis** — extremely variable. Many patients live >10 years and die of unrelated causes; median survival 4–6 years. Over time, CLL/SLL tends to transform to more aggressive tumours resembling prolymphocytic leukaemia or **diffuse large B cell lymphoma (Richter transformation)** → median survival <1 year after transformation.

### Follicular Lymphoma

**Key concepts:** Pathogenesis · Morphology · Clinical features · Prognosis · Transformation

Follicular lymphoma constitutes **40% of adult NHLs** in the United States and is much less common in Asian populations.

**Pathogenesis** — neoplastic cells express **BCL2**, which is absent from normal germinal centre B cells. >85% of tumours have a **(14;18) translocation** fusing the BCL2 gene (chromosome 18) to the IgH locus (chromosome 14) → inappropriate BCL2 overexpression → tumour cell survival. ~One third of cases also have loss-of-function mutations in genes encoding **histone acetyl transferases** → epigenetic dysregulation contributes to tumourigenesis.

**Morphology** — lymph nodes show a **distinctly nodular (follicular) proliferation.** Neoplastic cells resemble germinal centre B cells — predominantly small **"cleaved" cells (centrocytes)** with angular nuclei and linear infoldings, mixed with variable numbers of larger cells (centroblasts) with vesicular chromatin and prominent nucleoli.

### Burkitt Lymphoma

Burkitt lymphoma is among the **fastest-growing human neoplasms**, yet with very aggressive chemotherapy, the majority of patients can be cured. It primarily affects children and young adults. Accounts for ~30% of childhood NHLs in the United States. Disease usually arises at extranodal sites:
- **Endemic (African) form** — commonly presents as maxillary or mandibular jaw masses; strongly EBV-associated
- **Sporadic form** — abdominal tumours involving bowel, retroperitoneum, and ovaries; EBV association weaker
- Leukaemic presentation occurs but must be distinguished from ALL (treated with different drug regimens)

### Multiple Myeloma & Related Plasma Cell Tumours

**Key concepts:** Multiple myeloma · Pathogenesis · Morphology · Clinical features · MGUS · Lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma

Plasma cell neoplasms produce a single complete or partial immunoglobulin detectable in the serum — hence termed **monoclonal gammopathies** (**M proteins**). They account for **~15% of deaths from white blood cell tumours** and are most common in middle-aged and elderly persons.

**Six major variants:** (1) Multiple myeloma, (2) Solitary plasmacytoma, (3) Lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma, (4) Heavy-chain disease, (5) Primary amyloidosis, (6) MGUS.

### Multiple Myeloma

The most common variant. Approximately **20,000 new cases per year** in the United States. Median age at diagnosis is 70 years; more common in males and in people of African origin. Principally involves bone marrow with **lytic lesions** throughout the skeleton.

**M proteins produced:**
- IgG — 60% of cases
- IgA — 20–25%
- IgM, IgD, IgE — rare
- Free light chains only (κ or λ) — 15–20%; excreted in urine as **Bence Jones proteins**
- Most malignant plasma cells secrete both complete immunoglobulins and free light chains → both M proteins and Bence Jones proteins

**Pathogenesis:**
- Chromosomal translocations involving the IgH locus (chromosome 14) and various partners including cyclin D1, FGFR3, and cyclin D3 → **dysregulation of D cyclins is common**
- Late in course, MYC translocations may also occur
- Tumour cell proliferation is supported by **IL-6** produced by bone marrow stromal fibroblasts and macrophages
- Bone resorption results from myeloma cell secretion of IL-1β, TNF, and IL-6 → stimulate RANK-ligand production → osteoclast differentiation and activity
- Myeloma cells interfere with normal plasma cell function → **immunosuppression and defective antibody production** despite high total immunoglobulin (from M protein)

**Renal dysfunction** is a common, serious complication:
- Obstructive proteinaceous casts in distal tubules and collecting ducts (**Bence Jones proteins** + Tamm-Horsfall protein + albumin) → **myeloma nephrosis**
- Multinucleate giant cells from macrophages surround casts; adjacent epithelium becomes necrotic from toxic effects of Bence Jones proteins
- Light chain deposition in glomeruli or interstitium (as amyloid or linear deposits)
- Hypercalcaemia → dehydration and renal stones
- Recurrent bacterial pyelonephritis

**Morphology:**
- Multifocal destructive skeletal lesions most commonly in vertebral column, ribs, skull, pelvis, femur, clavicle, and scapula
- Lesions arise in medullary cavity → erode cancellous and cortical bone → pathologic fractures (most often vertebral column or femur)
- Bone lesions appear as **punched-out defects 1–4 cm** on X-ray or diffuse demineralisation
- Marrow shows >30% plasma cells, often with abnormal features: prominent nucleoli, abnormal cytoplasmic inclusions containing immunoglobulin
- Disease progression: plasma cells infiltrate spleen, liver, kidneys, lungs, lymph nodes, soft tissues; terminal stages may show leukaemic picture

**Clinical features:**
- **Bone pain** from pathologic fractures; vertebral fractures may cause spinal cord impingement — an oncologic emergency
- **Hypercalcaemia** → confusion, lethargy, renal dysfunction
- **Anaemia** from marrow replacement and suppressed haematopoiesis
- **Recurrent bacterial infections** (S. aureus, S. pneumoniae, E. coli) from suppressed humoral immunity
- **Renal insufficiency** in up to 50% of patients
- **AL-type amyloidosis** in 5–10%
- Hyperviscosity symptoms (less characteristic than in lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma)

**Diagnosis:** Characteristic punched-out skeletal defects on imaging + electrophoresis of serum and urine showing monoclonal complete immunoglobulin or free light chain in 99% of cases + bone marrow examination confirming plasma cell proliferation.

### Solitary Plasmacytoma

Plasma tumours manifesting as isolated lesions. **Skeletal plasmacytomas** occur in similar locations to myeloma and usually progress to full-blown multiple myeloma over **5–10 years.** **Soft tissue plasmacytomas** (most often upper respiratory tract) spread infrequently and are often cured by local resection.

### Monoclonal Gammopathy of Undetermined Significance (MGUS)

M proteins are found in the serum of **1–3% of otherwise healthy persons >50 years** — the most common plasma cell proliferation. Despite its name, MGUS is increasingly recognised as a **precursor lesion** — symptomatic plasma cell tumour (most commonly myeloma) develops at a rate of **1% per year.** Clonal plasma cells in MGUS contain the same chromosomal translocations as full-blown myeloma. Diagnosis requires <3 g/dL monoclonal protein in serum and no Bence Jones proteinuria.

### Lymphoplasmacytic Lymphoma / Waldenström Macroglobulinaemia

Composed of a mixture of small lymphocytes, plasmacytic lymphocytes, and plasma cells. Behaves like an indolent B cell lymphoma; involves lymph nodes, bone marrow, and spleen. Secretes **IgM (macroglobulin)** → blood viscosity increases → **Waldenström macroglobulinaemia:**
- Visual impairment from tortuous dilated retinal veins, retinal haemorrhages and exudates
- Neurological symptoms
- Bleeding from interference with platelet and coagulation factor function
- Raynaud phenomenon and cold urticaria (if IgM is a cryoglobulin)

Heavy and light chain synthesis is balanced → no free light chains → no Bence Jones proteinuria. **Unlike myeloma:** no lytic bone lesions; only rarely associated with amyloidosis.

**Other variants:**
- **Heavy-chain disease** — proliferations producing only heavy chains; IgA heavy-chain disease has predilection for GI/respiratory lymphoid tissue; may be a variant of extranodal marginal zone lymphoma
- **Primary amyloidosis** — monoclonal plasma cells secrete free light chains → AL-type amyloid deposits

### Hodgkin Lymphoma

**Key concepts:** Distinction from NHL · Reed-Sternberg cells · WHO classification · Spread pattern

Hodgkin lymphoma is set apart from NHLs by the presence of distinctive **Reed-Sternberg (RS) giant cells** — the neoplastic cell. RS cells are typically vastly outnumbered by non-neoplastic inflammatory cells (lymphocytes, eosinophils, plasma cells, macrophages). The biologic behaviour and treatment of Hodgkin lymphoma differ importantly from NHLs.

**Key clinical distinction from NHL:** Hodgkin lymphoma often arises at a **single site** and spreads in a predictable, contiguous fashion to adjacent lymph node groups → early-stage disease is sometimes treated with **local radiotherapy alone.** NHLs are usually widely disseminated at diagnosis and require systemic therapy.

**WHO classification of Hodgkin lymphoma:**

| Subtype | Key Features |
|---------|-------------|
| Nodular sclerosis | Most common; collagen bands divide lymph node into nodules; lacunar RS cell variant; young adults; mediastinal involvement common |
| Mixed cellularity | Classic RS cells with mixed inflammatory background; intermediate prognosis |
| Lymphocyte-rich | Abundant lymphocytes; rare RS cells; best prognosis |
| Lymphocyte depletion | Few lymphocytes; many RS cells or fibrosis; worst prognosis; often advanced stage |
| Nodular lymphocyte predominance | Distinct entity; "popcorn" (L&H) RS cell variant; CD20+; indolent |

**Reed-Sternberg cells** — large cells with abundant cytoplasm and bilobed or multilobed nuclei each containing a huge inclusion-like nucleolus (**"owl-eye" appearance**). RS cells derive from germinal centre B cells — they have rearranged immunoglobulin genes but have lost normal B cell gene expression programmes.

## Practice Questions
1. What is the critical neutrophil count below which the risk of fatal bacterial and fungal infection rises sharply? → Below 500 cells/µL.
2. What are the three diagnostic criteria for infectious mononucleosis, in increasing order of specificity? → Lymphocytosis with atypical lymphocytes, positive heterophil reaction (Monospot test), rising titres of antibodies specific for EBV antigens.
3. What is a key histologic feature pathognomonic for CLL/SLL? → The presence of proliferation centres.
4. Which chromosomal translocation is highly characteristic of Follicular Lymphoma and what gene is overexpressed as a result? → The t(14;18) translocation, leading to BCL2 overexpression.
5. In Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia, what gene fusion, created by the (9;22) translocation, indicates a poor prognosis in adult pre-B ALL? → The BCR-ABL fusion gene.
6. What are the major clinical complications of Multiple Myeloma related to bone involvement? → Bone pain from pathologic fractures and hypercalcaemia.
7. What is the most common plasma cell proliferation found in otherwise healthy individuals over 50 years old, and what is its clinical significance? → Monoclonal Gammopathy of Undetermined Significance (MGUS), recognized as a precursor lesion that progresses to symptomatic plasma cell tumour at a rate of 1% per year.
8. What is the key histological feature that distinguishes Hodgkin Lymphoma from non-Hodgkin Lymphoma? → The presence of distinctive Reed-Sternberg (RS) giant cells.
