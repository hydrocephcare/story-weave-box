### 1. True Pathogenic Fungi vs Opportunistic Fungi
**Question:** In what respect do mycoses caused by true pathogenic fungi differ from mycoses caused by fungi associated with opportunistic infections? What are the general consequences of these infections to the human host?
- True pathogens infect **healthy, immunocompetent** individuals with normal host defenses
- Opportunistic fungi only cause disease in **immunocompromised** hosts — HIV/AIDS, transplant recipients, diabetics, patients on steroids
- True pathogens are **geographically restricted** e.g. *Histoplasma capsulatum*, *Coccidioides immitis*
- Opportunistic fungi are **ubiquitous** in the environment e.g. *Candida*, *Aspergillus*, *Cryptococcus*
- True pathogens have defined virulence mechanisms independent of host immune status
- **Consequences:** chronic pulmonary disease, disseminated infection, CNS involvement, septicaemia, death in severe cases

### 2. Disease Name Matching
**Question:** Match the common name of the disease with the clinical name
- Darling's disease → **Histoplasmosis**
- Valley fever → **Coccidioidomycosis**
- South American / Brazilian Blastomycosis → **Paracoccidioidomycosis**
- Mycotic Mycetoma → **Pseudoallescheriasis**
- Balanitis → **Candidiasis of the penis**

### 3. Risk Factors for Fungal Infections
**Question:** Outline risk factors to fungal infections
- HIV/AIDS and other immunodeficiencies
- Prolonged or broad-spectrum antibiotic use — disrupts normal protective flora
- Corticosteroid or immunosuppressive therapy
- Diabetes mellitus — impairs phagocyte function
- Haematological malignancies e.g. leukaemia, lymphoma
- Organ or bone marrow transplantation
- Indwelling catheters and prosthetic devices
- Extremes of age — neonates and elderly
- Malnutrition and chronic illness

### 4. Virulence Factors of Fungi
**Question:** Explain any two virulence factors used by fungi to cause diseases in the host
**1. Capsule production** — e.g. *Cryptococcus neoformans*
- Polysaccharide capsule resists phagocytosis
- Inhibits complement activation
- Suppresses T-cell mediated immunity
**2. Dimorphism**
- Switch between mould (environmental) and yeast (tissue) form
- Yeast form resists host defenses and persists inside macrophages
- e.g. *Histoplasma*, *Blastomyces*, *Coccidioides*
**Other virulence factors:**
- Melanin production — protects against oxidative killing by phagocytes
- Enzyme secretion — proteases and lipases break down host tissue
- Biofilm formation — protects *Candida* from antifungals and immune attack

### 5. Eumycetoma vs Actinomycetoma
**Question:** Compare Eumycetoma and Actinomycetoma
| Feature | Eumycetoma | Actinomycetoma |
|---|---|---|
| Causative agent | True fungi e.g. *Madurella mycetomatis* | Filamentous bacteria e.g. *Nocardia*, *Actinomadura* |
| Grain colour | Black or white | White, yellow, or red |
| Grain texture | Hard, compact | Soft, friable |
| Treatment | Antifungals — ketoconazole, voriconazole | Antibiotics — TMP-SMX, streptomycin |
| Response to treatment | Poor | Better response |

### 6. Histoplasmosis
**Question:** Discuss the causative agent, clinical manifestation and treatment of Histoplasmosis
**Causative agent:**
- *Histoplasma capsulatum* — thermally dimorphic fungus
- Mould in environment, yeast in human tissue at 37°C
- Found in soil enriched with bird and bat droppings
- Endemic in Ohio and Mississippi River valleys; also found in parts of Africa
**Clinical manifestations:**
- Most infections (90%) are **asymptomatic**
- Acute pulmonary histoplasmosis — flu-like illness, self-limiting, fever, cough, chest pain
- Chronic pulmonary histoplasmosis — resembles TB, cavitation, fibrosis, haemoptysis
- Disseminated histoplasmosis — fever, hepatosplenomegaly, pancytopenia, weight loss; common in immunocompromised
- CNS involvement — meningitis in severe disseminated disease
**Treatment:**
- Mild to moderate: **Itraconazole** — drug of choice
- Severe or disseminated: **Amphotericin B** first, then step down to itraconazole
- Duration: 6–12 weeks minimum; longer in HIV patients
- Lifelong suppressive therapy with itraconazole in AIDS patients

### 7. Cryptococcosis
**Question:** Outline predisposing factors and diagnosis of Cryptococcosis. List four clinical manifestations of Cryptococcosis
**Predisposing factors:**
- HIV/AIDS — CD4 count below 100 cells/uL
- Corticosteroid use
- Organ transplantation
- Haematological malignancies
- Sarcoidosis
**Diagnosis:**
- **India ink preparation** — visualises capsule in CSF
- **Cryptococcal antigen test** (latex agglutination) — highly sensitive and specific
- **Culture** on Sabouraud dextrose agar — mucoid, cream-coloured colonies
- **Nigerseed/birdseed agar** — brown colonies due to melanin production
- **Histopathology** — mucicarmine stain highlights polysaccharide capsule
**Clinical manifestations:**
- Meningitis or meningoencephalitis — most serious
- Pulmonary cryptococcosis — cough, chest pain
- Skin lesions — umbilicated papules resembling molluscum contagiosum
- Bone and joint involvement

### 8. Dimorphism in Systemic Fungi
**Question:** Describe dimorphism in relation to systemic fungi
- Dimorphic fungi exist in **two morphological forms** depending on temperature
- At **25–30°C** (environment/laboratory): grow as **moulds** with hyphae and conidia
- At **35–37°C** (human body): convert to **yeast** form
- This thermal switching is a key virulence mechanism
- Yeast form evades phagocytic killing and disseminates via bloodstream
- Examples: *Histoplasma capsulatum*, *Blastomyces dermatitidis*, *Coccidioides immitis*, *Sporothrix schenckii*, *Paracoccidioides brasiliensis*

### 9. Paracoccidioidomycosis
**Question:** Describe the diagnosis of paracoccidioidomycosis
- **Direct microscopy** — sputum or tissue showing characteristic **"pilot's wheel" / "mariner's wheel"** yeast with multiple peripheral budding daughter cells — pathognomonic
- **Culture** on Sabouraud agar — slow growing, mould form at 25°C; yeast at 37°C
- **Histopathology** — GMS or PAS stain
- **Serology** — immunodiffusion, complement fixation tests
- **Chest X-ray** — bilateral butterfly infiltrates

### 10. Tinea Versicolor
**Question:** Explain the clinical manifestation of Tinea versicolor
- Caused by *Malassezia furfur*
- Hypopigmented or hyperpigmented macules on trunk, neck, upper arms
- More noticeable after sun exposure — affected areas fail to tan
- Mild scaling, rarely itchy
- Diagnosis by KOH prep — **"spaghetti and meatballs"** pattern (short hyphae + round spores)

### 11. Tinea Nigra Palmaris
**Question:** Describe etiology and symptoms of Tinea nigra palmaris
- Caused by *Hortaea werneckii* — a dematiaceous (black) fungus
- Superficial infection of the palms, rarely soles
- Presents as dark brown to black, non-scaly macule on palm
- Painless and non-inflammatory
- Can mimic **malignant melanoma** — important differential diagnosis
- Common in tropical and subtropical regions, especially young females

### 12. Five Types of Dermatophytoses
**Question:** Discuss five types of dermatophytoses
**1. Tinea capitis** — scalp
- *Trichophyton* and *Microsporum* species
- Hair loss, scaling, broken hairs, kerion formation
- Common in children
**2. Tinea corporis** — body ringworm
- Ring-shaped, scaly, erythematous lesions with central clearing
- Caused by *T. rubrum*, *M. canis*
**3. Tinea pedis** — athlete's foot
- Maceration and scaling between toes
- Caused by *T. rubrum*, *T. interdigitale*
**4. Tinea unguium / Onychomycosis** — nails
- Thickened, discoloured, brittle nails
- Caused by *T. rubrum*
**5. Tinea cruris** — jock itch
- Erythematous plaques in groin and inner thighs
- More common in males
- Caused by *T. rubrum*, *Epidermophyton floccosum*

### 13. Epidermophyton and Microsporum
**Question:** Describe diagnosis of Epidermophyton and Microsporum
- **KOH preparation** — skin scrapings, nail clippings, or hair samples
- **Wood's lamp** — *Microsporum* species fluoresce green; *Epidermophyton* does not
- **Culture** on Sabouraud dextrose agar with cycloheximide
- *Microsporum* — spindle-shaped, thick-walled macroconidia
- *Epidermophyton* — club-shaped macroconidia; **no microconidia** — key distinguishing feature
- Slide culture for definitive morphological identification

### 14. Modes of Action of Antifungals
**Question:** Explain any five modes of action of antifungals
1. **Disruption of cell membrane** — Polyenes (Amphotericin B) bind ergosterol, forming pores causing leakage and cell death
2. **Inhibition of ergosterol synthesis** — Azoles (fluconazole, ketoconazole) inhibit lanosterol 14-alpha demethylase, depleting ergosterol
3. **Inhibition of cell wall synthesis** — Echinocandins (caspofungin) inhibit beta-1,3-glucan synthase, disrupting cell wall integrity
4. **Inhibition of nucleic acid synthesis** — 5-Flucytosine converted intracellularly to 5-fluorouracil, inhibiting RNA and DNA synthesis
5. **Inhibition of mitosis** — Griseofulvin binds tubulin, disrupting spindle formation in dermatophytes
6. **Inhibition of squalene epoxidase** — Allylamines (terbinafine) block ergosterol synthesis at an earlier step than azoles

### 15. Candidiasis
**Question:** Discuss the clinical manifestation and treatment of candidiasis
**Clinical manifestations:**
- **Oral candidiasis (thrush)** — white plaques on tongue and mucosa, easily scraped off leaving erythematous base
- **Oesophageal candidiasis** — dysphagia, odynophagia; AIDS-defining illness
- **Vulvovaginal candidiasis** — thick white "cottage cheese" discharge, pruritus, erythema
- **Cutaneous candidiasis** — intertriginous areas, diaper rash in infants
- **Balanitis** — erythema and white plaques on glans penis
- **Invasive/systemic candidiasis** — fungaemia, endocarditis, meningitis in immunocompromised
**Treatment:**
- Topical: Clotrimazole, miconazole — for superficial infections
- Oral: **Fluconazole** — drug of choice for most mucosal infections
- Severe/systemic: **Amphotericin B** or **Echinocandins** (caspofungin)
- *C. krusei* and some *C. glabrata* are fluconazole-resistant — use echinocandins

### 16. Mycetoma
**Question:** Describe the pathogenesis and laboratory diagnosis of mycetoma
**Pathogenesis:**
- Entry via **traumatic inoculation** — thorns, splinters into skin; usually the foot
- Organism spreads locally through subcutaneous tissue
- Forms granulomatous reaction with **draining sinuses**
- Characteristic **grains** (compact colonies of organism) discharged through sinuses
- Can invade bone causing osteolysis if untreated
**Laboratory diagnosis:**
- **Macroscopic** — examine grain colour and texture from discharge
- **KOH preparation** — distinguish hyphae from bacterial filaments
- **Culture** on Sabouraud agar — identify genus and species by colony morphology
- **Histopathology** — GMS and PAS stain; identify grain architecture
- **X-ray/MRI** — assess bone involvement
- **PCR** — definitive species identification

### 17. Systemic Mycoses — Laboratory Diagnosis
**Question:** Describe the procedure involved in lab diagnosis of systemic mycoses
1. **Specimen collection** — sputum, BAL, blood, CSF, bone marrow, tissue biopsy depending on site
2. **Direct microscopy** — KOH prep, India ink for CSF (*Cryptococcus*)
3. **Special stains** — GMS (Gomori methenamine silver), PAS, Mucicarmine
4. **Culture** — Sabouraud dextrose agar at both 25°C and 37°C to demonstrate dimorphism; incubate up to 4–6 weeks
5. **Biochemical tests** — urease test (*Cryptococcus*), germ tube test (*Candida albicans*)
6. **Serology** — antigen detection (cryptococcal antigen, Histoplasma urine antigen), complement fixation, immunodiffusion
7. **Histopathology** — tissue sections with special stains
8. **Molecular methods** — PCR for confirmation

### 18. Characteristics of Systemic Mycoses
**Question:** List four characteristics of systemic mycoses
- Caused by thermally dimorphic fungi
- Primary infection usually via **inhalation** of conidia or spores
- Can disseminate from lungs to other organs via bloodstream
- Asymptomatic or mild in immunocompetent; severe and life-threatening in immunocompromised
- Not transmitted person-to-person

### 19. Classes of Fungi Based on Reproduction
**Question:** Discuss classes of fungi based on reproduction
**1. Zygomycetes (Zygomycota)**
- Sexual reproduction by fusion of hyphae forming **zygospores**
- Asexual reproduction by sporangiospores in sporangia
- e.g. *Rhizopus*, *Mucor* — cause mucormycosis
**2. Ascomycetes (Ascomycota)**
- Sexual reproduction produces **ascospores** within a sac called an ascus
- Asexual reproduction by conidia
- Largest class; includes *Aspergillus*, *Histoplasma*, dermatophytes
**3. Basidiomycetes (Basidiomycota)**
- Sexual reproduction produces **basidiospores** on club-shaped basidia
- e.g. *Cryptococcus neoformans* — only major pathogenic basidiomycete
- Also includes mushrooms, rusts, smuts
**4. Deuteromycetes / Fungi Imperfecti**
- **No known sexual stage** — reproduce only by asexual conidia
- Most medically important fungi placed here until sexual stage is discovered
- e.g. *Candida*, *Coccidioides*

### 20. Fungi with Special Characteristics
**Question:** Name one fungus with the following characteristic. List two fungi that multiply within macrophages, produce melanin, affect CNS. Name fungi that form yeast-like cells
- Production of sclerotic bodies → ***Fonsecaea pedrosoi***
- Characterized by erythema nodosum → ***Coccidioides immitis***
- Associated with pigeon droppings → ***Cryptococcus neoformans***
- Multiply within macrophages → *Histoplasma capsulatum*, *Cryptococcus neoformans*
- Produce melanin → *Cryptococcus neoformans*, *Fonsecaea pedrosoi*
- Affect CNS → *Cryptococcus neoformans*, *Coccidioides immitis*
- Etiology is a basidiomycete → *Cryptococcus neoformans*
- Form yeast-like cells → *Candida albicans*, *Histoplasma capsulatum*
