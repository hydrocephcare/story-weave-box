# Infective Endocarditis

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Cardiology**

> **In one line:** infective endocarditis (IE) is infection of the endocardial surface of the heart, usually a damaged or prosthetic valve, in which bacteria stick to a platelet-fibrin deposit and form a vegetation. It causes fever, new murmurs, embolic and immune-complex phenomena, and carries about 20% mortality. Diagnose with blood cultures plus echocardiography (modified Duke criteria) and treat with prolonged high-dose bactericidal antibiotics, with surgery for failure, heart failure, abscess or embolism.

## What you must know

- Healthy endocardium is almost never infected: IE needs **abnormal endocardium** (valve disease, congenital heart disease, prosthetic valve) plus **bacteraemia**.
- Commonest organism overall: ***Streptococcus viridans***; ***Staphylococcus aureus*** in IV drug users and in early (within 60 days) prosthetic valve IE.
- **Acute** IE (staph, normal valve, fatal in under 6 weeks) versus **subacute** (viridans streptococci or enterococci on a damaged valve, slower).
- Aortic and mitral valves are usually involved; in **IV drug users the right side (tricuspid)** is commonest.
- Diagnosis: **modified Duke criteria**; **echocardiography** (TTE, then TEE) and **at least 2 sets of blood cultures** before antibiotics.
- Treatment: **4 to 6 weeks of bactericidal** antibiotics; empiric **vancomycin plus gentamicin**.
- **Heart failure** is the commonest complication and the **strongest indication for surgery**.
- Prophylaxis is now limited to **highest-risk patients having highest-risk dental procedures**; oral hygiene matters more.

## 1. Definition

Infection of the endothelial surface of the heart, including:

- **Heart valves** (usually the cusps) and intracardiac foreign bodies (prosthetic valves, pacemaker leads, surgical conduits)
- **Septal defects**
- **Chordae tendineae**
- **Arteriovenous shunts** (e.g. dialysis fistulae)

The heart wall has three layers: endocardium (inflammation = endocarditis), myocardium (myocarditis) and epicardium (pericarditis).

Even with treatment it remains life-threatening, with mortality of about **20%**.

## 2. Pathogenesis

1. **Endothelial damage:** turbulent blood flow from congenital or acquired heart disease (flow from high to low pressure, or across a narrow orifice; jets from septal defects or a patent ductus) injures the endocardium. Matrix molecules, inflammatory cells and platelets deposit at the injured site.
2. **Non-bacterial thrombotic endocarditis (NBTE):** platelets and fibrin deposit on the damaged surface.
3. **Bacteraemia:** transient bacteraemia from trauma to a mucosa heavily colonised by normal flora (today mostly the **oral mucosa and gingiva**; the GI tract, urethra and vagina are controversial sources). Other sources: poor dental hygiene, IV drug use, soft tissue infection, dental treatment, intravascular cannulae, cardiac surgery, pacemakers.
4. **Bacterial adherence:** organisms stick to the NBTE and multiply to form an **infected vegetation** (bacterial thrombotic endocarditis).
5. **Consequences:** local destruction, extension (perivalvular abscess, fistula) and **embolisation**.

> **Remember:** vegetations cause two problems: **immunological** (antigen-antibody complexes: rash, glomerulonephritis, haematuria, Osler's nodes) and **vascular** (emboli to brain causing stroke or mycotic aneurysm, or to the periphery causing infarcts).

- **Which valves:** aortic and mitral are most commonly involved. **IV drug users: right-sided** lesions are commoner.
- **Prosthetic valves** are more dangerous than native valves: they have no blood supply, so antibiotics cannot reach them and cure usually needs **removal of the valve**.

## 3. Risk factors

### Who gets IE?

Those with abnormal endocardium: valvular heart disease (rheumatic, degenerative, bicuspid aortic valve, mitral valve prolapse with regurgitation), congenital heart disease, prosthetic valves, previous IE, IV drug use, indwelling lines, haemodialysis, immunosuppression, poor dental hygiene, and recent invasive procedures.

### Risk stratification for prophylaxis

| High risk (needs prophylaxis) | Lower risk (no prophylaxis) |
|---|---|
| **Prosthetic valves**, including transcatheter valves and homografts | Acquired valvular heart disease with stenosis or regurgitation |
| Prosthetic material used for valve repair (annuloplasty rings, chords) | Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy |
| **Previous infective endocarditis** | Other low-risk cardiac conditions |
| Complex **cyanotic congenital** heart disease (tetralogy, transposition, single ventricle); unrepaired cyanotic CHD; repaired CHD with residual shunts or valvular regurgitation next to prosthetic patch or device | |
| Cardiac transplant with valve regurgitation due to a structurally abnormal valve | |

> **Check:** the slide lists patent ductus arteriosus, VSD and coarctation among high-risk lesions; current ESC and AHA guidance restricts prophylaxis to the cyanotic and prosthetic categories above (and unrepaired lesions in the highest-risk group), so uncomplicated PDA, VSD or coarctation are not routinely covered.

- Risk depends on the **cardiac condition** and the **procedure**. Under the ACC 2017 guidelines, only dental procedures that involve **manipulation of gingival tissue, the periapical region of the teeth, or perforation of the oral mucosa** need prophylaxis.
- Prophylaxis is **not** recommended for routine respiratory tract, GI or urogenital procedures, or skin and soft tissue procedures.

## 4. Classification

| Feature | Acute | Subacute |
|---|---|---|
| Organism | **Staph. aureus** (virulent) | **Strep. viridans**, enterococci (less virulent) |
| Valve | Normal valve | Previously damaged valve |
| Course | Fulminant; fatal in under 6 weeks if untreated | Indolent; death takes much longer than 6 weeks |

By valve type: **native valve**, **prosthetic valve** (early or late), **IV drug user (right-sided)**, and healthcare-associated.

## 5. Clinical features

The picture depends on the organism and the underlying cardiac condition. A **high index of suspicion** is needed.

### High clinical suspicion

- New valve lesion or regurgitant murmur
- Embolic event of unknown origin
- Sepsis of unknown origin
- Haematuria, glomerulonephritis, suspected renal infarction
- **Fever plus:** prosthetic material in the heart; other predisposition (IV drug use); new ventricular arrhythmias or conduction disturbance; first presentation of heart failure; positive blood cultures with a typical organism; Osler, Janeway or Roth lesions; peripheral abscesses (renal, splenic, spine) of unknown origin; recent intervention known to cause significant bacteraemia

Low suspicion = fever with none of the above.

### Course

| Course | Features |
|---|---|
| **Indolent (subacute)** | Onset within about 2 weeks of infection; fever, malaise, fatigue, night sweats, anorexia, weight loss |
| **Explosive (acute)** | Heart failure, new or changing murmur, severe systemic sepsis |
| **Local destruction** | Mitral or aortic regurgitation, papillary muscle destruction, perivalvular abscess (heart block) |

### Signs (mnemonic: FROM JANE)

**F**ever, **R**oth spots, **O**sler's nodes, **M**urmur, **J**aneway lesions, **A**naemia, **N**ail-bed (splinter) haemorrhages, **E**mboli.

| Group | Signs |
|---|---|
| **Immunological** | **Osler's nodes** (painful, red, raised lesions on distal fingers and toes); **Roth spots** (retinal haemorrhages with white or pale centres); **glomerulonephritis**; **positive rheumatoid factor** |
| **Vascular / septic emboli** | **Splinter haemorrhages**; **Janeway lesions** (painless, flat, haemorrhagic macules on palms and soles; rare); subconjunctival haemorrhage; arthritis; **mycotic aneurysm** and stroke; haematuria from renal embolism |
| **Other** | Splenomegaly; petechiae (conjunctivae, buccal mucosa, palate, skin of the supraclavicular area) |

> **Exam tip:** Osler's nodes are **painful** and immune-mediated; Janeway lesions are **painless** and embolic. Both are classic exam distinctions.

## 6. Investigations

| Test | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Blood cultures** | At least 3 sets from different sites over time **before antibiotics** (positive in the majority) |
| **FBC** | Leucocytosis; anaemia of chronic disease; haematuria and bleeding may add |
| **ESR / CRP** | Raised |
| **Renal function, urinalysis** | Glomerulonephritis; haematuria, bacteriuria |
| **ECG** | Conduction defects (a prolonged PR interval or new AV block suggests aortic root abscess) |
| **Chest X-ray** | Septic pulmonary emboli or abscesses (right-sided disease), heart failure |
| **Echocardiography** | **Hallmark** for vegetations. **TTE first; if negative or inadequate (or prosthetic valve) do TEE** |
| Other | Rheumatoid factor, complement; CT/MRI for embolic complications; PET-CT in prosthetic valves |

### Culture-negative endocarditis (5 to 10%)

Usually from **prior antibiotic therapy**. Fastidious organisms: ***Coxiella burnetii*** (Q fever), *Chlamydia*, ***Bartonella*** (trench fever, cat scratch disease), *Legionella*, and HACEK organisms; use serology and PCR.

## 7. Causative organisms

| Setting | Organisms |
|---|---|
| **Native valve** | 1. **Streptococci, mainly viridans (50 to 70%)** 2. Staphylococci (*S. aureus*, *S. epidermidis*, about 25%) 3. **Enterococci (about 10%)** 4. **HACEK** group (*Haemophilus*, *Actinobacillus actinomycetemcomitans*, *Cardiobacterium hominis*, *Eikenella*, *Kingella*): treat with **ceftriaxone** |
| **IV drug users** | *S. aureus* about 60% (skin is the main source); streptococci and enterococci about 20%; Gram-negative bacilli about 10%; fungi (*Candida*, *Aspergillus*) about 5%. 70 to 100% of right-sided IE gives **septic pulmonary emboli and pneumonia**. A young man with fulminant endocarditis: suspect drug use even if there is no underlying heart disease |
| **Prosthetic valve, early (under 60 days)** | Perioperative contamination; incidence about 1%; **coagulase-negative staph (*S. epidermidis*) about 30%**, *S. aureus* about 20%, Gram-negative aerobes about 20%, fungi about 10%, strep and enterococci about 5 to 10% (the slide's pooled estimate for staphylococci is 45 to 50%) |
| **Prosthetic valve, late (over 60 days)** | After endothelialisation; incidence 0.2 to 0.5% per patient-year; transient bacteraemia from dental, GI or GU sources; microbiology resembles native valve (**streptococci**) |

> **Exam tip (frequently asked):** after cardiac surgery, IE **within 60 days = Staphylococcus**; **after 60 days = Strep. viridans**. *S. aureus* is the organism of IV drug users and the immediate post-operative period.

> **Remember:** ***Streptococcus gallolyticus (S. bovis)*** endocarditis is linked to **colonic pathology** (polyps, diverticula, colon cancer), so do a **colonoscopy**.

## 8. Diagnosis: modified Duke criteria (2015)

### Major criteria

1. **Blood cultures positive for IE:**
   - Typical organisms from **2 separate cultures**: viridans streptococci, *S. gallolyticus (bovis)*, HACEK, *S. aureus*; or community-acquired enterococci with no primary focus; **or**
   - Persistently positive cultures: 2 or more positive samples more than **12 hours apart**; or all of 3 or a majority of 4 or more separate cultures (first and last at least 1 hour apart); **or**
   - A single positive culture for *Coxiella burnetii* or phase I IgG titre above 1:800.
2. **Imaging positive for IE:**
   - Echocardiogram: vegetation, abscess, pseudoaneurysm, intracardiac fistula, valve perforation or aneurysm, new partial dehiscence of a prosthetic valve.
   - Abnormal activity around a prosthetic valve on **18F-FDG PET/CT** (implanted over 3 months) or radiolabelled leucocyte SPECT/CT.
   - Definite paravalvular lesions on cardiac CT.

### Minor criteria

1. **Predisposition:** predisposing heart condition or injection drug use
2. **Fever** above 38 °C
3. **Vascular phenomena** (including those found only on imaging): major arterial emboli, septic pulmonary infarcts, mycotic aneurysm, intracranial haemorrhage, conjunctival haemorrhage, Janeway lesions
4. **Immunological phenomena:** glomerulonephritis, Osler's nodes, Roth spots, rheumatoid factor
5. **Microbiological evidence:** positive blood culture not meeting a major criterion, or serology of active infection with a consistent organism

### Classification

| Category | Requirement |
|---|---|
| **Definite IE** | **Pathological criteria:** organisms or lesions on culture or histology of a vegetation, an embolised vegetation or an intracardiac abscess; **or clinical criteria:** 2 major, or 1 major + 3 minor, or 5 minor |
| **Possible IE** | 1 major + 1 minor, or 3 minor |
| **Rejected IE** | Firm alternative diagnosis; resolution of manifestations with antibiotics for 4 days or less; no pathological evidence at surgery or autopsy after 4 days or less of antibiotics |

## 9. Management

### Medical (given to all patients, including those who will have surgery)

- Principles: **prolonged (4 to 6 weeks), high-dose, bactericidal (not bacteriostatic)** antibiotics, because vegetations are avascular and bacteria are dormant.
- **Acute presentation:** take blood cultures and start treatment within about **3 hours**. **Subacute:** culture, then antibiotics can wait up to about **3 days** for results.
- **Best initial empiric regimen:** **vancomycin plus gentamicin**. For resistant organisms add an aminoglycoside and lengthen treatment.

| Organism | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Highly penicillin-sensitive viridans streptococci or *S. bovis*** | **Ceftriaxone once daily**, or IV benzylpenicillin or amoxicillin, for **4 weeks** (cure rate over 98%); or ceftriaxone 2 g for 2 weeks, then oral amoxicillin for 2 weeks. Prosthetic valves need longer. β-lactam allergy: **vancomycin** |
| **Methicillin-sensitive *Staphylococcus*** | **Flucloxacillin** (β-lactamase stable); allergy or non-response: **vancomycin** |
| **HACEK** | Ceftriaxone |

> **Remember:** Staph: flucloxacillin or vancomycin. Strep: penicillin or vancomycin.

### Surgical indications

1. **Heart failure** from valve destruction or a ruptured valve or chordae (**strongest indication**)
2. **Prosthetic valve** endocarditis
3. **Large vegetation**
4. **Recurrent embolisation** while on antibiotics
5. **Fungal** endocarditis
6. **Abscess** or perivalvular extension (new AV block)
7. Persistent infection or bacteraemia despite appropriate antibiotics

Surgery is followed by about 6 weeks of antibiotics.

## 10. Complications

| Area | Complications |
|---|---|
| **Cardiac** | **Congestive heart failure** (most common complication; valve destruction, myocarditis), myocardial abscess, conduction block, coronary embolism and MI |
| **Neurological** (about 1/3 of cases) | Major embolism, especially to the **middle cerebral artery** territory (about 25%); **mycotic aneurysm** (2 to 10%); intracranial haemorrhage; meningitis |
| **Renal** | Glomerulonephritis (immune complex), renal infarcts and abscess |
| **Metastatic infection, right-sided** | Lung abscesses, pyothorax or pyopneumothorax |
| **Metastatic infection, left-sided** | Pyogenic meningitis, splenic abscess, pyelonephritis, osteomyelitis |

## 11. Prevention

1. Antibiotic prophylaxis is kept for **patients with the highest risk undergoing the highest-risk dental procedures**. Two features are needed: a **significant cardiac defect** and a **procedure with a risk of bacteraemia**.
2. Dental prophylaxis: **amoxicillin or ampicillin** (clindamycin if penicillin allergic; some guidelines now use azithromycin or doxycycline).
3. **Good oral hygiene and regular dental review** matter more than antibiotics.
4. **Aseptic technique** with venous catheters and invasive procedures to reduce healthcare-associated IE.
5. Whether prophylaxis actually changes IE incidence needs further study.

## Key points to revise

1. IE needs damaged or prosthetic endocardium plus bacteraemia; vegetations are platelet-fibrin masses with bacteria.
2. Strep viridans is commonest overall; *S. aureus* in IV drug users (right-sided, septic lung emboli) and early prosthetic IE (within 60 days).
3. Signs: fever, new murmur, splinter haemorrhages, Osler's nodes (painful), Janeway lesions (painless), Roth spots, splenomegaly, glomerulonephritis.
4. Diagnose with blood cultures (before antibiotics) and echo (TTE then TEE) using modified Duke criteria: 2 major, or 1 major + 3 minor, or 5 minor = definite.
5. Treat 4 to 6 weeks with bactericidal antibiotics; empiric vancomycin + gentamicin; ceftriaxone or penicillin for sensitive streptococci, flucloxacillin for MSSA.
6. Surgery: heart failure (strongest), prosthetic valve, large vegetation, recurrent emboli, abscess, fungal IE, new AV block.
7. *S. bovis* (gallolyticus) bacteraemia: look for colon cancer.
8. Prophylaxis only for the highest-risk cardiac patients before high-risk dental procedures.
