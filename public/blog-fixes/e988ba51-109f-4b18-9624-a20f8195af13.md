# Envenoming (Snake Bite, Stings and Bites)

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Toxicology and Emergency Medicine**

> **In one line:** venoms are complex mixtures of toxins that cause local tissue damage (swelling, bleeding, necrosis) and systemic effects (neurotoxic paralysis, coagulopathy, shock, anaphylaxis). Management is rapid first aid, supportive care, airway and circulatory support, and specific antivenom when clinically indicated, and the exact picture depends on the animal.

## What you must know

- Venom is costly to make, so it is used sparingly: many bites are **"dry bites"** with no venom injected (20 to 25 per 100 pit viper bites; about 50% of coral snake bites).
- **Hymenoptera** (bees, wasps, ants) cause **more envenomation deaths than any other animal**, mostly by **anaphylaxis**, with 75% of deaths within 4 hours.
- Anaphylaxis: **IM adrenaline** first; then airway, fluids, antihistamines, steroids.
- Neurotoxic snake venom causes **descending flaccid paralysis**, which can end in respiratory failure. Post-synaptic toxins are reversible with antivenom; pre-synaptic toxins are rarely reversible.
- Pit viper venom is mainly **haemotoxic and necrotoxic**: bleeding, prolonged clotting times, shock.
- Snake bite work-up: **CBC, coagulation screen, U/E/Cs, CK, ECG**.
- **Do not** apply ice, an arterial tourniquet, cut and suck, or electric shock.
- Kenya: 13 of about 140 snake species are of medical importance; antivenom is scarce and imported.

## 1. Venom: general principles

- Venom is a complex mixture of toxins; each toxin may have several effects.
- **Dry bite:** bite without envenomation.
- Groups covered: arthropods (Hymenoptera, spiders, scorpions), reptiles (pit vipers, coral snakes and other elapids), and marine animals (jellyfish, stingrays).

## 2. The Kenyan picture

- Of about **140 snake species** in Kenya, only **13 are of medical importance**.
- Counties most affected: **West Pokot, Kajiado, Turkana, Narok, Baringo**.
- Incidence quoted at 60 to 120 per 100,000 bitten by snakes. Slides state **15 to 25 Kenyans die daily** from snake envenoming and at least 100 others have limbs amputated.
- **Antivenom is scarce**, and it is managed under the Division of Vector Borne and Neglected Tropical Diseases (MoH). Antivenoms are mostly specific to particular geographical areas.
- For decades Kenya has imported antivenom from **India** (VINS snake venom antiserum) and **Mexico** (Inoserp Pan-Africa).
- Local production plans: working with **camels, baboons and cows**. Traditionally horses and sheep are used.
- **How antivenom is made:** the animal is immunised with venom, then antibodies form, then the serum is harvested and the antibodies separated and refined.

> **Check:** the quoted death figure ("15 to 25 per day") is very high compared with published estimates, which are in the order of thousands of deaths a year in Kenya. Treat it as a lecture figure and quote it cautiously.

## 3. Clinical effects of envenoming

### Local effects

Pain, sweating, swelling, erythema, bleeding and bruising, blistering, necrosis; major direct tissue trauma (for example stingray injuries).

### Neurotoxic effects (snake venoms)

| Type | Site of action | Reversibility |
|---|---|---|
| **Post-synaptic** neurotoxins | Bind and block the receptors on the membrane but remain extracellular | May be **reversible**, because they are still available to be neutralised by antivenom |
| **Pre-synaptic** neurotoxins | Taken up into the presynaptic membrane and impair neurotransmitter release | **Rarely reversible**: intracellular, no longer reachable by antivenom |

- Either type can produce **descending flaccid paralysis**, progressing to airway compromise and life-threatening respiratory insufficiency.
- Descending pattern: ptosis and ophthalmoplegia first, then bulbar weakness, then limb and respiratory muscles.

> **Check:** the slides give alpha-bungarotoxin as the pre-synaptic example. Alpha-bungarotoxin is actually a **post-synaptic** toxin (it blocks the acetylcholine receptor); **beta-bungarotoxin** is the classic pre-synaptic example.

### Non-specific systemic effects

Headache, nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, abdominal pain, hypertension or hypotension, tachycardia or bradycardia, dizziness, collapse, convulsions, shock, cardiac arrest.

## 4. Hymenoptera (bees, wasps, hornets, ants)

- About **10 million stings a year** with **40 to 150 deaths a year**. Roughly 50% of deaths follow honeybee stings and 50% yellowjacket and other wasp stings.
- Death is usually **immunologically mediated** (anaphylaxis); toxic venom effects are rare.
- About **0.4% of the population** is at risk of serious allergic reaction. Most have a history of progressively severe reactions, but some deny prior stings; in one study (2006 patients), **50% of those with an allergic reaction had no previous warning symptoms**.

### Clinical features

| Type | Features |
|---|---|
| **Local reaction** | Wheal or urticaria, sharp burning pain, itching, oedema. Extensive reactions may involve the whole limb. Stings to the tongue or throat may cause **loss of the airway** |
| **Mild systemic** | Diffuse itching, urticaria, swelling distant from the sting site, flushing |
| **Severe systemic (anaphylaxis)** | Laryngeal oedema, severe bronchospasm, profound hypotension |

- **75% of deaths occur within 4 hours** of the sting.

### Treatment

**Local reaction**

- Remove a retained stinger (flick it off; avoid squeezing).
- Ice to the site, oral antihistamines, mild analgesics.

**Anaphylaxis**

- Call for help and manage the **airway**.
- **Adrenaline (epinephrine)**: 0.3 mL of 1:1000 solution every 15 minutes, subcutaneous or **IM**.
- IV fluids and antihistamines.

| Drug | Dose in the slides |
|---|---|
| Diphenhydramine | 50 mg PO/IM every 4 to 6 h (may also be given IV) |
| Epinephrine 1:1000 | 0.3 mL every 15 min; avoid IV, but if necessary use the 1:10,000 solution |
| Cimetidine | 300 to 800 mg IV every 6 h (ranitidine may be used) |
| Methylprednisolone | 125 mg IV loading dose, then 40 mg every 6 h |
| Albuterol (salbutamol) | For bronchospasm |

> **Check:** the standard adult adrenaline dose in anaphylaxis is **0.5 mg (0.5 mL of 1:1000) IM** in the mid-outer thigh, repeated after 5 minutes if needed; children use weight-based doses. IM is preferred to subcutaneous.

**Prevention:** avoid exposure, avoid bright clothing and sweet fragrances, and do not eat sweet foods outdoors. Patients with proven allergy should carry an adrenaline auto-injector.

## 5. Spiders

- About 50,000 species; few bite humans, and two are particularly dangerous: the **black widow** (*Latrodectus mactans*) and the **brown recluse** (*Loxosceles reclusa*). (These slides use US data.)

### Black widow

- Found in woodpiles, under rocks, rubbish dumps and outdoor structures; occasionally in houses. **Only females bite humans** and rarely leave the web.
- **Venom:** neurotoxic **alpha-latrotoxin**. It binds to nerve-ending calcium channels, **triggers neurotransmitter release**, blocks re-uptake, and inhibits normal nerve impulse transmission; it may lower serum calcium.
- Local discomfort at the bite; **neuromuscular twitching and severe muscle cramping**.
- **Course:** symptoms peak within a few hours, then diminish; usually last 24 hours but can persist up to 4 days. About 5% have delayed hypersensitivity 2 to 3 days after the bite. Most recover completely; mortality is unknown.

**Management**

- Wound care, ice, analgesia, observe for progression.
- Cramps and twitching: **calcium gluconate** infusion, **diazepam 5 to 10 mg IV**; analgesia with codeine or morphine; manage hypertension; pain control and sedation.
- **Antivenin** (horse serum based) 1 to 2 vials for: the very young, the very old, **hypertensive reactions**, acute respiratory distress.

### Brown recluse

- Shy, nocturnal; lives in dark closets, basements, behind furniture. **Venom damages endothelial cells.**
- **Local effects:** tissue necrosis, oedema, haemorrhage, thrombosis.
- **Systemic loxoscelism:** haemolysis, anaemia, DIC, renal failure.

| Time after bite | Findings |
|---|---|
| Initially | No pain or mild stinging |
| Within 2 hours | Local pain, blue-grey constrictive halo |
| 12 to 18 hours | Bleb formation, growing ischaemic zone |
| 5 to 7 days | Aseptic necrosis, eschar, necrotic ulcer (up to 30 cm in severe lesions) |

**Management:** supportive and symptomatic care; debride full-thickness lesions with subsequent grafts; **dapsone** may improve outcome (inhibits polymorph diapedesis and infiltration into the bite site); antivenin under development. Outcomes are **not improved** by early excision or steroids.

### Tarantulas

Large, wandering predatory spiders (about 30 US species); relatively docile and rarely bite. A bite gives local pain, oedema and lymph node swelling.

## 6. Scorpions

- About 40 US species; only one potentially lethal (*Centruroides exilicauda*, mainly Arizona). No deaths since 1969 (US).
- **Neurotoxic venom** acting on the neuronal synapse and neuromuscular junction: increases neuronal **sodium permeability**, causes neurotransmitter release at synapses, and increases acetylcholine release at the neuromuscular junction.
- **Local:** no swelling or inflammation; local pain with hyperaesthesia. Treat with ice, mild analgesia and wound care.
- **Systemic:** extreme restlessness and agitation, roving eye movements, poor coordination, slurred speech and difficulty swallowing (bulbar findings), salivation, wheeze, stridor, tachycardia, tachypnoea, hypertension, nausea, vomiting.
- **Severe envenoming:** **antivenin IV** (goat serum product; skin test precautions; 1 to 2 vials total). **Avoid over-sedation**, particularly with antivenin; use phenobarbital 5 to 10 mg/kg or diazepam.
- **Prazosin** (alpha-blocker) for hypertension or pulmonary oedema from scorpion sting cardiotoxicity.

## 7. Snakes

- US data in the slides: 45,000 bites a year, 8,000 from venomous snakes; 25% are dry strikes.

| Group | Examples | Main venom effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Pit vipers (Crotalidae)** | Rattlesnakes, copperheads, water moccasins (cottonmouth) | **Haemotoxic, necrotoxic** |
| **Elapids (coral snakes etc.)** | Cobras, mambas, kraits, coral snakes | Predominantly **neurotoxic** |

- **Pit viper features:** heavy body, diamond-shaped head, **vertical elliptical pupil**, **heat-sensing pit** between eye and nostril, moveable fangs.
- Rattlesnakes: 13 species, about 7,000 bites a year and 9 to 10 deaths (mostly eastern and western diamondback). Copperheads (*Agkistrodon contortrix*): deaths rare, minimal oedema and pain.

> **Remember:** in Africa the major medically important groups are Viperidae (puff adder, saw-scaled viper, Gaboon viper: local necrosis, bleeding), and Elapidae (cobras, mambas: neurotoxicity, and spitting cobras: local necrosis). The slides use US pit vipers as the model.

### Clinical presentation (pit viper)

Weakness, sweating, nausea, vomiting; tachycardia; hypotension and shock; **prolonged clotting times**; bleeding gums; haematemesis, melaena, haematuria. The **grade of envenomation** determines the need for antivenom.

### Antivenom

- Made by **hyperimmunising an animal** (usually horses) so that it makes antibodies against the venom. Once refined, these bind toxins and neutralise them or allow rapid clearance.
- Given **IV** mostly, with **adrenaline ready** in case of anaphylaxis.
- Example: **Crotalidae polyvalent Fab (ovine), FabAV** (sheep immunoglobulin Fab fragments): initial dose 4 to 6 vials, repeat 4 to 6 vials if no response in 1 hour (judged by clinical parameters and coagulation tests). It covers eastern/western/Mojave rattlesnake and cottonmouth, and can be used for copperhead.

### Other treatment

- **Anticholinesterases** (for example neostigmine) for post-synaptic paralysis.
- Tetanus toxoid IM should **not** be given until any coagulopathy has been reversed.

### First aid and management of snake bite

1. Seek **expert help early**; get away from the snake and identify it if possible; calm the victim.
2. Oxygen, monitoring, IV access.
3. Slides advise a proximal constricting band, wound cleaning and bandaging, immobilising the bitten area (kept dependent).
4. Watch bands, bandages and splints for vascular compromise from oedema; anticipate swelling (remove jewellery).
5. Transport to hospital.

**Do NOT:** apply ice, apply an arterial tourniquet, cut and suck, use electric shock, actively attempt to find the snake, or bring a live venomous snake to hospital.

> **Check:** current WHO and Kenya guidance for neurotoxic bites favours **pressure immobilisation** (broad bandage over the whole limb plus splint) and keeping the limb still; for haemotoxic or necrotising bites, avoid tight bands. A narrow tight constricting band is no longer recommended.

## 8. Marine envenoming

### Jellyfish

- **Features:** intense burning pain, red haemorrhagic lesions, nausea, vomiting, fever, chills, respiratory distress, wheeze, stridor, hypotension, shock, cardiovascular collapse.
- **Treatment:** remove tentacles (gloves or forceps); shaving cream or baking soda and shaving to remove nematocysts; **vinegar blocks discharge of nematocysts**, so apply liberally.

### Stingray

- Spine at the base of the tail has a venom gland (**vasoconstrictive**). Typically trodden on; **puncture wounds to the feet** are commonest.
- **Features:** pain, salivation, nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, cramps, dyspnoea, headache.
- **Treatment:** rinse and remove the spine, **soak in warm water**, cleanse the wound, update tetanus; antibiotics usually not necessary.

## 9. General diagnosis of envenoming

Suspect envenoming in unexplained **paralysis, myotoxicity, coagulopathy, nephrotoxicity, cardiotoxicity, pulmonary oedema, necrosis, collapse or convulsions**.

- History, examination and laboratory tests; get a description of the organism if possible.
- **Snake bite screen:** CBC, coagulation screen, U/E/Cs, CK, ECG.
- Lung function tests in respiratory failure.
- A bedside **20-minute whole blood clotting test** (clot in a clean glass tube) is a useful field test for venom-induced coagulopathy.

## Key points to revise

1. Many bites are dry bites; envenoming is not guaranteed.
2. Hymenoptera cause most envenomation deaths, by anaphylaxis, most within 4 hours; treat with IM adrenaline.
3. Post-synaptic neurotoxins are reversible with antivenom; pre-synaptic ones rarely are.
4. Neurotoxic snake bite causes descending flaccid paralysis and respiratory failure.
5. Pit viper (and African viper) bites cause bleeding, shock and necrosis; check clotting and give antivenom by grade.
6. Black widow causes cramps (alpha-latrotoxin); brown recluse causes necrotic ulcer and haemolysis; scorpion causes autonomic and neurological excitation.
7. Snake bite screen: CBC, coagulation, U/E/Cs, CK, ECG. No ice, tourniquet, cutting or sucking.
8. Jellyfish: vinegar; stingray: hot water immersion and tetanus update.
