# Histamine and Histamine Receptor Antagonists

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Pharmacology (allergy and immunology)**

> **In one line:** histamine is a vasoactive amine stored in mast cells, basophils and gastric ECL cells; it acts on four G-protein-coupled receptors (H1 to H4) to cause itching, vasodilation, bronchoconstriction and gastric acid secretion. Histamine itself has no clinical use, but H1 blockers (allergy, motion sickness, insomnia) and H2 blockers (acid) are widely used, and adrenaline is the physiological antagonist that saves life in anaphylaxis.

## What you must know

- Histamine is made by **decarboxylation of histidine** (histidine decarboxylase) and stored, inactive, in **mast cell and basophil granules**.
- Receptors: **H1** (Gq, raised IP3 and DAG), **H2** (Gs, raised cAMP), **H3** (Gi, presynaptic), **H4** (Gi, leukocytes, chemotaxis).
- **H1** causes bronchoconstriction, vasodilation, itch and increased capillary permeability; **H2** causes gastric acid secretion and cardiac stimulation.
- The **triple response** (red spot, flare, wheal) is the classic skin effect of histamine.
- **First-generation H1 blockers** are sedating and anticholinergic; **second-generation** (cetirizine, loratadine, fexofenadine) are non-sedating.
- **Adrenaline** (not an antihistamine) is the drug of choice in anaphylaxis, as a physiological antagonist.
- H1 antagonists are largely **ineffective in bronchial asthma**.

## 1. Role of histamine

- Mediator of **immediate (type I) allergic reactions** such as urticaria and hay fever, and of inflammation. It plays only a modest role in anaphylaxis (other mediators such as leukotrienes and prostaglandins matter too).
- Regulates **gastric acid secretion**, acts as a **neurotransmitter and neuromodulator**, and contributes to **chemotaxis** of white cells.
- **No clinical applications** for histamine itself; the clinical value lies in drugs that interfere with it.

### Type I (anaphylactic) hypersensitivity

- Mediated by **IgE** antibodies made in response to allergens (pollen, animal dander, dust mites, some foods).
- IgE bound to mast cells and basophils cross-links on re-exposure, causing degranulation and release of histamine and other mediators, with inflammation and swelling.
- Examples: **bronchial asthma, allergic rhinitis, allergic dermatitis, food allergy, allergic conjunctivitis, anaphylaxis**.
- **Anaphylaxis** is the most severe form and a medical emergency: sudden life-threatening respiratory failure, extreme difficulty breathing, swelling (angioedema), hypotension, cyanosis and shock.

## 2. Location, synthesis and release

### Where histamine is found

| Site | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Tissues** | Nearly all tissues; high in lungs, skin, blood vessels, GI tract and gastric fundus |
| **Cells** | Mainly **mast cells and basophils** (tissue content is proportional to mast cell content) |
| **ECL cells** | Enterochromaffin-like cells of the gastric fundus release histamine to stimulate **parietal cells** |
| **Neurons** | Histaminergic neurons in the brain: neuroendocrine control, cardiovascular, thermal and body-weight regulation, sleep and arousal |

- Mast cells are most numerous at sites exposed to injury: nose, mouth, feet, internal body surfaces and blood vessels.
- **Bound histamine is inactive**; it acts only when released as the free amine.

### Synthesis

- **Histidine → histamine** by **histidine decarboxylase**, expressed in CNS neurons, gastric parietal cells, mast cells and basophils.
- In mast cells it is stored in granules. Unstored histamine is rapidly inactivated by **amine oxidases** (diamine oxidase and histamine N-methyltransferase).

### Release

1. **Immunological release (most important):** IgE-sensitised mast cells and basophils degranulate explosively on exposure to the specific antigen (hay fever, acute urticaria).
   - Endogenous histamine also modulates inflammation: after tissue injury it causes local vasodilation and plasma leakage (bringing complement, C-reactive protein and antibodies) and attracts **neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils, monocytes and lymphocytes**.
2. **Chemical and mechanical release:** drugs such as **morphine and tubocurarine** displace histamine from its bound form (this is why they cause flushing, itch and hypotension).

## 3. Histamine receptors

| Receptor | Distribution | Signalling | Main effects |
|---|---|---|---|
| **H1** | Smooth muscle, endothelium, brain | **Gq**: raised IP3, DAG | Bronchospasm, smooth muscle contraction, vasodilation, itch, increased permeability |
| **H2** | Gastric mucosa, cardiac muscle, mast cells, brain | **Gs**: raised cAMP | Gastric acid (HCl) secretion, cardiac stimulation |
| **H3** | Presynaptic: brain and other neurons | **Gi**: lowered cAMP | Reduces release of histamine and other neurotransmitters |
| **H4** | Eosinophils, neutrophils, CD4 T cells | **Gi**: lowered cAMP | Chemotaxis (eosinophils and mast cells) |

- In the brain, H1 and H2 receptors are **postsynaptic**; H3 receptors are mainly **presynaptic** (autoreceptors).
- H4 receptors are found on leukocytes in bone marrow and blood.

## 4. Effects of histamine

| System | Effect | Receptor |
|---|---|---|
| **Nervous** | Strong stimulant of sensory nerve endings (pain, itch): urticaria, insect and nettle stings | H1 |
| **Cardiovascular** | Vasodilation (via endothelial **nitric oxide**), fall in systolic and diastolic BP, flushing, warmth, headache; increased capillary permeability and oedema | H1 |
| **Heart** | Raised heart rate: direct cardiac stimulation plus reflex tachycardia from vasodilation | H2 (direct) |
| **Bronchi** | Bronchoconstriction (asthmatics are very sensitive) | H1 |
| **Gut smooth muscle** | Contraction, may cause diarrhoea | H1 |
| **Other smooth muscle** | Little effect on eye and genitourinary tract; pregnant women in anaphylaxis may abort from uterine contraction | |
| **Stomach** | Powerful stimulant of acid secretion from parietal cells; also stimulates small and large bowel secretion | H2 |
| **Adrenal medulla** | Very high concentrations cause catecholamine discharge | |
| **Nose** | Runny nose and watery eyes (glandular hypersecretion), sneezing (sensory nerve stimulation), congestion (vasodilation and permeability) | H1 |
| **CNS** | Histaminergic neurons promote wakefulness; hypothalamic H1 receptors regulate diurnal rhythm of food intake and body weight. Blocking brain H1 receptors causes sedation | H1 |

### The triple response (Lewis)

Seen after intradermal histamine or skin trauma:

1. **Red spot:** capillary dilatation.
2. **Flare:** spreading redness (about 1 cm) caused by an **axon reflex**.
3. **Wheal:** oedema from exudation of fluid through increased capillary and venular permeability.

> **Exam tip:** itch, wheal and bronchospasm are H1 effects; acid secretion is H2. Questions often ask which receptor mediates which effect, so learn the table.

## 5. Ways of reducing histamine effects

1. **Physiological antagonists:** **adrenaline** has opposing actions at other receptors (bronchodilation via beta-2, vasoconstriction via alpha-1). Injected adrenaline can be lifesaving in **systemic anaphylaxis** and other states of massive histamine release.
2. **Release inhibitors (mast cell stabilisers):** **cromolyn (sodium cromoglicate), nedocromil, ketotifen**; used as prophylaxis in asthma. Beta-2 agonists (e.g. salbutamol) also reduce histamine release.
3. **Receptor antagonists:** H1 blockers (this note) and H2 blockers (acid suppression, covered with peptic ulcer disease).

> **Remember:** antihistamines do not stop mast cell degranulation and are poor in anaphylaxis. Adrenaline first, antihistamines as adjunct for skin symptoms.

## 6. H1-receptor blockers

Competitive antagonists (strictly inverse agonists) at the H1 receptor. Many are sold without prescription, alone or in "cold pills" and sleep aids.

### First-generation (sedating)

- Examples: **diphenhydramine, doxylamine, hydroxyzine, chlorpheniramine, cyproheptadine, cyclizine, meclizine, promethazine, dimethindene, clemastine, triprolidine, pheniramine**.
- Lipid soluble, so they **enter the CNS readily**.

| Degree of sedation | Drugs |
|---|---|
| **Strong** | Diphenhydramine, promethazine, doxylamine (used for insomnia), hydroxyzine (marked) |
| **Slight** | Chlorpheniramine, triprolidine, cyclizine, meclizine, dimethindene, clemastine, pheniramine |

- Many have additional actions: anticholinergic, antiemetic, anti-serotonin, local anaesthetic.
- Commonly combined with other drugs in cough and cold preparations (e.g. promethazine, chlorpheniramine, diphenhydramine, triprolidine) and in nasal sprays.

### Second-generation (non-sedating)

- Examples: **cetirizine, levocetirizine, desloratadine, loratadine, fexofenadine**.
- **Much less lipid soluble**, so little CNS entry; **no anticholinergic, antiemetic or anti-serotonin activity**.
- **Cetirizine and levocetirizine are partially sedating.**
- **Ketotifen and azelastine** combine H1 blockade with mast cell stabilisation. Topical forms: olopatadine (eye drops), ketotifen (eye drops, oral), azelastine (nasal spray).

> **Exam tip:** "non-sedating" does not mean "never sedating": cetirizine can cause mild drowsiness.

### Pharmacokinetics

- All are **active orally** (developed for chronic use); some topical (eye, nose).
- Extensively **metabolised in the liver**.
- Half-life: first generation **4 to 12 hours**; second generation (fexofenadine, cetirizine, loratadine) **12 to 24 hours**, so once-daily dosing.

## 7. Pharmacodynamics of H1 blockers (first generation)

| Action | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Sedation** | Varies with structure and lipophilicity; useful as sleep aids, unsuitable for daytime. At very high toxic doses: stimulation, agitation, convulsions, then coma |
| **Antiemetic / anti-motion sickness** | Blockade of central H1 and M1 muscarinic receptors. Work best **taken before** travel; poor once symptoms are established. Promethazine, cyclizine and meclizine (less sedating) |
| **Antimuscarinic** | Atropine-like: dry mouth and nose, may help non-allergic rhinorrhoea; also **urinary retention and blurred vision** |
| **Alpha-1 blockade** | Especially promethazine: **orthostatic hypotension**. No beta blockade |
| **Anti-serotonin** | Notably **cyproheptadine**, which competes with serotonin in gut smooth muscle; antagonism in the hypothalamic appetite centre explains its **appetite-stimulating** effect |
| **Local anaesthetic** | Block sodium channels like procaine and lidocaine; diphenhydramine and promethazine are more potent than procaine |

- **Cinnarizine (Stugeron)** is an H1 antihistamine plus calcium channel blocker, used for nausea and vomiting in motion sickness, chemotherapy, vertigo and Ménière's disease.

## 8. Clinical uses of H1 blockers

1. **Allergic and inflammatory conditions** (IgE-mediated)
   - **Allergic rhinitis and urticaria:** oral antihistamines are drugs of choice; second-generation preferred, especially in **chronic urticaria**.
   - **Allergic conjunctivitis:** ophthalmic agents (azelastine, ketotifen: H1 blocker plus mast cell stabiliser); very effective if given **before exposure**.
   - **Allergic rhinitis:** azelastine nasal spray.
   - **Atopic dermatitis:** drugs such as diphenhydramine are used mainly for their **sedative effect**, which reduces awareness of itch.
   - **Bronchial asthma:** H1 antagonists are **largely ineffective** because many mediators are involved.
2. **Motion sickness and nausea:** diphenhydramine, promethazine; cyclizine and meclizine are less sedating.
3. **Insomnia (hypnotic):** diphenhydramine, doxylamine.

## 9. Toxicity of H1 blockers

- First generation: **excitation and convulsions in children**, postural hypotension, allergic reactions.
- Anticholinergic effects: dry nose and mouth, **blurred vision, urinary retention**.
- Marked drowsiness: avoid driving and alcohol.
- Overdose in children: agitation, hallucinations, convulsions, fever, coma (atropine-like syndrome).

> **Remember:** avoid first-generation agents in the elderly, in prostatic enlargement (retention) and in glaucoma (angle closure).

## 10. Betahistine

- A **histamine analogue** licensed for **vertigo, tinnitus and hearing loss in Ménière's disease** (episodic vertigo, low-pitched tinnitus, hearing loss from inner-ear fluid excess).
- **Strong H3 antagonist** and **weak H1 agonist**.
  - Weak H1 agonism on inner-ear blood vessels causes vasodilation.
  - More importantly, H3 blockade removes presynaptic inhibition, so **histamine release increases**, giving potent vasodilation of the inner-ear vessels and relief of pressure from excess fluid.
- Available as Betaserc tablets (8 mg, 16 mg).

## Key points to revise

1. Histamine is formed from histidine by histidine decarboxylase and stored in mast cells, basophils and ECL cells; morphine and tubocurarine release it directly.
2. H1 (Gq): bronchospasm, vasodilation, itch, permeability. H2 (Gs): gastric acid, cardiac stimulation. H3 (Gi): presynaptic inhibition. H4 (Gi): chemotaxis.
3. Triple response: red spot (capillary dilatation), flare (axon reflex), wheal (permeability).
4. Adrenaline is the physiological antagonist and the lifesaving drug in anaphylaxis; mast cell stabilisers (cromolyn, nedocromil, ketotifen) are prophylactic.
5. First-generation H1 blockers: sedating, anticholinergic, antiemetic, alpha-blocking, anti-serotonin (cyproheptadine), local anaesthetic.
6. Second-generation H1 blockers (cetirizine, loratadine, fexofenadine, desloratadine, levocetirizine) are non-sedating, long acting, and preferred for rhinitis and chronic urticaria.
7. H1 blockers do not work well in asthma; take antiemetic antihistamines before travel.
8. Betahistine (H3 antagonist) treats Ménière's disease.
