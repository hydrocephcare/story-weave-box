## CEREBROVASCULAR DISEASE
*Dr. Lilian Bosire — MKU Lecturer, Pathologist*

## OVERVIEW & DEFINITIONS

**Cerebrovascular disease** = brain injury due to altered blood flow; grouped into ischemic and hemorrhagic etiologies — tissue infarction is the ultimate consequence of both.

**Stroke** = neurologic signs/symptoms explained by a vascular mechanism, with acute onset, persisting **beyond 24 hours**
- If symptoms resolve **within 24 hours** → **Transient Ischaemic Attack (TIA)**

**Epidemiology:** 3rd leading cause of death in the US (after heart disease and cancer); most prevalent cause of neurologic morbidity and mortality.

### Two Major Mechanisms of Stroke
| Mechanism | Details |
|---|---|
| **Ischaemia/Hypoxia** | Impaired blood supply/oxygenation; global or focal; embolism > thrombosis |
| **Haemorrhage** | Rupture of CNS vessels; causes include hypertension, aneurysms, vascular malformations |

## A. HYPOXIA AND ISCHAEMIA

### Why the Brain is So Vulnerable
- Brain = only **1–2% of body weight** but receives **~15% of resting cardiac output** and accounts for **~20% of body's oxygen consumption**
- Strictly dependent on **aerobic metabolism** — no significant anaerobic reserve
- Cerebral blood flow maintained constant over a wide BP range via **autoregulation of vascular resistance**
- Deprivation occurs via:
  - **Hypoxaemia** — low blood oxygen content
  - **Ischaemia** — inadequate blood flow (from ↓ perfusion pressure, small/large vessel obstruction, or both)

### Tissue Survival in Ischaemia Depends On
- Presence of **collateral circulation**
- **Duration** of ischaemia
- **Magnitude and rapidity** of flow reduction
- These factors determine the anatomic site, size of lesion, and resulting clinical deficit

### Special CNS Mechanism — Excitotoxicity
- Ischaemia → inappropriate release of **excitatory amino acids** (especially **glutamate**)
- Glutamate acts on **NMDA receptors** → excessive Ca²⁺ influx → neuronal death
- This is termed **excitotoxicity** — unique to CNS ischaemia

### The Penumbra — Clinically Important
- Zone of **"at-risk"** brain between necrotic core and normal tissue
- Cells here can die by **apoptosis** (not just necrosis)
- Can potentially be rescued by anti-apoptotic interventions → basis of thrombolytic therapy (tPA within 4.5 hours)

## B. FOCAL CEREBRAL ISCHAEMIA

**Definition:** Reduction or cessation of blood flow to a localized brain area due to partial or complete arterial obstruction → sustained ischaemia → infarction in the territory of the compromised vessel.

### Collateral Circulation — ⚠️ Know This
- **Primary collateral:** Circle of Willis (supplemented by external carotid-ophthalmic collaterals)
- **Secondary collateral:** Leptomeningeal vessels → supply distal branches of ACA, MCA, PCA via cortical-leptomeningeal anastomoses
- ⚠️ **Little to NO collateral flow** for deep penetrating vessels of thalamus, basal ganglia, and deep white matter → these areas are most vulnerable to small vessel disease

### Causes of Focal Ischaemia/Infarction
**1. Embolism** (more common than thrombosis in the brain)

| Source | Details |
|---|---|
| Cardiac mural thrombi | Most common; from MI, valvular disease, atrial fibrillation |
| Arterial thromboemboli | From atheromatous plaques — most often at **carotid bifurcation** |
| Paradoxical thromboemboli | In children with cardiac anomalies (R→L shunts) |
| Cardiac surgery-related | |
| Other | Tumour emboli, fat emboli (fractures), air emboli |

- Emboli lodge at **vessel branch points** or areas of pre-existing luminal stenosis
- **MCA territory** most commonly affected (direct extension of ICA) — equal incidence in both hemispheres
- **"Shower embolization"** (e.g., fat embolism after fractures) → generalised cerebral dysfunction, disturbances of consciousness, **often without localising signs**
- Widespread **haemorrhagic white matter lesions** = characteristic of **bone marrow embolization after trauma**

**2. Thrombosis**
- Most commonly from **acute change in vulnerable atherosclerotic plaques** (same mechanism as coronary artery disease)
- Most common sites: **carotid bifurcation**, origin of MCA, either end of basilar artery
- Thrombi → progressive luminal narrowing → anterograde extension → fragmentation → distal embolization
- Associated with systemic diseases: **hypertension and diabetes**

**3. Vasculitis**
- Inflammatory vessel involvement → luminal narrowing → occlusion → infarcts
- **Infectious vasculitis:** syphilis, TB; now more common with immunosuppression/opportunistic infections (e.g., aspergillosis)
- **Non-infectious vasculitis:** Polyarteritis nodosa → single or multiple infarcts
- **Primary angiitis of the CNS** — develops without systemic vasculitis
- **Other causes of thrombosis:** hypercoagulable states, dissecting aneurysm of neck vessels, drug abuse (amphetamines, heroin, cocaine)

### Types of Brain Infarcts — ⚠️ Key Distinction
| Type | Mechanism | Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Non-haemorrhagic (pale/anaemic)** | End-organ circulation; limited collateral supply | Starts pale; most occlusive infarcts begin this way |
| **Haemorrhagic (red)** | Ischaemia-reperfusion injury after dissolution/fragmentation of occlusive material | Secondary haemorrhagic transformation; punctate haemorrhages; can occur with anticoagulation |

⚠️ Non-haemorrhagic infarcts are called "ischaemic" clinically — confusing because ALL infarcts are caused by ischaemia; the term refers specifically to pale/non-haemorrhagic ones.

## C. MORPHOLOGY OF CEREBRAL INFARCTION — ⚠️ High Yield Timeline

### Gross Changes
| Time | Gross Appearance |
|---|---|
| **0–6 hours** | Little to no visible change |
| **By 48 hours** | Pale, soft, swollen; grey-white junction becomes indistinct |
| **2–10 days** | Gelatinous and friable; boundary between normal and infarcted tissue becomes more distinct as oedema resolves |
| **10 days–3 weeks** | Tissue liquefies → fluid-filled cavity that expands as dead tissue is removed |

### Microscopic Changes (Sequential)
| Time | Microscopic Finding |
|---|---|
| **6–12 hours** | **Eosinophilic neuronal necrosis** ("dead red neurons") — ↑ eosinophilia of cytoplasm → nuclear pyknosis → karyorrhexis; both cytotoxic and vasogenic oedema present; astrocytes and endothelial cells swell; myelinated fibres disintegrate |
| **Up to 48 hours** | Progressive **neutrophilic emigration** then falls off — never as prominent as in myocardial infarction |
| **48–72 hours** | **Phagocytic cells** (monocyte-derived macrophages + activated microglia) appear; become predominant cell type over next 2–3 weeks; macrophages stuffed with myelin breakdown products and blood → persist months to years |
| **1 week** | **Reactive astrocytes** and newly formed vessels appear at periphery of lesion |
| **Months** | Astrocytic response recedes → dense meshwork of **glial fibres** + new capillaries + perivascular connective tissue = **gliosis** |
| **Remote/healed** | Fluid-filled cavity surrounded by residual gliosis; pia and arachnoid **not affected** |

⚠️ Infarcts heal from the **edges inward** → different areas of same lesion may appear at different stages simultaneously.

### Haemorrhagic Infarction
- Temporal evolution **parallels** ischaemic infarction + blood extravasation and resorption
- In patients on **anticoagulants** → may be associated with extensive intracerebral haematomas

### Venous Infarcts
- Often **haemorrhagic**
- Occur after thrombotic occlusion of **superior sagittal sinus**, other sinuses, or deep cerebral veins
- Risk factors: neoplasms, localised infections, hypercoagulable states

## D. INTRACRANIAL HAEMORRHAGE

**Overview:** Haemorrhage may occur at any intracranial site:
- **Epidural/subdural** → typically **traumatic**
- **Intraparenchymal/subarachnoid** → more often manifestation of **underlying cerebrovascular disease**

### 1. INTRAPARENCHYMAL (PRIMARY) HAEMORRHAGE

**Definition:** Rupture of a small intraparenchymal vessel → primary haemorrhage within the brain → sudden onset neurologic symptoms (stroke). ⚠️ Must not be confused with secondary haemorrhagic transformation of an occlusive infarct.

**Epidemiology:** Peak incidence ~60 years of age; middle to late adult life.

### Patterns of Haemorrhage
| Pattern | Location | Main Cause |
|---|---|---|
| **Ganglionic haemorrhage** | Basal ganglia, thalamus | **Hypertension** (most common) |
| **Lobar haemorrhage** | Cerebral hemispheric lobes | **Cerebral Amyloid Angiopathy (CAA)** |

### A. Hypertensive Haemorrhage
- Hypertension = risk factor most commonly associated with **deep brain parenchymal haemorrhages**
- Accounts for **>50%** of clinically significant haemorrhages; responsible for **~15% of deaths** in chronic hypertensives
- **Sites of hypertensive haemorrhage (in order of frequency):**
  - **Putamen** — 50–60% of cases
  - Thalamus
  - Pons
  - Cerebellar hemispheres (rarely)

**Pathogenesis of vessel damage in hypertension:**

| Vessel Size | Change |
|---|---|
| Large arteries | Accelerated atherosclerosis |
| Small arteries | **Hyaline arteriolosclerosis** — thickened but more vulnerable to rupture |
| Arterioles (severe HTN) | Proliferative changes + frank necrosis |

⚠️ If hyaline-affected arterioles **rupture** → **ganglionic haemorrhage**; if they are **occluded** → **lacunar infarct**

### B. Cerebral Amyloid Angiopathy (CAA)
- Most commonly associated with **lobar haemorrhages**
- **Amyloidogenic peptides** (same as Alzheimer disease — Aβ peptides) deposited in walls of **medium- and small-calibre meningeal, cortical, and cerebellar vessels**
- Involved vessels are rigid → fail to collapse during tissue processing and sectioning (histological clue)
- On H&E: similar appearance to hyaline arteriolosclerosis BUT material is **β-amyloid, not collagen**
- Affects **leptomeningeal and cortical vessels** (NOT basal ganglia/white matter — that's HTN)
- Confirmed by **immunohistochemistry for Aβ**

### C. CADASIL (Cerebral Autosomal Dominant Arteriopathy with Subcortical Infarcts and Leukoencephalopathy)
- **Autosomal dominant** — mutations in **NOTCH3 gene** → misfolding of extracellular domain of NOTCH3 receptor
- Clinically: recurrent **small vessel strokes** (usually infarcts, less often haemorrhages) + **dementia**
- First detectable imaging changes in **white matter**, usually presenting around **35 years of age**

### Morphology — Intraparenchymal Haemorrhage
**Acute:**
- Central core of **clotted blood** compressing adjacent parenchyma → secondary infarction, anoxic neuronal and glial changes, oedema

**Evolving:**
- Oedema resolves → **haemosiderin- and lipid-laden macrophages** appear → reactive astrocyte proliferation at periphery
- Follows same time course as cerebral infarction

**Old/Remote:**
- Areas of **parenchymal cavitary destruction** with a **rim of brownish discolouration** (haemosiderin)

### Clinical Features — Intraparenchymal Haemorrhage
- Large haemorrhage → clinically devastating, especially if extending into the **ventricular system**
- Small haemorrhage → clinically silent OR evolves like an infarct; gradual haematoma removal over weeks/months → considerable clinical improvement
- Clinical manifestations determined by **location** of haemorrhage

### 2. SUBARACHNOID HAEMORRHAGE & RUPTURED SACCULAR ANEURYSM

**Most frequent cause of spontaneous SAH:** Rupture of a **saccular ("berry") aneurysm** in a cerebral artery.
⚠️ Brain **trauma** is the most common cause of SAH overall — but saccular aneurysm rupture is the most common **spontaneous** (non-traumatic) cause.

**Other causes of non-traumatic SAH:**
- Rupture of primary intracerebral haemorrhage into ventricular system
- Vascular malformation
- Haematologic disturbances
- Tumours

### Types of Intracranial Aneurysms
| Type | Features |
|---|---|
| **Saccular (berry)** | Most common; at arterial branch points; anterior circulation |
| **Atherosclerotic (fusiform)** | Mostly basilar artery |
| **Mycotic** | Infectious; anterior circulation |
| **Traumatic** | |
| **Dissecting** | |

⚠️ Atherosclerotic, mycotic, traumatic, and dissecting aneurysms — more often cause **cerebral infarction** rather than SAH.

### Saccular Aneurysms — Epidemiology
- Found in ~**2% of the population** (community-based radiologic studies)
- ~**90%** found near major arterial branch points in the **anterior circulation**
- **Multiple aneurysms** exist in 20–30% of cases

### Common Sites (Circle of Willis) — ⚠️ Know the Percentages
| Location | Frequency |
|---|---|
| Anterior communicating artery / ACA junction | **40%** |
| MCA bifurcation | **34%** |
| ICA / posterior communicating artery junction | **20%** |
| Posterior circulation (basilar tip) | **4%** |

### Pathogenesis of Saccular Aneurysms
- Exact aetiology obscure; structural abnormality of vessel wall: **absence of smooth muscle and intimal elastic lamina** → likely developmental anomalies
- NOT present at birth — develop over time due to **underlying defect in the vessel wall media**
- Although called "congenital," this is a misnomer
- Majority sporadic; **genetic factors** important — increased incidence in first-degree relatives

**Associated conditions (↑ risk):**
- Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD)
- Ehlers-Danlos syndrome type IV
- Neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1)
- Marfan syndrome
- Fibromuscular dysplasia of extracranial arteries
- Coarctation of the aorta
- Cigarette smoking
- Hypertension (~50% of affected individuals)

### Morphology — Saccular Aneurysm
**Unruptured:**
- Thin-walled outpouching at arterial branch point along Circle of Willis or just beyond
- Size: few mm to **2–3 cm** diameter
- **Bright red, shiny surface; thin, translucent wall**
- May contain: atheromatous plaques, calcification, thrombi in wall/lumen
- Brownish discolouration of adjacent brain/meninges = evidence of **prior haemorrhage**

**Histology:**
- Wall: **thickened hyalinised intima** + adventitia only
- Smooth muscle and intimal elastic lamina **absent from the sac**; do not extend into the neck
- Adjacent arterial wall: intimal thickening + attenuation of media at the neck

**Rupture:**
- Usually occurs at the **apex of the sac**
- Blood extravasates into subarachnoid space, brain substance, or both

## E. CEREBRAL OEDEMA, HYDROCEPHALUS, RAISED ICP & HERNIATION

*Dr. Lilian Bosire — MKU Lecturer, Pathologist*

### CEREBRAL OEDEMA

**Definition:** Increased fluid accumulation within the brain parenchyma from fluid leakage from blood vessels or cell injury.

### Two Main Types — ⚠️ Know the Difference
| Feature | Vasogenic Oedema | Cytotoxic Oedema |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | BBB disruption → ↑ vascular permeability | Cell membrane injury → ↑ intracellular fluid |
| Fluid location | Extracellular (intercellular spaces) | Intracellular |
| Cause | Inflammation, neoplasms, global ischaemia | Hypoxia/ischaemia, metabolic derangements |
| Distribution | Localised or generalised | Generalised |

- Brain lacks significant lymphatics → poor resorption of excess extracellular fluid → worsens vasogenic oedema
- **Gross appearance of generalised oedema:** flattened gyri, narrowed sulci, compressed ventricles → may progress to herniation

### HYDROCEPHALUS

**Definition:** Excessive accumulation of CSF within the ventricular system.

### CSF Pathway
> Choroid plexus → Ventricles → Foramina of Luschka & Magendie → Cisterna magna → Subarachnoid space → Arachnoid granulations (absorption)

### Types
| Type | Mechanism | Ventricular Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| **Noncommunicating (Obstructive)** | Focal obstruction within ventricular system (e.g., aqueduct stenosis, 3rd ventricle mass) | Focal dilatation proximal to obstruction |
| **Communicating** | Impaired CSF resorption; ventricles still connect to subarachnoid space (e.g., post-meningitis arachnoid fibrosis, choroid plexus tumour overproduction) | Entire ventricular system dilated |
| **Hydrocephalus ex vacuo** | Compensatory ventricular expansion due to loss of brain parenchyma (e.g., infarct, atrophy) | Dilated ventricles — NOT true ↑ ICP |

### Age-Dependent Presentation
- **Before suture closure (infants):** Head enlarges (macrocephaly)
- **After suture closure:** Ventricles expand → ↑ ICP; head circumference unchanged

⚠️ CSF overproduction is a **rare** cause — associated with choroid plexus tumours

### RAISED INTRACRANIAL PRESSURE (↑ ICP)

**Causes:**
- Generalised brain oedema
- Increased CSF volume
- Focal mass lesions — tumours, abscesses, haemorrhages

**Consequences:**
- ↑ ICP → CSF displaced → pressure builds further
- Compresses vasculature → ↓ cerebral perfusion → ischaemic injury → more oedema (vicious cycle)
- Ultimately → **herniation**

### BRAIN HERNIATION

**Definition:** Displacement of brain tissue past rigid dural folds (falx, tentorium) or through skull openings due to ↑ ICP.

### Three Major Types
### 1. Subfalcine (Cingulate) Herniation
- **Mechanism:** Unilateral/asymmetric hemispheric expansion → cingulate gyrus displaced **under the falx cerebri**
- **Complication:** Compression of **anterior cerebral artery** → secondary infarcts

### 2. Transtentorial (Uncal / Mesial Temporal) Herniation
- **Mechanism:** Medial temporal lobe compressed against free margin of tentorium cerebelli

| Structure Compressed | Consequence |
|---|---|
| CN III (oculomotor) | Ipsilateral **pupillary dilation** + impaired eye movements ("blown pupil") |
| Posterior cerebral artery | Infarct → **contralateral visual field defect** (primary visual cortex) |
| Contralateral cerebral peduncle | **Ipsilateral hemiparesis** — Kernohan's notch (false localising sign) |
| Midbrain/pons penetrating vessels | **Duret haemorrhages** — linear/flame-shaped midline and paramedian haemorrhages from tearing of penetrating veins and arteries |

⚠️ **Duret haemorrhages** = secondary haemorrhagic lesions in midbrain and pons; indicate **severe, advanced** transtentorial herniation

### 3. Tonsillar Herniation
- **Mechanism:** Cerebellar tonsils displaced through the **foramen magnum**
- **Consequence:** Brainstem compression → compromises **respiratory and cardiac centres in the medulla**
- ⚠️ **Most immediately life-threatening** herniation type

### Summary Table — Herniation
| Type | Structure Herniated | Through/Past | Key Complication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Subfalcine | Cingulate gyrus | Falx cerebri | ACA compression → infarct |
| Transtentorial | Medial temporal lobe (uncus) | Tentorial notch | CN III palsy, Duret haemorrhages |
| Tonsillar | Cerebellar tonsils | Foramen magnum | Medullary compression → death |

## KEY CONCEPTS — HIGH YIELD RECAP

- **Stroke** = neurologic deficit > 24 hours; TIA = < 24 hours; vascular mechanism for both
- **Embolism > thrombosis** as cause of cerebral vascular occlusion
- **MCA territory** most commonly affected by embolic infarction
- Non-haemorrhagic infarcts start pale; **haemorrhagic transformation** occurs with reperfusion
- **"Dead red neurons"** (eosinophilic necrosis) = hallmark of acute ischaemic injury at 6–12 hours
- **Macrophages** dominate subacute infarct (48hr–3 weeks); may persist months to years
- **Gliosis** = final healed state of infarct
- **Hypertension** → ganglionic haemorrhage (putamen most common site — 50–60%) OR lacunar infarct
- **CAA** → lobar haemorrhage; Aβ deposits in cortical/leptomeningeal vessels
- **CADASIL** = NOTCH3 mutation; white matter disease + recurrent strokes + dementia; onset ~35 years
- **Saccular (berry) aneurysm** = most common cause of **spontaneous** SAH (trauma = most common overall SAH cause)
- **90%** of saccular aneurysms in the anterior circulation; **40%** at anterior communicating artery
- Saccular aneurysm sac wall = hyalinised intima + adventitia only; **no smooth muscle, no elastic lamina**
- **Vasogenic oedema** = extracellular, BBB disruption | **Cytotoxic oedema** = intracellular, cell membrane injury
- **Hydrocephalus ex vacuo** ≠ true hydrocephalus — no ↑ ICP
- **Blown pupil** (fixed, dilated) = CN III compression = transtentorial herniation until proven otherwise
- **Tonsillar herniation** = most lethal; compresses medulla
- ↑ ICP → ↓ perfusion → ischaemia → more oedema → more ↑ ICP (self-perpetuating cycle)
