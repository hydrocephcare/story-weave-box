# Leishmaniasis

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Infectious Diseases (Tropical Medicine)**

> **In one line:** leishmaniasis is caused by obligate intracellular protozoa of the genus *Leishmania*, spread by female phlebotomine sandflies, which live as amastigotes inside macrophages of the reticuloendothelial system. It ranges from self-healing skin ulcers (cutaneous) through destructive mucosal disease to fatal visceral disease (kala-azar), which is common in northern and eastern Kenya.

## What you must know

- Three syndromes: **visceral (VL, kala-azar), cutaneous (CL)** and **mucosal (ML)**.
- Vector: female **sandfly** (*Phlebotomus* in the Old World, *Lutzomyia* in the New World). Forms: **promastigote** (flagellate, in the sandfly) and **amastigote** (intracellular, in the vertebrate host).
- VL (*L. donovani* complex): fever, **massive splenomegaly**, hepatomegaly, wasting, **pancytopenia**, hypergammaglobulinaemia, dark skin.
- Gold-standard diagnosis: **amastigotes (LD bodies) on Giemsa smear** of tissue aspirate; splenic aspirate more than 95% sensitive, marrow 60 to 85%, lymph node about 50%.
- First-line VL drugs: **pentavalent antimonials** (20 mg/kg/day for 28 to 30 days) in most areas; **liposomal amphotericin B** is least toxic and drug of choice in HIV co-infection.
- **PKDL** follows treated VL; in East Africa most heal spontaneously.
- *L. aethiopica* (highlands of Ethiopia, Kenya, Uganda; hyrax reservoir) causes CL and diffuse CL.
- No vaccine; control uses insecticide-treated nets, residual spraying and treating infected dogs.

## 1. Introduction and aetiology

- Caused by about **20 species** of the genus *Leishmania*; unicellular eukaryotic **obligate intracellular protozoa** that mainly affect the **reticuloendothelial system**.
- Transmitted by phlebotomine sandflies: *Phlebotomus* (Old World: Asia, Africa, Europe) and *Lutzomyia* (New World: the Americas).
- Transmission can be **anthroponotic** (person to person via the fly) or **zoonotic** (animal reservoirs such as dogs, rodents, hyraxes).
- Two forms:
  - **Promastigotes:** extracellular, flagellate, 10 to 20 micrometres, in the sandfly.
  - **Amastigotes:** intracellular, non-flagellate, in vertebrate hosts including humans.

### Life cycle

1. The female sandfly injects **promastigotes** through its proboscis into the skin.
2. **Neutrophils** first take up the promastigotes. Infected neutrophils may undergo apoptosis and release viable parasites.
3. Parasites are taken up by **macrophages** (directly, or via apoptotic neutrophils engulfed by macrophages and dendritic cells).
4. Inside macrophages they transform and multiply as **amastigotes**, rupturing the cell and invading further macrophages.
5. A sandfly feeding on an infected host ingests amastigotes, which become flagellate promastigotes in the **posterior midgut** and multiply by **binary fission**.
6. Promastigotes migrate to the **anterior midgut** and infect a new host at the next blood meal.

## 2. Epidemiology

- Occurs in **98 countries**, mostly developing, in tropical and temperate regions.
- **Visceral leishmaniasis (kala-azar)** is caused by the ***L. donovani* complex**: *L. donovani* (anthroponotic) and *L. infantum* (zoonotic).
- **India, Bangladesh, Sudan, South Sudan, Ethiopia and Brazil** are the largest foci (90% of the world burden). Others: **Kenya, Uganda, Somalia**, the Middle East, Pakistan and China. VL is rare in West and sub-Saharan Africa.
- In Kenya VL is found in the semi-arid counties of the east and north-west (Baringo, West Pokot, Marsabit, Isiolo, Kitui, Wajir, Mandera), associated with termite mounds and *Acacia* woodland.

## 3. Visceral leishmaniasis (kala-azar)

### Immunopathogenesis

- Infection with *L. donovani* or *L. infantum* is measured by the **leishmanin (Montenegro) skin test**: delayed-type hypersensitivity to killed promastigotes at 48 hours. It is **negative in active VL** (cell-mediated immunity is depressed) and becomes positive after cure.
- The **organs of the RES** are predominantly affected: spleen, liver and lymph nodes enlarge. Tonsils and intestinal submucosa are heavily infiltrated.
- **Bone marrow dysfunction** produces **pancytopenia**.

### Clinical features

- **Abrupt onset** of moderate to high-grade fever with rigors and chills (may be double-quotidian).
- Spleen palpable by the second week and enlarges progressively; **hepatomegaly**; lymphadenopathy.
- Weight loss and malaise.
- **Dark skin discoloration** (the origin of "kala-azar", black fever).
- **Pedal oedema and ascites** in advanced disease from hypoalbuminaemia.
- **Anaemia**; **epistaxis, retinal haemorrhages, gastrointestinal bleeding** from low platelets.
- Secondary infections are common (pneumonia, TB, dysentery) and are a major cause of death.
- Untreated, VL is almost always fatal.

### Differential diagnosis

Malaria, typhoid, TB, brucellosis, schistosomiasis, portal hypertension, **CML**, **tropical splenomegaly syndrome (TSS)**, histoplasmosis.

> **Remember:** fever with **neutropenia or pancytopenia** in a patient from an endemic region strongly suggests VL. **Hypergammaglobulinaemia** (with low albumin) in long-standing illness strengthens the diagnosis.

### Laboratory diagnosis

| Test | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Smear of tissue aspirate (Giemsa)** | **Gold standard**: amastigotes. Splenic aspirate sensitivity **more than 95%**; bone marrow **60 to 85%**; lymph node **about 50%** |
| Culture of aspirates | Raises sensitivity |
| ELISA, IFAT | In sophisticated laboratories |
| Rapid immunochromatographic test (rK39) | Field test used worldwide; lower sensitivity and specificity in East Africa |
| Blood count | Pancytopenia |
| Other | Raised globulin with low albumin; LFTs |

Splenic aspiration carries a risk of haemorrhage and is done only by experienced staff with platelets and INR checked.

### Treatment

**General:** transfuse for severe anaemia; treat malnutrition and concurrent illness (HIV, TB); antibiotics for secondary infection. Some patients relapse despite completing treatment.

| Drug | Dose and notes |
|---|---|
| **Pentavalent antimonials** (drug of choice in most endemic areas) | **Sodium stibogluconate** (100 mg SbV/ml; injection painful) and **meglumine antimoniate** (85 mg SbV/ml). **20 mg/kg daily IV or IM for 28 to 30 days.** Cure over 90% in Africa, the Americas and most of the Old World, but **under 50% in Bihar, India** because of resistance. Adverse effects: arthralgia, myalgia, raised AST/ALT, ECG changes (QT prolongation), pancreatitis |
| **Amphotericin B deoxycholate** | First line in Bihar; elsewhere when antimonial fails. **0.75 to 1.0 mg/kg on alternate days for 15 infusions.** Adverse effects: fever, chills, nausea and vomiting, thrombophlebitis. Pre-treat with antihistamine (chlorpheniramine) and antipyretic (paracetamol) |
| **Liposomal amphotericin B** | **3 mg/kg daily on days 1 to 5, 14 and 21 (total 21 mg/kg)**. Total dose varies by region: Asia 10 to 15 mg/kg, Africa about 18 mg/kg, Mediterranean/Americas 20 mg/kg or more. **Less toxic** |
| **Paromomycin (aminosidine)** | Aminoglycoside; **11 mg base/kg IM daily for 21 days** (approved in India) |
| **Miltefosine** | **First oral drug** for leishmaniasis. Long half-life, so resistance develops easily. Adverse effects: mild vomiting, diarrhoea, allergic dermatitis, hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity. Teratogenic |
| Pentamidine | Second-line alternative |

There is a role for **multidrug therapy** (in East Africa, sodium stibogluconate plus paromomycin for 17 days is used to shorten treatment).

> **Exam tip:** monitor antimonials with ECG, amylase/lipase and LFTs. Stop if there is QT prolongation.

### Response and prognosis

- Within **one week** of starting treatment: defervescence, regression of splenomegaly, weight gain, recovery of blood counts. Spleen regression may take months.
- Continued improvement over **6 to 12 months** suggests cure.
- Supportive care: rest, high-protein high-calorie diet, transfusions, wound care.

### VL in the immunosuppressed (HIV)

- VL behaves as an **opportunistic infection** where both are endemic.
- **Serological tests are often negative.**
- Parasites may be recovered from unusual sites (bronchoalveolar lavage, **buffy coat**).
- **Liposomal amphotericin B** is the drug of choice. **Most patients relapse within 1 year**, so secondary prophylaxis is used.

### Post kala-azar dermal leishmaniasis (PKDL)

- **2 to 50%** of patients develop skin lesions during or after cure of VL.
- Hypopigmented macules, papules, nodules or diffuse skin infiltration, sometimes oral mucosa.
- India: antimonials for **60 to 120 days**. **East Africa: most heal spontaneously.**
- Patients with PKDL are a parasite reservoir.

## 4. Cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL)

### Causative species

| Region | Species and features |
|---|---|
| **Old World, anthroponotic** | *L. tropica*; urban and suburban |
| **Old World, zoonotic** | Mostly *L. major* |
| **East Africa highlands** | ***L. aethiopica*** in Ethiopia, Kenya, Uganda; natural parasite of **hyraxes** |
| **New World** (mainly zoonotic) | *L. mexicana*, *L. (Viannia) panamensis*, *L. amazonensis* |

### Clinical features

- After a sandfly bite, a **papule grows into a nodule that ulcerates** over weeks to months.
- The ulcer base is **painless**, with necrotic tissue and crusted serum; secondary bacterial infection can occur.
- Lymphadenopathy may precede the skin lesion; **satellite lesions** are common, especially with *L. major* and *L. tropica*.
- Lesions **heal spontaneously in 2 to 15 months**, leaving a scar. *L. major* and *L. mexicana* heal faster; *L. tropica* and *Viannia* subspecies more slowly.
- Differential: cutaneous TB, fungal infections, leprosy, sarcoidosis, malignant ulcers (including squamous cell carcinoma).

### Diagnosis

- **Amastigotes in material from the lesion** (gold standard): slit-skin smear, aspirate or biopsy.
- Culture, **PCR**, isoenzyme profiling for species.

### Treatment

Most lesions heal spontaneously. **Systemic treatment is required for:** lesions on the face, hands or joints; multiple lesions; large ulcers; lymphatic spread; New World CL at risk of mucosal disease; CL in HIV co-infection.

- **First line: pentavalent antimonial, 20 mg/kg for 20 days.**
- ***L. (V.) guyanensis*:** pentamidine isethionate (two injections of 4 mg salt/kg, 48 hours apart).
- ***L. aethiopica*:** responds to **paromomycin 16 mg/kg daily**, **not to antimonials**.
- ***L. major*:** oral fluconazole 200 mg/day for 6 weeks; miltefosine also used.
- **Ketoconazole 600 mg/day for 28 days:** 76 to 90% effective for *L. (V.) panamensis* and *L. mexicana* in Panama and Guatemala.
- **Local therapies:** cryotherapy with liquid nitrogen; local heat at **40 to 42 degrees C** (radiofrequency generator); intralesional sodium stibogluconate 0.3 to 0.8 ml; topical paromomycin.

### Diffuse cutaneous leishmaniasis (DCL)

- Caused by *L. amazonensis* and *L. mexicana* (Americas) and ***L. aethiopica*** (Ethiopia and Kenya).
- **Absent cell-mediated immune response**, so parasites multiply unchecked; widespread papules, nodules, plaques and diffuse infiltration, symmetric or asymmetric.
- Lesions **do not ulcerate** and **do not heal spontaneously**; difficult to treat; 20-day courses of antimonials given (relapse is common).

## 5. Mucosal leishmaniasis (ML)

- Typically *L. (V.) braziliensis*; rarely *L. amazonensis*, *L. guyanensis*, *L. panamensis*. **Almost entirely confined to the Americas.**
- Parasite spreads by lymphatics or blood to the mucosa of the upper respiratory tract.
- Presents with **nasal stuffiness and bleeding**, then destruction of nasal cartilage, **septal perforation and collapse of the nasal bridge** (espundia). Lips, palate and pharynx may be involved.
- **Tissue biopsy** is essential to find parasites.
- **Treatment: pentavalent antimonial 20 mg SbV/kg for 30 days.**

## 6. Prevention

- No vaccine yet.
- Vector control: **insecticide-impregnated bed nets and curtains**, residual insecticide spraying.
- Treatment of infected domestic dogs and control of animal reservoirs.
- Early case detection and treatment (anthroponotic disease).

## Key points to revise

1. *Leishmania*: promastigote in sandfly, amastigote in macrophages; transmitted by female sandflies.
2. VL (kala-azar) = *L. donovani* complex: fever, massive splenomegaly, pancytopenia, dark skin.
3. Diagnosis: Giemsa smear of aspirate; spleen over 95%, marrow 60 to 85%, node about 50%; rK39 less reliable in East Africa.
4. Antimonials 20 mg/kg for 28 to 30 days; liposomal amphotericin B for HIV co-infection; miltefosine is oral.
5. HIV co-infection: serology often negative, relapse within a year.
6. PKDL: skin lesions after VL; heals spontaneously in most of East Africa.
7. CL: ulcers heal in 2 to 15 months; *L. aethiopica* (Kenya) responds to paromomycin, not antimonials.
8. Mucosal disease is American; prevention is nets, spraying and control of reservoirs.
