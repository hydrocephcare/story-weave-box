# Oesophageal Disease

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Gastroenterology (oesophagus)**

> **In one line:** dysphagia is the key oesophageal symptom and an alarm symptom, and its pattern (solids or liquids, intermittent or progressive, oropharyngeal or oesophageal) points to the cause. The main diseases are **achalasia** (failure of LES relaxation and peristalsis), **GORD** and its complications (oesophagitis, Barrett's, stricture), **oesophageal cancer**, infections, diverticula and diffuse oesophageal spasm.

## What you must know

- **Dysphagia** = difficulty swallowing; **odynophagia** = painful swallowing. Both lead to weight loss. Dysphagia always needs a cause found.
- **Solids only** suggests a mechanical obstruction; **solids and liquids together from the start** suggests a motility disorder (achalasia, scleroderma, spasm).
- **Achalasia:** hypertonic LES that fails to relax plus absent peristalsis; **bird's beak** on barium; **manometry is the gold standard**; treat with pneumatic dilatation or Heller myotomy.
- **GORD** affects about 30% of people; PPIs are the mainstay; complications are oesophagitis, stricture, Barrett's oesophagus and anaemia.
- **Barrett's oesophagus:** squamous replaced by columnar epithelium with intestinal metaplasia; **40 to 120-fold** raised risk of adenocarcinoma.
- **Oesophageal cancer:** SCC (upper and mid-oesophagus; alcohol, tobacco) vs adenocarcinoma (lower third; GORD, Barrett's); definitive diagnosis by endoscopy and biopsy; usually palliative because of late presentation.
- **Alarm symptoms** needing endoscopy: age over 50 to 60, anaemia, GI bleeding, weight loss, dysphagia, symptoms over 6 months.

## 1. Anatomy and physiology of swallowing

- Swallowing has three phases: **oral, pharyngeal, oesophageal**.
- The oesophagus is a fibromuscular tube: the **upper third is skeletal muscle**, the rest **smooth muscle**.
- It has two sphincters: **upper (UES)** and **lower (LES)**.
- Functions: **transport of food by peristalsis**, and **prevention of gastric regurgitation** by the UES and LES.
- Nerve supply: vagus nerve and sympathetic trunk.

## 2. Dysphagia

### Definitions

- **Dysphagia:** difficulty in swallowing; abnormal passage of liquids or solids from the mouth through the oesophagus into the stomach. It has mechanical and neuromuscular causes.
- **Odynophagia:** pain on swallowing.
- If no cause is found, it is **functional dysphagia**.

### Types and causes

| | Oropharyngeal ("transfer") dysphagia | Oesophageal dysphagia |
|---|---|---|
| Site | Oral cavity, pharynx, upper oesophagus, UES | Oesophageal body, LES, cardia |
| Symptoms | Difficulty **initiating** swallowing, food may come out of the **nose**, choking and coughing | Swallowing starts, but food **sticks** later |
| Causes | CVA, motor neurone disease, polymyositis, myasthenia gravis, striated muscle disease | **Mechanical** and **motor** (see below) |

**Oesophageal dysphagia**

| Mechanical (solids first) | Motor (solids and liquids) |
|---|---|
| Large food bolus | Smooth muscle disorders: **scleroderma**, **achalasia**, **oesophageal spasm** |
| Intrinsic narrowing: oesophagitis, webs and rings, tumour, strictures | |
| Extrinsic compression: enlarged thyroid, diverticulum, left atrial enlargement | |

### History: the key questions

1. Oropharyngeal or oesophageal? (Always ask the **location**.)
2. **Solids only** (mechanical) or **solids and liquids** (motor)?
3. **Intermittent or progressive?** Progressive suggests a benign or malignant stricture; rings give intermittent solid food dysphagia.
4. Associated symptoms: **heartburn** points to GORD; **weight loss** points to tumour.
5. Medical problems: diabetes mellitus, scleroderma, Sjogren's syndrome, overlap syndrome, AIDS, neuromuscular disease (stroke, Parkinson's disease, myasthenia gravis, muscular dystrophy, multiple sclerosis), cancer, **Chagas disease**.
6. Previous surgery (strictures) or radiotherapy.
7. Drugs causing **pill oesophagitis**: potassium chloride, alendronate, ferrous sulphate, quinidine, ascorbic acid, tetracycline, aspirin and NSAIDs.

### Examination

Look for **bulbar palsy** (lower motor neuron lesion of cranial nerves IX, X, XI, XII, in the medulla or outside the brainstem), dysarthria, **ptosis** (myasthenia gravis), CVA signs, **goitre**, and skin changes of connective tissue disease (scleroderma).

## 3. Achalasia

### Definition and pathology

- **Hypertonic LES that fails to relax on swallowing**, plus **failure of peristalsis** in the oesophageal body (the main diagnostic feature, shown by manometry), leading to progressive dilatation.
- **Idiopathic loss of intramural (myenteric) neurons** of the LES and oesophageal body. Most cases have an unknown cause.
- Secondary causes (few): **Chagas disease**, gastric carcinoma, infiltrating diseases such as lymphoma.
- The slides describe it as usually occurring in young females.

> **Check:** achalasia is rare and usually presents between 25 and 60 years, affecting men and women about equally.

### Clinical features

- **Progressive dysphagia to both solids and liquids simultaneously.**
- No relationship with alcohol or tobacco.
- **No heartburn**, but some patients have severe chest pain from oesophageal spasm.
- Regurgitation of undigested food; **nocturnal pulmonary aspiration** as the oesophagus dilates.
- Predisposes to **squamous cell carcinoma** of the oesophagus.

### Investigations

| Test | Findings |
|---|---|
| **Endoscopy (EGD)** | For alarm features, to exclude cancer (pseudoachalasia): onset after age 60, anaemia, haem-positive stools, symptoms over 6 months, weight loss |
| **Chest X-ray** | Absent gastric bubble, wide mediastinum (dilated oesophagus), fluid level |
| **Barium swallow** | Dilated oesophagus narrowing to a smooth **"bird's beak"** at the distal end |
| **Oesophageal manometry (gold standard)** | Raised resting LES pressure, no or partial LES relaxation, low-amplitude, non-propagating (simultaneous) contractions |

### Management (no cure)

- **Pneumatic dilatation:** fluoroscopically positioned balloon dilates the LES; improves symptoms in about 80% (treatment of choice in the slides).
- **Botulinum toxin** injection into the LES: for patients who refuse or fail dilatation (effect wears off).
- **Surgical (Heller) myotomy**, laparoscopic or open: extremely effective.
- Dilatation and myotomy can both cause GORD, so myotomy is often combined with an **anti-reflux procedure**, and a PPI is given.
- Drugs (**nitrates/nitroglycerin, calcium channel blockers, antimuscarinics**) for patients who cannot tolerate procedures.

> **Exam tip:** achalasia = dysphagia to solids and liquids from the start, bird's beak on barium, high LES pressure on manometry. Cancer = progressive dysphagia (solids then liquids) with weight loss and a ragged, irregular narrowing ("rat-tail") rather than a smooth taper.

## 4. Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD)

### Definition and background

- Reflux causing heartburn affects about **30%** of the general population. Occasional reflux is normal.
- Normally reflux is followed by peristaltic waves that clear the gullet and **alkaline saliva** neutralises residual acid.
- **GORD** develops when the oesophageal mucosa is exposed to **gastroduodenal contents for prolonged periods**, causing symptoms and in some cases oesophagitis. The damage depends on the **refluxed material** and the **duration**.
- Patients with **Sjogren's syndrome** produce little saliva and are prone to GORD.

### Factors in GORD

| Factor | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **LES abnormality** | The LES is normally tonically contracted and relaxes only on swallowing. Causes: transient LES relaxation, sustained loss of tone (scleroderma, surgical resection). Fat, chocolate, alcohol and coffee relax the LES. The LES is formed by oesophageal muscle and the diaphragm |
| **Hiatus hernia** | Loss of the pressure gradient between abdomen and thorax and loss of the oblique angle between cardia and oesophagus (see below) |
| **Delayed oesophageal clearance** | Defective peristalsis is common in oesophagitis. It is a primary abnormality (persists after healing) and increases acid exposure time |
| **Delayed gastric emptying** | Mechanical (pyloric stenosis, outlet obstruction) or motor (diabetes) |
| **Raised intra-abdominal pressure** | **Pregnancy and obesity**; weight loss may improve symptoms |
| **Reduced saliva** | Sjogren's syndrome |

### Hiatus hernia

- Herniation of the stomach through the diaphragm into the chest. Occurs in about **30% of people over 50**. GORD is particularly associated with the **sliding** type.
- Often asymptomatic; may cause heartburn and regurgitation. Symptoms correlate poorly with hernia size (common in people without symptoms; some symptomatic patients have a tiny or no hernia).
- Complications: oesophagitis, Barrett's oesophagus, peptic stricture; **gastric volvulus** with large para-oesophageal hernias.

### Clinical features

1. **Heartburn and regurgitation**, provoked by bending, straining or lying down.
2. **Water brash** (reflex salivation as acid enters the gullet).
3. Often overweight.
4. Waking at night **choking** as refluxed fluid irritates the larynx.
5. Odynophagia.
6. **Dysphagia** (a complication: stricture or oedema).
7. **Atypical chest pain** mimicking angina (reflux-induced spasm).
8. Hoarseness (acid laryngitis), recurrent chest infections, chronic cough, asthma (the true relationship to reflux is unclear).

### Complications

| Complication | Key points |
|---|---|
| **Oesophagitis** | Endoscopic appearance ranges from redness to bleeding, ulceration and stricture; may look normal. Poor correlation between symptoms and endoscopic or histological findings |
| **Barrett's oesophagus** | See below |
| **Anaemia** | Iron deficiency from chronic occult blood loss; large hiatus hernia with erosions at the neck of the sac (**Cameron lesions**). Hiatus hernia is common, so exclude other causes (especially **colorectal cancer**) even if oesophagitis is seen |
| **Benign stricture** | Fibrous stricture after long-standing oesophagitis, especially in the elderly and those with poor peristalsis |
| **Gastric volvulus** | A huge intrathoracic hernia twists on itself |

**Barrett's oesophagus**

- Pre-malignant: normal squamous lining of the lower oesophagus is replaced by **columnar mucosa** (goblet cells; areas of **intestinal metaplasia**).
- An adaptive response to chronic reflux; found in **10% of patients** having gastroscopy for reflux.
- Often asymptomatic until it presents with cancer. Risk of oesophageal (adeno)carcinoma **40 to 120-fold** raised.
- More common in men (especially white), the obese, those over 50, and smokers; prevalence is rising.
- **Diagnosis:** multiple systematic biopsies to show intestinal metaplasia and/or dysplasia.
- **Management:** treat reflux symptoms and complications; surveillance endoscopy **every 3 to 5 years** without dysplasia, **every 6 to 12 months** with low-grade dysplasia; **radiofrequency ablation or photodynamic therapy** for dysplasia or intramucosal cancer; **oesophagectomy or endoscopic resection** for high-grade dysplasia or intramucosal carcinoma.

**Benign oesophageal stricture**

- Dysphagia worse for solids than liquids; meat bolus obstruction causes absolute dysphagia. Heartburn is common but not invariable (many elderly patients have none).
- **Diagnosis:** endoscopy with biopsy to exclude malignancy. **Endoscopic balloon dilatation** helps.
- **Treatment:** long-term **full-dose PPI** to prevent recurrence; chew food thoroughly; ensure adequate dentition.

**Gastric volvulus**

- Severe chest pain, vomiting and dysphagia, with complete obstruction. **CXR** (air bubble in the chest) and barium swallow. Most resolve spontaneously, but recurrence is common; surgery is usually advised after nasogastric decompression.

### Investigation

- **Young patients with typical symptoms and no alarm features** (dysphagia, weight loss, anaemia) can be **treated empirically** without investigation.
- Investigate if: age over 50 to 55, atypical symptoms, or complications suspected.
- **Endoscopy** is the investigation of choice (excludes other upper GI disease and identifies complications).
- If endoscopy is normal and diagnosis unclear (or surgery being considered): **24-hour pH monitoring** (a slim catheter tethered in the oesophagus) and **manometry**.
- A **barium swallow** is the most definitive test for diagnosing a hiatus hernia.

### Management

| Step | Details |
|---|---|
| **Lifestyle** | Weight loss; avoid foods that worsen symptoms; raise the head of the bed for nocturnal symptoms; avoid late meals; stop smoking |
| **PPIs** | Usually effective for symptoms and for healing oesophagitis |
| **Other drugs** | Domperidone when dysmotility is prominent; antacids and alginates for symptom relief; H2 blockers relieve symptoms but do **not heal oesophagitis** |
| **Surgery** | Laparoscopic anti-reflux surgery (fundoplication), rarely needed |

**Adverse effects of long-term PPI:** reduced absorption of iron, B12 and magnesium; osteoporosis and fractures; enteric infections (Salmonella, Campylobacter, possibly *C. difficile*); progression of gastric mucosal atrophy in *H. pylori* infection.

## 5. Oesophageal cancer

- A disease of the elderly (over 50 years).
- **Two types:** **squamous cell carcinoma (SCC)**, historically up to 90% of cases; **adenocarcinoma** has risen sharply in the USA to about 50% of new cases.
- Location: about 15% upper third, 45% middle third, 40% lower third.

| | **Squamous cell carcinoma** | **Adenocarcinoma** |
|---|---|---|
| Epidemiology | Higher in African-American men | More common in Caucasians and men (5:1) |
| Site | Upper and mid-thoracic oesophagus (about one third in the distal 10 cm) | **Distal third / gastro-oesophageal junction** (80%) |
| Risk factors | **Alcohol and tobacco**, nitrosamines, betel nuts, hot foods and drinks (tea), HPV, achalasia, **Plummer-Vinson syndrome** (oesophageal web), caustic ingestion, nasopharyngeal carcinoma | **GORD and Barrett's oesophagus**; alcohol and tobacco less important |

> **Remember:** in Kenya and East Africa SCC remains the dominant type, with a high burden in the rift valley and highland regions.

**Prognosis:** poor. Five-year survival about 30 to 40% with locoregional disease, about 5% with distant metastasis at diagnosis.

**Staging (TNM-based):**

| Stage | Description |
|---|---|
| I | Invades lamina propria or submucosa; nodes negative |
| IIa | Invades muscularis propria or adventitia; nodes negative |
| IIb | Invades up to muscularis propria; regional nodes positive |
| III | Invades adventitia with positive regional nodes, or invades adjacent structures (nodes positive or negative) |
| IV | Distant metastasis |

**Clinical features**

1. **Dysphagia**: the commonest symptom (solids first, then liquids).
2. **Weight loss**: the second commonest.
3. Anorexia.
4. Odynophagia (late; suggests mediastinal invasion).
5. Haematemesis; hoarseness (**recurrent laryngeal nerve** involvement).
6. Aspiration pneumonia and respiratory symptoms (tracheobronchial involvement).
7. **Tracheo-oesophageal or broncho-oesophageal fistula**.
8. Chest pain.

**Diagnosis**

1. **Barium swallow:** presumptive diagnosis.
2. **Upper endoscopy with biopsy and brush cytology:** definitive; confirms the diagnosis in 95%.
3. **Endoscopic (transoesophageal) ultrasound:** depth of invasion, the most reliable test for local staging.
4. **Full metastatic work-up:** CT chest and abdomen, CXR, bone scan.

**Treatment**

- **Palliation** in most (advanced at presentation): stenting, radiotherapy, nutritional support.
- **Oesophagectomy** may be curative for stage 0, I or IIa.
- **Chemoradiotherapy before surgery** prolongs survival more than surgery alone.

## 6. Other oesophageal conditions

### Infectious oesophagitis

- Occurs in **immunocompromised** patients (HIV, steroids, chemotherapy).
- Viral (**HSV, CMV, VZV**), bacterial, or fungal (**Candida**, the commonest).
- Presents with dysphagia, odynophagia and bleeding. Diagnosed by barium swallow, endoscopy and biopsy.

### Diverticula (outpouchings)

- **Zenker diverticulum** (upper oesophagus/hypopharynx, posterior wall) and **epiphrenic diverticulum** (lower oesophagus).
- Usually asymptomatic; may present with regurgitation of food eaten days earlier (undigested), dysphagia, halitosis, recurrent aspiration.

### Diffuse oesophageal spasm ("corkscrew" or "nutcracker" oesophagus)

- A hypermotility disorder, seen most often in women with multiple complaints.
- Basic pathology: motor abnormality of the oesophageal body, mainly the lower two thirds; contractions are **repetitive, simultaneous and high amplitude**.
- **Chest pain and dysphagia**, related to eating or exertion; may mimic angina (squeezing pressure radiating to jaw, arms, upper back). Worse with emotional stress and cold liquids.
- Regurgitation of contents and saliva is common, but **acid reflux is not** (although it can aggravate symptoms).
- Associated with irritable bowel syndrome and pyloric spasm; gallstones, peptic ulcer and pancreatitis can trigger it.
- **Diagnosis:** barium swallow ("corkscrew" appearance) and manometry.

## 7. Exam practice points

- Non-obstructive cause of dysphagia in a list: **achalasia** (a motility disorder).
- Best test for a hiatus hernia: **barium meal/swallow**.
- **Barrett's:** metaplasia of squamous to columnar epithelium in the lower third of the oesophagus.
- Commonest cause of benign oesophageal stricture: **peptic stricture** (chronic acid reflux; about 80% of strictures). A patient with solid-then-liquid dysphagia, heartburn history, and no weight loss: think peptic stricture.
- Barium findings: dilated baglike proximal oesophagus with smooth tapered distal end = achalasia; ragged irregular narrowing = SCC.
- Polymyositis dysphagia comes with proximal muscle weakness.

## Key points to revise

1. Dysphagia: solids only means mechanical; solids and liquids means motor; progressive dysphagia with weight loss means cancer until proven otherwise.
2. Achalasia: loss of myenteric neurons, high LES pressure, no peristalsis, bird's beak; manometry is the gold standard; treat with dilatation or myotomy.
3. GORD results from LES dysfunction, hiatus hernia, poor clearance and raised abdominal pressure; treat with lifestyle changes and PPIs.
4. Complications of GORD: oesophagitis, stricture, Barrett's, iron-deficiency anaemia (Cameron lesions).
5. Barrett's oesophagus: intestinal metaplasia; 40 to 120-fold cancer risk; biopsy and surveillance.
6. SCC (alcohol, tobacco, upper and mid-oesophagus) versus adenocarcinoma (GORD, Barrett's, lower third).
7. Oesophageal cancer is diagnosed by endoscopy with biopsy, staged by endoscopic ultrasound and CT, and is usually palliated.
8. Infectious oesophagitis occurs in immunocompromised patients; diffuse spasm gives corkscrew oesophagus and angina-like pain.
