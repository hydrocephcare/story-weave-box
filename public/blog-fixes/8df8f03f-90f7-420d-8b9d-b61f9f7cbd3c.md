# Micronutrient Deficiencies and Excesses in Children

**Year 4 · Paediatrics · Nutrition**

> **In one line:** micronutrients are vitamins and minerals needed in small amounts as coenzymes, antioxidants and regulators of gene expression; each has a characteristic deficiency syndrome (vitamin A: xerophthalmia; thiamine: beriberi; niacin: pellagra; vitamin C: scurvy; folate and B12: megaloblastic anaemia), and the fat-soluble vitamins A and D also cause toxicity when taken in excess.

## What you must know

- **Vitamin A deficiency**: night blindness, then xerophthalmia, **Bitot spots**, corneal ulceration and **keratomalacia** (irreversible blindness); it also raises mortality from measles and diarrhoea.
- **Thiamine (B1) deficiency**: **dry beriberi** (neuropathy), **wet beriberi** (cardiac failure) and infantile beriberi; test with **erythrocyte transketolase**; give thiamine 10 mg IM/IV daily for the first week.
- **Niacin (B3) deficiency = pellagra**: the three Ds, **dermatitis, diarrhoea, dementia**; maize-based diets are the classic cause.
- **Vitamin B6**: suspect in any infant with seizures; a trial of **pyridoxine 100 mg** can stop them. Give pyridoxine with **isoniazid**.
- **Folate and B12** deficiency cause **megaloblastic anaemia** with hypersegmented neutrophils; folate prevents neural tube defects, B12 deficiency also damages the nervous system.
- **Vitamin C deficiency = scurvy**: subperiosteal haemorrhage, pseudoparalysis, bleeding gums, perifollicular haemorrhages, scorbutic rosary.
- **Hypervitaminosis D** gives hypercalcaemia; treat with saline, loop diuretic, steroids, calcitonin and bisphosphonates.
- **Hypervitaminosis A** gives raised intracranial pressure (bulging fontanelle), hyperostosis and teratogenicity; **carotenoids** are not toxic (carotenodermia only).

## 1. Vitamin A

### 1.1 Biology and sources

- **Fat-soluble** vitamin that mammals cannot synthesise, so it is an **obligatory dietary** factor. "Vitamin A" describes a group of compounds with the activity of **all-trans retinol**.
- Essential for **vision**, reproduction, cell and tissue differentiation, immune function, embryonic development, haematopoiesis, metabolism and growth.
- **Preformed vitamin A** comes from animal foods: **liver and kidney** are very rich; other meats, milk and cheese moderate.
- **Provitamin A carotenoids** (mainly **beta-carotene**) come from yellow-orange vegetables and fruit (pumpkin, squash, sweet potato) and dark green leafy vegetables (chard, spinach, broccoli).
- Active forms:
  - **All-trans retinoic acid**: the most bioactive form, needed for cell differentiation and gene transcription.
  - **11-cis retinal**: the light-absorbing chromophore of **rhodopsin** (rods) and iodopsin (cones).

### 1.2 Causes of deficiency

- Poor intake (low animal foods, low vegetable intake), a common problem in low-income settings.
- **Protein-energy malnutrition**: protein deficiency impairs synthesis of **retinol-binding protein**, so vitamin A cannot be transported even if the liver holds stores.
- **Zinc deficiency** (clinical or subclinical) increases the risk.
- Fat malabsorption (cholestasis, cystic fibrosis, coeliac disease, chronic diarrhoea) and infections (measles, diarrhoea) which both deplete stores.

### 1.3 Clinical features

Epithelial change is the core problem: basal cell proliferation, **hyperkeratosis** and stratified, cornified squamous epithelium replace normal mucus-secreting epithelium.

| System | Effect |
|---|---|
| Gut | Loss of mucus-secreting **goblet cells**, weak barrier, diarrhoea |
| Respiratory tract | Poor mucus clearance of pathogens and toxicants, more infections |
| Urinary tract | **Squamous metaplasia** of renal pelves, ureters, vagina, pancreatic and salivary ducts; infections; loss of bladder epithelial integrity gives **pyuria and haematuria** |
| Skin | Dry, scaly, hyperkeratotic patches on arms, legs, shoulders and buttocks (follicular hyperkeratosis) |
| General | Poor growth, diarrhoea, infections, anaemia, apathy, intellectual impairment, raised intracranial pressure with wide separation of sutures |

**Eye disease (xerophthalmia)** is the most characteristic and specific sign. It develops insidiously and rarely occurs before age 2 years.

1. **Delayed dark adaptation** (reduced resynthesis of rhodopsin), progressing to **night blindness (nyctalopia)**. Photophobia is common.
2. **Conjunctival xerosis**: dryness and keratinisation of the conjunctiva.
3. **Bitot spots**: foamy, triangular plaques on the conjunctiva.
4. **Corneal xerosis**, corneal opacity and ulceration.
5. **Keratomalacia**: lymphocyte infiltration, then irreversible corneal degeneration and **irreversible blindness**.

- These eye lesions are mainly diseases of the young and are a major cause of **preventable childhood blindness** in developing countries.
- Treatment with vitamin A up to the stage of keratomalacia rapidly replenishes the body and can save vision.

> **Exam tip:** the earliest symptom is **night blindness**; the earliest sign is conjunctival xerosis; keratomalacia is the irreversible stage and is a medical emergency.

### 1.4 Diagnosis

- Mostly clinical. **Dark adaptation tests** detect early deficiency.
- **Bitot spots** and **xerophthalmia** are characteristic (active Bitot spots are usually confined to preschool children).
- **Diet history** supports or rules out low intake.
- Serum retinol can be measured where available.

### 1.5 Treatment

- Latent deficiency: **1,500 microgram** vitamin A daily, then aim for the recommended daily allowance (RDA).
- Children without overt signs but suspected low stores: weekly doses at RDA level reduced morbidity and mortality from infections such as **measles**.
- Usual high-dose schedule: **30 to 60 mg retinol (100,000 to 200,000 IU) per child**, given once or twice, under careful monitoring to avoid toxicity.
- **Xerophthalmia**: 1,500 microgram/kg orally for 5 days, then **7,500 microgram IM** in oil until recovery.

> **Check:** the standard WHO schedule for xerophthalmia and for severe malnutrition or measles is a single oral dose on day 1, day 2 and at 2 weeks (50,000 IU under 6 months, 100,000 IU at 6 to 11 months, 200,000 IU from 12 months). Follow the schedule in the Kenya MoH guidelines.

### 1.6 Hypervitaminosis A

- **Causes**: excess supplements or food faddism, including very high intake of organ meats. Chronic daily intakes of **15,000 microgram in adults and 6,000 microgram in children** can be toxic.
- Vitamin A is stored in the liver and other tissues and there is **no antidote**. Symptoms often subside quickly once the vitamin is stopped; the speed depends on the amount stored. Extreme toxicity is fatal.
- **Early signs**: headache and vomiting, anorexia.
- **Chronic signs**: dry itchy desquamating skin, seborrhoeic lesions, fissuring at the corners of the mouth, **alopecia**, coarse hair, bone pain and swelling, **hepatosplenomegaly**, diplopia, raised intracranial pressure, irritability, stupor, dry mucous membranes, desquamation of the palms and soles.
- **Radiographs**: **hyperostosis** of several long bones, especially mid-shaft.
- **Young children**: vomiting, **bulging fontanelle**, anorexia, pruritus, poor weight gain. Less commonly diplopia, papilloedema, cranial nerve palsies, features of **pseudotumour cerebri**.
- **Teratogenicity**: high vitamin A or synthetic retinoids early in pregnancy cause severe congenital malformations. It is linked to therapeutic doses (0.5 to 1.5 mg/kg) of oral **13-cis-retinoic acid** (isotretinoin, used for acne or cancer) in the first trimester.
- **Carotenoids** are not toxic even in high doses, but cause yellow skin including the palms (**carotenodermia**) and high serum levels (**carotenaemia**). The sclerae stay white, which separates it from jaundice.

## 2. The B-complex vitamins

- Water-soluble: **thiamine (B1), riboflavin (B2), niacin (B3), pyridoxine (B6), folate, cobalamin (B12), biotin, pantothenic acid**. Choline and inositol are also grouped here, but no deficiency syndrome has been attributed to them.
- They act as **coenzymes** in closely related metabolic pathways. Diets poor in one B vitamin are usually poor in the others, so several deficiencies usually coexist.
- **If one B vitamin deficiency is proven, treat with the whole B complex.**

## 3. Thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency

### 3.1 Causes

- Severe malnutrition, malignancy, after surgery, critical illness (low levels are common).
- **Polished rice** diet (oriental beriberi); highly refined wheat flour; **alcohol** (occidental beriberi); food faddism.
- Refugee-camp populations on monotonous polished-rice diets.
- Prolonged **total parenteral nutrition** without thiamine.
- Infants of mothers on polished, repeatedly washed rice (thiamine-poor breast milk).

### 3.2 Clinical features

- Develops within **2 to 3 months** of deficient intake.
- Early, non-specific: fatigue, apathy, irritability, depression, drowsiness, poor concentration, anorexia, nausea, abdominal discomfort.
- **Dry (neuritic) beriberi**: peripheral neuritis (tingling, burning, paraesthesia of toes and feet), reduced deep tendon reflexes, loss of vibration sense, calf tenderness and cramps, muscle atrophy, ataxia, ptosis, optic atrophy, and **hoarseness or aphonia from laryngeal nerve paralysis** (characteristic).
- **Wet (cardiac) beriberi**: high-output **cardiac failure** with fluid accumulation from cardiac and renal dysfunction. Many patients have a mixture (cardiopathy with peripheral neuropathy).
- Late: raised intracranial pressure, meningism, coma.
- **Wernicke encephalopathy** (mental change, ocular signs, ataxia) is rare in children; seen with malignancy or defective formula.
- **Infantile beriberi**: vomiting, lethargy, restlessness, ophthalmoplegia, abdominal distension, developmental delay, failure to thrive, **lactic acidosis**, nystagmus, diarrhoea, apnoea, seizures, auditory neuropathy. A fulminant form presents with tachycardia, moaning and severe metabolic acidosis, responding to parenteral thiamine.
- **Death is usually from cardiac involvement**: cyanosis and dyspnoea, then tachycardia, hepatomegaly, loss of consciousness and convulsions. Cardiac signs revert quickly with treatment; without it, heart failure is fatal.

> **Remember:** thiamine is a cofactor for pyruvate dehydrogenase and transketolase, so deficiency causes **lactic acidosis** and energy failure in heart and nerves. Give thiamine before or with glucose in at-risk patients, because glucose alone can precipitate acute deficiency.

### 3.3 Diagnosis, prevention and treatment

- **Diagnosis**: clinical suspicion; keep a high index of suspicion for **unexplained cardiac failure** in children. Biochemical tests: **erythrocyte transketolase activity** and the **thiamine pyrophosphate effect**.
- **Prevention**: adequate maternal thiamine; varied weaning diet with meat and enriched or whole-grain cereals; thiamine in **TPN**.
- **Treatment**: oral thiamine is effective, but children with **cardiac failure, convulsions or coma** get **10 mg IM or IV daily for the first week**, then **3 to 5 mg/day orally for at least 6 weeks**. Give all other B vitamins.
- **Response**: dramatic in infants and in cardiac disease; the neurological response is slow and often incomplete. Survivors of severe infantile disease may have epilepsy, intellectual disability, and language and hearing problems.

## 4. Niacin (vitamin B3) and pellagra

- Niacin (nicotinamide or nicotinic acid) forms **NAD and NADP**, needed for the respiratory chain, fatty acid and steroid synthesis, cell differentiation and DNA processing.
- It is absorbed rapidly from the stomach and gut and can be **synthesised from tryptophan**.
- **Sources**: meat, fish, poultry, cereals, legumes, green leafy vegetables. Milk and eggs have little niacin but plenty of tryptophan.

### 4.1 Causes of pellagra

- Populations eating **maize** as the staple (poor in tryptophan; its niacin is bound and unavailable unless treated with alkali).
- Severe dietary imbalance: anorexia nervosa, war and famine.
- Disturbed tryptophan metabolism: **carcinoid syndrome**, **Hartnup disease**. Isoniazid also interferes with niacin.

### 4.2 Clinical features

- Early: anorexia, lassitude, weakness, burning sensations, numbness, dizziness.
- **Classic triad: dermatitis, diarrhoea, dementia** (and, if untreated, death, the fourth D).
- **Dermatitis** (the most characteristic feature): sudden or insidious, triggered by irritants and **sunlight**. Lesions are **sharply demarcated**, on the hands and feet in a **glove or stocking** distribution and around the neck as the **Casal necklace**. Vesicles and bullae (wet type) may occur; healed skin may stay pigmented.
- Stomatitis, glossitis, vomiting and diarrhoea may precede or accompany the rash. Neurological: depression, disorientation, insomnia, delirium.
- **Infants and young children** rarely show the classic picture; they have anorexia, irritability, anxiety, apathy, alternating diarrhoea and constipation, and anaemia.

### 4.3 Diagnosis, treatment, prevention

- **Diagnosis**: glossitis, GI symptoms and a symmetric photosensitive dermatitis; **rapid response to niacin** is confirmatory.
- **Treatment**: varied liberal diet plus **niacin 50 to 300 mg/day**; **100 mg IV** in severe disease or poor absorption. Add other B vitamins, avoid sunlight in the active phase, cover the skin with soothing applications, and treat coexisting deficiencies.
- **Prevention**: varied diet with meat, eggs, milk and fortified cereals. Adequate intake of niacin:

| Age | Adequate intake |
|---|---|
| 0 to 6 months | 2 mg |
| 7 to 12 months | 4 mg |
| 1 to 3 years | 6 mg |
| 4 to 8 years | 8 mg |
| 9 to 13 years | 12 mg |
| 14 to 18 years | 14 to 16 mg |

## 5. Vitamin B6 (pyridoxine)

- Group: pyridoxine, pyridoxal, pyridoxamine and phosphorylated forms. **Pyridoxal 5-phosphate (PLP)** is the main coenzyme for amino acid metabolism, **neurotransmitter synthesis (GABA)**, glycogen metabolism and steroid action.
- Human milk and infant formulas have enough. Sources: fortified cereals, meat, fish, poultry, liver, bananas, rice, some vegetables. Milling and high-temperature processing destroy B6; **parboiling rice prevents loss**.

### 5.1 Deficiency

- **Risk factors**: drugs that inhibit B6 (**isoniazid**, penicillamine, corticosteroids, phenytoin, carbamazepine), oral contraceptives, maintenance dialysis.
- **Infants**: listlessness, irritability, **seizures**, vomiting, failure to thrive.
- Peripheral neuritis occurs in adults but not usually in children.
- Skin: **cheilosis, glossitis, seborrhoeic dermatitis** around eyes, nose and mouth. Microcytic (sideroblastic) anaemia is uncommon in infants. Infections are more frequent.
- **Dependency syndromes** respond to very large pyridoxine doses: **pyridoxine-dependent epilepsy** (mutation in **ALDH7A1**, deficient **antiquitin**), a B6-responsive anaemia, xanthurenic aciduria, cystathioninuria and homocystinuria.

### 5.2 Diagnosis, treatment, prevention

- **Diagnosis**: suspect in **all infants with seizures**. After excluding common causes, give **100 mg pyridoxine** (EEG monitoring if possible); if seizures stop, suspect B6 deficiency or dependence. In older children, 100 mg IM during EEG recording; EEG improvement suggests deficiency.
- **Treatment**: **100 mg IM or IV** for B6-deficiency convulsions; one dose is enough if diet then adequate. For dependent children, **2 to 10 mg IM or 10 to 100 mg PO daily**.
- **Prevention**: varied diet; parboil rice; supplement in the first weeks for infants of mothers who took large pyridoxine doses in pregnancy (risk of dependency seizures); **any child on isoniazid should receive pyridoxine**.

> **Exam tip:** isoniazid causes B6 deficiency (peripheral neuropathy, seizures). Always co-prescribe pyridoxine in TB treatment, particularly in malnourished or HIV-positive children.

## 6. Folate

- Needed for **CNS development in embryogenesis**, DNA synthesis and red cell maturation.
- **Sources**: rice and cereals (especially enriched), beans, leafy vegetables, oranges, papaya. Absorbed in the small intestine; also made by colonic bacteria; half-life prolonged by enterohepatic recirculation.

### 6.1 Causes of deficiency

- Rapid growth or high cell turnover (infancy, adolescence).
- **Poor diet** (e.g. goat's milk, which is low in folate).
- **Malabsorption**: coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease.
- **Increased requirement**: sickle cell anaemia (chronic haemolysis), psoriasis, malignancy.
- **Impaired utilisation**: long-term high-dose NSAIDs, **phenytoin and phenobarbital**, **methotrexate** (also trimethoprim).
- Rare: hereditary folate malabsorption; inborn errors of folate metabolism.

### 6.2 Clinical features

- **Megaloblastic anaemia** with **hypersegmented neutrophils**.
- Glossitis, listlessness, growth retardation independent of anaemia.
- **Neural tube defects** (spina bifida, anencephaly) with low maternal folate; periconceptional folic acid prevents them.
- **Hereditary folate malabsorption** (1 to 3 months): chronic diarrhoea, failure to thrive, oral ulcers, neurological deterioration, megaloblastic anaemia, opportunistic infections.
- **Cerebral folate deficiency** (4 to 6 months): irritability, microcephaly, developmental delay, cerebellar ataxia, pyramidal signs, choreoathetosis, ballismus, seizures, optic atrophy and blindness.

### 6.3 Diagnosis, treatment, prevention

- **Diagnosis**: macrocytosis with low serum or red-cell folate. Marrow is **hypercellular** (erythroid hyperplasia) with prominent megaloblastic change and **giant metamyelocytes** with vacuolated cytoplasm. In cerebral folate deficiency, CSF **5-methyltetrahydrofolate** is low while plasma and red-cell folate are normal.
- **Treatment**: folic acid **0.5 to 1.0 mg/day** orally or parenterally for **3 to 4 weeks** or until a definite haematological response; maintenance **0.2 mg**.
- **Prevention**: breastfeeding (better folate nutrition than non-breastfed infants); folate-rich foods; **food fortification**; **400 to 800 microgram folic acid daily** for all women before and during pregnancy, started **at least 1 month before conception**; routine iron-folate tablets in at-risk children and pregnant women. Mandatory flour fortification plus education has substantially cut neural tube defects.

> **Remember:** before giving folic acid to a megaloblastic patient, make sure B12 deficiency is excluded. Folate alone can correct the anaemia while neurological damage from B12 deficiency progresses.

## 7. Vitamin B12 (cobalamin)

- **Functions**: cofactor for isomerisation of **methylmalonyl-CoA to succinyl-CoA** (lipid and carbohydrate metabolism). **Methylcobalamin** transfers methyl groups to convert **homocysteine to methionine** (needs folate); important for DNA and protein synthesis, haematopoiesis, **CNS myelination**, and mental and psychomotor development.
- **Sources**: almost entirely **animal foods**: organ and muscle meat, shellfish, fish, poultry, egg yolk. Fortified cereals and dairy for vegetarians. Human milk is adequate if maternal B12 is adequate.
- **Absorption**: in the **terminal ileum** bound to **intrinsic factor** (from gastric parietal cells). Enterohepatic circulation, direct absorption and gut bacteria help maintain stores.

### 7.1 Causes of deficiency

- **Inadequate intake**: strict vegetarian or vegan diets; exclusively breastfed infants of B12-deficient mothers.
- **Malabsorption**: coeliac disease, ileal resection, Crohn disease, ***Helicobacter pylori***, **autoimmune atrophic gastritis (pernicious anaemia)**.
- **Drugs**: metformin, proton pump inhibitors, H2 receptor antagonists.
- **Inborn errors**: hereditary intrinsic factor deficiency; **Imerslund-Grasbeck disease**.

### 7.2 Clinical features

- Haematological features same as folate deficiency (megaloblastic anaemia).
- **Infants**: irritability, hypotonia, developmental delay and regression, involuntary movements (**coarse tremors**).
- Older children: poor growth, poor school performance. Adults: sensory deficits, paraesthesia, peripheral neuritis, psychosis (subacute combined degeneration).
- **Hyperpigmentation of the knuckles and palms** is a common finding.
- Maternal B12 deficiency is an independent risk factor for fetal neural tube defects.

### 7.3 Treatment and prevention

- **Haematological response is prompt** to parenteral B12 **250 to 1,000 microgram**.
- Severe deficiency or neurological signs: repeated doses, daily or alternate days in week 1, **weekly for the first 1 to 2 months**, then **monthly**.
- Haematological-only disease recovers in **2 to 3 months**; neurological disease needs **at least 6 months** of treatment.
- Continuing malabsorption or inborn errors need **lifelong** treatment.
- **Prevention**: pregnant and breastfeeding women should eat animal products; vegans take B12 supplements; fortify foods in predominantly vegetarian populations.

## 8. Vitamin C (ascorbic acid)

### 8.1 Functions and sources

- **Collagen synthesis**: hydroxylation of **proline and lysine** in procollagen.
- Neurotransmitter metabolism (dopamine to noradrenaline, tryptophan to serotonin), conversion of cholesterol to steroid hormones and bile acids, **carnitine** synthesis.
- Keeps iron and copper in the **reduced (active)** state in metalloenzymes; a major aqueous **antioxidant**.
- **Enhances non-haem iron absorption**, transfer of iron from transferrin to ferritin, and formation of tetrahydrofolate.
- **Sources**: citrus fruits and juices, peppers, berries, melons, guava, kiwi, tomatoes, cauliflower, green leafy vegetables. Destroyed by prolonged storage, overcooking and processing.
- Absorbed in the upper small intestine (active transport; diffusion at high intakes). **Not stored**, but all tissues take it up; highest in the **pituitary and adrenal glands**.
- Requirements rise in **infections, diarrhoea** and with **tobacco smoke** exposure.
- Breast milk gives enough vitamin C throughout infancy (placental transfer is active). Infants fed **pasteurised or boiled animal milk** without other sources are at high risk, as are neonates with delayed feeding. TPN: **80 mg/day** for term infants, **25 mg/kg/day** for preterm.

### 8.2 Scurvy

- **Pathology**: defective collagen and connective tissue in skin, cartilage, dentine, bone and vessels, making them fragile. In long bones **osteoid is not laid down**, the cortex is thin and trabeculae are brittle.
- **Early**: irritability, anorexia, low-grade fever, limb pain and tenderness.
- Then **swelling of the legs** (knees and ankles) and **pseudoparalysis**; the infant lies with hips and knees semi-flexed and feet rotated outward ("frog leg"). **Subperiosteal haemorrhage** can worsen swelling and pain and mimic **osteomyelitis or arthritis**.
- **Scorbutic rosary** at costochondral junctions and sternal depression.
- **Gums**: bluish-purple, spongy swellings, especially over the upper incisors, only once teeth have erupted.
- **Haemorrhage**: petechiae, purpura, ecchymoses at pressure points, epistaxis, gum bleeding, **perifollicular haemorrhages**.
- Anaemia (impaired iron absorption plus iron, B12 and folate deficiency), poor wound and fracture healing, follicular hyperkeratosis, arthralgia, muscle weakness.
- **Radiology** (not in slides): Wimberger ring sign, Frankel line, Trummerfeld zone, pencil-thin cortex.

### 8.3 Treatment and prevention

- **Treatment**: **vitamin C 100 to 200 mg/day** orally or parenterally. Improvement is usually within **1 week**; continue for **up to 3 months** for full recovery.
- **Prevention**: breastfeeding; fortify formula; supplement children on restrictive diets, severely malnourished children and those with chronic illness (malignancy, neurological disorders). Antenatal vitamin C for smoking mothers may reduce harm to fetal lung development.

> **Exam tip:** a painful, pseudoparalysed infant with bleeding gums and a history of boiled or cow's milk only is scurvy until proven otherwise. Do not confuse with osteomyelitis, rickets or child abuse.

## 9. Hypervitaminosis D

### 9.1 Cause and mechanism

- Excess intake, long-term or a large acute ingestion; usually **misuse of prescribed or over-the-counter supplements**.
- It is **never caused by sunlight** (UV light converts vitamin D3 and precursors to inactive metabolites).
- Vitamin D increases gut calcium absorption, but the main cause of the hypercalcaemia is **excessive bone resorption**.

### 9.2 Clinical features (all due to hypercalcaemia)

| System | Features |
|---|---|
| GI | Nausea, vomiting, poor feeding, constipation, abdominal pain, **pancreatitis** |
| Cardiac | Hypertension, **shortened QT**, arrhythmias |
| CNS | Lethargy, hypotonia, confusion, disorientation, depression, psychosis, hallucinations, coma |
| Renal | Impaired concentrating ability (**nephrogenic diabetes insipidus**): polyuria, dehydration, hypernatraemia; acute kidney injury, **nephrolithiasis**, **nephrocalcinosis**, chronic renal insufficiency |

- Deaths are usually from arrhythmia or dehydration.

### 9.3 Investigations

- **Hypercalcaemia**, **25-hydroxyvitamin D above 100 ng/mL**, **hypercalciuria**, **suppressed PTH**, frequently **hyperphosphataemia**.
- **1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D usually normal.**
- **Renal ultrasound** shows nephrocalcinosis.

### 9.4 Management

1. **Stop the source** of vitamin D; avoid sun exposure (including sunscreen) and **restrict dietary calcium**.
2. **Rehydrate** with aggressive **normal saline**: dilutes calcium, corrects prerenal azotaemia and increases urinary calcium loss (sodium diuresis also increases calcium excretion).
3. Add a **loop diuretic** (furosemide) once volume replete; enough for mild or moderate hypercalcaemia.
4. Significant hypercalcaemia: **glucocorticoids** (block 1,25-D action and reduce intestinal calcium absorption).
5. **Calcitonin** (inhibits bone resorption) as an adjunct.
6. **Bisphosphonates**, IV or oral (inhibit osteoclasts): excellent response.
7. **Haemodialysis** with low or zero calcium dialysate for severe refractory hypercalcaemia.

- **Prognosis**: most children recover fully, but it can be fatal or leave chronic kidney disease. Because vitamin D is stored in fat, levels stay high for months: monitor 25-D, serum calcium and urine calcium regularly.

## 10. Summary table of deficiency syndromes

| Micronutrient | Deficiency syndrome | Key clue |
|---|---|---|
| Vitamin A | Xerophthalmia, night blindness | Bitot spots, keratomalacia |
| Thiamine (B1) | Beriberi, Wernicke | Neuropathy, cardiac failure, lactic acidosis |
| Niacin (B3) | Pellagra | Dermatitis, diarrhoea, dementia |
| Pyridoxine (B6) | Infantile seizures, cheilosis | Isoniazid, seizures that stop with B6 |
| Folate | Megaloblastic anaemia, NTDs | Low RBC folate, pregnancy |
| Cobalamin (B12) | Megaloblastic anaemia, neuropathy | Vegan diet, ileal disease, knuckle pigmentation |
| Vitamin C | Scurvy | Pseudoparalysis, bleeding gums, perifollicular haemorrhage |

## Key points to revise

1. Vitamin A deficiency: night blindness, Bitot spots, xerophthalmia and keratomalacia; treat with high-dose retinol, 100,000 to 200,000 IU in older children.
2. Hypervitaminosis A causes raised ICP, hyperostosis and teratogenicity; carotenoids only cause harmless yellow skin.
3. Thiamine deficiency presents as dry (neuropathic) or wet (cardiac) beriberi; give 10 mg IM or IV daily for week 1, then 3 to 5 mg orally for 6 weeks or more.
4. Pellagra (maize diet, carcinoid, Hartnup) is dermatitis, diarrhoea and dementia; treat with niacin 50 to 300 mg/day.
5. Test pyridoxine 100 mg in infantile seizures; always cover isoniazid with pyridoxine.
6. Folate (0.5 to 1 mg/day) and B12 (250 to 1,000 microgram parenteral) deficiency both give megaloblastic anaemia; B12 also causes neurological damage and needs at least 6 months of treatment when neurology is present.
7. Scurvy: collagen failure with bleeding and subperiosteal haemorrhage; treat with 100 to 200 mg vitamin C daily for up to 3 months.
8. Hypervitaminosis D: hypercalcaemia with 25-D above 100 ng/mL and low PTH; treat with saline, loop diuretic, steroids, calcitonin and bisphosphonates.
