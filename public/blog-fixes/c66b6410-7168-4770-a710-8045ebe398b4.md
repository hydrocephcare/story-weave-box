# Thrombophilia (Hypercoagulable State)

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Haematology**

> **In one line:** thrombophilia is a genetic or acquired shift of the haemostatic balance towards excess platelet aggregation and thrombin generation, causing thrombosis (mainly venous). Think of Virchow's triad (stasis, endothelial injury, hypercoagulability); common inherited causes are factor V Leiden and the prothrombin mutation, the key acquired cause is antiphospholipid syndrome, and venous thromboembolism is treated with heparin overlapped with warfarin or a DOAC.

## What you must know

- Normal haemostasis is a balance: too little clotting causes **bleeding**, too much causes **thrombosis**.
- **Virchow's triad:** stasis, endothelial injury, hypercoagulability.
- **Factor V Leiden** (APC resistance) is the commonest inherited thrombophilia worldwide (about 50% of inherited cases); **prothrombin G20210A** is second.
- **Protein C, protein S and antithrombin** deficiencies are autosomal dominant; protein C and S are **vitamin K dependent** and fall with warfarin.
- **Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS):** thrombosis (venous or arterial) and/or recurrent pregnancy loss, with persistent lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin or anti-beta-2-glycoprotein antibodies; prolonged APTT that does not correct on mixing.
- **DVT/PE diagnosis:** pre-test probability (**Wells score**), **D-dimer** if low probability, **compression ultrasound** if likely; **CTPA** for PE.
- Treatment: **heparin (LMWH or UFH) overlapping warfarin** for at least 4 to 5 days until **INR 2 to 3**, or a **DOAC**; thrombolysis for massive PE; IVC filter as a last resort.

## 1. Normal haemostasis

### Components

1. The **vessel wall** (endothelium)
2. **Platelets**
3. **Plasma proteins** (coagulation and fibrinolytic factors)

### Stages

| Stage | Events |
|---|---|
| **Pre-injury** | Endothelium produces antithrombotic agents; platelets and clotting factors circulate inactive |
| **Fibrin clot formation** | Endothelium is injured, platelets activate, the coagulation cascade is triggered and a fibrin clot forms |
| **Limiting clot formation** | Natural anticoagulants stop propagation of the clot once haemostasis is secure |
| **Fibrinolysis** | Plasminogen is activated by **tissue plasminogen activator (t-PA)** to **plasmin**, which breaks down fibrin into degradation products including **D-dimer**, allowing recanalisation |

### Natural anticoagulants

| Anticoagulant | Action |
|---|---|
| **Antithrombin** | Serine protease inhibitor made in the liver; inactivates thrombin (IIa), Xa and XIa (also IXa and XIIa) |
| **Tissue factor pathway inhibitor (TFPI)** | Binds and inactivates VIIa and Xa |
| **Protein C and protein S** | Thrombin binds **thrombomodulin** on endothelium and activates protein C; activated protein C with its cofactor **protein S** cleaves **Va and VIIIa**. Both are **vitamin K dependent** and depleted by warfarin |

### Antithrombotic functions of the endothelium

| Product | Role |
|---|---|
| **Prostacyclin (PGI2)** | Prevents platelet plug formation |
| **Nitric oxide** | Vasodilation, avoids stasis and lowers thrombus risk |
| **Thrombomodulin** | Binds thrombin and redirects it to activate protein C |
| **Heparan sulphate proteoglycans** | Surface and matrix molecules that bind antithrombin and other proteins |
| **TFPI** | Inhibits tissue factor pathway |
| **PAI-1 (plasminogen activator inhibitor)** | Inhibits urokinase plasminogen activator (the slide lists it among endothelial regulators of fibrinolysis; high PAI-1 is prothrombotic) |

> **Check:** the slide writes "nitrous oxide (NO2)"; the endothelial vasodilator is **nitric oxide (NO)**.

### Coagulation factors

I fibrinogen; II prothrombin; III tissue factor (thromboplastin); IV calcium; V proaccelerin; VII proconvertin; VIII antihaemophilic factor; IX Christmas factor; X Stuart-Prower factor; XI plasma thromboplastin antecedent; XII Hageman factor; XIII fibrin-stabilising factor.

> **Remember:** the **vitamin K dependent** proteins are factors **II, VII, IX, X** and proteins **C, S and Z**. Factor VIII is not vitamin K dependent.

## 2. Definition

**Thrombophilia** (hypercoagulable or prothrombotic state) is an alteration of the balance between blood fluidity and clot formation, from genetic or acquired disorders, that favours excessive platelet aggregation and thrombin generation and so leads to thrombosis. Thrombosis may be venous (commonest), arterial or both.

## 3. Risk factors: Virchow's triad (1856)

| Element | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Stasis** | Immobility, paralysis (e.g. stroke), obesity, post-operative state and casting, heart and respiratory failure. Heparin prophylaxis may be needed for up to 2 months after orthopaedic surgery; DVT risk rises with age, so older patients need prophylaxis most |
| **Endothelial injury** | Trauma, major surgery, central venous catheters (distinguish thrombosis from cellulitis at the catheter site; both may coexist) |
| **Hypercoagulability** | Inherited or acquired thrombophilias. **Pregnancy** is naturally prothrombotic (some factors rise); the **postpartum** period, especially the first 4 to 6 weeks and after caesarean section, is also high risk |

### Classification of venous thrombosis risk factors

| Acquired | Inherited | Mixed / unknown |
|---|---|---|
| Advancing age; prior thrombosis or family history; immobilisation; major surgery (especially orthopaedic: hip and knee replacement, depending on type and duration); **central venous catheter**; **malignancy** (raised tissue factor, immobility, chemotherapy-induced endothelial damage); **oestrogens** (OCP, HRT); **antiphospholipid syndrome**; **myeloproliferative disease** (essential thrombocythaemia, polycythaemia vera); **heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT)**; prolonged air travel | Antithrombin deficiency; protein C deficiency; protein S deficiency; **factor V Leiden** (Arg506Gln); **prothrombin gene mutation** (G20210A); dysfibrinogenaemia (rare) | **Hyperhomocysteinaemia** (congenital or from folate, B6, B12 deficiency); high factor VIII; acquired protein C resistance without factor V Leiden; high factor IX or XI |

- None of the inherited conditions is strongly associated with arterial thrombosis. All are associated with VTE and with pregnancy complications (**early fetal loss**).
- **Oestrogen plus factor V Leiden** multiplies thrombosis risk. All hormonal contraceptives (oestrogen or progestogen) raise risk.
- About half of DVT patients have one or more of these risk factors.

## 4. Inherited thrombophilias

### Factor V Leiden and prothrombin mutation

- With these mutations, factor V (or prothrombin) is **resistant to inactivation** and persists, so clots form excessively.

| Disorder | Key facts |
|---|---|
| **Factor V Leiden (APC resistance)** | Mutation at amino acid 506 (Arg506Gln) of factor V impairs activated protein C's ability to inactivate Va. Commonest inherited thrombophilia globally (about 50% of inherited cases) |
| **Prothrombin G20210A** | Second commonest. G-to-A substitution at position 20210; about **30% higher prothrombin levels**; heterozygotes have a **3 to 6 fold** increased risk of VTE at all ages in both sexes |

### Antithrombin deficiency

- Rare; **autosomal dominant**, equal in both sexes. Antithrombin irreversibly binds thrombin (IIa), IXa, Xa, XIa and XIIa; **heparin increases this 1000-fold** by causing a conformational change.
- Women are at very high risk during pregnancy: DVT in **18%** of deficient patients overall and **33% postpartum**.
- Heparin resistance is a clue.

### Protein C deficiency

- Vitamin K dependent glycoprotein made in the liver; activated by the thrombin-thrombomodulin complex to **activated protein C (APC)**, which degrades Va and VIIIa (protein S is required).
- Thrombosis occurs when levels fall to **50% or below**.
- Acquired deficiency: surgery, trauma, pregnancy, OCP, liver or renal failure, DIC, **warfarin**.
- **Homozygous deficiency:** severe neonatal purpura fulminans, retinal haemorrhage shortly after birth with blindness and generalised microthrombi.
- **Warfarin-induced skin necrosis** suggests protein C deficiency (warfarin first lowers protein C and so is initially prothrombotic).

### Protein S deficiency

- Cofactor of the protein C pathway. **60 to 70%** circulates bound to **C4b-binding protein**; the remaining **free protein S** is the active form.
- Inherited form is autosomal dominant, with thrombosis when levels fall to 50% or lower.
- Acquired causes: raised C4bBP (an acute-phase reactant: inflammation, pregnancy, SLE); reduced synthesis or function (vitamin K deficiency, warfarin, liver disease); increased consumption (acute thrombosis, DIC, myeloproliferative disease, sickle cell disease).
- The slide notes that protein C and S deficiencies are prominent causes of thrombophilia in the Middle East, linked to consanguineous marriage, whereas factor V Leiden predominates globally.

> **Exam tip:** commonest inherited thrombophilia = **factor V Leiden (APC resistance)**.

### Hyperhomocysteinaemia

Congenital or acquired (folate, B6, B12 deficiency); venous and arterial thrombosis; mentioned as low-yield.

## 5. Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS)

- Autoimmune condition with persistently positive **antiphospholipid antibodies**: directed against phospholipid-binding proteins. Includes **lupus anticoagulant (LAC)** and **anticardiolipin (aCL) antibodies** (and anti-beta-2-glycoprotein I). Patients may be positive for one or both.
- Two main clinical features: **thrombosis** and **recurrent pregnancy loss** (placental vein thrombosis leads to placental ischaemia and abortion).
- Can be primary or secondary (e.g. SLE).

### Diagnosis: at least one clinical criterion plus one positive antibody

| Clinical | Laboratory |
|---|---|
| Arterial or venous **thrombosis** | **IgG or IgM anticardiolipin**, medium or high titre (ELISA) |
| **Pregnancy loss** (recurrent abortion) | **Lupus anticoagulant** |
| Thrombocytopenia | Anti-beta-2-glycoprotein I |
| CNS: stroke, chorea | |
| Cardiac valve disease (Libman-Sacks) | |
| **Livedo reticularis** | |

- **Repeat the antibody test after 12 weeks** (the slide says 3 months): the test must be persistently positive, because infections can transiently raise antibodies.
- **Lupus anticoagulant:** the name is misleading: it **prolongs clotting tests in vitro** (it binds phospholipid and limits the surface needed for the prothrombinase complex) but causes **thrombosis** in vivo. It was named after first being found in lupus patients, but most patients do not have lupus.
  - **APTT is usually prolonged and does not correct on 1:1 mixing** with normal plasma.
  - **Dilute Russell viper venom time (dRVVT):** venom activates factor X directly; prolonged by LAC.
  - Prothrombin time is seldom prolonged.
- **Anticardiolipin antibodies:** detected by ELISA using plates coated with cardiolipin and beta-2-glycoprotein.

### Treatment

- Thrombosis: **long-term anticoagulation** (warfarin, target INR around 3 per the slide; commonly 2 to 3 for venous events). The recurrence risk is about **50% at 2 years after stopping**.
- Recurrent fetal loss: **LMWH plus low-dose aspirin** during pregnancy (the slide mentions LMWH and low-dose heparin).
- **Catastrophic APS** (clotting in more than 3 organs): anticoagulation, steroids, plasma exchange ("blood filtering") and IVIG.
- Do not use DOACs in high-risk (triple-positive) APS; warfarin is preferred.

## 6. Clinical manifestations of thrombosis

### Venous

- Commonest: **DVT of the leg** and **pulmonary embolism**. Rarer: jugular, upper-limb, **cerebral sinus**, intra-abdominal (e.g. **Budd-Chiari**, mesenteric thrombosis causing bowel ischaemia).

| DVT | Pulmonary embolism |
|---|---|
| Lower limb, starting in distal veins: pain, swelling, redness, warmth, dilated superficial veins; usually **unilateral**; clot may extend to the IVC. Signs often minimal | Sudden **breathlessness**; sharp **pleuritic** chest pain; palpitations (tachycardia); tachypnoea; sweating and anxiety; haemoptysis or pink frothy sputum; dizziness and syncope |
| Differentials: ruptured **Baker's cyst**, superficial thrombophlebitis, calf muscle tear, chronic venous insufficiency, cellulitis | **PE with systolic BP below 90 mmHg is massive PE** and needs thrombolysis (anticoagulation alone will not clear the clot); simple non-massive PE can be treated as an outpatient |

- **90%** of PE comes from proximal leg veins, so proximal DVT carries the highest morbidity and mortality.
- DVT prophylaxis is not 100% effective.
- A patient who has a PE often has another PE; one with DVT often recurs as DVT.

### Arterial (less common)

Acute MI, angina, stroke, TIA, claudication, limb ischaemia.

## 7. Who to test for thrombophilia

There is no consensus, but testing is more likely if:

- Age under 50 with no identifiable risk factor
- Family history
- Recurrent clots
- Unusual site (portal, hepatic, mesenteric, cerebral veins)
- Unprovoked upper-limb clot (no catheter or surgery)
- **Warfarin-induced skin necrosis** (protein C deficiency)

## 8. Diagnosis of DVT and PE

- Only 17 to 32% of patients with suspected leg DVT actually have it, so **estimate pre-test probability first** using the **Wells score**.
- **Low probability ("DVT unlikely"):** measure **D-dimer**; if normal, DVT is excluded. **Moderate or high probability ("likely")**, or raised D-dimer: **imaging**.
- **D-dimer:** sensitivity and negative predictive value about **99%**, so it is used to exclude VTE only in low-probability patients. It is also raised in trauma, pregnancy, malignancy, infection, post-surgery.

| Test | Role |
|---|---|
| Clinical examination | Palpable cord in calf, ipsilateral oedema and warmth, superficial venous dilatation (non-specific) |
| **Compression ultrasonography** | Most commonly used; a vein that does not compress is diagnostic of clot; clot and flow can be seen; for moderate to high probability |
| Impedance plethysmography | Non-invasive measurement of blood volume change |
| MR venography | Alternative |
| **CT (with contrast), CT pulmonary angiography** | Very useful in PE |
| **V/Q scan** | Useful in pregnancy or small clots |
| Echocardiography | RV strain in PE |
| Contrast venography | Invasive; reference standard |

### Worked case (summary)

A 75-year-old man 4 weeks after hip replacement has a swollen red right leg for 2 weeks with pitting oedema, previous DVT and no alternative diagnosis. Wells score = 3 (major surgery within 12 weeks, pitting oedema in the symptomatic leg, previous DVT): **DVT likely**. Next step: proximal leg vein ultrasound; if it cannot be done within 4 hours, do a D-dimer, give an interim dose of a parenteral anticoagulant and arrange ultrasound within 24 hours. If positive, diagnose DVT and start LMWH or another anticoagulant at once.

> **Exam tip:** recent surgery with prophylaxis does not exclude DVT.

## 9. Management of VTE

### Principles

- Start anticoagulation **during resuscitation**; if suspicion is high, start empirically while waiting for tests, after excluding absolute contraindications (active bleeding).

### Drugs

| Drug | Points |
|---|---|
| **Unfractionated heparin (UFH)** | IV; for unstable patients (poor SC absorption), those who may need procedures, or renal failure. Heparin infusion: 80 units/kg bolus then 18 units/kg/h; target APTT 1.5 to 2.5 times normal. Safe in CKD. Antidote **protamine sulphate** |
| **LMWH** (enoxaparin, tinzaparin, dalteparin) | SC; for stable patients. Enhances antithrombin's inactivation of thrombin and Xa. Enoxaparin **1 mg/kg twice daily or 1.5 mg/kg once daily**. Avoid in severe renal failure (stage 5 CKD). Treatment lasts **at least 5 days** |
| **Warfarin (vitamin K antagonist)** | Inhibits II, VII, IX, X (and C, S). Dose individualised by daily INR. **Target INR 2 to 3** (normal 0.9 to 1.3). Slow onset and initially **prothrombotic** (protein C and S fall first), so **overlap with heparin for about 4 to 5 days** and stop heparin once INR is therapeutic |
| **Fondaparinux** | Factor Xa inhibitor |
| **Hirudins** (lepirudin, bivalirudin) | Direct thrombin inhibitors; used in **HIT**, e.g. in a CKD patient who develops HIT on heparin, then convert to warfarin when stable |
| **DOACs** | Dabigatran (direct thrombin inhibitor); rivaroxaban, apixaban, edoxaban (Xa inhibitors) |

- **Rivaroxaban** has rapid onset and can be used from diagnosis without LMWH.
- Anticoagulants other than UFH and warfarin are generally avoided in advanced CKD.

### DOACs

| Pros | Cons |
|---|---|
| Fixed daily dose; no routine monitoring; quick onset; bleeding risk similar to warfarin overall (more GI bleeding, less intracranial bleeding); lower doses for prophylaxis | More expensive; reversal was a problem (antidotes now exist); cannot be used in end-stage renal failure or with **metallic heart valves** |

### Duration

- Usually **3 to 12 months**; longer or lifelong in **recurrent** VTE. **PE** needs longer therapy because it can be fatal.
- **Cancer:** LMWH. **Pregnancy:** heparin (does not cross the placenta); warfarin is teratogenic.
- **Aspirin is not used for venous thrombosis:** it is an antiplatelet drug, effective in **arterial** thrombosis (ischaemic heart disease, stroke) where platelet activation predominates.
- **Heparin side effect:** thrombocytopenia (HIT); also bleeding and osteoporosis with long-term use.

### Warfarin overdose and INR fluctuation

- Reversal: **vitamin K** (slow, many hours); in active bleeding add **fresh frozen plasma or prothrombin complex concentrate** (better than FFP).
- INR varies with non-compliance, changes in vitamin K intake, interacting drugs, changes in warfarin metabolism, factor synthesis, and laboratory error.

### Thrombolysis, thrombectomy, IVC filter

- **Thrombolysis** (alteplase/t-PA, urokinase): reserved for **massive PE**.
- **Embolectomy** (surgical or catheter): when thrombolysis is needed but contraindicated or fails; surgical thrombectomy for arterial thrombosis.
- **IVC filter (last resort, itself thrombogenic):**
  - Absolute contraindication to anticoagulation (e.g. active bleeding)
  - Recurrent PE despite adequate anticoagulation
  - Severe complication of anticoagulation
  - Poor cardiopulmonary reserve in whom a recurrent PE would be fatal
  - After embolectomy
  - Prophylaxis in selected patients (e.g. malignancy)

## 10. Quick MCQ pearls from the lecture

- Vitamin K dependent: II, VII, IX, X, protein C, S. **Factor VIII is not.**
- Pregnancy, obesity and jaundice are not contraindications to LMWH; **dialysis-dependent renal failure** is.
- Commonest inherited prothrombotic disorder: **activated protein C resistance (factor V Leiden)**.
- Isolated prolonged **PT** with normal APTT in a young man with recurrent haematomas: **factor VII deficiency**. Prolonged APTT with normal PT that corrects on mixing: haemophilia (low factor VIII or IX activity). An aPTT that does **not** correct on mixing suggests an inhibitor (acquired factor VIII inhibitor or lupus anticoagulant).

## Key points to revise

1. Virchow's triad: stasis, endothelial injury, hypercoagulability.
2. Factor V Leiden (APC resistance) is the commonest inherited thrombophilia, then prothrombin G20210A; protein C, S and antithrombin deficiencies are autosomal dominant.
3. Protein C and S are vitamin K dependent; warfarin lowers them first, so heparin cover is mandatory and skin necrosis can occur.
4. APS: thrombosis and recurrent miscarriage; lupus anticoagulant (prolonged APTT, no correction on mixing, dRVVT) and anticardiolipin antibodies repeated at 12 weeks; treat with long-term warfarin and, in pregnancy, LMWH plus aspirin.
5. Wells score, then D-dimer (if low probability) or compression ultrasound; CTPA or V/Q for PE.
6. Treat with heparin plus warfarin overlap (INR 2 to 3, at least 4 to 5 days) or DOAC; UFH if unstable or renal failure; thrombolysis for massive PE.
7. Aspirin is for arterial, not venous, thrombosis; heparin in pregnancy and LMWH in cancer.
8. IVC filter only when anticoagulation is contraindicated or fails.
