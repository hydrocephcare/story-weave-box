## Summary
These comprehensive notes cover MBChB-level cardiovascular pathology, focusing on common diseases affecting the heart and blood vessels. Topics include atherosclerosis, ischemic heart disease, valvular disorders, cardiomyopathies, and congenital anomalies, providing detailed information essential for examination preparation. This document outlines definitions, risk factors, pathogenesis, morphology, clinical manifestations, and complications for a thorough understanding of each condition.

## Key Points
- Atherosclerosis is a chronic inflammatory disease of arteries, driven by endothelial injury and lipid accumulation, leading to the formation of fibrofatty plaques and significant cardiovascular complications.
- Ischemic heart disease, primarily caused by coronary atherosclerosis, manifests as angina or myocardial infarction, with distinct pathological changes over time and various life-threatening complications.
- Valvular heart diseases, such as those arising from rheumatic fever or degenerative calcification, involve stenosis or regurgitation, profoundly affecting cardiac function and often leading to heart failure or emboli.
- Cardiomyopathies are primary myocardial diseases, classified into dilated, hypertrophic, and restrictive types, each presenting with unique structural and functional abnormalities that can result in heart failure, arrhythmias, or sudden death.
- Congenital heart defects range from simple shunts to complex structural anomalies, impacting blood flow and oxygenation, necessitating early diagnosis and often surgical intervention to prevent long-term complications like Eisenmenger syndrome.

## Detailed Notes
### TOPIC 1: ATHEROSCLEROSIS & ARTERIOSCLEROSIS

### Definitions
- **Arteriosclerosis** – General term for hardening and loss of elasticity of arterial walls
- **Atherosclerosis** – Most important form; a chronic inflammatory disease of large and medium arteries characterized by formation of **atheromatous plaques** in the intima

### Risk Factors
**Non-modifiable:**
- Age (men >45, women >55)
- Male sex
- Family history/genetics
- Race

**Modifiable:**
- **Hypertension**
- **Hyperlipidemia** (high LDL, low HDL)
- **Diabetes mellitus**
- **Cigarette smoking**
- Obesity
- Sedentary lifestyle
- Homocysteinemia

### Pathogenesis — Response to Injury Hypothesis
1.  **Endothelial injury/dysfunction** caused by: hypertension, smoking, hyperlipidemia, toxins
2.  Increased **permeability** → LDL enters intima → oxidized to **ox-LDL**
3.  Endothelium expresses **adhesion molecules** (VCAM-1, ICAM-1)
4.  **Monocytes** adhere and migrate into intima → become **macrophages**
5.  Macrophages engulf ox-LDL → become **foam cells** → **fatty streak** (earliest lesion, reversible)
6.  Macrophages release **cytokines and growth factors** → stimulate smooth muscle cell (SMC) migration from media to intima
7.  SMCs proliferate and produce **extracellular matrix** (collagen, proteoglycans)
8.  Formation of **fibrofatty plaque (atheromatous plaque)**

### Morphology of an Atheromatous Plaque
-   **Fibrous cap** – SMCs and dense collagen (overlying the lesion)
-   **Necrotic core** – lipid debris, foam cells, cholesterol crystals, dead cells
-   **Shoulder region** – active inflammation (macrophages, T cells)
-   **Neovascularization** at base

### Vulnerable (Unstable) Plaque
-   Thin fibrous cap
-   Large lipid core
-   Many inflammatory cells
-   Prone to **rupture** → thrombosis → acute coronary syndrome

### Progression of Atherosclerosis
1.  Normal artery
2.  Endothelial dysfunction
3.  **Fatty streak** – flat yellow intimal lesion; foam cells; reversible
4.  **Fibrofatty plaque** – raised yellow-white lesion; fibrous cap + lipid core
5.  **Complicated plaque** – calcification, ulceration, thrombosis, hemorrhage

### Complications of Atherosclerosis
-   **Myocardial infarction** (coronary arteries)
-   **Stroke** (cerebral/carotid arteries)
-   **Peripheral arterial disease** (lower limbs)
-   **Aortic aneurysm** (aorta)
-   **Mesenteric ischemia** (mesenteric arteries)
-   **Renal artery stenosis** → renovascular hypertension

### Types of Arteriosclerosis
1.  **Hyaline arteriolosclerosis** – Homogeneous pink hyaline thickening of arteriolar walls; seen in benign hypertension and diabetes mellitus
2.  **Hyperplastic arteriolosclerosis** – "Onion-skin" concentric laminated thickening; seen in malignant (severe) hypertension; causes marked luminal narrowing
3.  **Monckeberg medial calcific sclerosis** – Calcification of the media of muscular arteries; does NOT narrow lumen; not clinically significant usually; seen in elderly

### TOPIC 2: ISCHEMIC HEART DISEASE (IHD)

### Definition
A group of conditions resulting from **imbalance between myocardial oxygen supply and demand**, most commonly due to coronary atherosclerosis.

### Causes
-   **Coronary atherosclerosis** (>90% of cases)
-   Coronary vasospasm (**Prinzmetal angina**)
-   Thromboembolism
-   Coronary artery anomalies
-   Severe anemia
-   Aortic stenosis (reduced coronary perfusion)

### Types of IHD

**1. Stable (Chronic) Angina**
-   Fixed atherosclerotic stenosis (>70% occlusion)
-   Predictable chest pain on exertion, relieved by rest or nitrates
-   No myocardial necrosis
-   Caused by demand exceeding supply

**2. Unstable Angina**
-   Part of **Acute Coronary Syndrome (ACS)**
-   Plaque rupture + partial thrombosis
-   Chest pain at rest or minimal exertion
-   Increasing frequency and severity
-   No myocardial necrosis (**troponin negative** usually)

**3. Myocardial Infarction (MI)**
-   Part of ACS
-   **Irreversible** ischemic necrosis of myocardium
-   Complete occlusion of coronary artery
-   **Troponin positive**

**4. Sudden Cardiac Death**
-   Death within 1 hour of symptom onset
-   Usually due to fatal arrhythmia (**ventricular fibrillation**)
-   Most common cause: severe coronary atherosclerosis
-   May be first manifestation of IHD

### Myocardial Infarction — In Detail

#### Types by Depth
-   **STEMI (ST-elevation MI)** — **Transmural infarct**; full thickness of myocardial wall; complete occlusion
-   **NSTEMI (Non-ST-elevation MI)** — **Subendocardial infarct**; inner 1/3 of wall; partial occlusion

#### Most Commonly Affected Vessel
-   **Left anterior descending (LAD)** artery — most common (~50%); anterior wall + interventricular septum
-   **Right coronary artery (RCA)** — posterior/inferior wall
-   **Left circumflex artery** — lateral wall

#### Pathological Timeline of MI (MUST KNOW)

| Time | Gross Appearance | Microscopic Changes |
|---|---|---|
| 0–6 hours | Normal (invisible) | Wavy myofibers, early coagulative necrosis |
| 6–24 hours | Pale/pallor | Coagulative necrosis; pyknotic nuclei; wavy fibers |
| 1–3 days | Yellow-tan softening | **Neutrophil infiltration** (peak at 24–72h) |
| 3–7 days | Hyperemic border, yellow-soft center | **Macrophage infiltration**; removal of necrotic debris; **highest risk of rupture** |
| 1–2 weeks | Depressed, red-gray | **Granulation tissue** formation; new capillaries; fibroblasts |
| Weeks–months | Gray-white scar | **Dense fibrous scar** (collagen replacement) |

#### Complications of MI (MUST KNOW)
-   **Arrhythmias** — Most common cause of death in first 24 hours; ventricular fibrillation
-   **Left ventricular failure** → pulmonary edema
-   **Cardiogenic shock** — large infarct (>40% LV)
-   **Myocardial rupture** — Day 3–7; cardiac tamponade; free wall most common
-   **Papillary muscle rupture** → acute mitral regurgitation
-   **Interventricular septal rupture** → left-to-right shunt
-   **Ventricular aneurysm** — bulging thin fibrotic wall; late complication; risk of thrombus
-   **Pericarditis** — early (fibrinous, days 2–3) or late (**Dressler syndrome**, weeks later — autoimmune)
-   **Mural thrombus** → systemic emboli → stroke
-   **Reinfarction**

#### Biochemical Markers of MI
-   **Troponin I & T** — most sensitive and specific; rises 3–6 hours, peaks 12–24h, stays elevated 7–10 days
-   **CK-MB** — rises 4–8 hours, peaks 24h, returns to normal by 72h; used to detect reinfarction
-   **LDH** — late marker; rises 24–48h, peaks 3–6 days
-   **Myoglobin** — earliest marker (1–4h) but not specific

### TOPIC 3: HYPERTENSIVE HEART DISEASE

### Definition
Cardiac and vascular changes resulting from sustained **systemic arterial hypertension** (BP >140/90 mmHg).

### Systemic Hypertensive Heart Disease
**Pathological changes in the heart:**
-   **Left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH)** — concentric hypertrophy; increased wall thickness; normal cavity size initially
-   LVH → increased oxygen demand → ischemia → diastolic dysfunction
-   Eventually → **left ventricular dilation** → heart failure
-   Increased risk of **sudden death, MI, arrhythmias**

**Microscopic changes:**
-   Enlarged cardiomyocytes with large nuclei ("**boxcar nuclei**")
-   Interstitial fibrosis

### Pulmonary Hypertensive Heart Disease (Cor Pulmonale)
-   **Right ventricular hypertrophy** due to pulmonary hypertension
-   Causes: chronic lung disease (COPD), pulmonary emboli, pulmonary fibrosis

### Effects of Hypertension on Other Organs

| Organ | Effect |
|---|---|
| Heart | LVH → heart failure, IHD |
| Kidney | Nephrosclerosis → renal failure |
| Brain | Stroke (hemorrhagic or ischemic), hypertensive encephalopathy |
| Retina | Hypertensive retinopathy |
| Aorta | Aortic dissection, aneurysm |
| Blood vessels | Arteriolosclerosis |

### Benign vs Malignant Hypertension

| Feature | Benign Hypertension | Malignant Hypertension |
|---|---|---|
| BP | Gradually elevated | Very high (>180/120) |
| Onset | Slow, insidious | Rapid |
| Vascular change | Hyaline arteriolosclerosis | Hyperplastic arteriolosclerosis |
| Kidney | Gradual nephrosclerosis | Fibrinoid necrosis of arterioles |
| Prognosis | Managed with treatment | Medical emergency |

### TOPIC 4: VALVULAR HEART DISEASE

### Overview of Heart Valves
Any valve can be affected, but the **left-sided valves** (mitral and aortic) are most commonly involved due to higher pressures.

### Types of Valvular Lesions
-   **Stenosis** — failure of valve to open fully → obstruction to forward flow
-   **Regurgitation/Insufficiency** — failure of valve to close fully → backward flow

### Rheumatic Heart Disease (RHD) — MOST IMPORTANT

#### Pathogenesis
-   Follows **Group A β-hemolytic Streptococcal (GAS) pharyngitis** (2–4 weeks later)
-   **Molecular mimicry** — streptococcal M protein antigens mimic cardiac proteins (myosin, tropomyosin)
-   Autoimmune attack on heart valves, myocardium, pericardium
-   **Cross-reactive antibodies** damage cardiac tissues

#### Acute Rheumatic Fever (ARF) — Jones Criteria
**Major criteria:**
-   **Carditis** (pancarditis — affects all layers)
-   Migratory polyarthritis
-   Sydenham's chorea
-   Erythema marginatum
-   Subcutaneous nodules

**Minor criteria:**
-   Fever, elevated ESR/CRP, prolonged PR interval

Diagnosis: **2 major OR 1 major + 2 minor** + evidence of preceding streptococcal infection

#### Pathology of Acute Rheumatic Carditis
-   **Pancarditis** (endocarditis + myocarditis + pericarditis)
-   **Aschoff bodies** — pathognomonic lesion; granulomatous foci in myocardium; contain **Aschoff giant cells** (multinucleate) and **Anitschkow cells** (caterpillar cells — pathognomonic macrophages with distinctive chromatin)
-   **Fibrinous pericarditis** — "**bread and butter**" pericarditis
-   **Verrucous endocarditis** — small, warty vegetations along line of valve closure (on atrial surface of mitral, ventricular surface of aortic)
-   **MacCallum's patch** — irregular thickening on posterior left atrial wall

#### Chronic Rheumatic Heart Disease
-   Repeated attacks → progressive fibrosis and scarring of valves
-   **Most commonly affected: Mitral valve** (>50%), then aortic, then tricuspid (rarely pulmonary)
-   **Mitral stenosis** — fusion of commissures, thickening, calcification → "**fish mouth**" or "**button-hole**" deformity
-   Consequences:
    -   Left atrial enlargement → atrial fibrillation → mural thrombus → emboli → stroke
    -   Pulmonary hypertension → right heart failure
    -   Infective endocarditis superimposed

### Calcific Aortic Stenosis
-   Most common valvular disease in developed countries
-   **Degenerative calcification** of aortic valve
-   Seen in elderly (tricuspid valve) or younger patients with congenital bicuspid aortic valve
-   Symptoms: **Angina, Syncope, Heart failure** (SAD triad)
-   Can lead to sudden death

### Mitral Valve Prolapse (MVP)
-   Billowing of mitral leaflets into left atrium during systole
-   "**Floppy mitral valve**"
-   Usually benign; associated with **Marfan syndrome**
-   Complications: mitral regurgitation, infective endocarditis, arrhythmias, rarely sudden death

### TOPIC 5: INFECTIVE ENDOCARDITIS (IE)

### Definition
Microbial infection of the **endocardial surface of the heart**, most commonly affecting the **valves**.

### Types

| Feature | Acute IE | Subacute IE |
|---|---|---|
| Organism | Highly virulent (S. aureus) | Low virulence (S. viridans) |
| Valve | Previously normal | Previously damaged |
| Onset | Rapid, fulminant | Insidious, weeks-months |
| Destruction | Severe, rapid | Slower |
| Emboli | Septic emboli common | Less common |

### Risk Factors / Predisposing Conditions
-   Congenital heart disease
-   Rheumatic heart disease (damaged valves)
-   Prosthetic heart valves
-   IV drug use (right-sided IE — **tricuspid valve**; **S. aureus**)
-   Immunocompromised states
-   Poor dental hygiene (bacteremia)

### Common Organisms
-   **S. viridans** — most common overall; subacute; after dental procedures
-   **S. aureus** — most common in IV drug users and acute IE; most virulent
-   **Streptococcus bovis** — associated with **colorectal carcinoma**
-   **HACEK organisms** — Haemophilus, Aggregatibacter, Cardiobacterium, Eikenella, Kingella; culture-negative
-   **Candida** — in immunocompromised
-   **Culture-negative** — Coxiella burnetii (Q fever), Brucella

### Pathology
-   **Vegetations** — irregular, bulky, destructive masses on valves; contain: fibrin, platelets, inflammatory cells, microorganisms
-   Valve destruction → regurgitation
-   Ring abscess (perivalvular abscess)
-   Septic emboli → metastatic abscesses

### Classic Clinical Signs (Peripheral Manifestations)
-   **Janeway lesions** — painless hemorrhagic macules on palms/soles (septic emboli)
-   **Osler nodes** — painful nodules on fingers/toes (immune complex deposition)
-   **Splinter hemorrhages** — linear dark lines under nails
-   **Roth spots** — retinal hemorrhages with white center
-   **Clubbing** — chronic IE
-   **Fever, new murmur, emboli** — classic triad

### Complications
-   Valve destruction → heart failure
-   Septic emboli → stroke, renal infarction, splenic infarction
-   **Mycotic aneurysms**
-   Glomerulonephritis (immune complex deposition)
-   Metastatic abscesses

### Non-Bacterial Thrombotic Endocarditis (NBTE)
-   Also called **marantic endocarditis**
-   Small, sterile, non-destructive vegetations along valve closure line
-   Seen in debilitated patients (cancer, sepsis, SLE)
-   Vegetations can embolize

### Libman-Sacks Endocarditis
-   Associated with **Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE)**
-   Small, sterile vegetations on both surfaces of mitral valve (atrial AND ventricular)
-   Associated with antiphospholipid syndrome

### TOPIC 6: CARDIOMYOPATHIES

### Definition
Diseases of the **myocardium (heart muscle)** that result in cardiac dysfunction, **NOT** due to coronary disease, hypertension, valvular disease or congenital defects.

### Types — MUST KNOW

#### 1. Dilated Cardiomyopathy (DCM) — Most Common (~90%)
-   **Dilation of all four chambers** (predominantly ventricles)
-   **Systolic dysfunction** (reduced ejection fraction)
-   Heart is large, flabby, heavy
-   **Causes:**
    -   **Idiopathic** (most common, ~50%)
    -   Genetic (mutations in cytoskeletal proteins — titin, dystrophin)
    -   Alcohol (alcoholic cardiomyopathy)
    -   Viral myocarditis (Coxsackievirus B)
    -   Peripartum cardiomyopathy
    -   Drugs: doxorubicin (adriamycin), cyclophosphamide
    -   Hemochromatosis, thyroid disease
-   **Microscopy:** myocyte hypertrophy, interstitial fibrosis, non-specific changes
-   **Complications:** heart failure, arrhythmias, mural thrombi → emboli, sudden death

#### 2. Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy (HCM)
-   **Massive hypertrophy** of left ventricle especially **asymmetric septal hypertrophy**
-   Small or normal cavity size; **diastolic dysfunction** (stiff ventricle, impaired filling)
-   **Most common cause of sudden cardiac death in young athletes**
-   **Cause:** Autosomal dominant mutations in **sarcomere proteins** (β-myosin heavy chain, troponin T, troponin I, myosin binding protein C)
-   **Pathology:**
    -   Asymmetric septal hypertrophy
    -   **Myofiber disarray** — pathognomonic; haphazard arrangement of myocytes
    -   Interstitial fibrosis
    -   Thickened small intramyocardial coronary arteries
-   **Dynamic outflow obstruction** — hypertrophied septum + anterior movement of mitral valve during systole (**SAM** — systolic anterior motion) → obstructs LVOT
-   **Clinical:** exertional dyspnea, syncope, angina, palpitations, sudden death
-   **Complications:** arrhythmias, sudden death, heart failure, infective endocarditis

#### 3. Restrictive Cardiomyopathy (RCM) — Least Common
-   **Stiff, non-compliant ventricles** → impaired diastolic filling (restrictive physiology)
-   Normal or near-normal systolic function
-   Normal wall thickness, normal or small cavity
-   **Causes:**
    -   **Amyloidosis** (most common secondary cause)
    -   Sarcoidosis
    -   Hemochromatosis
    -   Endomyocardial fibrosis (tropical countries; eosinophilia)
    -   Löffler endocarditis (hypereosinophilic syndrome)
    -   Radiation fibrosis
-   **Microscopy:** depends on cause; amyloid — **Congo red stain** (apple-green birefringence)
-   **Complications:** heart failure (predominantly right-sided), arrhythmias

### Summary Table — Cardiomyopathies

| Feature | Dilated | Hypertrophic | Restrictive |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chamber size | Enlarged (all) | Small/normal | Normal/small |
| Wall thickness | Normal/thin | Markedly thick | Variable |
| Systolic function | Reduced | Normal/increased | Normal |
| Diastolic function | Reduced | Markedly reduced | Markedly reduced |
| Key feature | Dilation, flabby | Myofiber disarray | Stiff walls |
| Sudden death risk | Moderate | HIGH (young athletes) | Low-moderate |

### TOPIC 7: PERICARDIAL DISEASES

### Types of Pericarditis

#### 1. Serous Pericarditis
-   Small effusion of serous fluid
-   Causes: viral infections, SLE, rheumatic fever, uremia (early)
-   Usually mild, self-limiting

#### 2. Fibrinous Pericarditis — Most Common Type
-   Deposition of fibrin on pericardial surfaces
-   "**Bread and butter**" pericarditis — shaggy fibrinous exudate
-   Causes: **MI** (**Dressler syndrome**), uremia, rheumatic fever, viral, SLE, cardiac surgery
-   Clinical: **pericardial friction rub** on auscultation
-   Resolves or organizes → adhesive/constrictive pericarditis

#### 3. Purulent (Suppurative) Pericarditis
-   Pus in pericardial space
-   Causes: bacterial infection (S. aureus, Streptococcus, gram-negatives), direct spread from pneumonia/empyema
-   Serious; can cause cardiac tamponade

#### 4. Hemorrhagic Pericarditis
-   Blood-tinged fluid
-   Causes: TB, malignancy (metastatic), trauma, anticoagulation

#### 5. Constrictive Pericarditis
-   **Fibrotic thickening** and scarring of pericardium → restricts cardiac filling
-   Heart encased in dense fibrous shell
-   Causes: **TB** (most common globally), previous viral/bacterial pericarditis, cardiac surgery, radiation
-   Mimics restrictive cardiomyopathy
-   **Distinction from RCM:** pericardium thickened on imaging/CT; calcification may be visible; treated by pericardiectomy

### Pericardial Effusion
-   Accumulation of fluid in pericardial sac
-   Types: transudates (heart failure), exudates (infection), blood (hemopericardium), chyle (lymph)
-   Normal pericardial fluid: 15–50 mL
-   Large effusion → **cardiac tamponade**

### Cardiac Tamponade
-   **Compression of heart** by pericardial fluid → impaired filling → reduced cardiac output → shock
-   **Beck's Triad:**
    1.  Hypotension
    2.  Muffled/distant heart sounds
    3.  Raised JVP

### TOPIC 8: CONGENITAL HEART DISEASE (CHD)

### Classification
-   **Cyanotic** (right-to-left shunt)
-   **Acyanotic** (left-to-right shunt or obstruction)

### Acyanotic CHD (Left-to-Right Shunts)

**1. Ventricular Septal Defect (VSD)**
-   Most common CHD overall
-   Abnormal opening between ventricles
-   Blood shunts from left ventricle to right ventricle
-   **Small VSDs:** often asymptomatic, may close spontaneously
-   **Large VSDs:** pulmonary hypertension, right ventricular hypertrophy, Eisenmenger syndrome
-   **Holosystolic murmur** at lower left sternal border

**2. Atrial Septal Defect (ASD)**
-   Opening between atria
-   **Ostium secundum** type most common (90%)
-   Blood shunts from left atrium to right atrium
-   Often asymptomatic until adulthood
-   **Fixed wide splitting of S2**
-   Complications: pulmonary hypertension, right heart failure, paradoxical emboli (if right-to-left shunt develops)

**3. Patent Ductus Arteriosus (PDA)**
-   Failure of ductus arteriosus to close after birth
-   Connects aorta to pulmonary artery
-   Blood shunts from aorta to pulmonary artery
-   **"Machinery-like" continuous murmur**
-   Associated with **maternal rubella**
-   Treatment: **Indomethacin** (prostaglandin inhibitor) to close, or surgical ligation

**4. Coarctation of Aorta**
-   Narrowing of aorta, usually **juxtaductal** (distal to left subclavian artery)
-   **Preductal:** symptomatic early, dependent on PDA (cyanosis lower limbs)
-   **Postductal:** asymptomatic until later, **hypertension in upper extremities**, hypotension in lower extremities, **delayed/diminished femoral pulses**
-   Associated with: **Turner syndrome**, bicuspid aortic valve
-   **"Notching" of ribs** on CXR (due to dilated intercostal arteries)

### Cyanotic CHD (Right-to-Left Shunts)

**1. Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF)** — Most common cyanotic CHD
-   Four components:
    1.  **VSD**
    2.  **Pulmonary stenosis** (subvalvular)
    3.  **Overriding aorta** (over VSD)
    4.  **Right ventricular hypertrophy**
-   Severity depends on pulmonary stenosis: more stenosis → more right-to-left shunt → more cyanosis
-   **"Boot-shaped" heart** on CXR (due to RVH)
-   **Tet spells:** episodes of acute severe cyanosis
-   Treatment: surgical repair

**2. Transposition of Great Arteries (TGA)**
-   Aorta arises from right ventricle, pulmonary artery from left ventricle
-   **"Egg on a string"** appearance on CXR
-   Two separate circulations; incompatible with life unless a shunt exists (**VSD, ASD, PDA**)
-   Treatment: **balloon atrial septostomy (Rashkind procedure)** + surgical repair (arterial switch operation)

**3. Truncus Arteriosus**
-   Single arterial trunk (instead of separate aorta and pulmonary artery) arises from both ventricles
-   Always with VSD
-   Associated with **DiGeorge syndrome** (22q11 deletion)
-   Mixing of oxygenated and deoxygenated blood → cyanosis

### Eisenmenger Syndrome
-   Originally left-to-right shunt → **chronic pulmonary overcirculation** → pulmonary hypertension → pulmonary vascular resistance exceeds systemic → **reversal to right-to-left shunt** → **late cyanosis**
-   Occurs in: large VSD, ASD, PDA if uncorrected
-   Once Eisenmenger develops: surgical repair is contraindicated (would remove only relief for right heart)
-   Only treatment: lung transplantation

### TOPIC 9: DISEASES OF BLOOD VESSELS

### Aneurysms

#### Definition
Abnormal localized **dilation of a blood vessel** (>50% normal diameter) due to weakness in the vessel wall.

#### Types by Shape
-   **Saccular** — outpouching on one side; spherical; more prone to rupture
-   **Fusiform** — symmetric dilation of entire circumference of vessel

#### Types by Cause/Location

**1. Aortic Aneurysm**
-   **Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA):**
    -   Most common (>90%); **infrarenal aorta** most common site
    -   Cause: atherosclerosis (weakens media); also aging, hypertension, smoking
    -   >5.5 cm → surgical repair (risk of rupture)
    -   **Rupture** → sudden severe back/abdominal pain → hemorrhagic shock → death
    -   Pulsatile abdominal mass
-   **Thoracic aortic aneurysm:**
    -   Causes: **syphilis** (tertiary — obliterative endarteritis of vasa vasorum → ischemia of media), **Marfan syndrome** (cystic medial degeneration), atherosclerosis, hypertension
    -   **Syphilitic aneurysm:** typically ascending aorta; "**tree bark**" intima; aortic regurgitation; coronary ostial stenosis

**2. Aortic Dissection**
-   Tear in **tunica intima** → blood enters and tracks along media → creates false lumen
-   NOT an aneurysm but related
-   **Causes:** Hypertension (most important), Marfan syndrome (cystic medial degeneration), Bicuspid aortic valve, Pregnancy
-   **Types (DeBakey / Stanford classification):**
    -   **Type A (Stanford):** involves ascending aorta — surgical emergency
    -   **Type B (Stanford):** involves descending aorta — managed medically initially
-   **Presentation:** Sudden severe "**tearing/ripping**" chest pain radiating to back; unequal pulses; BP difference between arms
-   **Complications:** aortic rupture (hemopericardium → tamponade), aortic regurgitation, MI (coronary ostial occlusion), stroke, mesenteric/renal ischemia

**3. Berry (Saccular) Aneurysm**
-   At bifurcations of **Circle of Willis**
-   Most common site: **junction of anterior communicating artery and anterior cerebral artery**
-   Associated with: **ADPKD**, connective tissue disorders, coarctation, hypertension
-   **Rupture** → **subarachnoid hemorrhage** (sudden severe "**thunderclap headache**" — "worst headache of my life")

### Vasculitis
Inflammation of blood vessel walls → ischemia, infarction of supplied tissues.

#### Classification by Vessel Size

**Large Vessel Vasculitis:**

1.  **Giant Cell (Temporal) Arteritis — Most Common Vasculitis in Adults >50**
    -   **Granulomatous inflammation** of large/medium arteries, especially **temporal artery**
    -   Presents: headache, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication, **visual loss** (ophthalmic artery → blindness)
    -   Elevated ESR
    -   Diagnosis: **temporal artery biopsy** — granulomas, giant cells, fragmented internal elastic lamina
    -   Associated with **polymyalgia rheumatica**
    -   Treatment: **high-dose corticosteroids** (urgent — to prevent blindness)
2.  **Takayasu Arteritis ("Pulseless Disease")**
    -   Granulomatous inflammation of **aorta and its major branches**
    -   Affects young women (<40) in Asia
    -   **Absent pulses**, limb claudication, hypertension (renal artery involvement)
    -   Also called "aortic arch syndrome"

**Medium Vessel Vasculitis:**

3.  **Polyarteritis Nodosa (PAN)**
    -   **Necrotizing inflammation** of **medium muscular arteries**
    -   Spares lungs
    -   Associated with **Hepatitis B** (30%)
    -   Affects: kidney, heart, liver, peripheral nerves, GI
    -   **NOT associated with ANCA**
    -   Features: fever, weight loss, hypertension, renal failure, mononeuritis multiplex
    -   Biopsy: segmental fibrinoid necrosis, neutrophils, aneurysmal nodules
4.  **Kawasaki Disease**
    -   Children <5 years; Asian > others
    -   Fever >5 days + 4 of: conjunctival injection, oral changes (**strawberry tongue**), rash, lymphadenopathy, hand/foot changes
    -   **Coronary artery aneurysms** — most serious complication
    -   Treatment: **aspirin + IV immunoglobulin (IVIG)**

**Small Vessel Vasculitis:**

5.  **Granulomatosis with Polyangiitis (GPA/Wegener)**
    -   Granulomatous necrotizing vasculitis
    -   Classic triad: **Upper respiratory tract** (sinusitis, nosebleeds), **Lower respiratory tract** (lung nodules, cavities), **Kidneys** (glomerulonephritis)
    -   **c-ANCA (PR3-ANCA)** positive
    -   **Saddle-nose deformity** (nasal septum destruction)
6.  **Microscopic Polyangiitis**
    -   Necrotizing vasculitis of small vessels
    -   **p-ANCA (MPO-ANCA)** positive
    -   Involves lungs and kidneys (pulmonary-renal syndrome)
7.  **Eosinophilic Granulomatosis with Polyangiitis (EGPA/Churg-Strauss)**
    -   Granulomatous vasculitis + **asthma + eosinophilia**
    -   **p-ANCA** positive
    -   Involves heart, lungs, nerves, skin
8.  **Henoch-Schönlein Purpura (IgA Vasculitis)**
    -   Most common vasculitis in **children**
    -   **IgA immune complex deposition** in small vessels
    -   Tetrad: palpable purpura (buttocks, legs), arthralgia, abdominal pain, renal disease (IgA nephropathy)
    -   Often follows upper respiratory infection

### TOPIC 10: HEART FAILURE

### Definition
A clinical syndrome in which the heart is **unable to pump sufficient blood** to meet the body's metabolic demands, or can do so only at elevated filling pressures.

### Types

#### Left-Sided Heart Failure
**Causes:** IHD (MI), hypertension, aortic/mitral valve disease, dilated cardiomyopathy

**Pathophysiology:**
-   Left ventricle fails → reduced cardiac output → blood backs up into pulmonary circulation
-   Increased pulmonary venous pressure → **pulmonary edema**
-   Reduced perfusion → compensatory mechanisms: sympathetic activation, RAAS activation, ADH

**Effects on lung:**
-   Pulmonary congestion and edema
-   **Hemosiderin-laden macrophages** ("**heart failure cells**") in alveoli — from RBC breakdown
-   "**Brown induration**" of lung — hemosiderin + fibrosis

**Symptoms:**
-   Dyspnea, orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea (PND)
-   Cough, pink frothy sputum (pulmonary edema)
-   Fatigue, reduced exercise tolerance
-   Basal lung crepitations

#### Right-Sided Heart Failure
**Causes:** Left-sided heart failure (most common cause), cor pulmonale (COPD, pulmonary hypertension), tricuspid/pulmonary valve disease, RV infarction

**Effects:**
-   Blood backs up into **systemic venous circulation**
-   **Raised JVP**
-   **Hepatomegaly** → cardiac cirrhosis (**nutmeg liver** — centrilobular congestion)
-   **Splenomegaly**
-   **Peripheral edema** (ankles, sacral)
-   Ascites
-   Pleural effusion

#### Congestive Heart Failure (CHF)
-   Combined left and right heart failure
-   Features of both

### Compensatory Mechanisms in Heart Failure
1.  **Frank-Starling mechanism** — increased preload → increased contractility (up to a point)
2.  **Cardiac hypertrophy** — increased wall thickness to cope with increased load
3.  **Sympathetic activation** — increased heart rate and contractility; vasoconstriction
4.  **RAAS activation** — aldosterone → sodium and water retention → increased preload
5.  **ADH (vasopressin)** — water retention

These mechanisms are initially compensatory but become maladaptive in chronic failure → cardiac remodeling → worsening failure.

### Systolic vs Diastolic Heart Failure

| Feature | Systolic (HFrEF) | Diastolic (HFpEF) |
|---|---|---|
| Ejection fraction | Reduced (<40%) | Preserved (>50%) |
| Ventricular size | Dilated | Normal/small |
| Problem | Impaired contraction | Impaired relaxation/filling |
| Common cause | DCM, IHD | HCM, hypertension, RCM |

## Practice Questions

1.  What is the earliest, most sensitive, and specific biochemical marker for myocardial infarction? → Troponin I & T.
2.  Which type of cardiomyopathy is the most common cause of sudden cardiac death in young athletes? → Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy (HCM).
3.  Name the three major components of Beck's Triad, indicative of cardiac tamponade. → Hypotension, muffled/distant heart sounds, and raised JVP.
4.  A patient presents with a "thunderclap headache" and is diagnosed with a subarachnoid hemorrhage. Which type of aneurysm is most likely implicated? → Berry (saccular) aneurysm.
5.  What is the characteristic pathological lesion found in the myocardium during acute rheumatic carditis? → Aschoff bodies, containing Aschoff giant cells and Anitschkow cells.
6.  A young child presents with fever, a "strawberry tongue," and conjunctival injection. Which vasculitis is suspected, and what is its most serious complication? → Kawasaki disease; coronary artery aneurysms.
7.  What is the classic radiographic finding for Tetralogy of Fallot? → "Boot-shaped" heart due to right ventricular hypertrophy.
8.  A patient with hypertension develops "onion-skin" concentric thickening of arterioles in the kidney. What type of arteriolosclerosis is this, and what is its clinical significance? → Hyperplastic arteriolosclerosis, typically seen in malignant hypertension.
