# Approach to the Anaemic Patient

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Haematology**

> **In one line:** anaemia is almost always a secondary disorder, so the task is to find the cause. Start with the **reticulocyte count**: if it is raised, look for **blood loss or haemolysis**; if it is normal or low, the marrow is failing to respond, and the **MCV, RDW and blood film** direct the work-up (microcytic = iron or globin problem, macrocytic = DNA synthesis problem, normocytic = chronic disease, marrow failure or combined causes).

## What you must know

- Anaemia is a fall in circulating red cells and is **by far the commonest haematological disorder**.
- Four mechanisms: **blood loss, decreased production (hypoproliferative), maturation defects (ineffective erythropoiesis), and increased destruction (haemolysis).**
- First test: **reticulocyte count** (absolute count = retic % × RBC count; normal up to about 120,000/µL).
- **Production index above 2.5** suggests haemolysis (or bleeding with a good marrow); **below 2** suggests hypoproliferative anaemia or a maturation disorder.
- At least **75% of anaemias are hypoproliferative**, mostly iron deficiency or inflammation.
- Ferritin below 15 = iron deficiency; 120 or above excludes it; 15 to 120 needs TIBC and further tests.
- If **MCV is above 110 fL**, it is almost always B12 or folate deficiency; 100 to 110 fL needs other causes (alcohol, liver, thyroid, MDS).
- A normal blood smear does **not** exclude haemolysis.

## 1. Definition and causes

- **Definition:** a decrease in the number of circulating red blood cells (reduced Hb/haematocrit). The commonest haematological disorder and almost always secondary, so doctors must know how to find the cause.

| Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Blood loss** | GI bleeding, menstrual loss, trauma |
| **Decreased red cell production** (marrow failure, hypoproliferative) | Iron deficiency, inflammation, renal failure (low erythropoietin), marrow damage |
| **Red cell maturation defects** (ineffective erythropoiesis) | B12/folate deficiency, MDS, thalassaemia, sideroblastic anaemia |
| **Increased destruction (haemolysis)** | Hereditary and acquired haemolytic anaemias |

## 2. Initial assessment

Order of work-up:

1. **History and examination** (bleeding, diet, drugs, chronic illness, family history, jaundice, splenomegaly).
2. **Reticulocyte count.**
3. **Blood film.**
4. **MCV.**
5. **Ferritin.**
6. **WBC, differential and platelets** (pancytopenia points to marrow failure, hypersplenism or infiltration).

### The "exaggerated" work-up (what not to do blindly)

Ordering iron studies, folate/B12, LDH/bilirubin, haptoglobin, urine haemosiderin, direct and indirect Coombs test, Hb electrophoresis, acid haemolysis (PNH), osmotic fragility, trial of iron/folate/B12, crossmatch with 2 to 4 units of transfusion, GI consult and bone marrow for every anaemic patient is wasteful. The first test should guide the rest.

> **Exam tip:** **if the reticulocyte count is high, you do not need** iron studies, folate/B12, acid haemolysis, GI referral or bone marrow; look for haemolysis or bleeding instead.

## 3. Step one: the reticulocyte count

- **Absolute reticulocyte count = reticulocyte % × RBC count.** Example: 0.01 × 5,000,000 = 50,000/µL.
- Normal is **1 to 4%** of circulating red cells in adults; absolute **up to 120,000/µL**.
- It is the **most accurate way to judge the marrow's response** to anaemia. Correct for the degree of anaemia (production index).
- **Production index above 2.5**: haemolysis most likely.
- **Production index below 2**: hypoproliferative anaemia or maturation disorder.
- **Acute blood loss** does not change the retic count immediately (it takes several days to rise).

### Hypoproliferative anaemias

- No erythroid hyperplasia in the marrow.
- At least **75% of all anaemias**; most are due to **iron deficiency or inflammation**.
- Causes: marrow damage, iron deficiency, inadequate erythropoietin stimulation.
- May be mildly microcytic and hypochromic in early iron deficiency or chronic inflammation.

### Maturation disorders

- Anaemia with an **inappropriately low retic production index**, macro- or microcytosis, abnormal indices.
- Two broad groups:
  - **Nuclear maturation defects**: macrocytosis (B12, folate).
  - **Cytoplasmic maturation defects**: microcytosis and hypochromia (iron, globin or haem defects).

### Blood loss or haemolysis

- Haemolysis shows a **production index of 2.5 times normal or more**.
- Film: **polychromatophilic macrocytes** (young reticulocytes appear blue-grey and large).

## 4. The blood film

Look for:

- **Size and shape** of the red cells, especially variability.
- **Polychromasia** (often means reticulocytosis).
- A **dimorphic population** (after transfusion, or sideroblastic anaemia).
- Platelet and white cell abnormalities.

## 5. Classification by MCV

### 5.1 Normocytic anaemia (MCV 80 to 100 fL)

- Most commonly **anaemia of chronic disease**.
- **Early iron deficiency** is often normocytic.
- A particular hazard in ICU patients (inflammation, blood sampling, critical illness).
- Combined deficiencies (for example iron plus B12) can give a normal MCV.
- Others: marrow failure, renal failure, endocrine disease, haemolysis, acute blood loss.

#### Anaemia of chronic disease (ACD)

| Feature | Details |
|---|---|
| Frequency and course | Common; develops over 1 to 2 months; non-progressive |
| Severity | Usually mild to moderate (haematocrit below 0.20 occasionally) |
| Indices | Mostly normocytic; about **30% mildly microcytic**; low iron, low TIBC, low transferrin saturation |
| Other counts | WBC and platelets normal or increased |

**Pathophysiology:** inflammatory cytokines (**IL-1, TNF**) act through DNA/RNA iron-response elements to:

- Reduce erythropoietin responsiveness and production.
- Reduce transferrin synthesis.
- **Reduce iron mobilisation from macrophages** and iron re-use in erythropoiesis (hepcidin-mediated).
- Lower serum iron despite adequate stores; **raise serum ferritin**.
- Result: reticulocytopenia and anaemia.

**Effects of IL-1:** stimulates fever, granulopoiesis, thrombopoiesis, and synthesis of ferritin, immunoglobulins, fibrinogen, factor VIII, CRP, IL-2 and IL-6. It **inhibits erythropoiesis** and the synthesis of transferrin and albumin.

**Causes of ACD:** thyroid disease; collagen vascular disease (**rheumatoid arthritis, SLE**, polymyositis, polyarteritis nodosa); inflammatory bowel disease (ulcerative colitis, Crohn disease); **malignancy**; chronic infections; familial Mediterranean fever; **renal failure**.

### 5.2 Microcytic anaemia (MCV below 80 fL)

| Cause | RDW and notes |
|---|---|
| **Iron deficiency** | **High RDW** (normal RDW is 11.5 to 14.5%) |
| **Thalassaemia minor** | **Normal RDW** |
| Sideroblastic anaemia | Rare |
| Metal poisoning (lead, aluminium) | Rare |
| Occasional haemoglobinopathies; thalassaemia major | |

**Ferritin pathway for microcytic or hypoproliferative anaemia:**

| Ferritin | Interpretation and next step |
|---|---|
| **Below 15** | **Iron deficiency anaemia** |
| **15 to 120** | Check **TIBC**. High TIBC: iron deficiency likely, so give a **trial of iron** (anaemia corrects = iron deficiency; not corrected = examine marrow iron stores). Normal or low TIBC: anaemia of chronic disease |
| **120 or above** | **Iron deficiency excluded** |

### 5.3 Macrocytic anaemia (MCV above 100 fL)

- **MCV above 110 fL**: almost always folate or cobalamin (B12) deficiency.
- **MCV 100 to 110 fL**: look for other causes (alcohol, liver disease, hypothyroidism, reticulocytosis, MDS, drugs).

#### Macrocytosis of alcoholism

- Found in **25 to 96%** of alcoholics (direct toxic effect of alcohol on marrow).
- MCV rise usually slight (100 to 110 fL); minimal or no anaemia.
- Macrocytes are **round** (not oval); **no neutrophil hypersegmentation**; folate stores normal.

#### Megaloblastic haematopoiesis

- Marrow failure from **disrupted DNA synthesis and ineffective haematopoiesis**.
- Marrow: giant precursors and nuclear-cytoplasmic dyssynchrony.
- Blood: **neutrophil hypersegmentation and macro-ovalocytes**; anaemia, often leukopenia and thrombocytopenia.
- Almost always due to **cobalamin or folate deficiency**.

#### Evolving cobalamin deficiency (usual sequence)

1. Serum cobalamin falls.
2. Serum **methylmalonic acid and homocysteine** rise.
3. MCV rises within the normal range, with neutrophil hypersegmentation.
4. MCV rises above normal.
5. Anaemia and/or neuropathy (symptoms).

### 5.4 Dimorphic anaemias (two deficiencies together)

The MCV may look normal because large and small cells cancel out. The **blood smear is critical**.

- Folate and iron deficiency (pregnancy, alcoholism).
- B12 and iron deficiency (pernicious anaemia with atrophic gastritis).
- Thalassaemia minor with B12 or folate deficiency.
- Iron deficiency with haemolysis (prosthetic valve).
- Folate deficiency with haemolysis (HbSS disease).

## 6. Haemolytic anaemia

Anaemia of increased destruction: normocytic, normochromic, **shortened red cell survival**, with **reticulocytosis** as the marrow response.

**Tests to diagnose haemolysis:**

- Reticulocyte count (with serial Hb).
- **Haptoglobin** (low).
- **Urine haemosiderin**.
- Also helpful: serum bilirubin (unconjugated, raised), LDH (raised), haemoglobinuria.
- These have problems of sensitivity and specificity and none defines the cause.

> **Remember:** haemolysis is not excluded by a normal blood smear.

**Tests to define the cause (these do not show that haemolysis is present):**

| Test | Detects |
|---|---|
| Haemoglobin electrophoresis, HbA2 | Beta-thalassaemia trait, haemoglobinopathies |
| Red cell enzymes (G6PD, pyruvate kinase) | Enzyme deficiencies |
| Direct and indirect antiglobulin (Coombs) tests | Immune haemolysis |
| Cold agglutinins | Usually a response to infection |
| Osmotic fragility | Hereditary spherocytosis |
| Acid haemolysis test | PNH |
| Clotting profile | DIC |

## 7. Summary algorithm

1. **Check the reticulocyte count first.**
2. **Elevated:** look for increased destruction or bleeding.
3. **Normal or decreased:** look for marrow failure or a production problem. Tailor the work-up by **MCV, RDW and blood film**:
   - **Low MCV:** iron problems or globin problems.
   - **High MCV:** megaloblastic or DNA synthesis problems.
   - **Normal MCV:** look for **combined anaemias**.

## Key points to revise

1. Anaemia is nearly always secondary; the four mechanisms are loss, underproduction, maturation defects and destruction.
2. Reticulocyte count is the first test; production index above 2.5 means haemolysis or bleeding, below 2 means hypoproliferative or maturation disorder.
3. About 75% of anaemias are hypoproliferative, mostly iron deficiency or inflammation.
4. Normocytic: ACD (low iron, low TIBC, high ferritin), early iron deficiency, combined deficiencies.
5. Microcytic: iron deficiency (high RDW) versus thalassaemia minor (normal RDW); use ferritin below 15, 15 to 120 (plus TIBC), and 120 or above.
6. MCV above 110 fL is nearly always B12/folate deficiency; alcohol gives round macrocytes and mild MCV rise.
7. Dimorphic anaemias need a blood film; haemolysis needs haptoglobin, bilirubin, LDH and urine haemosiderin.
