# Pneumothorax

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Respiratory Medicine**

> **In one line:** pneumothorax is air in the pleural space, which makes the lung collapse away from the chest wall. It may be spontaneous (primary or secondary), traumatic or iatrogenic. A **tension pneumothorax**, where a one-way valve traps air under rising pressure, shifts the mediastinum and collapses the circulation, and must be decompressed at once without waiting for an X-ray.

## What you must know

- Presence of air in the pleural space: spontaneous, iatrogenic, or from trauma to the lung or chest wall.
- **Primary** spontaneous: no overt lung disease, rupture of a subpleural bleb or bulla (tall thin young men). **Secondary:** underlying lung disease (COPD, TB, asthma, etc.) with higher mortality.
- Classic signs: sudden unilateral pleuritic chest pain or breathlessness; **absent breath sounds with a resonant percussion note**.
- **Tension pneumothorax** = tachycardia, hypotension, cyanosis, tracheal deviation away from the side; a clinical diagnosis and an emergency.
- CXR: sharp edge of the deflated lung with no lung markings beyond it. Do not mistake a large bulla for a pneumothorax.
- Management by type: observe or aspirate small primary; chest drain for secondary; drain in 4th to 6th intercostal space, mid-axillary line.
- Advise patients not to fly until resolved, and to stop smoking.

## 1. Definition and classification

**Pneumothorax** is air in the pleural space. The negative intrapleural pressure is lost, so the lung recoils inwards.

| Type | Definition and causes |
|---|---|
| **Spontaneous, primary** | No evidence of overt lung disease. Air escapes through rupture of a small **subpleural emphysematous bulla or pleural bleb**, or the pulmonary end of a pleural adhesion. Typically tall, thin young men, smokers. |
| **Spontaneous, secondary** | **Underlying lung disease**: most commonly COPD, TB, asthma, lung abscess, pulmonary infarcts, bronchogenic carcinoma, all forms of fibrotic and cystic lung disease. **Higher mortality**. Others: cystic fibrosis, Pneumocystis pneumonia in HIV, Marfan syndrome. |
| **Traumatic** | **Iatrogenic** (after thoracic surgery, biopsy, central line, pleural aspiration, ventilation) or **chest wall injury** (rib fracture, penetrating wounds). |

Also described: **catamenial pneumothorax** (recurs with menstruation; thoracic endometriosis).

## 2. Physiological types

| Type | Mechanism | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| **Closed** | The communication between airway and pleural space seals off as the lung deflates and does not reopen. Mean pleural pressure stays negative. | Spontaneous reabsorption of air and re-expansion over **a few days or weeks** |
| **Open** | The communication fails to seal; air passes freely between bronchial tree and pleural space (**bronchopleural fistula**). Seen on rupture of an emphysematous bulla, tuberculous cavity or lung abscess into the pleural space. | Persistent leak; a large fistula can carry infection from the airways to the pleura, causing **empyema** |
| **Tension** | The communication acts as a **one-way valve**: air enters on inspiration but cannot escape on expiration. | Rising intrapleural pressure, mediastinal shift, circulatory collapse |

## 3. Clinical features

### Pneumothorax in general

- **Sudden-onset unilateral pleuritic chest pain or breathlessness.**
- In a patient with **underlying chest disease** the breathlessness can be severe and may not resolve spontaneously, because there is little reserve.
- **Small:** examination may be normal.
- **Larger (more than 15% of the hemithorax):** reduced or absent breath sounds; reduced chest movement and vocal resonance on the affected side.
- **Absent breath sounds plus a resonant percussion note is diagnostic.**

### Tension pneumothorax

1. The airway-pleura communication works as a one-way valve; air enters on inspiration only.
2. Large amounts of trapped air accumulate, so intrapleural pressure rises **well above atmospheric**.
3. The mediastinum is pushed to the opposite side.
4. The opposite, normal lung is compressed and **systemic venous return is impaired**, causing cardiovascular compromise.

**Findings:** rapidly progressive breathlessness with marked **tachycardia, hypotension, cyanosis**, raised JVP, and **tracheal displacement away from the side of the silent hemithorax** (hyper-resonant, hyperexpanded).

- Occasionally tension occurs **without mediastinal shift** when malignant disease or scarring has splinted the mediastinum.

> **Exam tip:** tension pneumothorax is a **clinical** diagnosis. Do not send the patient for a chest X-ray. Give high-flow oxygen and decompress immediately.

## 4. Investigations

### Chest X-ray (erect PA)

- Sharply defined **edge of the deflated lung**, with complete translucency (no lung markings) between this and the chest wall.
- Shows the extent of any **mediastinal displacement**, any **pleural fluid** (hydropneumothorax) and underlying pulmonary disease.
- A **large pre-existing emphysematous bulla** must be differentiated from a pneumothorax to avoid misdirected attempts at aspiration.
- **CT** distinguishes bullae from pleural air, and is useful for complex or recurrent cases.

### Others

- Arterial blood gases in breathless patients or those with underlying lung disease.
- ECG: may show axis shift in left-sided pneumothorax; useful to exclude MI as a cause of chest pain.

## 5. Management

### Primary pneumothorax

- Lung edge **less than 2 cm** from the chest wall and the patient **not breathless**: usually **resolves without intervention** (observe, with follow-up X-ray).
- Young patients with a moderate or large pneumothorax: **percutaneous needle aspiration** of air, which is simple and well tolerated, with a **60 to 80% chance of avoiding a chest drain**.

### Secondary pneumothorax

- Significant underlying chronic lung disease may cause respiratory distress; the **success rate of aspiration is much lower**.
- **Intercostal tube drainage and inpatient observation** are usually required, particularly in patients **over 50 years** and those with respiratory compromise.

### Intercostal drain: technique and care

- Insert in the **4th, 5th or 6th intercostal space in the mid-axillary line** (the safe triangle), above the rib to protect the neurovascular bundle.
- Connect to an **underwater seal** or a **one-way Heimlich valve**; secure firmly to the chest wall.
- **Clamping an intercostal drain is potentially dangerous** and rarely indicated.
- Remove **24 hours after the lung has fully re-inflated and bubbling has stopped**.
- **Continued bubbling after 5 to 7 days** is an indication for surgery.
- If bubbling stops **before** full re-inflation, the tube is blocked, kinked or displaced.
- **Supplemental oxygen** may speed resolution because it accelerates the rate at which nitrogen is reabsorbed by the pleura.

### Immediate decompression (tension pneumothorax)

Decompression is required before insertion of the intercostal drain.

1. Aspirate in the **2nd intercostal space anteriorly in the midclavicular line** with a **16 F cannula** (a large-bore cannula; some guidelines now prefer the 4th or 5th space, anterior axillary line). Discontinue if:
   - Resistance is felt
   - The patient coughs excessively
   - More than **2.5 L** of air has been removed
2. Follow with a formal chest drain.
3. **Beware:** the post-aspiration chest X-ray is not a reliable indicator of whether a pleural leak remains. Tell all patients to attend again immediately if they deteriorate.

> **Remember:** a rush of air after needle decompression confirms tension pneumothorax. Always follow with a chest drain.

### Surgical options

Indicated for persistent air leak (bubbling beyond 5 to 7 days), recurrence, bilateral pneumothorax, and high-risk occupations. Options include thoracoscopic (VATS) bullectomy with pleurodesis or pleurectomy. Chemical pleurodesis (talc) is an option in patients unfit for surgery.

### Closed pneumothorax: advice

- **Do not fly**: trapped gas expands at altitude. After complete resolution there is no clear evidence for how long to avoid flying, but guidelines suggest **1 to 2 weeks** after confirmation of full inflation.
- **Stop smoking**, which raises the risk of recurrence. Avoid diving unless definitive surgery has been done.

## 6. Complications

- Tension pneumothorax and cardiorespiratory arrest
- Persistent air leak and bronchopleural fistula
- Empyema
- Recurrence (about a third after a first primary episode)
- Re-expansion pulmonary oedema if a large collapse is drained rapidly
- Surgical emphysema

## Key points to revise

1. Pneumothorax is air in the pleural space: primary (no lung disease), secondary (COPD, TB, etc.) or traumatic.
2. Absent breath sounds plus a resonant percussion note is diagnostic; small ones can have a normal examination.
3. Tension: one-way valve, rising pressure, shift away, hypotension, cyanosis; decompress immediately.
4. CXR shows a lung edge with no markings beyond it; CT separates bullae from pneumothorax.
5. Primary under 2 cm and not breathless: observe; larger: aspirate (60 to 80% success).
6. Secondary: chest drain in 4th to 6th space, mid-axillary line; never clamp; surgery if bubbling beyond 5 to 7 days.
7. No flying until resolved (1 to 2 weeks after full re-inflation); stop smoking.
