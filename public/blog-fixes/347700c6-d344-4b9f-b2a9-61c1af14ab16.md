# HIV and the Lung

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · HIV and Respiratory Medicine**

> **In one line:** HIV-related lung disease is the commonest cause of illness and death in AIDS. Risk of each opportunistic infection follows the CD4 count, and the common pulmonary problems in Africa are bacterial pneumonia, tuberculosis (often atypical and smear-negative), *Pneumocystis jirovecii* pneumonia and Kaposi's sarcoma. Presentation is atypical, so diagnose promptly, treat empirically in resource-limited settings, and prevent with ART and prophylaxis.

## What you must know

- Pulmonary manifestations are common and often **atypical** in HIV.
- **PJP** (*Pneumocystis jirovecii*) is a **fungus**, not a protozoan; 90% of cases have **CD4 below 200**; treat with **high-dose co-trimoxazole for 21 days** plus **prednisone if hypoxic**.
- **TB** can occur at any CD4; in advanced HIV it is smear-negative, non-cavitating, often extrapulmonary; use **GeneXpert**.
- Bacterial pneumonia (*S. pneumoniae*) is the commonest pulmonary infection and occurs at any CD4.
- **Histoplasmosis:** disseminated at CD4 below 150; liposomal amphotericin B then itraconazole.
- Do **not** use macrolide monotherapy empirically (macrolide-resistant pneumococci); be careful with fluoroquinolones if TB is possible.
- Early diagnosis, early ART and primary prophylaxis of OIs prevent most of these.

## 1. Natural history of untreated HIV

| Stage | Duration |
|---|---|
| Transmission, then acute retroviral syndrome | 2 to 3 weeks |
| Recovery and seroconversion | 2 to 3 weeks (up to 4 weeks) |
| Asymptomatic HIV infection | Average about 8 years; over 30 years in elite controllers, under 2 years in rapid progressors |
| Symptomatic HIV / AIDS | About 1.3 years |
| Death | |

> **Remember:** in people with AIDS, death is usually from an **opportunistic infection** (including TB and pneumonia) or cancer, not from HIV itself.

## 2. Opportunistic infections: general principles

### Definition

- An infection caused by a pathogen that does **not usually cause disease in healthy hosts**; it exploits the weakened immune system.
- Also includes **atypical or more severe presentations** of diseases that can infect healthy hosts (e.g. TB, pneumococcal pneumonia).

### How HIV predisposes to OIs

- HIV infects CD4 cells and uses them to replicate. Turnover is about **2 × 10⁹ CD4 cells/day**; eventually replacement fails.
- Loss is by **direct infection, apoptosis, increased lymphocyte turnover, decreased production and redistribution**.
- Without enough functioning CD4 cells, **cellular immunity** falls, as does help to humoral and cytotoxic immunity.

### CD4 count and risk

- OIs follow a **continuum of risk**; they rarely occur above their usual CD4 threshold.
- **CD4 is the best predictor**, but a **rising viral load** is also a risk, especially when CD4 is above 300.

| CD4 (cells/µL) | Typical complications |
|---|---|
| **Over 500** | Candida vaginitis, pulmonary TB, *S. pneumoniae* |
| **200 to 500** | Pneumococcal disease, HSV, VZV, pulmonary TB, Kaposi's sarcoma, thrush |
| **Under 200** | **PJP**, miliary and extrapulmonary TB, non-Hodgkin's lymphoma, disseminated histoplasmosis, recurrent bacterial disease |
| **Under 100** | Toxoplasmosis, cryptococcosis, chronic cryptosporidiosis, microsporidiosis, candida oesophagitis |
| **Under 50** | CMV, *Mycobacterium avium* complex (MAC), CNS lymphoma, PML |

### Classification of OIs

- **By microbiology:**
  - Bacterial: TB, MAC, bacterial pneumonia, septicaemia.
  - Fungal: candidiasis, PJP, cryptococcosis, aspergillosis.
  - Viral: CMV, HSV, VZV, JC virus.
  - Parasitic: toxoplasmosis, cryptosporidium, microsporidium, leishmania.
- **By organ:**

| Site | Conditions |
|---|---|
| Brain | Cryptococcus, toxoplasmosis, lymphoma, PML, CMV, HIV-associated dementia |
| Eye | CMV, VZV |
| Mouth | HSV, Candida |
| Lungs | PJP, TB, bacterial pneumonia, fungal pneumonia |
| Gut | MAC, CMV, *Salmonella*, cryptosporidium, microsporidia |
| Bone marrow | TB, MAC, histoplasmosis, leishmania |
| Skin | HSV, VZV, mucocutaneous disease, pyogenic infections |
| Genitals | HSV, HPV, Candida |

## 3. Pulmonary problems in HIV

- **Common pulmonary OIs in the developing world:** bacterial (lobar) pneumonia, PJP, TB, Kaposi's sarcoma.
- **Uncommon:** histoplasmosis, *Penicillium* (talaromycosis), coccidioidomycosis, *M. avium*.
- Others: viral pneumonitis (CMV, HSV, VZV), malignancies (Kaposi's sarcoma, lymphomas, primary effusion lymphoma), lymphocytic interstitial pneumonitis, non-specific interstitial pneumonitis, pulmonary hypertension.

### Host lung defences

- Progressive HIV reduces **lung CD4 T cells**.
- There is intense infiltration of **CD8+ T cells** in the interstitium and alveolar spaces (**lymphocytic alveolitis**), prominent in early to middle disease, caused by compartmentalisation of HIV-specific cytotoxic T cells.

## 4. Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP/PCP)

### Organism and epidemiology

- **Fungus** (not a protozoan). Caused by *P. jirovecii* (formerly *P. carinii*, the rodent form). Ubiquitous; first infection usually in early childhood.
- May result from **reactivation or new exposure**; airborne spread is possible in immunosuppressed patients.
- Before ART: occurred in **70 to 80%** of AIDS patients; treated cases had **20 to 40% mortality**; **90% of cases had CD4 below 200**.

### Risk factors

CD4 below 200/µL, **CD4% below 14%**, previous PJP, oral thrush, recurrent bacterial pneumonia, unintentional weight loss, high HIV RNA.

### Clinical features

- **Progressive exertional dyspnoea**, fever (may predominate), **dry non-productive cough**, chest discomfort.
- **Subacute** onset over days to weeks (fulminant is uncommon).
- **Exertional desaturation.**
- Chest examination may be **normal** early, or show fine dry crackles, tachypnoea, tachycardia, hypoxia and fever.
- Extrapulmonary disease is rare, seen with aerosolised pentamidine prophylaxis.

### Diagnosis

- Clinical picture, blood tests and radiographs are suggestive but **not diagnostic**; the organism **cannot be cultured**.
- **Hypoxaemia** is characteristic (PaO₂ below 70 mmHg or A-a gradient above 35 mmHg). **LDH above 500 mg/dL** is common but non-specific.
- Specimens:

| Specimen | Sensitivity |
|---|---|
| Induced sputum | Under 50% to over 90% |
| Spontaneous sputum | Low |
| **Bronchoalveolar lavage** | 90 to 99% |
| Transbronchial biopsy | 95 to 100% |
| Open-lung biopsy | 95 to 100% |

- In resource-limited settings these cannot be done, so **diagnosis is clinical** (with response to treatment).
- **Chest X-ray:** may be normal early. Typical: **diffuse bilateral symmetrical perihilar interstitial infiltrates (butterfly appearance)**. Atypical: nodules, asymmetry, blebs, cysts, pneumothorax. Cavitation, intrathoracic adenopathy and pleural effusion are **uncommon** unless a second process is present.
- **HRCT:** bilateral patchy **ground-glass opacities**.
- Other tests: beta-D-glucan (raised), PCR on respiratory samples.

### Treatment

- **Co-trimoxazole (TMP-SMX) 15 mg/kg/day (trimethoprim component) in 3 divided doses for 21 days.** Oral dose: **2 double-strength tablets three times daily**.
- **Severe disease** (PaO₂ below 70, A-a gradient above 35, marked hypoxia or large fall in oximetry): add **prednisone 40 mg twice daily for 5 days, then 40 mg daily for 5 days, then 20 mg daily for 11 days**.
- Clinical response in **5 to 7 days**.
- If resistant or not tolerated: **clindamycin plus primaquine**; **atovaquone 750 mg twice daily orally** (avoid in pregnancy per the slide); **IV pentamidine**.
- Check G6PD before primaquine or dapsone. Start ART within about 2 weeks.
- Secondary prophylaxis: co-trimoxazole until CD4 is above 200 (or per local guideline) on ART.

## 5. Bacterial pneumonia

- Organisms: ***S. pneumoniae*** (drug-resistant strains increasingly common in the developed world), ***H. influenzae***, *P. aeruginosa*, *S. aureus*, atypicals (less common). No organism is identified in up to **33%**.
- **Presentation** is as in HIV-negative patients with **acute symptoms**; a **subacute** illness suggests an alternative diagnosis (PJP, TB, chronic fungal disease). Examination shows focal consolidation or effusion; **WBC usually raised**.
- **Chest X-ray:** focal, segmental or lobar consolidation (e.g. right middle lobe pneumococcal pneumonia), but may be multilobar, nodular or reticulonodular.
- **Diagnosis:** routine pathogen testing is controversial. Gram stain and culture of sputum, **2 blood cultures** (low sensitivity but very specific). **Consider TB:** if the presentation is compatible, manage as possible TB. Evaluate for PJP if clinically indicated (can coexist). Do **diagnostic thoracentesis** if effusion.
- **Treatment:** target *S. pneumoniae* and *H. influenzae*; principles as for HIV-negative patients, guided by severity; start empirically; narrow when the pathogen is known.
  - **No empiric macrolide monotherapy** (macrolide-resistant pneumococcus).
  - Use **fluoroquinolones cautiously** if TB is suspected but untreated (they act as TB monotherapy and promote resistance).
- Prevention: pneumococcal vaccination, ART, co-trimoxazole prophylaxis.

## 6. Tuberculosis in HIV

### Typical TB (HIV negative)

Cough over 3 weeks, weight loss, night sweats, **upper lobe cavitary disease**, smear positive.

### TB in the era of HIV

- **Atypical symptoms:** no cough, acute or very long history.
- **Smears often negative**; disease can occur anywhere in the lung.
- **Cavities are rare** with CD4 below 200.
- **Chest X-ray normal in up to 1 in 3.**
- **Extrapulmonary TB is common:** in HIV-negative patients about 85% of TB is pulmonary, whereas in HIV-positive patients **more than 50% have extrapulmonary disease** (autopsy study from Nairobi, Kenya).
- Screening data from a South African ART clinic: a cough-based screen missed half of TB cases; 19% of patients entering the clinic and not on TB treatment had TB; 22% of TB patients had **no symptoms**; only 9% of proven TB cases were sputum AFB positive.

> **Exam tip:** in HIV, "no cough" does not exclude TB. Use the four-symptom screen (cough, fever, night sweats, weight loss) and test all with symptoms, plus consider urine LAM in advanced disease.

### Diagnosis

- **GeneXpert (CBNAAT, cartridge-based nucleic acid amplification test):** rapid, detects *M. tuberculosis* and **rifampicin resistance**, useful in smear-negative disease; the test of choice.
- **ADA (adenosine deaminase):** raised in TB, especially **TB effusions** (pleural, pericardial, peritoneal). Also raised in typhoid, infectious mononucleosis, liver disease, sarcoidosis, leukaemia, brucellosis, acute pneumonia and malignancies, so it is supportive, not specific.
- Chest X-ray, culture, histology, and urine LAM.

### Treatment and prevention

- **Treat as in HIV-negative patients** (standard 2HRZE/4HR), with attention to drug interactions with ART and IRIS.
- **Isoniazid preventive therapy (IPT)** for those in whom active TB has been ruled out (treatment of latent TB).

## 7. Histoplasmosis

- *Histoplasma capsulatum*; endemic in the US midwest, Puerto Rico, parts of Central America, the Amazon basin, sub-Saharan Africa and Asia. Occurs in up to **5%** of untreated AIDS patients in endemic areas. In non-endemic areas, seen in those who previously lived in endemic areas.
- Acquired by **inhalation**; latent infection may reactivate. **Disseminated** disease usually at **CD4 below 150**; pulmonary disease may occur at CD4 above 300. Incidence has fallen with ART.

### Clinical features

- **Disseminated multi-organ:** fever, fatigue, weight loss, **hepatosplenomegaly, lymphadenopathy**, pallor, abdominal tenderness; cough, chest pain and dyspnoea in 50%; skin lesions; CNS (fever, headache, seizures, focal deficits, altered mental state); GI (fever, diarrhoea, abdominal pain, weight loss).
- **Isolated pulmonary** disease in higher CD4.
- Labs: anaemia, leucopenia, **LDH > AST > ALT**, **ferritin often over 1,000**; blood smear may show intracellular organisms.

### Diagnosis

- **Histoplasma antigen** in serum (over 85%) or urine (over 95%): sensitive for disseminated disease, **insensitive for pulmonary** disease.
- Biopsy with **budding yeast**; culture from blood, marrow, respiratory secretions or lesions (over 85% sensitive but takes 2 to 4 weeks).
- Serology is usually not useful in the immunosuppressed.

### Treatment

| Severity | Regimen |
|---|---|
| **Moderate to severe disseminated** | Induction (2 weeks or until improved): **liposomal amphotericin B 3 mg/kg IV daily** (alternatives: amphotericin B 0.7 mg/kg or lipid complex 5 mg/kg IV daily). Then maintenance **itraconazole 200 mg three times daily for 3 days, then twice daily** (liquid preferred) |
| **Less severe** | Itraconazole alone as above |
| **Duration** | At least **12 months** |

- **Primary prophylaxis:** consider itraconazole if CD4 below 150 and living in an endemic region.
- **Secondary prophylaxis:** 1 year; may stop if treated more than 12 months, ART over 6 months, CD4 above 150, and urine/serum antigen below 4.1.

## 8. Other pulmonary problems

- **Viral pneumonitis:** CMV, HSV, VZV.
- **Malignancies:** **Kaposi's sarcoma** (HHV-8; pulmonary form with nodules, effusions, poor prognosis), lymphomas, primary effusion lymphoma.
- **Lymphocytic interstitial pneumonitis** (mostly children), non-specific interstitial pneumonitis, **pulmonary hypertension**.

## Key points to revise

1. OI risk follows the CD4 count: above 500 TB/pneumococcus; under 200 PJP; under 100 toxoplasma and cryptococcus; under 50 CMV and MAC.
2. PJP: fungus; dry cough, exertional dyspnoea, desaturation on exercise, butterfly infiltrates; treat with high-dose co-trimoxazole 21 days plus prednisone if PaO₂ below 70.
3. Bacterial pneumonia: *S. pneumoniae*; acute onset; no macrolide monotherapy.
4. TB in HIV: atypical, smear-negative, frequently extrapulmonary; use GeneXpert; treat as in HIV-negative; offer IPT after excluding active TB.
5. Histoplasmosis: antigen testing; liposomal amphotericin B then itraconazole for 12 months.
6. Kaposi's sarcoma and lymphomas are the main HIV lung malignancies.
7. Early diagnosis, early ART and primary prophylaxis prevent most pulmonary OIs.
