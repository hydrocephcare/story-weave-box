# Acute Viral Hepatitis

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Gastroenterology and Hepatology**

> **In one line:** acute viral hepatitis is inflammation of the liver caused by the hepatitis viruses (A to E) lasting under 6 months. **A and E** spread by the faecal-oral route and never become chronic; **B, C and D** spread by blood and body fluids and can become chronic, leading to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma. Diagnosis rests on **raised transaminases (ALT above AST) plus viral serology**; treatment of acute disease is mostly supportive.

## What you must know

- **Acute** hepatitis lasts **under 6 months**; **chronic** lasts **more than 6 months**.
- **HAV and HEV: faecal-oral**, self-limiting, no chronic state. **HEV is most dangerous in pregnancy.**
- **HBV: blood, sexual and perinatal spread.** Chronic infection in **90% of infected infants/children** but only **about 5% of adults**.
- **HCV: blood-borne, chronic in about 90%**; acute infection is often silent. Antiviral therapy for 12 weeks now cures over 90%.
- **HDV needs HBsAg** to replicate: coinfection or superinfection of a chronic HBV carrier.
- In viral hepatitis **ALT is higher than AST** (opposite of alcoholic hepatitis); ALT often above 1,000 U/L; ALP rarely above twice normal; **prolonged PT shows severity**.
- **Fulminant hepatitis** (liver failure within 8 weeks) is commonest with HBV, HDV and HEV.
- Anti-HAV **IgM** = acute infection; **HBsAg** = infection; **anti-HBs** = immunity or vaccination; **anti-HBc IgM** = recent infection or window period.

## 1. Definition and classification

Acute hepatitis is infection or inflammation of the liver. Most cases of acute viral hepatitis are due to **hepatitis A or B**. Hepatitis C, for unknown reasons, rarely presents as an acute illness and is usually found as a silent infection on blood tests.

| Infectious | Non-infectious |
|---|---|
| Hepatitis **A, B, C, D, E** | **Alcoholic** hepatitis |
| EBV, CMV, HSV (especially in the immunocompromised) | **Drug-induced** hepatitis |
| | **Autoimmune** hepatitis |
| | Hereditary diseases (**haemochromatosis, Wilson's disease**) |

- **Acute viral hepatitis:** under 6 months. **Chronic viral hepatitis:** over 6 months.
- Hepatitis E is mainly reported from India, Pakistan, South-East Asia and parts of Africa.
- HAV and HBV are more prevalent in developing countries.
- **HAV and HEV: faecal-oral** (mnemonic: **E**ating = **A** and **E**).
- **HDV** requires the outer envelope (HBsAg) of HBV, so it spreads only as **coinfection with HBV** or as **superinfection in a chronic HBV carrier**.

## 2. Epidemiology (from the lecture, with local context)

**Prevention success stories (example of Saudi data used in the lecture):**

- HBsAg prevalence in the 1980s was **5 to 10%** (very high). After **universal infant vaccination** (at birth and at school entry) and mandatory vaccination of at-risk groups, including health workers, from 1990, prevalence in children fell to **0 to 2%**. Screening of all incoming expatriates was also introduced.
- In one study (2009 to 2014) only **about 1%** of adults aged 18 to 70 had HBV.
- Anti-HBs may fall over time, but **repeat vaccination is not needed in everyone** because of immune memory. Groups at higher risk (for example **health workers**) need antibody monitoring and boosters.
- A vaccinated household contact of a carrier is protected from infection.
- A carrier with **HBV DNA below 2,000 IU/mL** is an **inactive carrier** (disease not active).
- **HCV prevalence** was low in Saudi Arabia but high in Egypt, because of past mass treatment of **schistosomiasis** with repeated injections using poorly sterilised (boiled) equipment.
- **HAV:** highest prevalence in border regions of low socioeconomic status (for example Jazan and Tabuk). Adult anti-HAV fell from **53% to 18%** as living standards improved.

**Kenya:** HAV is a disease of poor sanitation and childhood; HBV is endemic (intermediate prevalence, roughly 5%) and HBV vaccine is part of the national childhood schedule (pentavalent); HEV outbreaks occur in camps and flood areas with poor water supply.

> **Remember:** improved sanitation shifts HAV infection from childhood (usually mild) to adulthood (more severe). Better hygiene therefore means more susceptible adults.

## 3. Clinical features

Acute hepatitis has a wide spectrum: from **asymptomatic** to **fulminant liver failure**. Some patients have only transient flu-like symptoms (fever, myalgia, malaise).

| Phase | Features |
|---|---|
| **Incubation** | Silent; varies by virus (HAV about 2 to 6 weeks; HBV 1 to 6 months) |
| **Prodromal (pre-icteric)** | Malaise, anorexia, nausea, vomiting, low-grade fever, myalgia, right upper quadrant discomfort; smokers lose the taste for cigarettes; arthralgia and rash (more in HBV) |
| **Icteric** | Jaundice (icteric = jaundice), dark urine, pale stools, itching, tender hepatomegaly, sometimes mild splenomegaly |
| **Convalescence** | Symptoms settle over weeks; fatigue may persist |

## 4. Diagnosis: four steps

1. **History** (travel, contacts, injections, drugs including herbal medicines, alcohol, sexual and blood exposure).
2. **Physical examination.**
3. **Liver function tests:**
   - **ALT and AST raised, often 5 to 10 times normal or more.** Transaminases are helpful but **not diagnostic**.
   - **ALT is typically higher than AST** in all viral hepatitis (**opposite in alcoholic hepatitis**).
   - In acute hepatitis ALT is often **above 1,000 U/L**, but generally not as high as in drug-induced injury.
   - **Bilirubin** reflects the degree of liver damage.
   - **ALP** rarely exceeds twice the upper normal limit.
   - **Prolonged prothrombin time (PT)** indicates severity.
4. **Serological tests** (markers for A, B, C, E, or autoimmune markers): the most important step in diagnosing viral hepatitis.

### Differential diagnosis

1. Infectious mononucleosis.
2. **Drug-induced hepatitis** (the most common; always ask about drugs and **herbal substances**).
3. Chronic hepatitis.
4. Alcoholic hepatitis.
5. Cholecystitis / cholelithiasis (consider in women, especially over 50): suspect obstruction when ALP is high and transaminases are low.
6. Autoimmune hepatitis.
7. In Kenya also: malaria, leptospirosis, yellow fever, typhoid and sepsis.

> **Exam tip:** a "hepatocellular" pattern (ALT and AST very high, ALP near normal) points to hepatitis; a "cholestatic" pattern (ALP high) points to obstruction, so ultrasound is of no help in the former and liver biopsy is not indicated in typical acute viral hepatitis.

## 5. Hepatitis A

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| **Virus** | Picornavirus (RNA) |
| **Transmission** | **Faecal-oral (95%)**: person to person, contaminated food or water, overcrowding and poor sanitation. Shellfish and water in outbreaks. Infected plasma (under 5%); sexual route (under 5%) |
| **Course** | Self-limiting; full recovery in the great majority; rarely fulminant |
| **Diagnosis** | **Anti-HAV IgM** = acute infection (falls to low levels within about 3 months). **Anti-HAV IgG** = past infection or immunity (persists for years) |
| **Prevention** | Hygiene (hand washing), sanitation (clean water), **hepatitis A vaccine** (active, pre-exposure), **immunoglobulin** (passive, pre- and post-exposure) |

Immunisation should be considered in **close contacts of cases, the elderly, people with other major disease, perhaps pregnant women, and those with chronic hepatitis B or C** (HAV on top of chronic liver disease can be serious or life-threatening).

## 6. Hepatitis B

### Transmission

1. **Horizontal** (person to person), the main route overall, for example sexual intercourse (including heterosexual).
2. **Perinatal / vertical** from HBsAg-positive mothers, especially if **HBeAg-positive**: the most common cause of infection worldwide and **the highest risk of chronic infection**.
3. Injection drug use.
4. Contaminated equipment for therapeutic injections and healthcare procedures.
5. Folk medicine practices (scarification, circumcision, tattooing).
6. Transfusion of unscreened blood and blood products.

**Concentration of virus in fluids:** highest in **blood, serum and wound exudate**; moderate in **semen, vaginal fluid and saliva**; lowest in urine, faeces, sweat, tears and breast milk.

**Chronicity:** about **90% in children (especially infants)** versus **about 5% in adults**. Childhood infection is now less common because of vaccination.

### Serology (important)

| Marker | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **HBsAg** | Present in acute or chronic infection; detectable 1 to 2 weeks after infection (in practice a few weeks); persisting beyond 6 months = chronic. Undetectable if the virus is cleared |
| **HBeAg** | Active viral replication; **indicates infectivity**; appears shortly after HBsAg |
| **Anti-HBs** | After **vaccination** or after clearance; usually detectable 1 to 3 months after infection; indicates **immunity** |
| **Anti-HBc (IgM and IgG)** | May be the **only marker in the "window period"** when HBsAg has gone but anti-HBs is not yet detectable. **IgM** = recent infection |
| **HBV DNA (PCR)** | Viral load; persistence beyond **6 weeks** suggests likely progression to chronic disease |

> **Remember:** in **acute liver failure from hepatitis B** the damage is immune-mediated viral clearance, so **HBsAg may be negative**; the clue is **anti-HBc IgM**.

### Prevention

- HBV vaccine in the national immunisation programme (Expanded Programme on Immunisation).
- Screening of blood donors (and migrants where policy requires).
- Vaccination of risk groups; health education, especially for medical personnel.
- Post-exposure: HBV vaccine plus HBV immunoglobulin for non-immune exposed people and for babies of HBsAg-positive mothers.

## 7. Hepatitis C

### Transmission

| Route | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Percutaneous** | Injecting drug use; clotting factors before viral inactivation; transfusion or transplant from an infected donor; occupational needlestick |
| **Permucosal** (not common) | Perinatal; sexual |
| **Nosocomial** | Haemodialysis, endoscopy, unsafe injection practices, plasmapheresis, phlebotomy, multi-dose vials, therapeutic injections |

### Natural history and diagnosis

- **Chronic infection is common (about 90%).**
- **Anti-HCV** is the key marker but may be **undetectable for months** after infection, so a negative test does not exclude early infection.
- **HCV RNA (PCR)** is detectable **1 to 2 weeks** after infection and is more sensitive than the antibody.
- **12 weeks of antiviral therapy cures more than 90%.**

### Prevention

- Do not share razors, brushes or any item that pierces the skin.
- Strict universal (standard) precautions in health facilities.
- Educate and train health workers in standard precautions.

## 8. Complications

1. **Fulminant hepatitis** (acute liver failure within 8 weeks): uncommon, life-threatening, needs urgent treatment. Occurs more commonly in **hepatitis B, D and E** than in other types. Complications:
   - **Hepatic encephalopathy** (look for **asterixis** and **palmar erythema**).
   - **Hepatorenal syndrome**: rapid deterioration of kidney function in cirrhosis or fulminant liver failure.
   - **Bleeding diathesis** (prolonged PT).
   - Cerebral oedema, hypoglycaemia and sepsis.
2. **Chronic hepatitis, cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)** with chronic **B, C and D**.

## 9. Management

- Most patients **do not need hospital care**. Acute hepatitis is usually self-limiting, and the liver returns to normal structure and function.
- **Avoid:** sedatives and narcotics (liver-metabolised), **alcohol** during the acute illness, and **elective surgery** (risk of post-operative liver failure).
- Admit for vomiting and dehydration, prolonged PT, encephalopathy, pregnancy or suspected fulminant disease.

| Virus | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **A and E** | Supportive |
| **B** | Acute: supportive. Chronic: interferon or lamivudine (also tenofovir, entecavir, adefovir) |
| **C** | Historically dual therapy with weekly **pegylated interferon-alpha** (subcutaneous) plus oral **ribavirin** (a synthetic nucleoside analogue); only acute hepatitis C is treated early |

- **Goals of chronic hepatitis B therapy:** reduce DNA polymerase/HBV DNA to undetectable levels and convert HBeAg-positive patients to anti-HBe.
- Acute hepatitis B with persistent HBsAg and features suggesting chronic infection can be treated without waiting 6 months.
- **Liver transplantation** is very rarely needed for acute viral hepatitis with liver failure but is commonly done for **cirrhosis from chronic B and C**.

> **Check:** the slide describes interferon-ribavirin for hepatitis C; current practice (including Kenya MoH and WHO) uses **direct-acting antivirals** (for example sofosbuvir-based regimens) for 12 weeks, with cure above 90%. Interferon is obsolete for HCV.

## Key points to revise

1. Acute viral hepatitis is under 6 months; chronic is over 6 months.
2. HAV and HEV are faecal-oral and self-limiting; HEV is dangerous in pregnancy.
3. HBV chronicity is about 90% in infants and about 5% in adults; HDV needs HBV (coinfection or superinfection).
4. HCV: usually silent acute illness, about 90% chronic; HCV RNA is the earliest test; DAAs cure over 90%.
5. ALT is higher than AST in viral hepatitis; ALP rarely exceeds twice normal; prolonged PT marks severity.
6. Anti-HAV IgM = acute; HBsAg = infection; anti-HBs = immunity; anti-HBc IgM = recent infection or window period.
7. Fulminant failure (within 8 weeks) is commonest in B, D and E; complications are encephalopathy, hepatorenal syndrome and coagulopathy.
8. Avoid alcohol, sedatives and elective surgery; chronic B and C lead to cirrhosis and HCC.
