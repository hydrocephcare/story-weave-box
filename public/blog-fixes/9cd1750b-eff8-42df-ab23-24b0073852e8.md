# Shoulder Dystocia and Umbilical Cord Prolapse

**Year 4 · Obstetrics and Gynaecology · Obstetric emergencies**

> **In one line:** in **shoulder dystocia** the anterior shoulder impacts behind the pubic symphysis after the head is born, and in **cord prolapse** the cord descends below the presenting part and is compressed; both are unpredictable emergencies in which foetal hypoxia develops within minutes, so management is a rehearsed sequence (HELPERR; relieve compression and deliver immediately).

## What you must know

- Shoulder dystocia: incidence about **0.6-1.4%**; **cannot be predicted reliably**. Sign: **turtle sign**, no restitution.
- Risk factors: macrosomia (over 4.5 kg), **maternal diabetes**, previous shoulder dystocia, obesity, post-term, prolonged second stage, instrumental delivery.
- Management: **HELPERR**; **McRoberts** and **suprapubic pressure** first. **No fundal pressure.**
- Complications: hypoxia, **Erb's palsy (C5-C6)**, fractured clavicle or humerus, PPH, perineal tears.
- Cord prolapse: incidence about 0.2-0.5%; push up the presenting part, position the mother, **immediate caesarean section**.
- Most important risk factor for cord prolapse: **malpresentation** and an unengaged head with membrane rupture (including **amniotomy**).

## 1. Shoulder dystocia

### 1.1 Definition and incidence
The anterior shoulder impacts behind the pubic symphysis after the head has been delivered, so that routine traction fails. Incidence is about **0.6-1.4%** of vaginal births and it **cannot be predicted reliably**; most cases occur in babies of normal weight.

### 1.2 Risk factors
- Foetal **macrosomia** (over 4.5 kg).
- **Maternal diabetes** (the baby is bigger across the shoulders and chest).
- Previous shoulder dystocia (recurrence is increased).
- **Obesity**, **post-term** pregnancy.
- Prolonged second stage and **instrumental delivery**.

### 1.3 Recognition
The **turtle sign** (the head retracts against the perineum) and **no restitution** of the head after delivery. Do not pull harder.

### 1.4 Management: HELPERR
Call for help first, and **note the time**.

| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| **H** | **Help**: call obstetrician, anaesthetist, paediatrician, midwife |
| **E** | **Evaluate for episiotomy** (it does not release bony obstruction but makes room for internal manoeuvres) |
| **L** | **Legs**: **McRoberts position** (hyperflex the thighs onto the abdomen). This resolves about 40-50% |
| **P** | **Pressure**: **suprapubic pressure** just above the symphysis, directed to adduct the shoulder (Rubin's manoeuvre) |
| **E** | **Enter** the vagina: internal rotation (**Rubin**, **Woods screw**) |
| **R** | **Remove the posterior arm** (sweeping across the chest) |
| **R** | **Roll** the patient to **all fours** (Gaskin manoeuvre) |

- Do **not** apply **fundal pressure** (it worsens impaction) or excessive downward traction (brachial plexus injury).
- **Last-resort options**: **cleidotomy**, **symphysiotomy** and the **Zavanelli manoeuvre** (cephalic replacement followed by caesarean section).

> **Exam tip:** the first two actions after recognition are call for help and McRoberts; never fundal pressure.

### 1.5 Complications

- **Foetal**: hypoxia (time is critical, deliver within about 5 minutes), **brachial plexus injury** (**Erb's palsy, C5-C6**), fractured clavicle or humerus.
- **Maternal**: postpartum haemorrhage and perineal tears.

### 1.6 After the event
Document the sequence, times and manoeuvres; debrief the parents; and plan for a recurrence (consider elective caesarean in a future pregnancy with macrosomia or diabetes). Prevent through control of maternal diabetes and weight, and avoiding the delivery of a very large baby by the vaginal route when estimated foetal weight is high in a diabetic.

## 2. Umbilical cord prolapse

### 2.1 Definition
The cord descends **below the presenting part** after membrane rupture (**overt prolapse**), or lies alongside in front of it with intact membranes (**cord presentation**). Incidence about **0.2-0.5%**.

### 2.2 Risk factors
- Malpresentation (**breech, transverse lie**).
- Polyhydramnios, prematurity, multiple pregnancy, long cord.
- High or unengaged presenting part.
- **Amniotomy** and external cephalic version.

### 2.3 Diagnosis
A cord felt in the vagina or visible at the introitus, or sudden foetal bradycardia (variable decelerations) after rupture of the membranes. Always perform a vaginal examination when there is abnormal CTG after rupture.

### 2.4 Management (an emergency)

1. **Call for help** and prepare for immediate **caesarean section**.
2. **Relieve compression**:
   - The examiner's hand pushes the presenting part up and keeps it there.
   - **Knee-chest** or Trendelenburg/left lateral position.
   - Fill the bladder with **500 mL saline** via a catheter.
3. **Minimise handling** of the cord (spasm) and keep it warm and moist.
4. **Tocolysis** (terbutaline) if contractions are strong.
5. **Deliver quickly**: caesarean section unless full dilatation and instrumental delivery is imminent; resuscitate the baby.

> **Remember:** do not push the cord back into the uterus; keep the presenting part off the cord until the baby is delivered.

## Key points to revise

1. Shoulder dystocia: anterior shoulder impacted behind the symphysis; turtle sign.
2. McRoberts and suprapubic pressure first (HELPERR); never fundal pressure.
3. Internal manoeuvres: Rubin, Woods screw, posterior arm, all fours; last resort cleidotomy, symphysiotomy, Zavanelli.
4. Excess traction causes Erb's palsy (C5-C6).
5. Cord prolapse: malpresentation, polyhydramnios, prematurity, amniotomy.
6. Push up the presenting part, knee-chest position, fill the bladder with 500 mL saline, give tocolysis.
7. Immediate caesarean section for cord prolapse unless delivery is imminent.
