## Part 1: Bacteriophages

## 1. Introduction

**[MCQ — HIGH YIELD]**

Bacteriophages — commonly called phages — are viruses that infect bacteria. They are the most abundant biological entities on Earth, outnumbering bacteria by a factor of 10 to 1. In medicine, they are important for several reasons — they carry toxin genes that make bacteria pathogenic through lysogenic conversion, they are used as tools in molecular biology and research, and they are increasingly being explored as therapeutic agents against antibiotic-resistant bacterial infections in what is called phage therapy. At MKU, bacteriophages appear in MCQs and the course outline specifically highlights their use in combating human and animal diseases.

## 2. Structure of Bacteriophages

**[MCQ — HIGH YIELD]**

Bacteriophages vary in morphology but the classic bacteriophage — the T4 phage — has a complex structure:

**Head (Capsid):**
- Icosahedral protein capsid
- Contains the viral genome — double-stranded DNA in most phages
- Protects the genome

**Tail:**
- Hollow protein tube attached to head
- Used to inject DNA into the bacterial cell
- Components:
  - **Tail sheath** — contractile; contracts to drive tail core through bacterial wall
  - **Tail core** — hollow tube through which DNA is injected
  - **Base plate** — platform at base of tail
  - **Tail fibres** — long thin protein fibres extending from base plate; mediate attachment to bacterial surface receptors
  - **Tail pins** — short spikes on base plate; help anchor to bacterial surface

**Other phage morphologies:**
- Filamentous phages — long thin rods; no tail; e.g., M13
- Icosahedral phages without tails — e.g., PhiX174
- Enveloped phages — rare

## 3. Lytic Cycle of Bacteriophages

**[MCQ — HIGH YIELD]**

The lytic cycle results in destruction of the bacterial host cell and release of new phage particles.

### Steps
**Step 1: Adsorption**
- Tail fibres of phage bind specifically to receptors on bacterial cell surface
- Receptors may be: lipopolysaccharide (LPS), teichoic acids, pili, outer membrane proteins
- Highly specific — phage can only infect certain bacterial strains

**Step 2: Injection**
- Tail sheath contracts
- Tail core penetrates bacterial cell wall and membrane
- Phage DNA injected into bacterial cytoplasm
- Capsid and tail remain outside — the empty ghost

**Step 3: Early Gene Expression**
- Phage DNA transcribed by bacterial RNA polymerase → early mRNA → early proteins
- Early proteins:
  - Shut down bacterial gene expression — degrade bacterial DNA
  - Encode new enzymes for phage DNA replication

**Step 4: Phage DNA Replication**
- Phage DNA polymerase replicates phage genome many times
- Many copies of phage genome accumulate

**Step 5: Late Gene Expression**
- Late mRNA transcribed → late structural proteins
- Capsid proteins, tail proteins, tail fibres

**Step 6: Assembly**
- Heads assembled and filled with phage DNA
- Tails assembled separately
- Heads and tails joined

**Step 7: Lysis**
- Phage encodes **lysin** (endolysin) — enzyme that degrades bacterial peptidoglycan cell wall
- Also encodes **holin** — creates pores in bacterial inner membrane allowing lysin access to cell wall
- Bacterial cell wall weakened → osmotic lysis → cell bursts
- Releases 100–200 new phage particles per infected bacterium

## 4. Lysogenic Cycle

**[MCQ — HIGH YIELD]**

In the lysogenic cycle, phage DNA integrates into the bacterial chromosome and replicates with it without killing the cell.

### Steps
**Step 1: Adsorption and Injection**
- Same as lytic cycle — phage DNA injected into bacterium

**Step 2: Integration**
- Phage DNA circularizes
- Phage integrase enzyme catalyses site-specific recombination
- Phage DNA integrates into bacterial chromosome at a specific attachment site (attB)
- Integrated phage DNA called **prophage**

**Step 3: Replication with Host**
- Prophage replicates passively every time bacterium divides
- All daughter cells carry prophage — vertical transmission
- No phage particles produced
- Bacterium appears normal

**Step 4: Induction**
- Certain stimuli trigger excision of prophage and switch to lytic cycle:
  - UV radiation
  - Chemical mutagens
  - DNA damage (activates SOS response in bacteria)
- **RecA protein** activated → cleaves CI repressor protein → lytic genes expressed → lytic cycle begins

**Step 5: Excision**
- Phage integrase + excisionase catalyse excision of prophage
- Phage DNA circularizes → lytic cycle proceeds → cell eventually lysed

## 5. Lysogenic Conversion — Most Medically Important Concept

**[MCQ — HIGH YIELD] [ESSAY — MCQ CONTEXT]**

Lysogenic conversion occurs when integration of a prophage changes the phenotype of the host bacterium. This is medically critical because many bacterial virulence factors and toxins are encoded on prophages.

**Key examples — must know all of these:**

| Bacterium | Phage | Virulence Factor Added | Disease |
|---|---|---|---|
| Corynebacterium diphtheriae | Corynephage beta (carries tox gene) | Diphtheria toxin | Diphtheria — only toxigenic strains cause disease |
| Vibrio cholerae | CTXφ phage | Cholera toxin (CT) | Cholera |
| Clostridium botulinum | Various phages | Botulinum toxin | Botulism |
| Streptococcus pyogenes | Phage T12 | Erythrogenic toxin (Spe A) | Scarlet fever |
| Staphylococcus aureus | Various phages | Enterotoxins, PVL toxin | Food poisoning, necrotising pneumonia |
| Escherichia coli O157:H7 | Phage 933W | Shiga toxin | Haemorrhagic colitis, HUS |

**Clinical significance of lysogenic conversion:**
- A bacterium is only virulent if it carries the prophage encoding the toxin
- Explains why not all strains of C. diphtheriae cause diphtheria — only phage-carrying strains
- Loss of prophage = loss of virulence
- Phage can transfer toxin genes between bacteria — contributes to emergence of new virulent strains

**Superinfection immunity:**
- A lysogenized bacterium is immune to superinfection by the same or closely related phage
- CI repressor protein produced by prophage represses both prophage genes and incoming phage genes

## 6. Phage Therapy — Medical Applications

**[MCQ — HIGH YIELD] [ESSAY — ONCE based on course outline emphasis]**

Phage therapy is the use of bacteriophages to treat bacterial infections, particularly those caused by antibiotic-resistant bacteria. It is one of the most promising emerging approaches to the global antibiotic resistance crisis.

### How Phage Therapy Works
- Lytic phages administered — target specific bacterial pathogens
- Phage infects and lyses bacteria — kills them
- Highly specific — targets only the pathogenic bacterium; does not harm normal microbiota or human cells
- As bacteria develop resistance to phage — phage can co-evolve — unlike antibiotics

### Advantages of Phage Therapy

- **High specificity** — targets only the specific bacterial strain; does not disrupt normal flora
- **Self-amplifying** — phage replicates at the site of infection; dose increases where needed
- **Co-evolution** — phage can evolve to counter bacterial resistance mechanisms
- **Effective against biofilms** — phage produce enzymes that degrade biofilm matrix
- **Synergy with antibiotics** — phage and antibiotics work well together; phage can re-sensitize resistant bacteria to antibiotics
- **Low inherent toxicity** — phage proteins generally non-toxic to humans

### Disadvantages and Challenges

- **Narrow host range** — phage only infects specific bacterial strains; need to identify correct phage for each infection
- **Bacterial resistance to phage** — bacteria can develop resistance by modifying surface receptors
- **Immune response** — human immune system can neutralize phages, especially with repeated use
- **Regulatory challenges** — difficult to fit into standard drug approval frameworks
- **Need for phage banks** — require large collections of characterized phages
- **Limited clinical trial data** — most evidence from case reports and small studies

### Current Applications
- Compassionate use for life-threatening antibiotic-resistant infections — MDR Pseudomonas, Staphylococcus, Acinetobacter
- Wound infections, prosthetic joint infections, endocarditis
- Several clinical trials ongoing globally
- Eastern Europe (Georgia, Poland) — phage therapy has been used clinically for decades

### Other Medical Applications of Phages
- **Phage typing** — using phages to identify and subtype bacterial strains for epidemiological investigations
- **Phage display** — technique for discovering new antibodies and drugs
- **Gene delivery vectors** — phage used to deliver DNA in research
- **Diagnostic tools** — phage-based rapid bacterial detection systems

## Part 2: Laboratory Diagnosis of Viral Infections

## 1. Introduction

**[MCQ — HIGH YIELD] [ESSAY — ONCE in context of HIV and CMV diagnosis]**

Laboratory diagnosis of viral infections is essential for confirming clinical diagnoses, guiding antiviral therapy, monitoring treatment response, public health surveillance, and epidemiological investigation. No single test is perfect for all viruses — the choice of diagnostic method depends on the virus suspected, the stage of infection, the specimen available, the urgency, and the resources available. At MKU, laboratory diagnosis appears in MCQs and is embedded in essay questions on specific viruses such as HIV, CMV, rabies, and HSV.

## 2. Specimen Collection — General Principles

**[MCQ — HIGH YIELD]**

The choice of specimen depends entirely on which virus is suspected and which organ it targets.

| Virus | Preferred Specimens |
|---|---|
| Influenza | Nasopharyngeal swab, bronchoalveolar lavage |
| HSV | Vesicle fluid, genital swab, CSF (encephalitis) |
| CMV | Blood (PCR), urine (culture/PCR), tissue biopsy |
| HIV | Blood — serum for antibody, plasma for viral load |
| Hepatitis viruses | Serum — antibodies and antigens |
| Rabies | Brain tissue (post-mortem), skin biopsy (ante-mortem), saliva, CSF |
| Enterovirus/meningitis | CSF, stool, throat swab |
| Rotavirus | Stool |
| Adenovirus | Respiratory secretions, stool, conjunctival swab, urine |
| HBV | Blood — serum |

**General principles:**
- Collect specimen as early as possible in infection — viral load highest early
- Use appropriate transport medium — viral transport medium (VTM) for cell culture
- Keep specimens cold — most viruses labile at room temperature
- CSF specimens — process immediately; do not refrigerate

## 3. Methods of Viral Diagnosis

### Method 1: Direct Microscopy

**[MCQ — HIGH YIELD]**

**Electron Microscopy (EM):**
- Visualizes virus particles directly
- Can identify virus by morphology — shape, size, surface features
- Very fast — results within hours
- Used for: rotavirus in stool, poxvirus, herpesvirus
- Limitation: expensive, requires expertise, cannot distinguish closely related viruses

**Light Microscopy — Cytopathic Effects and Inclusion Bodies:**
- Infected cells show characteristic changes visible under light microscope
- **Cytopathic effect (CPE)** — cell damage caused by viral replication; used in cell culture diagnosis
- **Inclusion bodies** — accumulations of viral components inside cells; visible on stained tissue sections

**Must-know inclusion bodies:**

| Virus | Inclusion Body | Location | Description |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rabies | Negri bodies | Cytoplasm of neurons | Eosinophilic; hippocampus and Purkinje cells |
| CMV | Owl-eye inclusions | Intranuclear | Large nucleus with clear halo around inclusion |
| HSV/VZV | Cowdry type A bodies | Intranuclear | Eosinophilic intranuclear inclusions; multinucleated giant cells |
| Adenovirus | Smudge cells | Intranuclear | Basophilic intranuclear inclusions |
| Measles | Warthin-Finkeldey giant cells | Lymphoid tissue | Multinucleated giant cells |
| Poxvirus | Guarnieri bodies | Cytoplasm | Cytoplasmic inclusions |
| Yellow fever | Councilman bodies | Hepatocytes | Eosinophilic degeneration |

**Tzanck Smear:**
- Scraping from base of vesicle → stained with Giemsa or Wright stain
- Shows multinucleated giant cells — indicates herpesvirus infection (HSV or VZV)
- Cannot distinguish HSV from VZV — not specific
- Fast, cheap, bedside test

### Method 2: Viral Culture

**[MCQ — HIGH YIELD]**

Viral culture is the gold standard for many viral diagnoses — it produces live virus that can be further characterized. However it is slow and requires specialized facilities.

**Cell Culture:**
- Specimen inoculated onto monolayer of susceptible cells
- Incubated at 37°C
- Observed daily for cytopathic effects (CPE):
  - Cell rounding and death
  - Syncytia formation — multinucleated giant cells (paramyxoviruses, HSV)
  - Plaques — clear areas of cell lysis
  - Inclusion body formation
- CPE may take days to weeks depending on virus

**Shell Vial Culture (Rapid Culture):**
- Centrifugation of specimen onto cell monolayer — enhances viral adsorption
- After 24–48 hours — stain with fluorescent antibody to detect early viral antigens
- Much faster than conventional culture — results in 24–48 hours
- Used for: CMV, HSV, influenza, adenovirus

**Embryonated Eggs:**
- Virus inoculated into different compartments of fertilized egg
- Used for: influenza virus (allantoic cavity), poxviruses
- Largely replaced by cell culture for most viruses
- Still used in influenza vaccine production

**Limitations of culture:**
- Slow — days to weeks
- Some viruses cannot be cultured — e.g., HBV, HCV, HPV, norovirus
- Requires BSL laboratory
- Risk to laboratory staff

### Method 3: Serology — Detection of Antiviral Antibodies

**[MCQ — HIGH YIELD]**

Serology detects antibodies produced by the patient in response to viral infection. It is one of the most widely used diagnostic approaches in virology.

**Principles:**
- **IgM antibodies** — appear early (5–14 days after infection); indicate acute/recent infection; short-lived (3–6 months)
- **IgG antibodies** — appear later (2–4 weeks); indicate past infection or vaccination; persist lifelong
- **Four-fold rise in IgG titre** between acute and convalescent samples — confirms recent infection
- **Seroconversion** — appearance of antibodies in a previously seronegative patient — confirms primary infection

**Common serological tests:**

**ELISA (Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay):**
- Most widely used serological method
- High sensitivity — good screening test
- Can detect both IgM and IgG
- Automated — high throughput
- Used for: HIV screening, HBsAg, anti-HCV, anti-HAV, CMV, EBV, rubella

**Western Blot:**
- Confirmatory test — high specificity
- Viral proteins separated by electrophoresis → transferred to membrane → probed with patient serum
- Detects antibodies against specific viral proteins
- Used for: HIV confirmation, HCV confirmation, Lyme disease confirmation

**Haemagglutination Inhibition (HAI):**
- Patient serum + virus + red blood cells
- If patient has antibodies → inhibit viral haemagglutinin → red cells do not agglutinate
- Used for: influenza, measles, mumps serology

**Complement Fixation Test (CFT):**
- Older method — largely replaced by ELISA
- Detects antibodies against many viruses

**Neutralisation Test:**
- Gold standard for measuring protective antibody
- Patient serum mixed with live virus → inoculated onto cells
- If neutralising antibodies present → no CPE
- Used for: polio antibody titres, rabies antibody titres post-vaccination

**Paul-Bunnell (Monospot) Test:**
- Detects heterophile antibodies — non-specific antibodies produced during EBV infection
- These antibodies agglutinate sheep or horse red blood cells
- Used for: diagnosis of infectious mononucleosis
- Positive in 85–90% of EBV mononucleosis cases
- False negatives in young children and early disease

**Limitations of serology:**
- Window period — antibodies not yet detectable in early infection
- Cannot distinguish active from past infection (IgG)
- Cross-reactivity between related viruses — e.g., flaviviruses (Zika, dengue, yellow fever)
- Immunocompromised patients may not mount antibody response

### Method 4: Antigen Detection

**[MCQ — HIGH YIELD]**

Directly detects viral proteins (antigens) in patient specimens — does not wait for antibody response.

**Direct Fluorescent Antibody (DFA) Test:**
- Fluorescently labelled antibodies applied to patient specimen (cells from lesion, tissue)
- If viral antigen present → antibodies bind → fluorescence visible under UV microscope
- Fast — results within hours
- Used for: rabies (brain tissue), HSV, VZV, RSV, adenovirus, influenza

**Rapid Antigen Tests:**
- Lateral flow immunoassay — similar to pregnancy test
- Result in 15–30 minutes
- Used for: influenza (rapid flu test), RSV, adenovirus (stool), COVID-19
- Less sensitive than PCR — negative result does not exclude infection

**Specific Antigen Tests:**
- **HBsAg** — hepatitis B surface antigen; first marker of HBV infection
- **HBeAg** — hepatitis B e antigen; marker of active replication
- **HIV p24 antigen** — detectable before antibodies; combined with antibody in 4th generation HIV tests
- **CMV pp65 antigenemia** — detects CMV antigen in leukocytes; monitors transplant patients
- **Dengue NS1 antigen** — detectable in first 5 days of dengue fever

### Method 5: Molecular Diagnosis — PCR and Nucleic Acid Tests

**[ESSAY — REPEATED] [MCQ — HIGH YIELD]**

Molecular methods detect viral nucleic acid (DNA or RNA) directly. They are the most sensitive and specific methods available and have revolutionized virology diagnostics.

**Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR):**
- Amplifies specific viral DNA sequences millions of times
- Detects even tiny amounts of viral DNA
- For RNA viruses — reverse transcription PCR (RT-PCR):
  - RNA first converted to cDNA by reverse transcriptase
  - cDNA then amplified by PCR
- Results: positive or negative

**Real-Time PCR (Quantitative PCR — qPCR):**
- PCR with fluorescent detection in real time
- Quantifies viral load — number of viral copies per mL of blood or CSF
- Used for: HIV viral load monitoring, HBV DNA, HCV RNA, CMV DNA in transplant patients
- Guides treatment decisions and monitors response to antivirals

**Applications of PCR in virology:**

| Virus | Specimen | Use |
|---|---|---|
| HIV | Plasma | Viral load monitoring; early infant diagnosis |
| HSV | CSF, vesicle fluid | Gold standard for HSV encephalitis and genital herpes |
| CMV | Blood, urine, CSF | Diagnosis and monitoring in immunocompromised |
| HCV | Serum | Confirms active infection; monitors treatment |
| HBV | Serum | Viral load; guides treatment |
| Influenza | Nasopharyngeal swab | Rapid diagnosis; subtyping |
| Rabies | Saliva, CSF, skin biopsy | Ante-mortem diagnosis |
| Enteroviruses | CSF, stool | Meningitis/encephalitis diagnosis |
| COVID-19 | Nasopharyngeal swab | Diagnosis |

**Advantages of PCR:**
- Extremely sensitive — detects very few copies of virus
- Very specific — detects specific viral sequences
- Fast — results within hours
- Can detect viruses that cannot be cultured
- Quantitative — measures viral load
- Can detect virus in window period before antibodies appear

**Disadvantages of PCR:**
- Expensive — limits use in resource-poor settings
- Risk of contamination — false positives
- Requires specialized equipment and trained staff
- Detects nucleic acid — cannot distinguish live from dead virus

**Other Molecular Methods:**
- **In situ hybridisation** — detects viral nucleic acid within tissue sections
- **Branched DNA (bDNA) signal amplification** — quantitative; used for HBV and HCV viral loads
- **NASBA (Nucleic Acid Sequence-Based Amplification)** — isothermal amplification; used for RNA viruses
- **Next Generation Sequencing (NGS)** — identifies all nucleic acid in specimen; used for unknown pathogens and drug resistance testing

### Method 6: Haematology and Biochemistry

**[MCQ — HIGH YIELD]**

Routine blood tests provide supportive evidence for viral diagnosis:

- **Full Blood Count (FBC):**
  - Lymphocytosis with atypical lymphocytes — EBV mononucleosis
  - Leukopenia and thrombocytopenia — dengue fever, influenza
  - CD4 count — monitors HIV disease progression
- **Liver Function Tests (LFTs):**
  - Raised ALT and AST — hepatitis (viral hepatitis, EBV hepatitis, CMV hepatitis)
  - ALT more specific for hepatocyte damage than AST
- **CSF Analysis:**
  - Lymphocytic pleocytosis + normal glucose + mildly elevated protein → viral meningitis/encephalitis
  - Distinguishes viral from bacterial meningitis (bacterial → neutrophilic pleocytosis, low glucose)

## 4. Choosing the Right Test — Summary

**[MCQ — HIGH YIELD]**

| Situation | Best Test |
|---|---|
| Acute viral infection — first few days | PCR or antigen detection |
| Acute viral infection — after 7–10 days | Serology (IgM) |
| Past infection or immunity | Serology (IgG) |
| Monitoring antiviral treatment | Quantitative PCR (viral load) |
| Unknown virus in specimen | Electron microscopy or NGS |
| Vesicular skin lesion | Tzanck smear (rapid), PCR (definitive) |
| Viral encephalitis | CSF PCR |
| HIV screening | 4th generation ELISA (p24 Ag + Ab) |
| HIV confirmation | Western blot |
| HBV acute infection | HBsAg + anti-HBc IgM |
| Infectious mononucleosis | Monospot test + EBV IgM serology |

## Part 3: HPV and Parvovirus B19

## 1. Introduction

**HPV: [MCQ — HIGH YIELD] [ESSAY — once in context of oncogenesis]**
**Parvovirus B19: [MCQ — HIGH YIELD]**

Human Papillomavirus (HPV) and Parvovirus B19 are both non-enveloped DNA viruses that cause important clinical diseases. HPV is one of the most significant oncogenic viruses in human medicine — responsible for virtually all cases of cervical cancer and many other anogenital and oropharyngeal malignancies. Parvovirus B19 is the cause of fifth disease in children and aplastic crisis in patients with haemolytic anaemia — it has a unique and clinically important tropism for erythroid progenitor cells. Both viruses appear in MCQs at MKU; HPV also appears in the context of oncogenesis essays.

## 2. Human Papillomavirus (HPV)

### Classification and Structure

**[MCQ — HIGH YIELD]**

- **Family:** Papillomaviridae
- **Genome:** Circular double-stranded DNA
- **Non-enveloped** — icosahedral capsid
- **Very stable** — resistant to drying, heat, many disinfectants — explains persistence on surfaces and fomites
- Over 200 types identified — classified by DNA sequence of L1 gene
- **Cannot be cultured in standard cell culture** — major limitation for research and diagnosis

**Viral proteins:**

**Early proteins (E proteins) — regulatory:**
- **E1, E2** — involved in DNA replication and transcription regulation
- **E6** — binds and degrades p53 tumour suppressor protein → prevents apoptosis
- **E7** — binds and inactivates Rb (retinoblastoma) protein → drives cell cycle progression
- E6 and E7 are the primary oncoproteins — most important for understanding HPV carcinogenesis

**Late proteins (L proteins) — structural:**
- **L1** — major capsid protein; basis of HPV vaccines and HPV genotyping
- **L2** — minor capsid protein

### HPV Types and Clinical Associations

**[MCQ — HIGH YIELD]**

| HPV Type | Risk Category | Disease |
|---|---|---|
| 6, 11 | Low risk | Genital warts (condylomata acuminata), laryngeal papillomatosis |
| 16, 18 | High risk | Cervical cancer (70% of cases), anal cancer, oropharyngeal cancer |
| 31, 33, 45, 52, 58 | High risk | Cervical cancer (remaining 30%) |
| 1, 2, 4 | Low risk | Common warts (verruca vulgaris) |
| 3, 10 | Low risk | Flat warts |

**Must know:**
- HPV 16 and 18 — cause 70% of all cervical cancers
- HPV 6 and 11 — cause 90% of genital warts — do NOT cause cancer
- HPV 16 — most strongly associated with oropharyngeal cancer and anal cancer

### Transmission

**[MCQ — HIGH YIELD]**

- **Sexual contact** — primary route for genital HPV; skin-to-skin genital contact, not just penetrative sex
- **Direct skin contact** — for cutaneous warts
- **Vertical transmission** — mother to infant during delivery → juvenile-onset recurrent respiratory papillomatosis
- **Fomites** — HPV stable on surfaces; rarely via contaminated objects
- **Auto-inoculation** — spreading warts to other body sites by touching

### Pathogenesis of HPV Infection

**[MCQ — HIGH YIELD] [ESSAY — oncogenesis context]**

**Step 1: Infection**
- Virus infects basal epithelial cells — requires microtrauma or break in epithelium to access basal layer
- Basal cells are the only dividing cells in epithelium — virus needs dividing cells for replication

**Step 2: Productive Infection (Non-oncogenic course)**

- In low-risk HPV types or in non-oncogenic infection:
- Virus replicates in basal cells → as cells differentiate and move to surface → viral DNA amplified
- L1 and L2 capsid proteins expressed in superficial layers → new virions assembled → shed from skin surface
- Koilocytes — characteristic cytological change — cells with perinuclear halo and irregular nuclei — diagnostic of HPV infection on Pap smear

**Step 3: Oncogenic Transformation (High-risk HPV)**
- In high-risk HPV types (16, 18) — viral DNA integrates into host chromosome
- Integration disrupts E2 gene → loss of E2 → loss of repression of E6 and E7
- E6 and E7 overexpressed → p53 degraded, Rb inactivated
- Cell loses growth control → accumulation of further mutations → invasive carcinoma
- This process takes 10–20 years from initial infection to invasive cancer — explains why cervical screening works

### Cervical Cancer and HPV — Must Know

**[ESSAY — once] [MCQ — HIGH YIELD]**

**Natural history of HPV and cervical cancer:**

1. HPV infection — most sexually active people infected at some point
2. 90% of infections cleared by immune system within 2 years
3. 10% develop persistent infection
4. Persistent high-risk HPV → cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN)
   - CIN 1 — mild dysplasia; most regress spontaneously
   - CIN 2 — moderate dysplasia
   - CIN 3 — severe dysplasia/carcinoma in situ; highest risk of progression
5. CIN 3 → invasive cervical cancer over 10–20 years if untreated

**Risk factors for progression from HPV to cancer:**
- Persistent high-risk HPV type (especially 16, 18)
- Immunosuppression (HIV infection)
- Smoking — impairs local immune response in cervix
- High parity
- Long-term oral contraceptive use
- Co-infection with other STIs (HSV, Chlamydia)

### Diseases Caused by HPV

**[MCQ — HIGH YIELD]**

**Genital Warts (Condylomata Acuminata):**
- Caused by HPV 6 and 11
- Soft, flesh-coloured, cauliflower-like lesions on genitalia, perianal region
- Usually multiple
- Not premalignant — low-risk types
- Treatment: topical podophyllin, podophyllotoxin, imiquimod, trichloroacetic acid, cryotherapy, surgical excision

**Cervical Cancer:**
- HPV 16 and 18 — 70% of cases
- Preceded by CIN — detectable by Pap smear
- Treatment: surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy depending on stage

**Other HPV-Associated Malignancies:**
- Vulvar cancer — HPV 16
- Vaginal cancer — HPV 16
- Penile cancer — HPV 16
- Anal cancer — HPV 16; especially in MSM and HIV-positive individuals
- Oropharyngeal cancer (tonsil, base of tongue) — HPV 16; rising incidence

**Recurrent Respiratory Papillomatosis (RRP):**
- HPV 6 and 11
- Papillomas (warts) in larynx and trachea
- Causes hoarseness and airway obstruction
- Juvenile-onset form — acquired during birth from infected mother
- Requires repeated surgical removal — not curable
- Can undergo malignant transformation — rare

**Common Warts (Verruca Vulgaris):**
- HPV 1, 2, 4
- Rough, raised papules on hands, fingers, knees
- Self-limiting in immunocompetent
- Treatment: salicylic acid, cryotherapy

**Plantar Warts (Verruca Plantaris):**
- HPV 1
- On soles of feet — grow inward due to pressure
- Painful

**Flat Warts (Verruca Plana):**
- HPV 3, 10
- Multiple flat-topped papules on face and hands
- Common in children

### Diagnosis of HPV

**[MCQ — HIGH YIELD]**

- **Pap smear (Papanicolaou smear)** — cervical cytology; detects abnormal cells (CIN) and koilocytes
- **Colposcopy** — visual examination of cervix with magnification; guided biopsy of abnormal areas
- **HPV DNA testing by PCR** — detects and genotypes HPV; used in cervical screening (co-testing with Pap)
- **Histology** — biopsy of cervix or wart; shows koilocytes, dysplasia, carcinoma in situ
- **No blood test for HPV** — no serological diagnosis

### Treatment of HPV Infections

**[MCQ — HIGH YIELD]**

- **No systemic antiviral for HPV** — no equivalent of acyclovir
- Treatment is directed at the lesion — not the virus itself

| Condition | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Genital warts | Podophyllotoxin, imiquimod, cryotherapy, TCA, surgical excision |
| CIN 1 | Watchful waiting — most regress |
| CIN 2/3 | LLETZ (Large Loop Excision of Transformation Zone), cone biopsy, cryotherapy |
| Cervical cancer | Surgery, radiotherapy, cisplatin-based chemotherapy |
| RRP | Repeated surgical debulking; adjuvant interferon-alpha |
| Common warts | Salicylic acid, cryotherapy |

### Prevention of HPV — Vaccines

**[ESSAY — oncogenesis context] [MCQ — HIGH YIELD]**

HPV vaccination is one of the most successful cancer prevention strategies in medicine.

**Available vaccines:**

| Vaccine | Types Covered | Doses | Target |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cervarix (bivalent) | 16, 18 | 2–3 doses | Cervical cancer prevention |
| Gardasil 4 (quadrivalent) | 6, 11, 16, 18 | 2–3 doses | Cervical cancer + genital warts |
| Gardasil 9 (nonavalent) | 6, 11, 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, 58 | 2–3 doses | Broader cancer prevention |

**Vaccine composition:**
- All HPV vaccines are **recombinant subunit vaccines** — contain L1 capsid protein only — no viral DNA
- L1 proteins self-assemble into **virus-like particles (VLPs)** — look like virus but contain no genetic material — cannot cause infection

**Efficacy:**
- Near 100% protection against HPV types covered when given before exposure
- Most effective when given before sexual debut — age 9–14 years
- Some benefit in older individuals not yet exposed to vaccine types

**Who should receive it:**
- Girls and boys — 9–14 years (primary target)
- Young women up to 26 years
- Men up to 26 years — especially MSM
- Immunocompromised up to 45 years

**HPV vaccine does NOT:**
- Treat existing HPV infection
- Replace cervical screening — vaccinated women still need Pap smears

## 3. Parvovirus B19

### Classification and Structure

**[MCQ — HIGH YIELD]**

- **Family:** Parvoviridae
- **Genus:** Erythroparvovirus
- **Genome:** Single-stranded DNA — linear
- **Non-enveloped** — extremely small; 18–26 nm — smallest human DNA virus
- **Receptor:** Erythrocyte P antigen (globoside) — expressed on erythroid progenitor cells in bone marrow, mature red blood cells, megakaryocytes, fetal liver cells, and fetal myocardial cells
- The receptor determines tissue tropism — explains why parvovirus B19 selectively attacks red blood cell precursors

### Transmission

**[MCQ — HIGH YIELD]**

- **Respiratory droplets** — primary route; virus shed in respiratory secretions
- **Blood transfusion** — virus present in blood during viraemic phase
- **Vertical transmission** — transplacental; mother to fetus
- **NOT fecal-oral** — distinguishes it from enteroviruses

### Pathogenesis

**[MCQ — HIGH YIELD]**

1. Virus inhaled → infects respiratory epithelium → viraemia
2. Virus binds P antigen on erythroid progenitor cells (BFU-E, CFU-E) in bone marrow
3. Lyses erythroid progenitors → stops red blood cell production for 7–10 days
4. In normal individuals — brief interruption of RBC production; compensated; no significant anaemia
5. Immune response (IgM then IgG) clears viraemia → RBC production resumes
6. Rash and arthralgia — immune complex mediated — not direct viral effect

**In patients with chronic haemolytic anaemia:**
- Normal RBC lifespan already shortened
- Even brief interruption of RBC production → rapid fall in haemoglobin → **aplastic crisis**
- Medical emergency

### Clinical Syndromes

**[MCQ — HIGH YIELD]**

**1. Fifth Disease (Erythema Infectiosum) — Healthy Children**
- Most common manifestation
- Called fifth disease — historically fifth in list of childhood exanthems
- **Incubation:** 4–14 days
- **Stage 1:** Mild prodrome — low fever, malaise, headache
- **Stage 2:** Characteristic facial rash — **slapped cheek appearance** — bright red rash on cheeks; circumoral pallor; pathognomonic
- **Stage 3:** Lacy reticular (net-like) rash on trunk and limbs — may last weeks; worsens with heat, exercise, sunlight
- Child is **no longer infectious** by the time rash appears — rash is immune-mediated
- Self-limiting — full recovery

**2. Aplastic Crisis — Patients with Haemolytic Anaemia**
- Most serious manifestation
- Occurs in patients with: sickle cell disease, hereditary spherocytosis, thalassaemia, G6PD deficiency, pyruvate kinase deficiency, autoimmune haemolytic anaemia
- Sudden severe anaemia — haemoglobin may drop dramatically within days
- Requires blood transfusion
- No rash — viraemia still present; contagious
- Medical emergency

**3. Chronic Pure Red Cell Aplasia — Immunocompromised Patients**
- Patients with: HIV/AIDS, organ transplant, congenital immunodeficiency, leukaemia on chemotherapy
- Cannot produce antibodies to clear virus
- Persistent viraemia → continued destruction of erythroid progenitors → chronic severe anaemia
- No rash (no immune response to produce rash)
- Treatment: IVIG — provides passive antibodies to clear virus

**4. Hydrops Fetalis — Infection in Pregnancy**
- Parvovirus B19 crosses placenta → infects fetal erythroid progenitors
- Fetal bone marrow immature → more susceptible
- Fetal anaemia → high-output cardiac failure → generalised oedema (hydrops fetalis)
- Can cause fetal death
- Risk highest before 20 weeks gestation
- Management: fetal blood sampling and intrauterine blood transfusion
- NOT associated with congenital malformations — unlike CMV and rubella

**5. Arthropathy — Adult Women**
- Symmetrical small joint arthritis — hands, wrists, knees, ankles
- Common in adult women — 50–60% of infected adult women
- Usually resolves in weeks — can persist months
- Can mimic rheumatoid arthritis

**6. Myocarditis**
- Rare but important
- P antigen expressed on fetal myocardium — explains fetal myocarditis contributing to hydrops
- Adult myocarditis — rare

### Diagnosis

**[MCQ — HIGH YIELD]**

- **Serology — IgM anti-B19** — confirms acute infection in immunocompetent patients; gold standard for fifth disease and aplastic crisis diagnosis
- **Serology — IgG anti-B19** — past infection and immunity; present in 60–70% of adults
- **PCR — B19 DNA** — gold standard for immunocompromised patients (cannot make antibodies); also for diagnosis in aplastic crisis, hydrops fetalis
- **Blood film** — giant pronormoblasts with intranuclear inclusions — pathognomonic; seen in bone marrow during aplastic crisis
- **Ultrasound** — for hydrops fetalis in pregnancy

### Treatment

**[MCQ — HIGH YIELD]**

- **Fifth disease** — supportive only; self-limiting
- **Aplastic crisis** — blood transfusion; supportive; self-limiting once immune response clears virus
- **Chronic pure red cell aplasia in immunocompromised** — IVIG (intravenous immunoglobulin); provides passive antibodies
- **Hydrops fetalis** — intrauterine blood transfusion
- **No specific antiviral drug** for parvovirus B19

### Prevention

**[MCQ — LOW YIELD]**

- No vaccine available
- Pregnant women and immunocompromised — advised to avoid contact with known cases
- Exclusion from school/work — not recommended once rash appears (no longer infectious at that stage)
- Good hand hygiene
