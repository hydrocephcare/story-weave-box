## Vector Control Methods
**1. Explain how Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) works as a biological control agent and why it's preferred over chemical insecticides.**

**Answer:** Bt is a bacterial biopesticide that blocks insect chitin biosynthesis pathways, killing mosquito larvae. It's preferred over chemical insecticides because:

- Specific to target insects (narrow spectrum)
- Doesn't persist in the environment
- No bioaccumulation in fatty tissues
- Minimal impact on non-target organisms

**2. Compare and contrast organophosphates and carbamates in terms of mechanism of action and safety profile.**

**Answer:**

- **Mechanism:** Both block/bind to acetylcholinesterase, preventing acetylcholine breakdown
- **Safety differences: **Carbamates have lower mammalian/human toxicity
- Carbamates have better biodegradability
- Carbamate inhibition is **reversible**
- Chronic carbamate exposure less likely to cause illness than organophosphates

**3. Why were chlorinated hydrocarbons like DDT banned despite their effectiveness? Discuss two specific concerns.**

**Answer:**

- **Long environmental persistence** - Relatively stable, don't break down easily
- **Bioaccumulation** - Lipid/fat soluble, accumulate in fatty tissues through the food chain, reaching toxic levels in top predators

**4. Describe the sterile male technique and explain why it's considered a genetic engineering approach to vector control.**

**Answer:** Sterile males are produced and released to mate with wild females, resulting in no viable offspring and population reduction. It's considered genetic engineering because it modifies the reproductive capacity of the vector population, changing the target site/genetic makeup without using chemicals.

**5. Explain the mechanism of action of pyrethroids and why they are commonly used in ITNs and LLINs.**

**Answer:** Pyrethroids bind to voltage-gated sodium channels and delay their inactivation, increasing membrane permeability. This results in excitation, lack of coordination, and paralysis (knockdown effect). They're used in ITNs/LLINs because:

- Relatively safe for humans
- Rapid action (knockdown effect)
- Effective at low doses
- Example: Permethrin, Deltamethrin

**6. What is IRS (Indoor Residual Spraying) and why is it effective against endophilic mosquitoes?**

**Answer:** IRS is the application of insecticides on interior walls of houses and structures. It's effective against endophilic mosquitoes (those that rest indoors) because:

- Mosquitoes rest on walls after blood feeding
- Contact with treated surfaces kills them
- Provides long-lasting protection (weeks to months)
- Targets mosquitoes when they're most vulnerable

**7. Describe how Toxorhynchites species function as biological control agents.**

**Answer:** Toxorhynchites mosquito larvae (e.g., T. rutilus) are predators that feed on larvae of disease-transmitting mosquitoes including Anopheles, Culex, and Aedes. Adults feed only on sugar solutions (not blood), making them non-vectors. They provide natural population control without chemicals.

**8. What are Insect Growth Regulators (IGRs) and how do they control vector populations?**

**Answer:** IGRs are chemicals that block insect sugar/chitin biosynthesis pathways, disrupting normal development and molting. Examples include Pyriproxyfen and Diflubenzuron. They prevent larvae from developing into adults, reducing vector populations without killing adult mosquitoes directly.

## Mosquito Biology and Control
**9. Explain why Anopheles mosquitoes are more efficient malaria vectors than Culex or Aedes species. Include at least two behavioral characteristics.**

**Answer:** Anopheles behavioral characteristics that enhance malaria transmission:

- **Anthropophilic** - Prefer human blood meals
- **Endophagic** - Feed indoors where humans sleep
- **Endophilic** - Rest indoors after feeding These behaviors maximize human-mosquito contact and facilitate Plasmodium parasite transmission.

**10. How can you distinguish between Anopheles and Culex larvae in their aquatic habitat?**

**Answer:**

- **Anopheles larvae:** Lie **parallel** to water surface, breathe through spiracles on body side
- **Culex larvae:** Hang at **45° angle** to surface, breathe via a **siphon**

**11. Describe the bimodal biting pattern of Aedes mosquitoes and explain its significance for disease transmission.**

**Answer:** Aedes mosquitoes have **two peak feeding times:**

- Early morning
- Late evening

**Significance:** This pattern increases disease transmission because mosquitoes bite multiple hosts during peak human activity periods, facilitating arbovirus spread (dengue, Zika, chikungunya, yellow fever).

**12. What is transovarial transmission? Provide one example of a disease transmitted this way.**

**Answer:** Transovarial transmission is when a pathogen passes from infected female mosquito to her eggs, so adults emerge already infected with the virus.

**Examples:**

- Culex: Rift Valley fever, West Nile virus
- Aedes: Dengue, Zika, yellow fever, chikungunya

**13. Compare the breeding sites of Anopheles, Culex, and Aedes mosquitoes.**

**Answer:**

- **Anopheles:** Stagnant water with vegetation and algae; clean natural water bodies
- **Culex:** Contaminated water (sewage, drains); polluted water
- **Aedes:** Clean containers (tires, flower vases, artificial containers) - "container breeders"

**14. Describe the egg-laying behavior of Anopheles, Culex, and Aedes mosquitoes.**

**Answer:**

- **Anopheles:** Eggs laid **singly**, one at a time; float individually on water surface
- **Culex:** Eggs laid **simultaneously**, attached to each other forming a **raft**; float as a raft
- **Aedes:** Eggs laid in containers; can survive desiccation

**15. What is the significance of An. stephensi being recently reported in Africa?**

**Answer:** An. stephensi is a supervector that:

- Transmits malaria primarily in Asia
- Unlike other African Anopheles species, can **withstand high temperatures and arid conditions**
- Its presence in Africa threatens malaria control because it can breed in urban areas and drought-prone regions where other vectors cannot
- Expands malaria transmission to new geographic areas

**16. Explain the complete metamorphosis (holometabolous) life cycle of mosquitoes.**

**Answer:** Complete metamorphosis includes four distinct stages:

- **Egg** - Laid on/in water
- **Larva** - 4 instars (L1-L4); aquatic, feeding stage
- **Pupa** - Aquatic, resting/non-feeding stage; comma-shaped; emerges after 2-3 days
- **Adult** - Terrestrial, flying, reproductive stage Duration depends on temperature (optimal 25°C) and humidity (78%)

**17. Why do female mosquitoes require blood meals while males do not?**

**Answer:**

- **Females:** Require blood meals for egg development (protein needed for oogenesis) - they are **anautogenous**
- **Males:** Feed only on sugar solutions (plant nectar) for energy
- Both sexes feed on sugar solutions, but only females need blood for reproduction

**18. Describe the resting position and antenna characteristics that distinguish male from female Anopheles mosquitoes.**

**Answer:**

- **Resting position:** Both rest at **45° angle** to surface (unlike Culex at nearly parallel)
- **Antennae:****Males:** Bushy/plumose (feathery)
- **Females:** Smooth/pilose (few hairs)

- **Feeding:** Only females take blood meals

## Sandflies and Phlebotominae
**19. Describe the characteristics that make sandflies (Phlebotominae) important disease vectors.**

**Answer:**

- **Weak fliers** - stay close to breeding sites
- **Short mouthparts** - bite exposed body parts
- **Bimodal biting** - early morning and evening
- **Prefer high humidity** - cannot withstand desiccation
- **Anthropophilic** - prefer human blood

- **Sergentomyia** - Zoophilic species found in both regions

**21. Name the three clinical forms of leishmaniasis and their causative agents.**

**Answer:**

- **Visceral leishmaniasis** - Leishmania donovani (affects internal organs)
- **Cutaneous leishmaniasis** - Leishmania tropica (Old World) - skin lesions
- **Mucocutaneous leishmaniasis** - Leishmania braziliensis complex - affects mucous membranes (nose, mouth)

## Biting Midges (Ceratopogonidae)
**24. What are "no-see-ums" and why are they medically important?**

**Answer:** "No-see-ums" are biting midges (Family Ceratopogonidae, genus Culicoides and Leptoconops). They're medically important because:

- Transmit Mansonella ozzardi (filarial nematode)
- Very small size makes them difficult to see
- Painful bites
- Active in wind (unlike other vectors)
- Both diurnal and nocturnal feeders

**25. Describe the breeding preferences of biting midges.**

**Answer:** Biting midges prefer:

- Wet mud/sand for oviposition
- Both salt and fresh water habitats
- Pupae develop in soil or mud
- Found in tropics, subtropics, Caribbean, and Pacific regions

**26. Why is control of biting midges difficult to achieve?**

**Answer:**

- Very small size makes them hard to target
- Difficult to control with insecticides effectively
- Control achieved mainly through:Avoiding activity during peak biting times
- Personal protection with repellents
- Use of fine-mesh screens/netting (regular screens don't work)

## Black Flies (Simuliidae)
**27. What is onchocerciasis and which vector transmits it?**

**Answer:** Onchocerciasis (river blindness) is caused by the filarial nematode **Onchocerca volvulus**, transmitted by **Simulium species** (black flies). The disease causes:

- Skin manifestations
- Eye lesions leading to blindness
- Associated with fast-flowing rivers where black flies breed

**28. Describe the breeding habitat requirements of black flies.**

**Answer:** Black flies breed:

- Along riverbanks
- In **fast-flowing, well-oxygenated water**
- Larvae attach to rocks and vegetation in streams
- Require clean, moving water (unlike mosquitoes)

**29. What are the feeding behaviors and biting patterns of black flies?**

**Answer:**

- **Both sexes** feed on plant juices
- **Females** require blood meal for egg viability
- **Bimodal biting:** Early morning and early evening (diurnal)
- **Exophilic and exophagic** (outdoor feeders and resters)
- Prefer feeding on **exposed body parts** (head and arms more than legs)
- Few members are autogenous

**30. Describe control measures for black flies.**

**Answer:**

- **Physical:** Wear clothing covering exposed parts, apply repellents, time outdoor activities
- **Chemical: **Spray insecticides on larval breeding sites and resting areas
- Larvicides on water surfaces (15-30 min duration)
- Use of Bti (Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis)

- **Habitat modification: **Straighten rivers
- Remove obstructions to increase water velocity

## Horse Flies and Deer Flies (Tabanidae)
**31. Distinguish between Tabanus, Chrysops, and Haematopota in the family Tabanidae.**

**Answer:**

- **Tabanus (horse fly):** Green head, breeds in swamps, larger size
- **Chrysops (deer fly):** Yellow/brown color, smaller than Tabanus
- **Haematopota (clegs):** European species All have painful bites due to large mouthparts

**32. What diseases do Chrysops species transmit and in which geographic regions?**

**Answer:** Chrysops transmits **Loa loa** (African eye worm) in **West and Central Africa**. Key species:

- C. silacea (attracted by darkness, must hang from objects)
- C. dimidiata The parasite causes loiasis (Calabar swellings and eye migration)

**33. Describe the feeding behavior and host detection methods of tabanid flies.**

**Answer:**

- Females feed **during day in bright sunlight**
- **Diurnal feeders**
- Locate hosts by: **Sight** (visual cues) - large object detectors
- **Olfactory cues** (smell)

- **Purely exophagic** (except C. silacea)
- Painful bites due to large mouthparts

**34. Why is habitat management for tabanid control often cost-prohibitive?**

**Answer:** Tabanid larvae breed in:

- Large swampy areas
- Wetlands covering extensive geographic areas
- Efficient drainage of these large larval habitats requires:Massive infrastructure
- High costs
- Environmental concerns

- Often impractical for widespread implementation

## Tsetse Flies (Glossinidae)
**35. What is adenotrophic viviparity and which vector exhibits this unique reproductive strategy?**

**Answer:** Adenotrophic viviparity (larviparous reproduction) is found in **tsetse flies (Glossinidae)**. The larva develops inside the mother's uterus, nourished by an accessory milk gland. A single mature larva is deposited and immediately pupates in soil/shaded areas (2.5cm deep). Adults emerge after 4-5 weeks.

**36. Describe the three habitat groups of tsetse flies with examples.**

**Answer:**

- **Forest Group (Fusca):** G. palpalis, G. tachinoides, G. fuscipes - forested areas
- **Savanna Group (Morsitans):** G. morsitans, G. swynnertoni, G. pallidipes - savanna regions of East and Southern Africa
- **Riverine Group (Palpalis):** G. palpalis, G. tachinoides, G. fuscipes - vegetation along rivers, lakes, swamps in humid coastal areas

**37. Which tsetse fly species transmit West African vs. East African trypanosomiasis?**

**Answer:**

- **West African (Gambian) trypanosomiasis** (T. brucei gambiense):G. palpalis
- G. tachinoides

- **East African (Rhodesian) trypanosomiasis** (T. brucei rhodesiense):G. morsitans
- G. swynnertoni
- G. pallidipes

**38. Describe the feeding and resting behavior of tsetse flies.**

**Answer:**

- **Feeding: **Both males and females feed on blood every 2-3 days
- Diurnal feeders
- Host detection by sight and olfactory cues
- Attracted to dark moving objects

- **Resting: **Rest 23 hours/day
- At night: prefer resting on vegetation
- During day: rest at lower levels

**39. What control measures are used against tsetse flies?**

**Answer:**

- **Physical/Mechanical: **Creating barriers
- Aerial sprays (especially in dry season)
- Use of traps (Ngu trap)

- **Habitat Management: **Clearing vegetation
- Bush clearing along rivers

- **Chemical:** Insecticides, targets for insecticide application
- **Biological:** Sterile insect technique (SIT)

**40. Where are tsetse flies geographically distributed?**

**Answer:** Tsetse flies are found **only in Sub-Saharan Africa** (tropical Africa between **15°N and 20°S latitude**). They do not occur outside Africa, making African trypanosomiasis a geographically restricted disease.

- **Air curtains/barriers** - At shop entrances to reduce fly entry

- Plastic bags with openings tightly closed
- Optimize/clean larval habitats

## Myiasis
**45. Define myiasis and list the three types.**

**Answer:** **Myiasis** is the invasion of organs and tissues of humans and animals by fly larvae that feed on living/dead tissues or ingested food.

**Types:**

- **Accidental myiasis** - Consuming food contaminated with eggs/larvae of non-parasitic flies
- **Obligatory myiasis** - Larvae must live in live host for at least part of life cycle
- **Facultative myiasis** - Free-living larvae that attack carcasses but can infect living tissues

**46. Describe cutaneous (furuncular) myiasis and its treatment.**

**Answer:**

- Also called "creeping eruption"
- Boil-like inflammation of wound infected with larvae
- Only wounds attract flies which lay eggs
- Eggs hatch and larvae develop under skin
- **Treatment:** Remove larvae under aseptic conditions

**47. What is ophthalmic myiasis and what are its consequences?**

**Answer:** Ophthalmic (ocular) myiasis occurs when:

- Flies attracted to dirty eyes deposit eggs on/near the eye
- Larvae (maggots) invade and feed on living eye tissues
- **Symptoms:** Lacrimation, redness, pain
- **Treatment: **Removal of larvae
- If deeply embedded, eyes may need enucleation (removal)
- If damage irreversible, results in eye removal

- **Prevention:** Personal hygiene, eyewear

**48. Describe intestinal myiasis including route of entry and symptoms.**

**Answer:**

- **Route:** Mouth - food contaminated with eggs/larvae is swallowed
- **Occurs in:** Accidental myiasis (no species adapted to cause intestinal myiasis in humans)
- **Symptoms: **Diarrhea
- Dysentery
- Abdominal pain
- Vomiting

- Larvae may be passed alive in stool or vomited

**49. What is maggot therapy and what fly species is used?**

**Answer:** Maggot therapy uses **live, sterile larvae of Lucilia sericata** for wound management. The larvae:

- Clean wounds by feeding on dead/necrotic tissue (debridement)
- Leave healthy tissue intact
- Promote healing
- Reduce bacterial load

**50. Describe nasopharyngeal myiasis and its complications.**

**Answer:**

- **Major route:** Nose
- Larvae crawl down to nasopharynx
- **Can cause: **Tonsillitis
- Tonsillar irritation
- Discomfort and pain

## Fleas (Siphonaptera)
**51. Describe the life cycle of fleas with emphasis on larval feeding requirements.**

**Answer:**

- **Eggs:** Female deposits eggs in debris/dust in host habitat; eggs fall off host
- **Larvae:** Feed on organic matter/debris BUT must feed on **partly digested blood meal regurgitated from adult fleas or their feces** to successfully develop
- **Pupae:** Larvae spin silken cocoon (4-12 days, avg 7-8 days); cocoon covered with dust/debris
- **Adults:** Emerge and seek host; duration dependent on temperature and stimulus

**52. What is the medical importance of Xenopsylla cheopis?**

**Answer:** X. cheopis (rat flea) is the primary vector for:

- **Yersinia pestis** (bubonic plague) - Most important
- **Rickettsia typhi** (murine typhus)
- Common in tropics due to warm temperatures
- Feeds on rats and humans

**53. Describe the unique behavior of Tunga penetrans (jigger flea).**

**Answer:** Tunga penetrans (chigoe/jigger flea):

- **Burrows into people's feet**
- Gravid female flea **penetrates the host**
- Deposits eggs in the **dermis**
- Causes painful swelling and secondary infections
- Common in sandy, tropical areas

**54. What cestode parasites use Ctenocephalides fleas as intermediate hosts?**

**Answer:** Ctenocephalides (C. felis - cat flea; C. canis - dog flea) are intermediate hosts for:

- **Dipylidium caninum** (dog tapeworm)
- **Hymenolepis diminuta** (rat tapeworm) Also transmit Bartonella henselae (cat scratch disease)

**55. Explain why fleas rapidly abandon dead hosts and the significance for plague transmission.**

**Answer:** When no suitable (living) host is available:

- Fleas rapidly abandon their dead hosts in search of living hosts
- This behavior presents a **new wave of plague transmission**
- Dead plague-infected rodents release infected fleas
- Fleas seek new hosts (including humans)
- Creates epidemic spread

**56. Describe the jumping mechanism of fleas.**

**Answer:** Fleas have a protein called **resilin** in their legs:

- Highly compressed state
- Produces rapid expansion from compressed state
- Gives power for jumping (can jump 150x their body length)
- On host, move rapidly by crawling

**57. What control measures are used against fleas?**

**Answer:**

- **Repellents:** DEET on clothing
- **Rodent fleas and larvae:** Hand-operated dusters with insecticidal dusts:Carbaryl
- Pyrethroids
- Organophosphates applied to animals/pets

- **Cat/dog fleas:** Insecticides (pyrethroids, OPs) applied directly to animals
- **Environmental:** Treat carpets, bedding, animal resting areas

## Lice (Phthiraptera)
**58. Name the three types of blood-sucking lice in humans and their preferred locations.**

**Answer:**

- **Pediculus humanus corporis** - Body louse (lives in clothing)
- **Pediculus humanus capitis** - Head louse (scalp hair)
- **Pthirus pubis** - Pubic/crab louse (pubic hair, beard, armpits, chest hair)

**59. What is pediculosis and what are its symptoms?**

**Answer:** Pediculosis is the condition of lice infestation ("lousy disease"). Symptoms include:

- Intense itching/irritability
- Pessimistic mood
- Dermatitis and severe itching
- May cause asthma or bronchitis
- Risk of secondary bacterial infection (impetigo - contagious)
- Fever from scratching

**60. Name three vector-borne diseases transmitted transcutaneously by body lice.**

**Answer:**

- **Epidemic typhus** (Rickettsia prowazekii)
- **Relapsing fever** (Borrelia recurrentis)
- **Trench fever** (Bartonella quintana)

**Transmission:** Pathogens in louse feces are scratched into skin

**61. Describe control measures for body lice.**

**Answer:**

- Louse-infested clothing subjected to:Washing or ironing
- Dry cleaning

- Impregnation of clothing with **pyrethroid insecticides** (permethrin) for long-lasting protection
- Oral **ivermectin** kills lice (trials shown effective)
- Improved personal hygiene

**62. What are the medical importance and control of head lice?**

**Answer:** **Medical importance:**

- Causes pediculosis capitis
- Can result in hair loss (alopecia)
- Intense itching

**Control:**

- Regular combing of hair
- Shaving of head hair
- Insecticidal formulas/lotions/shampoos

**63. Describe the appearance and control of pubic lice.**

**Answer:** **Appearance:**

- Eggs attached to hair shaft (can be laid on single hair strand)
- Crab-shaped body
- Found on pubic hair, beard, armpits, occasionally chest hair

**Control:**

- Shaving pubic hair
- Insecticidal formulations (dusts, lotions, emulsions - NOT alcohol-based in genital area)
- Malathion (may cause irritation)
- For eyelashes: Petroleum jelly twice daily for 8-10 days
- Wash affected areas, iron clothing, dry in sunlight

**65. Describe the feeding and resting behavior of bedbugs.**

- In temperate regions, may remain active during day

**Resting:**

- Mattresses and beds

**Answer:**

- Repeated vigorous feeding can contribute to **iron deficiency anemia**, especially in:Infants
- Young children
- Elderly people

**67. Why has there been an increase in bedbug infestations?**

**Answer:**

- **Global resistance to insecticides** - Bedbugs developed resistance
- **Warmer temperatures** - Climate change favors bedbug survival
- Increased international travel
- Reduced use of effective (but banned) insecticides

## Triatomine Bugs (Reduviidae)
**68. What disease do triatomine bugs transmit and what is the mechanism of transmission?**

**Answer:** Triatomine bugs (kissing bugs) transmit **Trypanosoma cruzi**, causing **Chagas disease (American trypanosomiasis)**.

**Mechanism:**

- Bugs feed on blood, then defecate near bite site
- Feces contain T. cruzi parasites
- Person scratches bite, rubbing feces (with parasites) into wound
- Parasites enter through skin or mucous membranes

**69. Name three genera of triatomine bugs and key species.**

**Answer:**

- **Triatoma** - T. infestans, T. dimidiata
- **Rhodnius** - R. prolixus
- **Panstrongylus** - P. megistus

**70. Describe the medical importance of triatomine bug bites.**

**Answer:**

- Vector transmission of Chagas disease (T. cruzi)
- When bugs bite in large numbers, can cause **anemia**
- Some people exhibit hypersensitivity:Edema (swelling)
- Pruritus (itching)
- Erythema (redness)

**71. What control measures are effective against triatomine bugs?**

**Answer:**

- **Spraying** interior surfaces of walls and floors of houses
- Target preferred warm areas:Chicken sheds
- Goat pens

- **Pyrethrin** works during house fumigation
- **Reduction of hiding places** (prefer dark places)
- **Housing improvement:** Seal cracks, improve construction

- Seal cracks and crevices

## Hard Ticks (Ixodidae)
**81. Name five genera of hard ticks (Ixodidae).**

**Answer:**

- **Dermacentor**
- **Amblyomma**
- **Haemaphysalis**
- **Rhipicephalus**
- **Hyalomma**
- **Boophilus** (1-host tick)

**82. Explain what questing behavior is and which tick family exhibits it.**

**Answer:** **Questing behavior** is exhibited by **Ixodidae (hard ticks)**, particularly the larvae:

- Climb up vegetation (grass, stems, limbs)
- Wait with extended front legs for potential host
- When host brushes against vegetation, tick drops onto or grasps host's fur/skin
- Use chemical and physical cues to detect hosts

**83. Differentiate between 1-host, 2-host, and 3-host ticks with examples.**

**Answer:**

**3-Host Tick:**

- Each stage (larva, nymph, adult) feeds on different host
- Drop off after each feeding to molt
- **Examples:** Dermacentor, Amblyomma, Rhipicephalus, Hyalomma

**2-Host Tick:**

- Larvae molt into nymphs on first host
- Nymphs drop off and seek second host
- Molt to adults on second host
- **Examples:** Some Rhipicephalus, Hyalomma species

**1-Host Tick:**

- All stages remain on same host
- Larvae molt to nymphs on host
- Nymphs molt to adults on host
- **Example:** Boophilus

**84. What is tick paralysis and how does it occur?**

**Answer:** Tick paralysis occurs due to:

- **Toxins in female tick saliva** pumped into host during feeding period
- Results in ascending paralysis
- Followed by difficulties in:Breathing
- Swallowing
- Speaking

- Can be fatal if tick not removed
- Symptoms resolve after tick removal

**85. Name four diseases transmitted by hard ticks (Ixodidae).**

**Answer:**

- **Tick-borne encephalitis** (arbovirus)
- **Q fever** (Coxiella burnetii)
- **Rocky Mountain spotted fever** (Rickettsia rickettsii)
- Can cause poliomyelitis in people with allergies

**86. Why are ticks difficult to control? Provide two reasons.**

**Answer:**

- **Nutrient requirements:** Can survive long periods without feeding (months to years)
- **Host adaptability:** Ticks can adapt to various host types, increasing survival chances as parasites
- **Multiple habitats:** Found in diverse environments
- **Resistance:** Developing resistance to acaricides

**87. Describe proper tick removal technique.**

**Answer:**

- **Pull carefully and slowly** with tweezers close to skin
- Grasp tick near head/mouthparts, not body
- Pull straight out with steady, even pressure
- Don't twist or jerk (can leave mouthparts in skin)
- Don't squeeze tick body
- Clean area with antiseptic after removal
- Save tick for identification if needed

**88. What control measures are used against hard ticks?**

**90. Describe the diagnostic features of scabies.**

**91. Describe the difference between classic scabies and crusted (Norwegian) scabies.**

**Crusted (Norwegian) Scabies:**

**92. What are the characteristics of Sarcoptes scabiei mites?**

**93. Describe the symptoms and response produced by classic scabies.**

- May see linear burrow tracks

**Sites affected:**

- Wrists, elbows
- Armpits
- Genitals

**94. What treatments are available for scabies?**

**B) Benzyl Benzoate:**

- Re-application, then wash on 2nd and 3rd day

## Vector Biology Concepts
**101. Define and differentiate between cyclopropagative, cyclodevelopmental, and propagative transmission.**

**Answer:**

**Cyclopropagative:**

- Disease agent undergoes cyclic development **AND multiplies** in vector
- Both development and increase in pathogen number
- Example: Plasmodium in Anopheles

**Cyclodevelopmental:**

- Disease agent undergoes cyclic development when transmitted by vector
- Cyclic change but does **NOT multiply** in vector body
- Example: Filarial worms in mosquitoes

**Propagative:**

- **Only multiplication** stage of disease transmission
- No development, only pathogen number increases
- Example: Some viruses in mosquitoes

**102. What are passive vectors and give an example?**

**Answer:** **Passive vectors:**

- **Mechanical transmission** - No biological development of pathogen in vector
- Composed of outdoor components only
- **Non-living vectors (fomites)** can also act as passive vectors
- **Example:** Houseflies carrying bacteria on their feet/mouthparts

**103. Define the following terms: anthropophilic vs zoophilic.**

**Answer:**

- **Anthropophilic:** Preference for human blood meals (e.g., Anopheles gambiae)
- **Zoophilic:** Preference for animal blood meals (e.g., An. arabiensis)

This affects vector competence and disease transmission efficiency.

**104. Define the following mosquito behavioral terms: endophagic, exophagic, endophilic, exophilic.**

**Answer:**

- **Endophagic:** Feeds indoors
- **Exophagic:** Feeds outdoors
- **Endophilic:** Rests indoors
- **Exophilic:** Rests outdoors

These behaviors determine effectiveness of control measures like IRS and ITNs.

**105. What is the difference between autogenous and anautogenous mosquitoes?**

**Answer:**

- **Autogenous:** Can develop eggs **without** a blood meal (e.g., some Culex species)
- **Anautogenous:** **Requires** blood meal for egg development (e.g., Anopheles females, most mosquitoes)

Blood provides protein necessary for egg production (vitellogenesis).

**106. Define parthenogenesis in the context of mosquito reproduction.**

**Answer:** **Parthenogenesis** is a form of asexual reproduction where:

- Offspring develop **without fertilization**
- Eggs develop into viable adults without male involvement
- Possible in some mosquito species
- Results in female-only or clonal populations

**107. What is behavioral resistance in vectors?**

**Answer:** **Behavioral resistance** occurs when vectors change their behavior to avoid control measures:

- Shift from endophagic to exophagic (feed outdoors to avoid IRS)
- Shift from endophilic to exophilic (rest outdoors to avoid treated walls)
- Change biting times to avoid ITNs
- Results from selection pressure by control interventions
- Makes existing control methods less effective

**108. What is a supervector and give two examples?**

**Answer:** **Supervector:** A highly efficient disease vector with characteristics that maximize transmission:

- High anthropophilic behavior
- High population density
- Close association with humans
- Long lifespan
- High vectorial capacity

**Examples:**

- **Anopheles gambiae complex** - Malaria in Africa
- **An. stephensi** - Malaria in Asia, recently in Africa

**109. What is vectorial capacity?**

**Answer:** **Vectorial capacity** is a measure of disease transmission potential that includes:

- Vector density relative to host
- Vector biting rate
- Vector competence (ability to transmit pathogen)
- Vector survival/longevity
- Extrinsic incubation period of pathogen
- Mathematical measure of transmission risk

**110. Define transgenic vectors and their purpose in vector control.**

**Answer:** **Transgenic vectors:** Include a foreign gene into vector's genome to create GMOs (Genetically Modified Organisms).

**Purpose:**

- **Gene therapy**
- **Reducing vector competence** (make vectors unable to transmit disease)
- **Mosquito population suppression** (sterile insect technique)
- **Resistance to pathogens** (vectors that cannot support pathogen development)
- **Altering vector behavior** (making them less attracted to humans)

**111. What is IRS (Indoor Residual Spraying) and what are its challenges?**

**117. Explain the sterile insect technique (SIT) and its application.**

**Importance:**

- **Prevents mosquito production** at source

**139. Why is transovarial transmission important in arbovirus epidemiology?**

**Answer:** Transovarial transmission is important because:

- **Adults emerge already infected:** No need to acquire virus from host
- **Amplifies transmission:** Increases infected vector population
- **Overwintering mechanism:** Virus survives in eggs during dry/cold seasons
- **Rapid outbreak potential:** Large numbers of infected vectors can emerge simultaneously

- **Squeezing tick body forces: **Regurgitation of gut contents into wound
- Injection of more saliva (containing pathogens)
- Increased pathogen transfer

- **Correct removal:** Grasp near head with tweezers, pull straight out steadily
- **Don't squeeze, twist, or burn tick**
- **Pathogens in tick gut/saliva:** Borrelia, Rickettsia, viruses
- **Risk increases with:** Duration of attachment and manipulation during removal

**145. A rural community in West Africa wants to reduce tsetse fly populations. What integrated control strategy would you recommend?**

**Answer:**

**Integrated Tsetse Management:**

**1. Physical/Mechanical:**

- Deploy Ngu traps (blue/black targets)
- Targets impregnated with insecticides

**2. Habitat Management:**

- Selective clearing of riverine vegetation
- Bush clearing along water sources
- Maintain some vegetation for ecological balance

**3. Chemical:**

- Aerial spraying in dry season
- Pour-on insecticides on livestock (attract tsetse)

**4. Biological:**

- Sterile Insect Technique (SIT)
- Release sterile males

**5. Community Participation:**

- Education on tsetse habitat
- Livestock management
- Reporting sick animals

**146. Why do some mosquito species become resistant to pyrethroids used in ITNs, and what are the resistance mechanisms?**

**Answer:**

**Resistance Mechanisms:**

**1. Target site resistance:**

- Mutation in voltage-gated sodium channel (kdr - knockdown resistance)
- Changes binding site so pyrethroid cannot attach
- Most common mechanism

**2. Metabolic resistance:**

- Increased detoxification enzymes (P450s, esterases, GSTs)
- Break down pyrethroid before it acts
- Overexpression of metabolic genes

**3. Behavioral resistance:**

- Shift to exophagic/exophilic behavior
- Avoid contact with treated surfaces
- Feed/rest outdoors

**Why it develops:**

- Selection pressure from widespread ITN use
- High pyrethroid usage (only class approved for ITNs)
- Genetic selection over generations

**147. A patient with HIV/AIDS presents with thick, crusted skin lesions crawling with mites. What type of scabies is this and why is it highly contagious?**

**Answer:**

- **Diagnosis:** Crusted (Norwegian) scabies
- **Why highly contagious:****Thousands to millions of mites** on skin (vs. 10-15 in classic scabies)
- Mites easily shed from hyperkeratotic crusts
- Direct skin contact spreads massive numbers of mites
- Fomites (bedding, clothes) heavily contaminated
- Patient often immunocompromised (reduced immune response)

- **Risk factors:** HIV/AIDS, elderly, immunosuppressed, institutionalized patients
- **Treatment:** Multiple applications of scabicide, ivermectin, treat all contacts

**148. Why is source reduction considered the most sustainable mosquito control strategy?**

**Answer:**

**Advantages of Source Reduction:**

**1. Environmental sustainability:**

- No chemicals or insecticides needed
- No environmental pollution
- Safe for humans and non-target organisms

**2. No resistance development:**

- Physical removal of breeding sites
- Cannot develop biological resistance

**3. Cost-effective long-term:**

- One-time infrastructure improvements
- Reduces need for ongoing insecticide purchases
- Community-based, low-tech

**4. Reduces multiple vector species:**

- Targets breeding sites of Anopheles, Culex, Aedes
- Not species-specific

**5. Community empowerment:**

- Engages communities in their own protection
- Education and behavior change
- Sustainable beyond project duration

**Challenges:** Requires sustained community participation and behavior change

**149. Explain why black fly control is achieved by treating fast-flowing rivers rather than stagnant water.**

**Answer:**

- **Black fly breeding habitat:** Fast-flowing, well-oxygenated water
- **Larvae attach to:** Rocks and vegetation in streams/rivers
- **Require:** High oxygen levels (unlike mosquitoes)
- **Larvicide application:** Applied to water surfaces, flows downstream
- **Bti (Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis):** Applied to rivers, kills larvae for 15-30 minutes as it flows
- **Habitat modification:** Straighten rivers, remove obstructions to increase flow velocity
- **Different from mosquitoes:** Mosquitoes breed in stagnant water, black flies in moving water

**150. A blood smear shows microfilariae with diurnal periodicity. The patient lives in West Africa. What is the diagnosis and vector?**

**Answer:**

- **Diagnosis:** Loiasis (Loa loa infection)
- **Vector:** Chrysops species (deer flies) - diurnal feeders
- **Microfilariae characteristics:****Diurnal periodicity:** Peak levels in blood during day (matches vector feeding time)
- Sheathed microfilariae

- **Clinical features:** Calabar swellings, eye worm migration
- **Contrast with: **W. bancrofti - nocturnal periodicity (Culex feeds at night)
- Onchocerca - in skin, not blood

**151. Why are ITNs less effective against Aedes mosquitoes compared to Anopheles?**

**Answer:**

**Behavioral differences:**

**Anopheles:**

- Nocturnal feeders (peak midnight-dawn)
- Endophagic/endophilic
- People protected under nets during feeding times
- **ITNs highly effective**

**Aedes:**

- **Diurnal feeders** (early morning, late afternoon)
- Bimodal biting pattern
- **Exophagic/exophilic**
- People active and outdoors during Aedes feeding times
- **Not protected by ITNs** during peak biting

**Better Aedes control:**

- Source reduction (remove containers)
- Personal protection (repellents, long sleeves)
- Larvicides in containers
- Community-based container management

**152. Describe the "fly belt" of Africa and its significance for African trypanosomiasis.**

**Answer:**

**"Fly Belt" or "Tsetse Belt":**

- Region of Sub-Saharan Africa between **15°N and 20°S latitude**
- Geographic distribution of tsetse flies (Glossina spp.)
- Covers ~10 million km² across 37 African countries

**Significance:**

- **Sleeping sickness endemic zone:** Human African trypanosomiasis limited to this area
- **Animal trypanosomiasis (nagana):** Major constraint to livestock development
- **Economic impact:** Limits cattle raising, agriculture, development
- **No tsetse outside Africa:** Disease geographically restricted

**Habitat types within belt:**

- Forest (palpalis group)
- Savanna (morsitans group)
- Riverine (palpalis group)

**153. A homeless person presents with severe itching, fever, and multiple bite marks. Lice are found in clothing seams. What diseases should you be concerned about?**

**Answer:**

**Diagnosis:** Body louse infestation (pediculosis corporis)

**Vector-borne diseases to screen for:**

**1. Epidemic typhus:**

- Rickettsia prowazekii
- Most serious concern
- Symptoms: High fever, headache, rash

**2. Louse-borne relapsing fever:**

- Borrelia recurrentis
- Relapsing fevers

**3. Trench fever:**

- Bartonella quintana
- Chronic bacteremia

**154. Why is the pupal stage of mosquitoes a non-feeding stage and what are the implications for larval control?**

**Answer:**

**Pupal characteristics:**

- **Non-feeding (resting) stage**
- Comma-shaped
- Mobile but doesn't feed
- Metamorphosis occurring inside
- Lasts 2-3 days
- **Resistant to larvicides** (doesn't ingest)

**Implications for control:**

- **Larvicides ineffective against pupae:** Must target earlier larval stages
- **Timing critical:** Apply larvicides when larvae present (L1-L4)
- **Pupae near emergence:** Source reduction still effective (physically remove)
- **Why target larvae:** Feeding stages vulnerable to stomach poisons and Bti
- **Pupae as indicators:** Presence indicates imminent adult emergence

**155. What is the "knockdown resistance" (kdr) mutation and why is it a concern for malaria control?**

**Answer:**

**kdr (knockdown resistance) mutation:**

- Mutation in **voltage-gated sodium channel** gene
- Target site of pyrethroid and DDT insecticides
- Changes amino acid sequence (L1014F, L1014S mutations common)
- Prevents insecticide binding

**Concern for malaria control:**

- **Pyrethroids only class** approved for ITNs/LLINs
- **Widespread:** kdr found in Anopheles across Africa, Asia
- **Reduces ITN effectiveness:** Mosquitoes survive contact with nets
- **Cross-resistance:** Affects both pyrethroids and DDT
- **Limited alternatives:** Few insecticide classes safe for ITNs
- **Threatens control gains:** Malaria resurgence risk

**Mitigation strategies:**

- Rotate insecticide classes (for IRS)
- Combination nets (pyrethroid + PBO synergist)
- New insecticide development
- Non-chemical methods (source reduction)

**156. Explain why removing termite mounds is an effective control measure against sandflies.**

**Answer:**

**Sandfly biology:**

- Oviposit in **small cracks and holes** in ground
- **Base of termite mounds** is preferred breeding site
- Also breed in termite mound crevices
- Require high humidity (termite mounds provide this)

**Why termite mound removal works:**

- **Eliminates breeding habitat**
- Reduces humidity microhabitats
- Removes resting sites for adults
- Disrupts life cycle

**Additional sandfly control:**

- Spray insecticides on cracks in walls
- Target animal burrows
- Remove rubbish heaps
- Personal protection (repellents, clothing)

**Challenge:** Labor-intensive, termite mounds widespread in endemic areas

**157. A patient presents with a painful boil-like lesion on the arm. Examination reveals a small breathing hole in the center and movement inside. What is the diagnosis?**

**Answer:**

- **Diagnosis:** Cutaneous (furuncular) myiasis
- Also called **"creeping eruption"**
- **Cause:** Fly larvae developing under skin
- **Common flies:** Dermatobia hominis (human botfly), Cordylobia anthropophaga (tumbu fly)

**Characteristics:**

- Boil-like inflammation
- Small hole (spiracle) for larva to breathe
- Movement/sensation of something crawling inside
- Pain, itching
- Secondary bacterial infection risk

**Treatment:**

- Remove larvae under aseptic conditions
- Methods: Occlusion (petroleum jelly blocks spiracle, forces larva out), surgical extraction
- Antibiotics if secondary infection
- Tetanus prophylaxis

**158. Why do Culex mosquitoes breed in polluted water while Anopheles prefer clean water?**

**Answer:**

**Larval adaptations:**

**Culex:**

- **Tolerate organic pollution:** Sewage, drains, pit latrines
- Feed on bacteria and organic particles
- Siphon allows breathing at surface despite scum
- **Ecological niche:** Exploits polluted urban water
- High organic matter supports larval nutrition

**Anopheles:**

- **Require cleaner water:** Vegetated, sunlit pools
- Feed on algae and microorganisms
- Lie parallel to surface (need clear water)
- **Ecological niche:** Natural, rural water bodies
- Presence of algae and vegetation essential

**Control implications:**

- **Culex:** Sanitation, proper sewage systems
- **Anopheles:** Source reduction, drainage of clean pools
- Different breeding sites require different strategies

**159. Describe the epidemiological significance of zoophilic mosquitoes in disease transmission.**

**Answer:**

**Zoophilic species (e.g., An. arabiensis):**

**Reduced human disease transmission:**

- Prefer feeding on animals over humans
- **Zooprophylaxis:** Animals act as "dead-end" hosts for human pathogens
- Diverts mosquito bites away from humans
- **Lower vectorial capacity** for human diseases

**Applications in control:**

- **Livestock as barriers:** Place animals between humans and mosquito breeding sites
- **Cattle pens:** Attract zoophilic vectors away from houses
- **Can treat animals** with insecticides (pour-ons)

**Limitations:**

- Opportunistic feeding still occurs
- Some pathogens cycle in animal reservoirs
- May still transmit zoonotic diseases
- **Anthropophilic species** (An. gambiae) remain primary vectors

**Contrast:** Anthropophilic vectors (human-preferring) are most efficient disease transmitters

**160. Why is maggot therapy making a comeback in modern wound care?**

**Answer:**

**Benefits of maggot therapy (Lucilia sericata larvae):**

**1. Selective debridement:**

- Larvae feed **only on dead/necrotic tissue**
- Leave healthy tissue intact
- More precise than surgical debridement

**2. Antimicrobial effects:**

- Secrete antimicrobial substances
- Reduce bacterial load in wounds
- Effective against MRSA and other resistant bacteria

**3. Promotes healing:**

- Stimulates granulation tissue formation
- Growth factors in secretions
- Improves wound healing rates

**4. Cost-effective:**

- Less expensive than multiple surgeries
- Reduces hospital stay
- Outpatient application possible

**5. Antibiotic resistance era:**

- Alternative to antibiotics
- Effective against resistant bacteria
- No resistance development to maggots

**Applications:** Diabetic ulcers, pressure sores, post-surgical wounds, burns

**Preparation:** Sterile, laboratory-reared larvae used

**161. Explain the concept of extrinsic incubation period (EIP) and its importance in vector-borne disease transmission.**

**Answer:**

**Extrinsic Incubation Period (EIP):**

- Time from vector ingesting pathogen to when vector can transmit
- Period pathogen needs to **develop in vector** to infectious stage
- Temperature-dependent (warmer = shorter EIP)

**Importance:**

**1. Vector must survive EIP to transmit:**

- If vector dies before EIP complete, no transmission
- Longer EIP = lower transmission (more vectors die)

**2. Temperature effects:**

- **Warmer climates:** Shorter EIP, more transmission
- **Climate change:** Expanding disease ranges as EIP shortens

**3. Examples:**

- **Malaria (Plasmodium):** 10-14 days in mosquito
- **Dengue:** 8-12 days
- **West Nile virus:** 10-14 days

**4. Control implications:**

- Reducing vector longevity very effective
- Adult mosquito control critical
- Insecticides targeting resting adults

**Formula component:** EIP affects vectorial capacity calculations

**162. A traveler returns from Brazil with skin lesions that start as papules but progress to destructive lesions of the nasal mucosa. What is the diagnosis and vector?**

**Answer:**

- **Diagnosis:** Mucocutaneous leishmaniasis (espundia)
- **Causative agent:** Leishmania braziliensis complex
- **Vector:** Lutzomyia species (New World sandflies)
- **Geographic distribution:** Central and South America

**Clinical progression:**

- Primary cutaneous lesion (papule/ulcer)
- Months to years later: **metastatic spread** to mucosa
- **Destructive lesions** of nose, mouth, pharynx
- Can cause disfigurement
- Secondary bacterial infections

**Diagnosis:** Parasite identification, PCR, serology

**Treatment:** Antimonials, amphotericin B (more difficult to treat than cutaneous form)

**164. Describe the concept of "refractory vectors" in malaria control using Wolbachia.**

**Answer:**

**Refractory vectors:**

- Mosquitoes that **cannot transmit** malaria parasites
- Genetically or biologically altered to block Plasmodium development

**Wolbachia approach:**

**Mechanism:**

- Wolbachia bacteria infect mosquito
- **Blocks Plasmodium development** in mosquito gut/salivary glands
- Parasite cannot complete life cycle
- Mosquito becomes transmission-incompetent

**Advantages:**

- **Self-sustaining:** Wolbachia passed to offspring (cytoplasmic inheritance)
- **Population replacement:** Infected mosquitoes gradually replace wild population
- **No insecticides:** Biological control
- **No resistance concern:** Not killing mosquitoes, just blocking transmission

**Challenges:**

- **Wolbachia naturally infects some mosquitoes** but not all Anopheles
- Technical challenges in establishing stable infections
- Regulatory and ethical considerations

**Success in other diseases:** Wolbachia-infected Aedes effective against dengue

**Alternative approaches:** Transgenic mosquitoes, gene drives

**165. Why do some arbov

iruses show seasonality in their transmission?**

**Answer:**

**Factors causing seasonal arbovirus transmission:**

**1. Vector population dynamics:**

- **Rainy season:** Increased breeding sites, more mosquitoes
- **Temperature:** Warmer temperatures = faster mosquito development
- Vector abundance peaks seasonally

**2. Extrinsic Incubation Period (EIP):**

- **Temperature-dependent:** Warmer = shorter EIP
- Cold temperatures slow/stop virus replication in vector
- Transmission efficiency varies by season

**3. Vector behavior:**

- **Activity levels:** Higher biting rates in warm weather
- Some vectors less active in cold/dry seasons
- Diapause in winter (temperate regions)

**4. Host behavior:**

- **Human:** More outdoor activity in warm weather = more exposure
- **Amplification hosts:** Migratory birds seasonal

**5. Rainfall patterns:**

- Creates breeding sites (mosquitoes)
- Floods can wash away larvae (complex relationship)

**Examples:**

- **West Nile virus:** Summer/fall peaks in temperate regions
- **Dengue:** Rainy season in tropics
- **Rift Valley fever:** Heavy rains trigger outbreaks

**Control implications:** Target interventions before/during transmission season
