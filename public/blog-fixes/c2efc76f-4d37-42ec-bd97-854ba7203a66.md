# Coronary Artery Disease and Stable Angina

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Cardiology**

> **In one line:** coronary artery disease (CAD) is almost always atherosclerosis of the coronary arteries. A fixed plaque causes exertional angina (supply cannot meet demand); plaque rupture with thrombosis causes unstable angina or myocardial infarction. Management combines risk-factor control, antiplatelet and anti-anginal drugs, and revascularisation (PCI or CABG) for high-risk anatomy or refractory symptoms.

## What you must know

- CAD is the commonest form of heart disease. In 2018 it accounted for about **2.95% of all deaths in Kenya**.
- Cause: **atherosclerosis and its complications, especially thrombosis**. Rare causes: aortitis, vasculitis, autoimmune connective tissue disease.
- Angina = **transient myocardial ischaemia** from an imbalance between oxygen supply (coronary flow) and demand (cardiac work). Stable angina is **exertional and relieved promptly by rest or GTN**.
- Clinical manifestations: stable angina, unstable angina, MI, heart failure, arrhythmias, sudden death.
- First-line test for stable angina: **exercise ECG** (down-sloping or planar ST depression). Coronary angiography is done when PCI or CABG is being considered.
- Drugs: **aspirin 75 mg** (clopidogrel 75 mg if aspirin not tolerated) plus anti-anginals (nitrates, beta-blockers, calcium-channel blockers, nicorandil, ivabradine).
- Risk-factor control (smoking, hypertension, diabetes, cholesterol) is the foundation.

## 1. Atherosclerosis

- **Definition:** thickening of the intima from plaque formation in medium and large arteries. It is a progressive **inflammatory** disorder of focal, lipid-rich deposits (atheroma) that stay silent until they become large enough to impair perfusion, or until the lesion ruptures causing thrombotic occlusion or embolisation.
- Commonly affects the **abdominal aorta, popliteal and coronary arteries** (also carotids and cerebral vessels).

**Pathogenesis in brief**

1. Endothelial injury or dysfunction (smoking, hypertension, hyperglycaemia, LDL).
2. LDL enters the intima and is oxidised; monocytes become foam cells, forming the **fatty streak**.
3. Smooth muscle migrates in and lays down a fibrous cap over a lipid core (**fibrofatty plaque**).
4. A thin cap can rupture, exposing thrombogenic material and triggering platelet-fibrin **thrombosis**.

**Risk factors**

| Non-modifiable | Modifiable |
|---|---|
| Age, male sex, genetic predisposition (family history of premature CAD) | **Smoking, diabetes, hypercholesterolaemia, hypertension**, obesity, sedentary lifestyle |

- Other factors: haemostatic (platelet activation, high fibrinogen); disease rates are lower in pre-menopausal women, and HRT is associated with increased risk (it is not used for cardioprotection).

**Complications of atherosclerosis by mechanism**

| Mechanism | Result |
|---|---|
| **Stenosis** | Angina (coronary), peripheral artery disease (popliteal), ischaemic bowel disease (mesenteric/abdominal) |
| **Plaque rupture with thrombosis** | Myocardial infarction; stroke (middle cerebral artery) |
| Plaque rupture with embolisation | Distal ischaemia (for example blue toe, retinal and cerebral emboli) |
| **Aneurysm** | Weakened vessel wall (abdominal aortic aneurysm) |

## 2. Stable angina

**Definition and aetiology**

- A symptom complex caused by transient myocardial ischaemia, from imbalance between oxygen supply and demand.
- Commonest cause: **coronary atheroma**. Others: **coronary artery spasm** (Prinzmetal), aortic stenosis, hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy, aortitis; anaemia and thyrotoxicosis can provoke it.
- In atheromatous angina a fixed stenosis (usually over 70%) limits flow so that demand rises with exertion and flow cannot match it; rest reduces demand and the pain settles.

**Clinical features**

- Central chest pain or discomfort, with breathlessness, **precipitated by exertion** and **promptly relieved by rest**.
- Some patients get **"warm-up angina"**: discomfort on starting to walk that later does not return despite greater effort.

**Pain history (SOCRATES style)**

| Feature | Typical angina |
|---|---|
| Site | Central chest |
| Character | Tight, squeezing, choking |
| Radiation | Jaw, neck, shoulder, arm, back |
| Relieving factors | Rest and nitrates |
| Exacerbating factors | Exertion and emotion (also cold and heavy meals) |
| Associated | Breathlessness |

> **Remember:** typical angina has all three: central tightness, provoked by exertion, relieved by rest or GTN within minutes. Two of three is atypical; one or none is non-anginal. Angina at rest or lasting over 20 minutes means acute coronary syndrome.

**Canadian Cardiovascular Society (CCS) grading** (documents severity and prognosis)

| Class | Description |
|---|---|
| I | Angina only with strenuous or prolonged exertion |
| II | Slight limitation: angina with vigorous activity |
| III | Marked (moderate) limitation: angina with everyday activities |
| IV | Unable to do any activity without angina, or angina at rest |

## 3. Diagnosis

**History:** the pain history above plus risk factors: hypertension, diabetes, smoking, sedentary lifestyle, obesity/hypercholesterolaemia, family history.

**Examination:** usually unremarkable. Look for:

- Aortic valve disease (murmur, dyspnoea lying down).
- Left ventricular dysfunction (cardiomegaly, gallop rhythm).
- Other arterial disease (carotid bruits, absent pulses, xanthelasma, corneal arcus).
- Conditions that aggravate angina (anaemia, thyrotoxicosis).

**Investigations**

- **Baseline:** FBC (anaemia), TFTs, lipid profile, random/fasting glucose, urea and electrolytes; resting ECG (often normal between attacks; may show old infarction or LVH).
- **Exercise ECG** (treadmill or bicycle ergometer with pulse, BP and clinical monitoring): first-line. **Down-sloping or planar ST depression** indicates ischaemia; the exercise tolerated and the extent of ST change reflect severity.
- If the exercise ECG is normal or inconclusive: **myocardial perfusion imaging or stress echocardiography**.
- **CT coronary angiography** is increasingly used to show presence or absence of CAD.
- **Invasive coronary angiography:** usually when CABG or PCI is being considered.
- Echocardiography for LV function and valve disease.

> **Exam tip:** exercise ECG is contraindicated in unstable angina, recent MI, severe aortic stenosis, uncontrolled hypertension or arrhythmia, and decompensated heart failure.

## 4. Management

**Principles**

1. Carefully assess the likely extent and severity of arterial disease.
2. Identify and control risk factors (stop smoking, treat hypertension, diabetes, lipids, weight, exercise).
3. Control symptoms with drugs.
4. Identify high-risk patients for treatment that improves life expectancy.

**Drug therapy**

| Class | Examples and role |
|---|---|
| **Antiplatelet** | **Low-dose aspirin 75 mg** daily reduces the risk of MI. **Clopidogrel 75 mg daily** for those with aspirin dyspepsia or intolerance |
| **Nitrates** | GTN sublingually for acute relief and before exertion; long-acting nitrates for prophylaxis. Cause headache, flushing, hypotension; need a nitrate-free period to avoid tolerance |
| **Beta-blockers** | Atenolol, bisoprolol: slow heart rate, reduce ventricular workload and lengthen diastolic filling. Avoid in asthma and heart block |
| **Calcium-channel blockers** | Verapamil/diltiazem (rate-limiting; do not combine with beta-blockers) or amlodipine (dihydropyridine) |
| **Potassium-channel activators** | Nicorandil |
| **If-channel blocker** | **Ivabradine** lowers heart rate by acting directly on the sinus node |
| Secondary prevention | Statin, ACE inhibitor (especially with diabetes, LV dysfunction or hypertension) |

> **Remember:** sublingual GTN is the immediate treatment; a beta-blocker or rate-limiting calcium-channel blocker is usually the first regular anti-anginal. Aspirin and a statin are given to nearly everyone.

**Invasive treatment**

- **Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI):** a guidewire is passed across the stenosis under radiographic control and a balloon is positioned and inflated to dilate it (usually with a stent).
- **Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG):** preferred for left main stem disease, triple-vessel disease and diabetics with multivessel disease, and when LV function is impaired; improves survival in these groups.
- Revascularisation is indicated for symptoms uncontrolled on medical therapy and for high-risk anatomy.

## 5. Clinical spectrum of CAD

- **Stable angina** (fixed stenosis, exertional).
- **Unstable angina / acute coronary syndrome** (plaque rupture, new, crescendo or rest angina).
- **Myocardial infarction.**
- **Heart failure** (ischaemic cardiomyopathy).
- **Arrhythmias** and **sudden death** (ventricular fibrillation).

## Key points to revise

1. CAD is nearly always atherosclerosis; thrombosis on a ruptured plaque causes acute events.
2. Risk factors: age, sex, family history (fixed); smoking, diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidaemia (modifiable).
3. Stable angina: central, tight pain on exertion, relieved by rest or GTN; warm-up angina can occur; graded by CCS class I to IV.
4. Exercise ECG is first-line (ST depression); use stress imaging or CT/invasive angiography if inconclusive or if revascularisation is planned.
5. Aspirin 75 mg (clopidogrel 75 mg if intolerant) is the antiplatelet of choice.
6. Anti-anginals: GTN, beta-blocker, calcium-channel blocker, nicorandil, ivabradine.
7. PCI dilates a stenosis with a balloon and stent; CABG is for left main or multivessel disease.
8. Exclude exacerbating conditions: anaemia, thyrotoxicosis, aortic stenosis.
