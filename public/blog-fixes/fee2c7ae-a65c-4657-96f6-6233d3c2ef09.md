# Approach to the Poisoned Patient

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Toxicology and Emergency Medicine**

> **In one line:** manage poisoning systematically: secure **airway, breathing and circulation first**, then take a focused history and toxicology examination to recognise a **toxidrome**, then decontaminate, enhance elimination and give a specific antidote where one exists. Most treatment is supportive.

## What you must know

- **Poison:** any substance that, introduced into or in contact with the body, produces ill effects or death by local or systemic action.
- Types: **fulminant** (massive dose, sudden collapse), **acute** (single large or several small doses over a short time), **chronic** (small doses over a long time).
- Kenya (Kenyatta National Hospital data): **pesticides (43%)** and household/industrial chemicals (24%) were the commonest agents; **organophosphates (57.4%)** and rodenticides (31%) among pesticides; **kerosene** accounted for 66% of household poisonings; mortality **7.0%**.
- Order of care: **A, B, C, D (diagnosis and decontamination), E (enhance elimination), F (find antidote), G (get toxicology advice)**.
- **Activated charcoal** within an hour is the main decontamination method; **gastric lavage is no better** and carries risk. **Syrup of ipecac is not used.**
- Cholinergic toxidrome (organophosphates): **atropine, pralidoxime (2-PAM), diazepam**.
- Know the toxidromes: sympathomimetic, anticholinergic, cholinergic, opioid, sedative-hypnotic.

## 1. Definitions and epidemiology

### Types

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Fulminant** | Massive dose; death very rapid, patient collapses suddenly, often without symptoms |
| **Acute** | A single large dose or several small doses over a short period |
| **Chronic** | Small doses over a long period |

### Epidemiology

- Acute poisoning accounts for about **1% of hospital admissions in the UK**.
- Patients poisoned through parental negligence (accidental) are more likely to survive than those poisoned in family disputes, love affairs, snakebites and psychiatric illness.
- **Kenyatta National Hospital study (2002 to 2003):** 58.9% of patients were male. Pesticides (43%) and household/industrial chemicals (24%) were the main agents.
  - Pesticides: organophosphates 57.4%, rodenticides 31%.
  - Household agents: kerosene 66%.
  - **Self-poisoning** was commonest at ages **21 to 30 years (70.7%)**; **accidental poisoning (mostly kerosene)** at **0 to 5 years (83.9%)**.
  - Overall mortality **7.0%**.

## 2. Routes of exposure

Ingestion, inhalation, skin and eye contact, injection (including bites and stings), and rectal or mucosal routes. The route determines the decontamination method (see section 6).

## 3. History: the 5 Ws

| W | Ask |
|---|---|
| **Who** | Age and weight |
| **What** | Name, dose, amount of toxin; single or multiple agents |
| **When** | Time of ingestion |
| **Where** | Route of exposure; geographical location |
| **Why** | Intentional (self-harm) or accidental |

Also ask about alcohol, a **witness**, psychiatric illness, and past medical history. Ask to see containers, tablets and bottles; contact family or the pharmacy.

## 4. ABCs of toxicology

| Letter | Action |
|---|---|
| **A** | Airway |
| **B** | Breathing |
| **C** | Circulation |
| **D** | Diagnosis and decontamination |
| **E** | Enhance elimination |
| **F** | Find the antidote |
| **G** | Get toxicology advice if needed |

Clinical clues: the **altered**, **acidotic**, **hypotensive or hypertensive**, **screaming (agitated)** and **sleeping** patient should all prompt a toxicological cause.

> **Remember:** if the patient is in danger, correct immediate life-threatening problems first (A, B, C, D); only then do a thorough examination. Always check blood glucose in an altered patient.

## 5. Quick toxicology examination

- **Mental status** (seizures, confusion, level of consciousness).
- **Skin:** temperature, colour, sweating, piloerection, flushing, blisters.
- **Pupil size:** mydriasis or miosis.
- **Bowel sounds and bladder function.**
- **Neurology:** reflexes (tendon, plantar), tremor, tone, ataxia, paralysis.
- Vital signs: respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, pulse, BP.
- Chest: aspiration, bronchoconstriction.
- Mouth: salivation or dryness.

### Syndromes seen in envenoming and poisoning

| Syndrome | Features |
|---|---|
| **Neurotoxic paralysis** | Drooping eyelids ("sleepy"), difficulty swallowing, dysarthria, drooling, limb weakness, respiratory distress |
| **Excitatory neurotoxicity** | Sweating, salivation, piloerection, tingling around the mouth or tongue, muscle twitching, dyspnoea (pulmonary oedema) |
| **Coagulopathy** | Oozing from bite site and gums, bruising, melaena, haematemesis |

## 6. Toxidromes

A toxidrome is a group of signs and symptoms associated with exposure to a particular substance or class.

| Toxidrome | Features | Treatment and notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Sympathomimetic** (cocaine, amphetamines) | Tachycardia, hypertension, hyperthermia, agitation/confusion, seizures, rhabdomyolysis | Benzodiazepines (control agitation and seizures); phentolamine or nitroprusside for severe hypertension |
| **Anticholinergic / antimuscarinic** | **Anhidrosis, tachycardia, mydriasis, ileus, urinary retention** (peripheral block); **confusion, agitation, myoclonus, hallucinations, tremor, altered mental state** (central block) | Supportive; physostigmine in selected cases |
| **Cholinergic** (organophosphates, carbamates) | **DUMBBBELS:** Diarrhoea, Urination, Miosis, Bradycardia, Bronchorrhoea, Bronchospasm, Emesis, Lacrimation (and salivation). **SLUDGE:** Salivation, Lacrimation, Urination, Diarrhoea, GI upset, Emesis | See below |
| **Opioid** | Respiratory depression, miosis, coma | Naloxone |
| **Sedative-hypnotic** | CNS depression, lethargy, ± respiratory depression, bradycardia, hypotension | Supportive; **flumazenil** (benzodiazepine receptor antagonist) is rarely indicated and may precipitate status epilepticus |

### Nicotinic effects (mnemonic MTWHF)

**M**ydriasis, **T**achycardia, **W**eakness, **H**ypertension, **F**asciculations.

### Treatment of cholinergic toxidrome

1. **Atropine** to antagonise the muscarinic symptoms (titrate to drying of bronchial secretions).
2. **Pralidoxime (2-PAM)** to stop "ageing" of the enzyme blockade (reactivates acetylcholinesterase).
3. **Diazepam** to prevent and stop seizures.
4. Supportive management (airway, oxygen, fluids, decontaminate skin and remove clothing).

> **Exam tip:** organophosphate poisoning is the commonest pesticide poisoning in Kenya. Atropine treats muscarinic effects only; it does not help the muscle weakness (nicotinic), which needs pralidoxime and ventilatory support.

## 7. Clinical assessment and investigations

- Bedside: blood glucose, ECG, pulse oximetry, temperature, pupils.
- Blood: U&E, creatinine, **blood gas (anion gap acidosis)**, liver function, FBC, clotting, osmolality, drug levels where useful (paracetamol, salicylate, lithium, digoxin, theophylline, iron), carboxyhaemoglobin if CO suspected, cholinesterase for organophosphates.
- Urine: toxicology screen (limited value).
- Pregnancy test in women of reproductive age.

## 8. General management: decontamination

### Activated charcoal

- Large surface area, no smell, **use within 1 hour** of ingestion.
- **Aspiration risk**: protect the airway (cuffed endotracheal tube in a drowsy patient).
- **Multiple doses** for quinine, theophylline and phenobarbital (for example 50 g four times daily).
- A laxative is generally given with the charcoal.

**Poorly absorbed by activated charcoal:**

- Chemicals: acids, alkalis, ethanol, ethylene glycol, mercury, methanol, petroleum distillates.
- Medicines: **lithium, iron**.

### Gastric aspiration and lavage

No more effective than charcoal, and complications (especially aspiration) are common. Not routine.

### Whole bowel irrigation

- Used for ingested **packets of illicit drugs** or slow-release tablets (for example **iron and lithium**) that charcoal does not absorb.
- Polyethylene glycol and electrolyte solution at 1 to 2 L/hour via nasogastric tube.

## 9. Enhancing elimination

| Method | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Urinary alkalinisation** | Sodium bicarbonate (for example 1.5 L of 1.26% over 2 hours); enhances excretion of weak acids: **salicylate**, methotrexate |
| **Haemodialysis** | Ethylene glycol, isopropanol, **methanol**, **salicylates**, sodium valproate, lithium |
| **Haemoperfusion** | Theophylline, phenytoin, carbamazepine, phenobarbital, amobarbital |
| **Multiple-dose charcoal** | Quinine, theophylline, phenobarbital |

### Lipid emulsion therapy

For poisoning by **lipid-soluble agents**: local anaesthetics, tricyclic antidepressants, calcium channel blockers, lipid-soluble beta-blockers (propranolol). Give **20% lipid emulsion (for example Intralipid) 1.5 mL/kg**, then an infusion of **0.25 mL/kg/min** until clinical improvement.

## 10. General principles of supportive management

- **Respiratory failure:** clear airway; mechanical ventilation in severe cases. (Nikethamide 2 mL IV was used previously; it is obsolete.)
- **Circulatory failure:** raise the foot of the bed, IV fluids, and vasopressors such as **dopamine or adrenaline**.
- **Hypothermia:** warm room and blankets, warm water bath (42 °C).
- **Hyperthermia:** remove clothes and pack the body with ice, or immerse up to the neck in a cold water bath (25 °C).
- **Correct fluid and electrolyte imbalance.**
- **Convulsions:** slow IV diazepam, or phenytoin 10 mg/kg.
- **Antidotes** (examples to know): N-acetylcysteine (paracetamol), naloxone (opioids), atropine and pralidoxime (organophosphates), flumazenil (benzodiazepines, rarely), vitamin K (warfarin and some rodenticides), 100% oxygen (carbon monoxide), antivenom (snakebite), desferrioxamine (iron), fomepizole or ethanol (methanol, ethylene glycol).

## 11. Worked questions from the lecture

| Question | Answer and reason |
|---|---|
| 35-year-old swallowed about 50 paracetamol tablets 30 minutes ago: most appropriate initial treatment | **Activated charcoal** (within 1 hour); N-acetylcysteine is given by timed paracetamol level (4 hours); ipecac and lavage are not used |
| Carbon monoxide: which symptom can begin at carboxyhaemoglobin 10 to 20%? | **Headache** (mild; 10 to 20% gives headache and mild dyspnoea; confusion and impaired judgement occur at higher levels) |

> **Check:** the slide lists difficulty concentrating, headache, impaired judgement and confusion as options; the classic teaching is that headache starts first (10 to 20%), with impaired judgement and confusion at 30 to 40%, then coma at higher levels.

## Key points to revise

1. Poison = any substance causing ill effects by local or systemic action; types are fulminant, acute and chronic.
2. In Kenya, organophosphate and rodenticide pesticides and kerosene are the commonest poisons; self-poisoning peaks at 21 to 30 years, accidental at 0 to 5 years.
3. Treat ABCs first, then history (5 Ws), toxidrome recognition, decontamination, elimination, antidote, advice.
4. Cholinergic toxidrome: DUMBBBELS; treat with atropine, pralidoxime and diazepam.
5. Activated charcoal within an hour; poorly absorbs alcohols, acids, alkalis, lithium and iron; gastric lavage is no better and risky.
6. Urinary alkalinisation for salicylate; haemodialysis for methanol, ethylene glycol, salicylate, lithium, valproate; haemoperfusion for theophylline, phenytoin, carbamazepine and barbiturates.
7. Lipid emulsion (20%, 1.5 mL/kg) for lipid-soluble drug toxicity.
8. Flumazenil is rarely indicated and can precipitate seizures.
