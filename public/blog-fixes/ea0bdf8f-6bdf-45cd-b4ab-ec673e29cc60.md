# Acute and Chronic Hepatitis

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Gastroenterology and Hepatology**

> **In one line:** hepatitis is inflammation and necrosis of the liver; it is **acute** if it lasts under 6 months and **chronic** if it persists beyond 6 months. Hepatotropic viruses (A to E) are the commonest cause, with HBV and HCV the main drivers of chronic disease, cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Damage is mostly immune mediated, not a direct viral effect.

## What you must know

- Acute hepatitis causes: viruses (A, B, C, D, E), drugs (paracetamol), toxins, alcohol, ischaemia, Wilson disease, pregnancy-related disease.
- **HAV and HEV** are faeco-oral and never become chronic (HEV can in the immunosuppressed). **HBV, HCV, HDV** are blood-borne and can become chronic.
- Hepatitis A: ALT greater than AST, diagnosed by anti-HAV IgM; no specific treatment.
- Hepatitis B in Kenya: HBsAg prevalence about **8.8%**; over 70% of HCC patients are HBsAg positive. Treat by **HBV DNA, ALT and HBeAg status**.
- First-line HBV drugs: **pegylated interferon, entecavir, tenofovir**. Nucleos(t)ide analogues are used in decompensation, chemotherapy, immunosuppression, pregnancy and when interferon is contraindicated.
- Hepatitis C: commonest Kenyan genotypes are **4 then 1**; curable with direct-acting antivirals (sofosbuvir-based).
- Chronic hepatitis is graded (necro-inflammation) and staged (fibrosis); autoimmune hepatitis is treated with steroids and azathioprine.

## 1. Acute hepatitis: causes

| Group | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Hepatotropic viruses** | Hepatitis A, B, C, D, E |
| Other viruses | EBV, CMV, herpes viruses, yellow fever (severe in the immunocompromised) |
| Non-viral infections | Leptospira, Klebsiella, toxoplasma |
| **Drugs** | Direct toxic (dose related): paracetamol, amoxicillin. Idiosyncratic: chlorpromazine |
| **Toxins** | Mushroom (Amanita), carbon tetrachloride, aflatoxins |
| **Alcohol** | Acute alcoholic hepatitis |
| **Hypoperfusion** | Shock, severe hypotension, severe left ventricular failure (ischaemic hepatitis) |
| **Hereditary** | Wilson disease |
| **Pregnancy-associated** | Acute fatty liver of pregnancy, HELLP, cholestasis, HEV (severe) |
| **Rare** | Impaired venous return to the heart: hepatic vein occlusion, veno-occlusive disease, constrictive pericarditis; malignancy |

## 2. Acute viral hepatitis

### Classification of the viruses

| Virus | Family | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **HAV** | Picornaviridae (discovered 1973) | RNA virus, related to enteroviruses |
| **HBV** | Hepadnaviridae (1970) | Partially double-stranded circular DNA |
| **HCV** | Flaviviridae (1988) | RNA virus |
| **HDV** | Defective RNA virus | Needs HBsAg to replicate |
| **HEV** | Hepeviridae | Faeco-oral; severe in pregnancy |
| HFV | Not a separate virus; a mutant of HBV | |
| HGV | Flaviviridae | Not clearly pathogenic |

### Clinical course

1. **Incubation period**
2. **Prodromal (pre-icteric) phase:** fatigue, joint and abdominal pain, anorexia, nausea and vomiting, low fever, tender hepatomegaly. Smokers often lose the taste for cigarettes.
3. **Icteric phase:** jaundice, dark urine, pale stools, itching, high bilirubin; prodromal symptoms often ease as jaundice appears.
4. **Recovery phase:** most recover fully; ALT returns to normal over weeks.

## 3. Hepatitis A

### Virology and transmission

- Related to enteroviruses; difficult to grow in cell culture.
- Seven genotypes: **I to III and VII infect humans**; IV to VI are simian.
- Transmission: **contaminated water or food** and close personal contact (faeco-oral). Outbreaks in crowded areas with poor sanitation.

### Pathogenesis

1. Faeco-oral entry; multiplication in the intestinal epithelium.
2. Spread through the blood to the liver, reached within about 1 week.
3. Replication in hepatocytes, then shed into the intestine with bile and appearing in faeces.
4. **Incubation period 2 to 6 weeks.**
5. After replication and discharge, hepatic injury begins. Activated T cells release **interferon-gamma**, which increases HLA expression on liver cells, and **cytotoxic T cells** kill infected hepatocytes. The injury is immune mediated.

### Clinical features

- **Pre-icteric:** fatigue, joint and abdominal pain, anorexia, vomiting, hepatomegaly.
- **Icteric:** jaundice with raised bilirubin.
- Children are often asymptomatic; severity rises with age. Does not become chronic.

### Laboratory diagnosis (applies to all viral hepatitis)

- **Nucleic acid testing (NAT)** is the gold standard for viraemic stages (HAV RNA, HBV DNA, HCV RNA, HDV RNA, HEV RNA).
- Virus can be shown in faeces.
- Antibody detection by ELISA (**anti-HAV IgM = acute**; IgG = past infection or vaccination).
- **LFTs:** ALT and AST raised, **ALT greater than AST**; usually return to normal over 5 to 20 weeks; bilirubin raised; protein modest fall; ALP marginally raised (especially in the cholestatic phase).
- Imaging: abdominal ultrasound, CT.

> **Exam tip:** in acute viral hepatitis transaminases are in the hundreds to thousands, with ALT above AST. The reverse (AST over ALT, ratio above 2) points to alcohol.

### Management

Supportive: rest, hydration, avoid alcohol and hepatotoxic drugs. Admit for encephalopathy, coagulopathy (INR raised) or inability to drink. Prevention: hygiene, safe water, hepatitis A vaccine.

## 4. Hepatitis B

### Virology

- Member of the **Hepadnaviridae**; circular, partially double-stranded DNA virus.
- Replication involves **reverse transcriptase** through an **RNA intermediate**; viral DNA can **integrate** into the host genome (explains HCC risk).
- Impossible to propagate in cell culture.

### Transmission

- **Parenteral** (blood, needles)
- **Vertical** (mother to child; risk highest when mother is **HBeAg positive**)
- **Sexual**

### Epidemiology

- About **350 million** carriers worldwide; about 120 million in China.
- High prevalence in sub-Saharan Africa, accounting for about 80% of adults with sporadic hepatitis there.
- **Kenya:** HBsAg prevalence **8.8%**; urban areas 8 to 30%; **over 70% of HCC patients are HBsAg positive**.
- The slides note 50% of children born to mothers with chronic HBV in the US are Asian American.

### High-risk groups

IV drug users, babies of mothers with chronic HBV, people with multiple sexual partners, healthcare workers, HIV-positive patients, patients needing blood and blood products.

### Pathogenesis and immunity

- Virus enters hepatocytes via the blood.
- **The immune response to viral antigens on the hepatocyte surface** causes the clinical illness (the virus is not directly cytopathic).
- About **5% of adults become chronic carriers** (much higher after perinatal infection).
- Anti-HBs may confer lifelong immunity. Transmissibility is low when anti-HBe is present.

### Clinical features

- Incubation period average 60 to 90 days (range 45 to 180 days).
- Insidious onset: anorexia, fatigue.
- One third of adults are asymptomatic; jaundice in 30 to 50%.
- Premature death from chronic liver failure in 15 to 25% of chronic carriers.

### Serology in brief

| Marker | Meaning |
|---|---|
| HBsAg | Infection (acute or chronic); present over 6 months = chronic |
| Anti-HBs | Immunity (recovery or vaccination) |
| Anti-HBc IgM | Acute infection (and the window period) |
| Anti-HBc IgG | Past or ongoing infection; not made by vaccination |
| HBeAg | High replication and infectivity |
| Anti-HBe | Lower infectivity |
| HBV DNA | Viral load |

### Natural history: phases of chronic hepatitis B

The phases are defined by **serological markers, HBV DNA (viral load) and the degree of necro-inflammation on histology**.

| Phase | Features |
|---|---|
| **Immune tolerant** | High replication, **HBeAg positive**, normal or minimally raised ALT, little liver damage |
| **Immune clearance** | HBeAg-positive chronic hepatitis, intermittent or persistent ALT rise, high HBV DNA |
| **Inactive carrier (latent)** | Very low or undetectable HBV DNA, normal ALT, HBeAg negative |
| **Reactivation** | Return of replication and ALT rise (often HBeAg negative, precore mutant) |

**Factors influencing progression:** gender, age at infection, host immune status, viral mutations, HBeAg status, alcohol use, co-infection with HCV or HIV.

### Goals of treatment

- Reduce the risk of progression to chronic liver disease
- Prevent transmission to others
- Prevent long-term complications (cirrhosis, HCC)

### Who to treat

Eligibility depends on **ALT, HBeAg status, HBV DNA** and, where indicated, liver biopsy and HBsAg quantification.

| Group | HBV DNA | ALT | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| **HBeAg positive** | above 20 000 IU/ml | above 2 x ULN | Treat without biopsy |
| HBeAg positive | above 20 000 IU/ml | 1 to 2 x ULN | Liver biopsy; treat if moderate to severe necro-inflammation |
| HBeAg positive, normal ALT, **over 40 years** | above 20 000 | Normal | Check ALT every 3 to 6 months; treat if two consecutive ALT above 2 x ULN; biopsy if it stays 1 to 2 x ULN |
| HBeAg positive | below 20 000 | Normal | No treatment; monitor ALT and HBV DNA every 6 to 12 months |
| **HBeAg negative** | 2000 IU/ml or above | 2 x ULN or above | Treat, no biopsy |
| HBeAg negative | 2000 IU/ml or above | below 2 x ULN | Monitor every 3 to 6 months or consider biopsy |
| HBeAg negative (inactive carrier) | below 2000 IU/ml | Normal | No treatment; monitor every 6 to 12 months |

**Who not to treat:** immune-tolerant individuals (HBeAg positive, **under 40 years**, normal ALT, HBV DNA above 20 000 IU/ml) and inactive HBsAg carriers (HBeAg negative, normal ALT, HBV DNA below 2000 IU/ml). Over 40 years: monitor ALT every 3 to 6 months and do a biopsy if ALT stays above 1 to 2 x ULN; treat if moderate to severe necro-inflammation.

### Treatment options

Five **nucleos(t)ide analogues** and two **immunomodulators** (pegylated interferons).

| Drug | Comment |
|---|---|
| **Peg interferon alfa (2a, 2b)** | Response rate 30 to 40%; finite course |
| Lamivudine (3TC) | Well tolerated but high resistance |
| Adefovir | Less resistance than lamivudine |
| **Entecavir** | Most potent antiviral; compared with adefovir |
| **Tenofovir (TDF)** | Preferred, high barrier to resistance |
| Telbivudine | Resistance |

- **Preferred nucleos(t)ide analogues: tenofovir and entecavir.**
- **Recommended first line: peg interferon, entecavir, tenofovir.** Where there is no contraindication, peg interferon is the preferred choice.
- **Nucleoside analogues are the choice in:** hepatic decompensation, patients receiving chemotherapy, immunosuppressed patients, pregnant patients who need treatment, and patients with contraindications to peg interferon (cardiovascular, thyroid or psychiatric disorders).
- **Peg interferon dose and duration (48 weeks):** peg-IFN alfa-2a **180 microgram** SC weekly; peg-IFN alfa-2b **1.5 microgram/kg** SC weekly.

> **Check:** the slide writes peg-IFN alfa-2b as "15 ug/kg"; the standard dose is 1.5 microgram/kg weekly (as used for hepatitis C below).

### Markers of treatment efficacy

1. **Virological response:** HBV DNA undetectable.
2. **Serological response:** loss of HBeAg with or without anti-HBe.
3. **Biochemical response:** ALT normal.
4. **Histological response:** less necro-inflammation, with or without improved histology.
5. **Loss of HBsAg and appearance of anti-HBs:** the optimal end point, rarely achieved.

### Prevention

- **Vaccination** with recombinant vaccine (part of the infant schedule).
- **Hepatitis B immunoglobulin (HBIG):** within 48 hours of exposure, and for neonates of **HBsAg and HBeAg positive** mothers (given with the first vaccine dose).
- Screening of blood and blood products.

## 5. Hepatitis C

### Epidemiology

- Kenyan genotypes: **4 and 1, with genotype 4 the commonest**.
- HIV and HBV co-infection add to morbidity and mortality.
- Higher incidence in North Africa.

### Transmission risk factors

Sexual contact, haemodialysis, transfusion or transplant from an infected donor, needle-stick and sharps injuries, birth to an infected mother, IV drug use.

### Chronic hepatitis C

The spectrum of disease resembles chronic hepatitis B but at a lower frequency (most infections become chronic, about 70 to 80%; cirrhosis and HCC may follow after decades).

### Laboratory diagnosis

- **Anti-HCV antibody:** screening/diagnosis; **not useful in the acute phase** (window period).
- **HCV RNA** (PCR, branched DNA): confirms active infection, tests response.
- HCV antigen: used like HCV RNA.
- Genotype to plan management.

### Treatment

- **Interferon-era regimen:** peg-IFN alfa-2a 180 microgram SC weekly, or peg-IFN alfa-2b 1.5 microgram/kg SC weekly, plus **ribavirin** 1000 mg/day (under 75 kg) or 1200 mg/day (over 75 kg). **Monotherapy is not recommended.** Peg interferon is contraindicated in hepatic decompensation (for example ascites).
- **Direct-acting antivirals:** sofosbuvir (oral; approved 2013) and ledipasvir/sofosbuvir combination (approved 2014). These are now the standard and cure over 90%.

**Contraindications to interferon-based treatment:**
- Depression, psychosis, epilepsy
- Uncontrolled autoimmune disease
- Pregnancy
- Poorly controlled hypertension, diabetes, heart failure, COPD
- Low Hb, neutropenia (below 1500/mm3), platelets below 90 000/mm3
- Untreated thyroid disease

## 6. Drug- and toxin-induced hepatitis

- Diagnosis: **good history** (timing, all drugs including herbal), specific assay (for example paracetamol level), full blood count (**eosinophilia** suggests drug hypersensitivity), occasionally liver biopsy.
- Management: stop the culprit; N-acetylcysteine for paracetamol.

## 7. Chronic hepatitis

### Definition

Hepatic inflammation and necrosis lasting **more than 6 months**. Milder forms are non-progressive or only slowly progressive; severe forms progress to cirrhosis.

### Causes

| Group | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Viral** | HBV, HBV with HDV, HCV |
| **Drugs** | Methyldopa, isoniazid, ketoconazole, nitrofurantoin |
| **Autoimmune** | Autoimmune hepatitis (can accompany SLE) |
| **Alcohol** | Alcoholic liver disease |
| **Metabolic/hereditary** | Wilson disease, haemochromatosis, alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency |
| **Idiopathic** | Cryptogenic |

### Classification

- By **aetiology**.
- By **histological activity index**: **grade** (necro-inflammation) and **stage** (degree of fibrosis, which marks progression).

### Investigation

| Suspected cause | Tests |
|---|---|
| Liver biochemistry | All patients |
| **HBV** | HBsAg first; if positive, HBeAg, anti-HBe, HBV DNA, rarely genotype |
| **HCV** | Anti-HCV, HCV RNA, genotype to plan treatment |
| **HDV** | In a background of positive HBsAg: anti-HDV, HDV RNA, IgG anti-HBc |
| **Autoimmune hepatitis** | ANA, SMA, anti-SLA, anti-LKM; hypergammaglobulinaemia |
| **Wilson disease** | Serum ceruloplasmin, 24-hour urine copper |
| **Haemochromatosis** | Raised transferrin saturation and ferritin; HFE gene |
| Imaging | Ultrasound, CT |
| Liver biopsy | Grade and stage |

### Treatment

- **Hepatitis B:** pegylated interferon or nucleos(t)ide analogues.
- **Hepatitis C:** pegylated interferon with ribavirin (now DAAs).
- **Autoimmune hepatitis:** steroids and azathioprine.
- **Alcohol:** stop alcohol, give B vitamins.
- Treat metabolic causes specifically (copper chelation, venesection).

### Complications

Cirrhosis, portal hypertension, ascites, encephalopathy, variceal bleeding, HCC (screen with ultrasound and AFP every 6 months in cirrhosis and high-risk HBV).

## Key points to revise

1. Acute hepatitis is under 6 months; chronic is over 6 months of inflammation and necrosis.
2. HAV is faeco-oral with a 2 to 6 week incubation, ALT above AST, never chronic.
3. HBV is a DNA virus using reverse transcriptase; Kenyan HBsAg prevalence 8.8%; over 70% of HCC is HBsAg positive.
4. HBV treatment depends on HBeAg status, HBV DNA (20 000 vs 2000 IU/ml) and ALT (2 x ULN).
5. First-line HBV drugs: peg interferon, entecavir, tenofovir; nucleos(t)ide analogues in decompensation, pregnancy, immunosuppression.
6. HBIG within 48 hours plus vaccine protects exposed persons and neonates of HBeAg-positive mothers.
7. HCV: anti-HCV then HCV RNA; genotype 4 commonest in Kenya; sofosbuvir-based DAAs cure.
8. Chronic hepatitis causes: viral, drugs, autoimmune, alcohol, Wilson, haemochromatosis, alpha-1 antitrypsin; grade and stage on biopsy.
