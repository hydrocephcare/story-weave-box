# Fluid and Electrolyte Disorders

**Year 4 · Internal Medicine · Nephrology and electrolytes**

> **In one line:** the body protects its **effective circulating volume (ECV)** and its **plasma osmolality** with baroreceptors, osmoreceptors, the sympathetic system, RAAS, ANP and AVP. Sodium disorders are really disorders of **water balance** (osmolality), whereas potassium disorders threaten the **heart and muscle**. Correct the cause, correct slowly, and protect the brain and the heart first.

## What you must know

- Total body water is about **60% of body weight**; two thirds intracellular, one third extracellular (of which two thirds interstitial and one third plasma).
- **Serum osmolality = 2[Na] + glucose + urea** (mmol/L). Always check osmolality before calling hyponatraemia "true".
- **Hyponatraemia** (Na below 135 mmol/L) is classified by volume state: oedematous, volume-depleted or isovolaemic (SIADH is the classic isovolaemic cause).
- Treat hyponatraemia by restricting free water, promoting water loss, giving hypertonic saline only if symptomatic, and treating the cause.
- **Hypernatraemia** (Na above 145 mmol/L) is nearly always a **water deficit**; correct slowly, orally or by nasogastric water where possible.
- **Hypokalaemia** causes weakness, U waves and arrhythmias; **hyperkalaemia** causes peaked T waves, wide QRS and sine wave, and needs **calcium gluconate** first to protect the heart.

## 1. Body fluid compartments

| Compartment | Share |
|---|---|
| Total body water | about 60% of body weight (55 to 75% intracellular, 25 to 45% extracellular) |
| Extracellular fluid | two thirds interstitial (extravascular), one third intravascular (plasma) |

- This is the **rule of thirds**: one third of total water is extracellular, and one third of the extracellular fluid is plasma.
- Water moves freely across cell membranes, so **osmolality is the same in all compartments**; sodium is the main extracellular osmole, so serum sodium reflects water balance, not sodium stores.

## 2. Effective circulating volume (ECV) and its control

- **ECV** is the part of intravascular volume that maintains organ perfusion pressure. It is "jealously protected": the body will sacrifice osmolality to protect volume.
- ECV is not the same as total extracellular volume. In heart failure or cirrhosis the extracellular fluid is increased, but the **ECV is low**, so the kidney retains sodium and water.

### Sensors

| Sensor | Trigger | Response |
|---|---|---|
| **Baroreceptors** (carotid sinus, aortic arch, afferent arteriole, atria) | fall in blood pressure or stretch | sympathetic activation, RAAS, AVP release |
| **Osmoreceptors** (anterolateral hypothalamus) | rise in effective osmolality of 2% or more | AVP release and thirst: water conservation |
| **Chemoreceptors** (macula densa cells, distal nephron) | fall in sodium delivery | renin release, RAAS stimulation |

AVP also causes vasoconstriction, which helps renal perfusion.

### Effector (neuro-humoral) systems

- **Sympathetic system, RAAS, ANP and AVP.** Each is linked to the others.
- **Rapid responses (minutes)** have haemodynamic effects (vasoconstriction, heart rate).
- **Slow responses (hours)** change sodium and water balance (renal retention or excretion).
- **ANP** acts in the opposite direction: natriuresis and vasodilatation when the atria are stretched.

### Volume loss

- Clinical situations altering ECV include haemorrhage, GI, skin and renal losses (fall in ECV) and heart failure, cirrhosis and nephrotic syndrome (low ECV with oedema).
- Diagnosis of volume loss is clinical: thirst, postural hypotension, tachycardia, reduced skin turgor, dry mucous membranes, low JVP, oliguria; supported by raised urea and creatinine and concentrated urine.
- **Management principle:** replenish the intravascular volume (oral where possible, otherwise IV crystalloid; blood in haemorrhage).

## 3. Hyponatraemia

**Definition:** serum [Na+] below **135 mmol/L**. It is a marker of a **hypo-osmolar (hypotonic)** state, except in pseudohyponatraemia.

### Pseudohyponatraemia

Plasma is about 93% water; the rest is protein and lipid. Sodium is dissolved only in the water, so if the non-water fraction rises, the measured sodium falls although the plasma water sodium is normal. Plasma osmolality is **normal or high**.

| Type | Causes |
|---|---|
| **Hypertonic hyponatraemia** (water pulled out of cells) | hyperglycaemia, IV mannitol |
| **Isotonic hyponatraemia** | hyperproteinaemia (e.g. myeloma), hyperlipidaemia, bladder irrigation fluid absorbed after TURP (glycine) |

> **Remember:** in the osmolality formula sodium is multiplied by 2 to account for its accompanying anions. Normal serum osmolality is about 275 to 295 mmol/kg.

> **Check:** for each 5.6 mmol/L rise in glucose above normal, serum sodium falls by about 1.6 mmol/L (a standard correction factor) so correct sodium for hyperglycaemia before treating.

### True (hypotonic) hyponatraemia: three clinical states

**I. Oedema states** (interstitial volume expansion)
- Low ECV leads to thirst, RAAS activation, raised AVP and fall in GFR.
- There is primary sodium gain with a **secondary, larger water gain**.
- Examples: **heart failure, nephrotic syndrome, hepatic cirrhosis**.

**II. Volume-depleted state** (ECF volume depletion)
- Thirst and raised AVP drive water intake, so there is primary sodium loss with secondary water gain.
- Loss routes: **skin** (sweating, burns), **GI** (vomiting, diarrhoea) and **renal** (diuretics, mineralocorticoid deficiency, salt-wasting).

**III. Isovolaemic (euvolaemic) state**
- Normal sodium stores with a **total body excess of free water**; mild oedema may be present. Primary water gain with secondary sodium loss.
- Causes: **primary polydipsia**; AVP release (pain, nausea, drugs); **glucocorticoid deficiency** (glucocorticoids normally promote water diuresis, GFR and renal plasma flow); **hypothyroidism**; chronic renal insufficiency; **SIADH**.

> **Exam tip:** SIADH is a diagnosis of exclusion: euvolaemic hyponatraemia, low plasma osmolality, urine osmolality above 100 mOsm/kg and urine sodium above 30 mmol/L, with normal renal, adrenal and thyroid function and no diuretics.

### Clinical features

- Water shifts into cells by osmosis, causing **brain cell swelling and brain oedema**.
- Symptoms depend on (i) the **rapidity** of onset and (ii) the **degree** of hyponatraemia.
- Sequence: asymptomatic, then mild (nausea, malaise), then headache, lethargy and confusion, then stupor, seizures and coma. Worse with rapid falls.
- **Chronic hyponatraemia:** adaptive mechanisms (loss of Na+, K+ and organic osmolytes from brain cells) protect cell volume, so symptoms are milder.

### Management principles

1. **Restrict free water intake.**
2. **Promote water loss** (diuretics).
3. **Raise plasma sodium:** **hypertonic saline** for symptomatic patients.
4. **Correct the underlying disorder** (stop the offending drug, replace volume in the depleted state, treat cardiac failure, replace hormones).

- In the volume-depleted state, isotonic saline restores volume and switches off AVP.
- **Do not correct too fast.** Aim for a rise of no more than about 8 to 10 mmol/L in 24 hours in chronic hyponatraemia; overcorrection risks **osmotic demyelination** (central pontine myelinolysis: quadriparesis, dysarthria, locked-in state).

## 4. Hypernatraemia

**Definition:** plasma [Na+] above **145 mmol/L**. It is always a **hyperosmolar** state. Most cases are due to **water deficit**; primary sodium gain is uncommon.

### Normal response

Hyperosmolality causes thirst and raised AVP, with excretion of **maximally concentrated urine**. Hypernatraemia therefore stays mild unless the thirst mechanism is abnormal or access to water is limited.

### Who is at risk

Infants, the physically handicapped, those with impaired mental status, post-operative patients and the elderly.

### Causes

| Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Free water loss** | insensible skin loss; lungs (fever, burns, hyperventilation); GI (diarrhoea); **renal loss (commonest)**; loop diuretics; osmotic diuresis (mannitol, hyperglycaemia, urea, high protein diet); **diabetes insipidus** |
| **Primary sodium gain (infrequent)** | 3% hypertonic saline; sodium bicarbonate (resuscitation); infant formula wrongly made (salt instead of sugar) |

### Clinical features

- Water shifts out of cells, so **brain cell volume falls**; the brain shrinks and risks **subarachnoid and intracerebral bleeds**.
- Altered mental status, weakness, neuromuscular irritability, focal deficits, seizures, coma.
- Polyuria and polydipsia, plus signs of volume depletion.
- **High mortality when plasma [Na+] exceeds 180 mmol/L.**
- **Chronic hypernatraemia** is less symptomatic: cells take up Na+, K+ salts and **organic osmolytes** (e.g. inositol) to protect volume.

### Management

1. Treat the underlying cause.
2. Stop ongoing water loss.
3. Correct the water deficit:
   - **Water deficit = total body water × (plasma [Na+] − 140) / 140.**
   - **Correct slowly** (aim for a fall of no more than about 10 mmol/L in 24 hours); the **safest route is oral or nasogastric water**.
   - IV options range from varying strengths of saline to **5% dextrose in water**.

> **Exam tip:** correcting chronic hypernatraemia too fast causes **cerebral oedema** because brain cells have accumulated osmolytes; correcting chronic hyponatraemia too fast causes **osmotic demyelination**. The common thread is: slow correction.

## 5. Potassium

- **Major intracellular cation.** Plasma 3.5 to 5.0 mmol/L; intracellular about 150 mmol/L.
- Distribution between cells and plasma is influenced by **hormones** (insulin, catecholamines), **acid-base balance**, **osmolality** and **cell turnover**.
- Dietary intake 40 to 120 mmol/day; excretion is mainly **renal** (about 90% handled at the proximal tubule, with the final adjustment in the distal nephron under aldosterone).

### Hypokalaemia

> **Check:** the slide gives below 3 mmol/L; the standard definition is a plasma potassium **below 3.5 mmol/L** (moderate 2.5 to 3.0, severe below 2.5).

**Causes**
1. **Reduced intake:** poor diet, **geophagia** (earth eating).
2. **Shift into cells:** alkalosis, insulin, beta-adrenergic agonists.
3. **Increased loss:** GI (diarrhoea, vomiting); sweat; renal (diuretics, mineralocorticoid excess, etc.).

**Features:** fatigue, myalgia, muscle weakness, paralysis, hypoventilation. ECG: flat or inverted T waves, **U waves**, ST depression, prolonged QU and PR, wide QRS, ventricular arrhythmias.

**Management**
1. Correct the potassium deficit: **oral** replacement is best; **IV KCl** (given diluted and slowly, with ECG monitoring) when severe.
2. Minimise losses (stop or change the diuretic, treat diarrhoea).

> **Remember:** check and replace magnesium too; hypokalaemia will not correct if magnesium is low.

### Hyperkalaemia

**Definition:** plasma potassium above 5 mmol/L.

**Causes**
- Release from cells: **acidosis**.
- Reduced excretion: **renal failure**.
- Excessive parenteral potassium.
- Reduced distal tubular secretion: **hypoaldosteronism**.
- Drugs: potassium-sparing diuretics, **ACE inhibitors, ARBs**, beta-blockers, **NSAIDs**.

**Features:** weakness, paralysis, hypoventilation and metabolic acidosis, cardiac toxicity. ECG sequence: **peaked T waves**, prolonged PR, wide QRS, AV conduction delay, **sine wave** and then asystole or VF.

**Management** (three actions)

| Aim | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Protect the heart** | **calcium gluconate** IV (stabilises the membrane; effect lasts minutes) |
| **Shift K+ into cells** | insulin with dextrose; sodium bicarbonate (if acidotic); beta-agonists (salbutamol nebulised) |
| **Remove K+ from the body** | loop diuretics; cation-exchange resins; **dialysis** (in renal failure or refractory cases) |

> **Exam tip:** the first drug in hyperkalaemia with ECG changes is **calcium gluconate**; it does not lower potassium, it buys time.

## Key points to revise

1. Total body water is 60% of body weight; two thirds intracellular, and the extracellular third is two thirds interstitial and one third plasma.
2. ECV is sensed by baroreceptors, osmoreceptors (2% change) and macula densa chemoreceptors, acting through sympathetics, RAAS, ANP and AVP.
3. Serum osmolality = 2[Na] + glucose + urea; pseudohyponatraemia has normal or high osmolality.
4. Hyponatraemia (below 135): oedematous, volume-depleted or isovolaemic (SIADH, polydipsia, hypothyroidism, cortisol deficiency); treat by water restriction, diuretics, hypertonic saline if symptomatic.
5. Hypernatraemia (above 145) is mostly water deficit; replace the deficit slowly, preferably orally.
6. Hypokalaemia: weakness, flat T, U waves, arrhythmias; treat orally or with slow IV KCl and stop the losses.
7. Hyperkalaemia: peaked T, wide QRS, sine wave; calcium gluconate, then insulin/dextrose, bicarbonate, salbutamol, and removal by diuretics, resins or dialysis.
