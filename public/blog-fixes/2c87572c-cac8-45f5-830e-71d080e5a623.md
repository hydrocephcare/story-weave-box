## MYCOSES — SUPERFICIAL, CUTANEOUS, SUBCUTANEOUS, SYSTEMIC & OPPORTUNISTIC

## CLASSIFICATION OF MYCOSES

| Type | Depth of Infection | Host Response | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Superficial | Outermost skin layer only | None | Pityriasis versicolor, Tinea nigra |
| Cutaneous | Skin, hair, nails (keratinized tissue) | Cellular immune response | Dermatophytoses (ringworm) |
| Subcutaneous | Skin + subcutaneous tissue | Granulomatous | Mycetoma, Sporotrichosis |
| Systemic | Deep organs | Variable | Histoplasmosis, Blastomycosis |
| Opportunistic | Any site in immunocompromised host | Variable | Candidiasis, Cryptococcosis, Aspergillosis |

## A. SUPERFICIAL MYCOSES

Infection restricted to the **outermost horny layer** of skin, hair and nails. No cellular response, no pathological changes, purely cosmetic. No discomfort to the patient.

**Predisposing factors:** humidity, immunosuppression, poor hygiene

### 1. Pityriasis Versicolor (Tinea Versicolor)

Also called: Dermatomycosis furfuracea, Tinea flava

**Causative agents:** *Malassezia globosa* and *Malassezia furfur*
- These are **lipophilic yeasts** (feed on skin oils/lipids)
- Part of normal skin flora — only become pathogenic under warm, humid conditions

**Clinical features:**
- Hypopigmented or hyperpigmented skin patches
- Usually on trunk, back, underarm
- Occasionally on face

**Diagnosis:**
- KOH mount of skin scales → **"spaghetti and meatballs"** appearance (short curved septate hyphae + budding yeast cells)
- Under Wood's lamp → fluoresces **yellow**
- Culture media must be supplemented with fatty acids (lipophilic organism)

**Treatment:** Topical imidazole

### 2. Tinea Nigra
- Dark, non-scaly patches on palms
- Caused by *Hortaea werneckii* (dematiaceous/dark fungus)
- Diagnosis: KOH mount showing dark septate hyphae
- Treatment: Topical azoles or salicylic acid

### 3. Black Piedra
- Hard dark nodules on hair shafts
- Caused by *Piedraia hortae*
- Treatment: Shaving affected hair + topical azoles

### 4. White Piedra
- Soft white/cream nodules on hair shafts
- Caused by *Trichosporon* spp.
- Treatment: Shaving affected hair + topical azoles

## B. CUTANEOUS MYCOSES (DERMATOPHYTOSES)

Involves skin, nails and hair infections. Caused by fungi that invade **superficial keratinized tissue**. Clinical manifestation = **ringworm/tinea**. Evoke cellular immune response and may cause hypersensitivity (id reaction).

### Causative Agents — Dermatophytes

Dermatophytes are **keratinophilic** (keratin-loving). They use keratin as nitrogen source and produce **keratinases** (extracellular enzymes that hydrolyze keratin).

Three genera:

| Genus | Sites Infected | Age Group | Macroconidia | Microconidia |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *Microsporum* | Hair, skin; rarely nails | Children mainly | Numerous, thick-walled, rough, spindle-shaped | Rare |
| *Trichophyton* | Hair, skin AND nails | Both children & adults | Rare, thin-walled, smooth, pencil-shaped | Abundant |
| *Epidermophyton* | Skin, nails; rarely hair | Adults mainly | Numerous, smooth, club-shaped in clusters | Absent |

### Ecology — Sources of Dermatophytes

| Type | Source | Transmission | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anthropophilic | Humans only | Man to man by close contact or contaminated objects (comb, hat) | *T. rubrum*, *E. floccosum*, *M. audouinii* |
| Zoophilic | Animals | Close contact with animals (cats, dogs, cows) or contaminated products | *M. canis*, *M. nanum*, *T. verrucosum* |
| Geophilic | Soil | Direct exposure to soil | *M. gypseum* |

### Clinical Forms of Dermatophytosis

Spread peripherally from foci to produce **ring-like lesions** — hence "ringworm" or "tinea." Named by site affected:

**Tinea Pedis (Athlete's Foot)**
- Most prevalent of all dermatophytoses
- Caused by *T. rubrum*, *T. mentagrophytes*, *E. floccosum* (anthropophilic)
- Starts itchy between toes → small vesicles rupture → skin macerated, peels, cracks
- Prone to secondary bacterial infection → lymphangitis and lymphadenitis

**Tinea Corporis (Ringworm of the Body)**
- Also called Tinea circinata, Tinea glabrosa
- Infection of glabrous (hairless) skin
- Annular lesions with active border — may be pustular or vesicular
- Caused by *T. rubrum*, *T. mentagrophytes*, *M. canis*, *E. floccosum*
- Often from a pet or another body site
- Usually resolves in several months
- Treatment: topical ketoconazole, miconazole

**Tinea Capitis** — scalp and hair; mainly children
**Tinea Barbae** — beard area
**Tinea Cruris (Jock Itch)** — groin area
**Tinea Unguium (Onychomycosis)** — nails
**Tinea Manum** — hands
**Tinea Faciei** — face
**Tinea Imbricata** — concentric ring pattern; tropical regions

### Laboratory Diagnosis of Dermatophytoses

**Specimen collection:**
- Skin scales, nail scrapings, hair clippings
- Scrape outwards from the edge of lesion with scalpel
- Stop antifungals one week before nail specimen collection

**1. Wood's Lamp (Macroscopic)**
- Infected hair/scalp lesions fluoresce under UV light
- *Microsporum* spp. fluoresce bright green
- *Trichophyton* spp. generally do NOT fluoresce

**2. Direct Microscopy — KOH Mount (10–20% KOH)**
- KOH digests protein debris and dissolves keratin, leaving fungal elements visible
- DMSO can be added to hasten clearing
- In skin/nails → branching hyphae or chains of arthrospores seen
- In hair → hyphae or spores detected:
  - **Ectothrix** — spores OUTSIDE the hair shaft
  - **Endothrix** — spores INSIDE the hair shaft

**3. Calcofluor White (CFW)**
- Fluorescent stain — binds specifically to glucan and chitin in fungal cell wall
- Emits blue fluorescence under illumination
- Excellent morphology of pathogenic fungi

**4. Culture**
- **Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (SDA)** containing:
  - Actidione (cycloheximide) — inhibits saprophytes
  - Chloramphenicol — inhibits bacteria
- Incubated at **room temperature** for up to **4 weeks**
- Colonies identified by morphology, colour, texture
- Microscopic examination using **Lactophenol Cotton Blue (LPCB)** stain:
  - Lactic acid — preserves fungal structure
  - Phenol — kills live organisms
  - Glycerol — prevents drying
  - Cotton blue — imparts blue colour to structures

### Treatment of Dermatophytoses

**Topical:**
- Non-specific: Whitfield's ointment
- Specific: azole creams/lotions/shampoos — clotrimazole, ketoconazole, miconazole

**Oral (for nail, scalp, severe skin infections):**
- Griseofulvin, terbinafine, itraconazole

**Prevention:** proper hygiene, sterilization of instruments with hot mineral oil, effective treatment of cases, reduce contact with infectious materials

## C. SUBCUTANEOUS MYCOSES

Chronic, localized infections of skin and subcutaneous tissue following **traumatic implantation** of the fungus (e.g. thorn prick, splinter). All causative fungi are **soil saprophytes**. Infection evolves slowly as the fungus adapts to host tissue. Difficult to treat — surgical intervention (excision or amputation) frequently required.

### 1. Mycetoma (Madura Foot)

Mycotic infection of humans and animals caused by different fungi and actinomycetes.

**Two types:**
- **Eumycotic mycetoma** — caused by true fungi
- **Actinomycotic mycetoma** — caused by Actinomycetes (bacteria-like organisms)

**Distribution:** Worldwide but most common in barefoot populations in tropical/subtropical regions. Endemic in Africa, India, Central and South America. Mainly affects feet — **mycetoma pedis (Madura foot)**

**Clinical features:**
- Involves cutaneous and subcutaneous tissue; may reach bone
- Enters through wounds on feet, hands or back
- Starts as small hard painless nodule → softens → ulcerates → discharges viscous purulent fluid containing **granules**
- Granule characteristics (colour, size, hardness) depend on causative organism:
  - **Yellow granules** → actinomycetes
  - **Black granules** → fungi (e.g. *Madurella mycetomatis*)
  - **White granules** → fungi (e.g. *Pseudallesceria boydii*)

**Causative Agents:**

| Actinomycotic | Eumycotic |
|---|---|
| *Nocardia asteroides*, *N. brasilensis*, *N. caviae* | *Madurella mycetomatis*, *M. grisea* |
| *Actinomadura madurae*, *A. pelletieri* | *Pseudallesceria boydii* |
| *Actinomyces israelii* | *Exophiala jeanselmei*, *Phialophora verrucosa* |

**Laboratory Diagnosis:**
1. Specimen: tissue biopsy, serosanguinous fluid with granules
2. Macroscopic exam of granules: yellow (actinomycetes) vs black/white (fungi)
3. Microscopy: KOH + calcofluor white; H&E staining shows true septate hyphae in eumycotic type
4. Culture: SDA at room temperature for mould isolation

**Treatment:**
- Eumycotic mycetoma is **resistant to antibiotics**
- Requires surgical amputation + prolonged antifungal therapy (ketoconazole, itraconazole, voriconazole)
- Actinomycotic mycetoma responds to prolonged antibiotic therapy (sulfonamides + streptomycin)

### 2. Sporotrichosis ("Rose Gardener's Disease")

Chronic mycotic infection of cutaneous/subcutaneous tissues and adjacent lymphatics.

**Agent:** *Sporothrix schenckii* — found in soil, decaying vegetation, thorns

**Three types:**

**Cutaneous Sporotrichosis (commonest):**
- Traumatic implantation of conidia into skin (typically thorn prick)
- Small painless bumps develop 1–12 weeks after exposure
- Bumps grow larger → open sore/ulcer
- Elevated subcutaneous nodules develop along regional lymphatics (**lymphocutaneous pattern**)

**Pulmonary Sporotrichosis (rare):**
- Inhalation of spores
- Symptoms: cough, shortness of breath, chest pain, fever

**Disseminated Sporotrichosis:**
- Spreads to joints (joint pain), CNS (headache, seizures, cognitive difficulties)

**Distribution:** Worldwide; higher incidence in agricultural workers and gardeners; tropical and temperate regions

**Diagnosis:** Culture on SDA; microscopy showing cigar-shaped yeast cells in tissue

**Treatment:** Itraconazole (drug of choice); potassium iodide (saturated solution) for cutaneous form; amphotericin B for disseminated disease

### 3. Chromoblastomycosis

Mycotic infection of cutaneous and subcutaneous tissues characterized by development of **dematiaceous (brown-pigmented), planate-dividing, rounded sclerotic bodies** in tissue.

**Agents (all dematiaceous fungi from soil/decaying vegetation):**
- *Phialophora verrucosa*
- *Cladophialophora carrionii*
- *Fonsecaea pedrosoi*
- *Fonsecaea compacta*

**Clinical features:**
- Chronic, slowly progressive, localized infection
- Warty cutaneous nodules raised 1–3 cm above skin surface (verrucous/cauliflower-like)
- Tissue hyperplasia
- Common in barefoot populations in tropical regions

**Diagnosis:** KOH mount or histology showing **sclerotic (Medlar) bodies** — pathognomonic
**Treatment:** Itraconazole + flucytosine; surgical excision for small lesions

## D. SYSTEMIC (DEEP) MYCOSES

Fungal infections of deep body organs caused by **dimorphic fungal pathogens** that can overcome normal host defenses by changing morphological form. Most infections are **asymptomatic or self-limiting** in immunocompetent hosts. In immunocompromised hosts, often **fatal**.

**Route of infection:** Inhalation of spores (mould form in soil) → spores differentiate into yeast forms in lungs → dissemination via bloodstream to other organs

### 1. Coccidioidomycosis

**Agent:** *Coccidioides immitis*
**Endemic area:** Arid regions of southwestern USA, Latin America, Mexico

**Properties:**
- Dimorphic — mould in soil, **spherule** (multinucleated structure) in tissue
- Hyphae tip cells differentiate into **arthrospores** (asexual) in soil
- Inhaled arthrospores → differentiate into spherules in tissue → undergo internal cleavage → produce **endospores**

**Transmission & Pathogenesis:**
- Arthrospores are very light → carried by wind → inhaled
- Spreads via bloodstream; dissemination in patients with defective CMI
- Can affect meninges, bone, skin — forming granulomatous lesions

**Clinical features:**
- Pulmonary infection (primary) — often asymptomatic or flu-like
- Disseminated disease — skin lesions, bone lesions, meningitis
- Characteristic rash (immunologic response to fungus)

**Laboratory Diagnosis:**
- Tissue: spherules seen microscopically on H&E staining
- Culture on SDA at 25°C → hyphae with arthrospores (**HIGHLY INFECTIOUS — handle with care**)
- Skin test: coccidioidin or spherulin → ≥5mm induration at 48 hrs (delayed hypersensitivity)
- Serology: complement fixation, immunodiffusion, ELISA (now preferred over culture)

**Treatment:**
- Amphotericin B — for persistent lung lesions or disseminated disease
- Ketoconazole — for lung disease and chronic infections
- Bone infection: debridement and drainage
- Prevention: avoid endemic areas; antifungal prophylaxis for high-risk individuals

### 2. Histoplasmosis

**Agent:** *Histoplasma capsulatum*
**Endemic area:** Mississippi and Ohio River valleys (USA); worldwide in tropical regions
**Reservoir:** Soil enriched with bird or bat droppings

**Properties:** Dimorphic — mould in soil, yeast in tissue
**Transmission:** Inhalation of microconidia from soil

**Clinical features:**
- Most infections asymptomatic
- Pulmonary histoplasmosis — flu-like symptoms
- Disseminated histoplasmosis — in immunocompromised; affects liver, spleen, lymph nodes, bone marrow
- Oral ulcers common in disseminated disease

**Diagnosis:** Culture, histology (small intracellular yeast in macrophages on H&E), urine/serum antigen detection, serology

**Treatment:** Itraconazole (mild-moderate); Amphotericin B (severe/disseminated)

### 3. Blastomycosis

**Agent:** *Blastomyces dermatitidis*
**Endemic area:** North America (Mississippi/Ohio River valleys), Africa
**Reservoir:** Moist soil and decaying wood

**Properties:** Dimorphic — mould in soil, large yeast with **broad-based budding** in tissue (characteristic feature)

**Transmission:** Inhalation of conidia

**Clinical features:**
- Pulmonary blastomycosis — flu-like, may progress to ARDS
- Disseminated: skin (verrucous/ulcerated lesions), bone, genitourinary tract, CNS

**Diagnosis:** KOH mount/histology showing large yeast with broad-based buds; culture on SDA

**Treatment:** Itraconazole (mild-moderate); Amphotericin B (severe)

### 4. Paracoccidioidomycosis

**Agent:** *Paracoccidioides brasiliensis*
**Endemic area:** Latin America (especially Brazil)
**Most common systemic mycosis in Latin America**

**Properties:** Dimorphic — mould in soil, **"pilot wheel" yeast** (multiple buds around mother cell) in tissue — pathognomonic

**Transmission:** Inhalation of conidia

**Clinical features:**
- Pulmonary (primary) — often asymptomatic
- Disseminated — mucous membranes (painful oral/nasal ulcers), lymph nodes, adrenal glands, skin

**Diagnosis:** KOH/histology showing pilot wheel cells; culture; serology (immunodiffusion)

**Treatment:** Itraconazole; Amphotericin B (severe); TMP-SMX (alternative)

## E. OPPORTUNISTIC MYCOSES

Produced by relatively non-pathogenic or contaminant fungi in hosts whose immunological defenses are weakened by endogenous causes (cancer, leukemia) or exogenous causes (immunosuppressive therapy, AIDS). Important cause of morbidity and mortality in hospitalized patients.

### 1. Candidiasis

**Agent:** *Candida albicans* (most common) + ~8 other species:
- *C. glabrata*, *C. tropicalis*, *C. krusei*, *C. parapsilosis*, *C. dubliniensis*, *C. lusitaniae*, *C. pseudotropicalis*, *C. guilliermondii*

**Epidemiology:**
- Usually inhabit oral, intestinal and urogenital mucous membranes as commensals
- Candidiasis is the **most common fungal infection in HIV-infected individuals**
- Systemic candidiasis seen in patients with CMI deficiency, aggressive cancer treatment, transplantation therapy

**Predisposing factors:**
- Antibiotics — suppress normal bacterial flora → fungal overgrowth
- IV catheters, prosthetic implants, GI surgery
- Immunosuppressive conditions — HIV, steroids, pregnancy, diabetes

**Pathogenesis:**
- Mannoproteins (adhesins) in fibrillar layer → bind fibronectin receptors on mucosal cells
- **Dimorphism** — shift from yeast to hyphal form associated with pathogenicity; hyphae penetrate mucosal barriers
- Extracellular enzymes (proteases, elastases) digest epithelial cells → facilitate invasion
- *C. albicans* can acquire iron from RBCs and suppress complement production

**Clinical manifestations:**

*Mucocutaneous:*
- **Oral thrush** — white plaques on oral mucosa; common in HIV/AIDS and neonates
- **Vulvovaginal candidiasis** — common in pregnancy and diabetics; caused by suppression of vaginal lactobacilli
- **Esophageal candidiasis** — dysphagia; AIDS-defining illness
- **Chronic Mucocutaneous Candidiasis (CMC)** — in T-lymphocyte immunodeficiencies; chronic, relapsing

*Cutaneous:*
- Skin folds — axilla, groin, vulva, glans penis, around umbilicus
- Napkin area in infants (diaper rash)
- Interdigital clefts, nails, skin folds — common in people with hands frequently in water

*Systemic:*
- Neutropenia is key predisposing factor
- Affects: urinary tract, GIT, pulmonary, carditis, meningitis, ocular, osteoarticular
- **Candidemia** (disseminated candidiasis) — most serious form

**Laboratory Diagnosis:**
1. Specimen: exudates, epithelial scrapings, aspirates, biopsy, blood
2. Direct microscopy: KOH and/or Gram stain → budding yeast and pseudohyphae
3. Culture: Bird Agar and SDA at 37°C, 12–24 hrs → off-white colonies with yeast smell
4. **Germ tube test** — positive = presumptive diagnosis of *C. albicans* (forms germ tube in serum at 37°C in 2–3 hrs)
5. Species identification:
   - **CHROM Agar** — different species produce different coloured colonies (*C. albicans* = green, *C. tropicalis* = blue, *C. krusei* = pink)
   - Carbohydrate assimilation/fermentation (API system): *C. albicans* = glucose and maltose positive, sucrose and lactose negative
   - Pseudohyphae and chlamydospore production on corn meal agar
   - PCR

**Treatment:**
- *C. krusei* — intrinsic resistance to fluconazole
- *C. lusitaniae* — usually resistant to Amphotericin B
- Superficial: reduce moisture; topical nystatin or azoles
- Chronic mucocutaneous: fluconazole
- Disseminated/relapsing: Amphotericin B + fluconazole ± flucytosine

### 2. Cryptococcosis

**Agent:** *Cryptococcus neoformans*
- Encapsulated yeast, round/ovoid, 4–6 µm diameter
- Reproduces by budding
- Produces **urease enzyme**
- In culture: whitish mucoid colonies within 2–3 days

**Epidemiology:**
- Reservoir: **pigeon droppings**, nesting places, contaminated soil (nitrogenous soil, creatine favour growth)
- Old buildings are a common source
- Human infection by **inhalation** — no human-to-human transmission
- 4 capsular serotypes: A, B, C, D
  - Serotypes A & D — worldwide distribution
  - Serotypes B & C — mainly tropics and subtropics

**Risk factors:** HIV/AIDS (defective T-cell immunity), post-transplantation, corticosteroid therapy, Hodgkin's disease

**Virulence factors:**
1. **Polysaccharide capsule** — inhibits phagocytosis; main virulence factor
2. **Melanin production** via phenol oxidase enzyme (L-DOPA → melanin) — antioxidant that protects against intracellular killing by phagocytes

**Pathogenesis:**
1. Inhalation of yeast from environment
2. Yeast engulfed and retained by lung macrophages
3. Circulated to other body parts inside macrophages — particular attraction to **CNS**
4. Lodge and multiply in CNS
5. Extreme immunosuppression → disease in other organs

**Clinical features:**
- **Cryptococcal meningitis/meningoencephalitis** — most common presentation; increasing severity headache + fever → typical meningitis signs. **Most common meningitis in AIDS patients**
- **Pulmonary cryptococcosis** — usually asymptomatic
- Other sites: skin lesions, bone, oral cavity, vulva; prostatic cryptococcosis; post-transplant pyelonephritis

**Laboratory Diagnosis:**
1. Specimen: CSF, blood, sputum, biopsy, pus, urine
2. **India ink / nigrosin staining of CSF** — demonstrates encapsulated yeast (clear halo around yeast cell)
3. **Latex agglutination test** on CSF or serum — detects polysaccharide capsular antigen; rapid test
4. Culture: SDA at 37°C for 48 hrs → creamy mucoid colonies; Birdseed agar → brown colonies (melanin production)
5. Biochemical: urease test positive; API carbohydrate assimilation tests

**Treatment:**
- **Amphotericin B + 5-Flucytosine** OR **Amphotericin B + Fluconazole** (induction)
- **Fluconazole** — long-term suppression in AIDS patients (fluconazole crosses blood-brain barrier)

### 3. Aspergillosis

**Agent:** *Aspergillus fumigatus* (most common), *A. flavus*, *A. niger*, *A. terreus*
- Deuteromycetes; septate hyphae with characteristic conidiophores
- Ubiquitous in environment — soil, decaying organic matter, air

**Forms of disease:**

| Form | Host | Features |
|---|---|---|
| Allergic BronchoPulmonary Aspergillosis (ABPA) | Asthmatic/atopic patients | Type I & III hypersensitivity; eosinophilia, elevated IgE |
| Aspergilloma (fungus ball) | Pre-existing lung cavities (TB) | Non-invasive; ball of hyphae in cavity; haemoptysis |
| Invasive Aspergillosis | Severely immunocompromised (neutropenic) | Most serious; invades blood vessels → haematogenous spread; high mortality |
| Chronic Pulmonary Aspergillosis | Mildly immunocompromised | Slowly progressive lung destruction |

**Diagnosis:** Culture, microscopy (septate hyphae with 45° branching), galactomannan antigen detection in blood/BAL, CT scan (halo sign in invasive form)

**Treatment:** Voriconazole (drug of choice for invasive); itraconazole (ABPA, aspergilloma); Amphotericin B (alternative)

### 4. Zygomycosis / Mucormycosis

**Agents:** *Rhizopus*, *Mucor*, *Cunninghamella* — Zygomycetes
- Non-septate (aseptate) hyphae — distinguishing feature

**Risk factors:** Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), neutropenia, iron overload, deferoxamine therapy, burns

**Clinical forms:**
- **Rhinocerebral** (most common) — sinuses → orbit → brain; associated with DKA
- Pulmonary, cutaneous, gastrointestinal, disseminated

**Pathogenesis:** Hyphae invade blood vessels → thrombosis → tissue infarction and necrosis (characteristic black necrotic tissue)

**Diagnosis:** Histology showing broad non-septate hyphae with wide-angle (90°) branching; culture on SDA
**Treatment:** Amphotericin B + surgical debridement; control underlying condition; high mortality if untreated

### 5. Pneumocystosis

**Agent:** *Pneumocystis jirovecii* (formerly *P. carinii*) — reclassified as a fungus (previously thought to be a protozoan)

**Risk factors:** HIV/AIDS (CD4 count <200 cells/µL), immunosuppression

**Clinical features:** **Pneumocystis Pneumonia (PCP)** — progressive dyspnoea, dry cough, fever, bilateral interstitial infiltrates on chest X-ray. **AIDS-defining illness**

**Diagnosis:** Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) or induced sputum; Gomori methenamine silver (GMS) stain or immunofluorescence showing cysts

**Treatment:** **TMP-SMX** (drug of choice); pentamidine (alternative); prophylaxis with TMP-SMX when CD4 <200
