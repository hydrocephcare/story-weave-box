# Psychiatry Topic 2: Suicide and Suicidal Behaviour
## Section 1 — Introduction, Definitions, Clinical Importance and Basic Concepts
**Level:** MBChB Year 4  
**Unit:** Psychiatry  
**Learning focus:** Clinical understanding and examination preparation

---

## 1. Learning objectives

By the end of this section, the learner should be able to:

1. Define suicide, suicidal ideation, suicide attempt, self-harm and suicidal intent.
2. Distinguish passive death wishes from active suicidal ideation.
3. Explain why suicide is a major psychiatric and public-health concern.
4. Identify important risk factors, protective factors and warning signs.
5. Describe the first steps when a patient may be at risk of suicide.
6. Recognise when urgent intervention and a safe clinical environment are required.

## 2. Core definitions

### Suicide
Suicide is death resulting from an intentional, self-directed act undertaken with the intention of ending one's life.

### Suicidal ideation
Suicidal ideation refers to thoughts about ending one's own life. It may vary in intensity, frequency, duration and controllability.

- **Passive death wish:** The person wishes they were dead or would not wake up, without necessarily thinking of taking action to cause their death.
- **Active suicidal ideation:** The person thinks about taking action to end their life. Further assessment must establish intent, planning, access to means and immediacy.

A passive death wish should not automatically be considered harmless. It warrants exploration in the person's clinical context.

### Suicidal intent
Suicidal intent is the person's intention or expectation that an act may result in their death. Intent may be ambivalent and can change over time. It should be assessed directly rather than inferred from the apparent seriousness of an act alone.

### Suicide plan
A suicide plan refers to the person's thoughts about how, when or where they might act. Ask about planning and access to means sensitively and directly; avoid assuming that the absence of a detailed plan means there is no risk.

### Suicide attempt
A suicide attempt is a non-fatal, self-directed potentially injurious act undertaken with at least some intent to die. The medical severity of an attempt and the person's intent are related but are not identical.

### Self-harm
Self-harm commonly refers to intentional self-poisoning or self-injury, irrespective of the apparent purpose. Some episodes involve suicidal intent; others may serve different functions or involve mixed or unclear intent. Assess each episode individually and avoid dismissive labels.

### Suicidal behaviour
This is a broad term for behaviour related to suicide, including suicide attempts and other actions undertaken with suicidal intent. Terminology varies across clinical guidelines and research settings, so define terms clearly when documenting or discussing a case.

## 3. Why suicide matters in clinical practice

Suicidal thoughts and behaviour may occur in the context of psychiatric disorders, substance use, physical illness, severe distress, trauma or difficult social circumstances. Suicide is not explained by one diagnosis or one event. Usually, several vulnerabilities and immediate stressors interact.

Suicide prevention is part of routine clinical care, not only specialist psychiatry. Clinicians in emergency departments, medical wards, primary care, paediatrics and other settings may encounter a person who is suicidal or has recently self-harmed.

Important clinical principles:

- Take every disclosure of suicidal thoughts seriously and respond without judgement.
- Ask directly about suicide rather than relying on hints or assumptions.
- Do not assume a patient is safe because they appear calm, are young, have no known psychiatric diagnosis, or deny distress at first.
- Do not use a risk score alone to predict suicide or determine discharge. Clinical assessment, current circumstances, collateral information and safety planning are essential.
- A person may experience ambivalence: part of them may want their distress to stop while another part wants to live. Explore this compassionately.

## 4. Psychiatric and other associations

Suicidal behaviour can be associated with several clinical and psychosocial factors.

### Psychiatric associations
- **Depressive disorders:** Persistent low mood, anhedonia, hopelessness, guilt and feelings of worthlessness may contribute to suicidal thinking.
- **Bipolar disorder:** Risk may arise during depressive episodes, mixed states or periods of severe mood instability.
- **Psychotic disorders:** Command hallucinations, persecutory beliefs, severe distress or impaired judgement may increase concern.
- **Substance-use disorders:** Intoxication, withdrawal, disinhibition and longer-term social consequences can contribute to risk.
- **Anxiety, trauma-related and personality disorders:** Severe distress, impulsivity, trauma symptoms or interpersonal crises may be relevant in some patients.
- **Other conditions:** Eating disorders and other psychiatric illnesses may also be associated with suicidal behaviour.

Having a diagnosis does not mean a person will attempt suicide, and suicidal behaviour can occur in people without a diagnosed mental disorder.

### Medical and social context
Consider chronic pain, disabling illness, recent diagnosis of a serious disease, bereavement, relationship breakdown, violence, abuse, bullying, academic or employment difficulties, financial stress, social isolation and barriers to care. These factors must be explored in context and should not be treated as proof of suicidal intent.

## 5. Risk factors, protective factors and warning signs

These concepts are related but different.

### Risk factors
Risk factors are characteristics or circumstances statistically associated with increased likelihood of suicidal behaviour. Examples include:

- Previous suicide attempt, particularly a recent attempt.
- Current suicidal ideation, intent or planning.
- Severe hopelessness or acute psychiatric symptoms.
- Alcohol or other substance misuse.
- Recent major loss, humiliation, violence or interpersonal crisis.
- Social isolation or limited access to support.
- Access to potentially lethal means.
- Recent discharge from psychiatric care or interruption of treatment in a vulnerable patient.

A history of previous attempts is especially important to establish. Ask about the circumstances, intent, medical consequences, treatment received and what has changed since then.

### Protective factors
Protective factors may help reduce risk or support recovery. Examples include:

- Supportive relationships and reliable people who can help.
- Reasons for living, personal values and future goals.
- Willingness to seek help and engage with treatment.
- Effective treatment of underlying psychiatric or medical conditions.
- A collaborative safety plan and a safer environment.
- Reduced access to lethal means during a crisis.

Protective factors do not cancel out acute danger. A person with family support may still require emergency intervention.

### Warning signs
Warning signs are changes that may suggest a current or worsening crisis. Examples include:

- Talking about wanting to die, feeling trapped or being a burden.
- Increasing hopelessness, agitation, severe anxiety or distress.
- Marked withdrawal from other people.
- Escalating substance use.
- Sudden changes in behaviour, sleep or mood.
- Seeking access to lethal means, making preparations or saying goodbye in a concerning context.

No single sign reliably predicts what a person will do. Treat concerning changes as a reason to ask, assess and act.

## 6. First clinical response: what should a medical student do?

When a patient expresses suicidal thoughts or presents after self-harm, remain calm, respectful and non-judgemental.

### Step 1: Address immediate medical needs
If the patient has self-poisoned, sustained injuries or may have taken an overdose, assess and treat urgent medical problems first according to emergency protocols. Do not delay medical stabilisation while completing a psychiatric interview.

### Step 2: Establish immediate safety
If there is imminent danger, a recent medically serious attempt, severe intoxication, acute psychosis or inability to remain safe, seek urgent senior help. Maintain appropriate observation, use a safe environment and follow local protocols. Do not leave a person at imminent risk alone while arranging help.

### Step 3: Ask directly
Use clear, compassionate questions, for example:

- “Have you been thinking that life is not worth living?”
- “Have you had thoughts of ending your life?”
- “Are you thinking about suicide right now?”
- “Have you thought about how you might do it?”
- “Do you feel you might act on these thoughts?”
- “Have you done anything recently to hurt yourself or end your life?”

Ask about intent, planning, access to means, timing and any recent preparatory actions as clinically appropriate. Asking directly about suicide does not, by itself, cause suicidal behaviour.

### Step 4: Explore the clinical context
Assess mood symptoms, psychosis, substance use, previous attempts, current stressors, physical illness, supports and reasons for living. With appropriate consent and in accordance with safety and confidentiality requirements, obtain collateral information when it can clarify risk.

### Step 5: Escalate, document and arrange care
Discuss concerning findings promptly with the supervising clinician or mental-health team. Document the patient's words where relevant, key findings, immediate safety measures, consultations and follow-up plan. Disposition should be based on a comprehensive clinical assessment and local policy, not a checklist or score alone.

## 7. When is urgent assessment needed?

Urgent emergency assessment is needed when a person has made a potentially dangerous recent attempt, has current suicidal intent or a plan with access to means, is severely intoxicated or psychotic with immediate safety concerns, or cannot maintain their safety.

If the danger is immediate, activate the local emergency response and involve the responsible senior clinician. Keep communication respectful and explain each step where possible.

## 8. A simple framework for remembering the assessment

Think of these domains:

**THOUGHTS — INTENT — PLAN — MEANS — HISTORY — CONTEXT — PROTECTION — SAFETY**

- **Thoughts:** Are suicidal thoughts present? How frequent and persistent are they?
- **Intent:** Does the person intend to act?
- **Plan:** Have they considered how or when?
- **Means:** Do they have access to what they might use?
- **History:** Have there been previous attempts or episodes of self-harm?
- **Context:** What psychiatric symptoms, substance use, illness or recent stressors are present?
- **Protection:** What support, treatment, coping strategies and reasons for living are available?
- **Safety:** What action is needed now, and who will take responsibility for follow-up?

This is a memory aid, not a validated prediction tool and not a substitute for a full assessment.

## 9. Common examination pitfalls

1. **Self-harm is not synonymous with a suicide attempt.** Establish whether there was any intent to die.
2. **Medical lethality and suicidal intent are not identical.** A medically serious act does not by itself reveal the person's intent, and a less medically serious act still requires careful assessment.
3. **A passive death wish still matters.** Explore it rather than dismissing it.
4. **Risk factors do not predict an individual's actions with certainty.** Use them to guide clinical enquiry and prevention.
5. **A risk scale alone is insufficient.** Formulate the clinical situation and decide what safety measures and care are required.
6. **Medical stabilisation comes first when needed.** Psychiatric assessment should not delay treatment of poisoning, trauma or other emergencies.

## 10. Quick revision questions

**1. What is the key distinction between self-harm and a suicide attempt?**  
A suicide attempt involves at least some intent to die; self-harm is a broader term that does not necessarily imply suicidal intent.

**2. What is the difference between passive and active suicidal ideation?**  
A passive death wish involves wishing to be dead without necessarily thinking of taking action. Active suicidal ideation involves thoughts of taking action to end one's life.

**3. Does the absence of a detailed plan mean there is no suicide risk?**  
No. Assess current thoughts, intent, access to means, previous behaviour, acute symptoms, context and ability to remain safe.

**4. What is the first priority after a potentially serious self-poisoning?**  
Immediate medical assessment and stabilisation, alongside appropriate safety precautions and timely psychiatric assessment.

**5. Can protective factors rule out acute risk?**  
No. They inform the formulation but do not eliminate the need to respond to immediate danger.

## Key takeaway

Suicide assessment is a direct, compassionate and structured clinical process. Clarify thoughts, intent, planning, access to means, previous behaviour and current circumstances; address medical emergencies; and arrange appropriate safety measures and follow-up.
