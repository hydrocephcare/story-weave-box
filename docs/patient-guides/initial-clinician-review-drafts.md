# OmpathStudy Lite — initial patient-guide drafts

> **STATUS: DRAFT FOR CLINICIAN REVIEW — NOT APPROVED FOR PATIENT DISTRIBUTION**
>
> These drafts are plain-language starting points for the main OmpathStudy patient-information library. They are not medical-school notes, diagnoses, or treatment protocols. A currently registered clinician must review the clinical wording, referral thresholds, local emergency pathways, and Kiswahili translation before any article is published or approved in the Lite register.

## 1. Pregnancy: warning signs that need urgent care

**If you are pregnant and notice any of the signs below, go to a health facility immediately. Do not wait for your next antenatal appointment or for an SMS reply.**

- Bleeding from the vagina.
- Severe or persistent headache, blurred vision, or seeing spots.
- Fits or convulsions.
- Severe pain in the abdomen.
- Fever or feeling very unwell.
- Sudden swelling of the face or hands.
- Difficulty breathing.
- Fluid leaking from the vagina before labour.
- Your baby's movements become less than usual or stop.

Tell the health worker that you are pregnant and, if you know, how many weeks pregnant you are. If you feel faint, have severe pain, or are very unwell, ask someone to help you reach emergency care. Do not travel alone if you may collapse.

Antenatal clinic visits are still important when you feel well. Attend all appointments and discuss any new symptom with your maternity care team.

**Important:** This information cannot tell you whether you or your baby are safe. A health worker needs to assess you.

## 2. Lower abdominal or pelvic pain

Pain in the lower abdomen can have several causes. It is not possible to identify the cause from the location of pain alone.

**Seek emergency care now** if the pain is sudden and severe, rapidly worsening, or accompanied by fainting, collapse, difficulty breathing, or heavy vaginal bleeding.

**Seek urgent same-day assessment** if:
- You are pregnant or could be pregnant.
- You have missed a period and have abdominal pain, especially with vaginal bleeding.
- You have fever, repeated vomiting, unusual vaginal discharge, or bleeding.
- The pain is getting worse or you feel increasingly unwell.

If the pain is mild but persists, keeps returning, or worries you, arrange assessment at a health facility. Tell the clinician when the pain started, where it is, whether it is getting worse, and whether pregnancy is possible.

Do not assume that the pain is “just period pain”. Do not delay care because a symptom checker has suggested a possible cause.

## 3. Vaginal discharge

Some vaginal discharge is normal. It is often clear or white and does not have a strong unpleasant smell. The amount and appearance can change during the menstrual cycle and pregnancy.

Arrange assessment at a health facility if the discharge:
- Changes colour, smell, or texture.
- Becomes much heavier than usual.
- Comes with itching, soreness, sores, or pain when passing urine.
- Occurs with pelvic pain or bleeding between periods or after sex.

**Seek urgent same-day care** if discharge occurs with fever, significant lower abdominal pain, pregnancy, or feeling very unwell.

Avoid washing inside the vagina (douching), scented vaginal products, and perfumed washes. These can irritate the area. Do not start antibiotics or vaginal medicines without advice from a qualified health worker. Some infections need testing and treatment for sexual partners as well.

## 4. Heavy or troublesome menstrual bleeding

Menstrual bleeding may be heavier than usual for several reasons. It deserves assessment when it affects your daily life or may be causing anaemia.

Arrange a clinical review if:
- You need to change a pad every one to two hours.
- Bleeding lasts longer than seven days.
- You pass large blood clots or bleed through clothes or bedding.
- You regularly need more than one menstrual product at a time.
- You feel unusually tired, weak, or short of breath.
- Period pain is severe or bleeding is affecting school, work, or daily activities.

**Seek emergency care** if bleeding is very heavy and ongoing, or you feel faint, collapse, have chest pain, or have difficulty breathing. If pregnancy is possible, mention this immediately.

Keep a note of when bleeding starts and stops, how often you change pads, any clots, pain, and medicines you use. A clinician may need to check for anaemia and investigate the cause.

## 5. Vaginal bleeding outside your usual period

Bleeding between periods, after sex, after menopause, or when pregnancy is possible should be assessed by a health professional.

If you have missed a period and now have vaginal bleeding with lower abdominal pain, seek urgent assessment immediately. Pregnancy outside the womb (ectopic pregnancy) is one important condition clinicians must exclude.

Go to emergency care now if bleeding is heavy, you feel faint or collapse, you have severe pain, or you are pregnant and bleeding.

Do not assume that unexpected bleeding is harmless or that it is always caused by contraception. Tell the clinician about your last menstrual period, pregnancy possibility, contraception, recent delivery or pregnancy loss, and any pain or fever.

## Review and publication checklist

Before any of these drafts can become a patient guide:

1. A clinician checks accuracy against current Kenya Ministry of Health guidance and the intended level of care.
2. The clinician confirms the emergency and same-day referral wording, including the local referral pathway.
3. A Kiswahili translation is prepared and independently checked for clinical meaning, not just literal wording.
4. The main OmpathStudy article is published as patient-facing content and marked reviewed using the main site's content-review process.
5. The clinician's identity and current professional registration are independently verified.
6. The article is listed in the Lite review register and separately approved for a stated audience and language.
7. A test encounter confirms that the correct language-specific guide is selected and that no SMS is sent without explicit consent.
