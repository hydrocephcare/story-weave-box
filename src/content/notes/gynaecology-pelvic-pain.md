# Pelvic Pain in Gynaecology: Acute and Chronic

*Year 4 MBChB | Obstetrics & Gynaecology | CAT revision*

## 1. Definition and clinical approach

**Pelvic pain** is pain perceived in the lower abdomen or pelvis. It may be acute (sudden or short duration), chronic (commonly at least 6 months, depending on definition) or cyclical (related to menstruation/ovulation). It may arise from reproductive, urinary, gastrointestinal, musculoskeletal, vascular or neuropathic structures.

The first task is not to name the cause—it is to identify instability and time-critical disease. In every reproductive-age patient, consider pregnancy and exclude ectopic pregnancy.

## 2. Triage: identify emergencies first

Assess ABCs, vital signs, mental state, perfusion and pain severity. Look for:
- Hypotension, tachycardia, syncope, pallor or shock.
- Peritonism: guarding, rebound or rigid abdomen.
- Positive pregnancy test with pain or bleeding.
- Sudden severe unilateral pain with vomiting (torsion).
- Fever, sepsis or suspected tubo-ovarian abscess.
- Heavy bleeding, suspected ruptured cyst/haemoperitoneum.
- Severe pain with persistent vomiting or inability to tolerate oral intake.

Resuscitate and obtain senior/gynaecology or surgical help early when unstable. Do not delay emergency treatment for non-essential investigations.

## 3. Causes

### Gynaecological
- Ectopic pregnancy, miscarriage and other pregnancy complications.
- PID, endometritis and tubo-ovarian abscess.
- Ovarian torsion, haemorrhagic/ruptured ovarian cyst.
- Endometriosis and adenomyosis.
- Dysmenorrhoea and ovulatory pain (mittelschmerz).
- Fibroids, especially degeneration or prolapse.
- Ovarian, uterine or cervical malignancy.

### Non-gynaecological
- Appendicitis, gastroenteritis, constipation, IBS, inflammatory bowel disease.
- UTI, pyelonephritis, ureteric stones and bladder pain syndrome.
- Hernia, abdominal wall pain, pelvic floor dysfunction, musculoskeletal disease.
- Neuropathic pain and chronic pain syndromes.

## 4. History: use a structured pain history

**Pain analysis**
- Onset: sudden or gradual; first episode or recurrent.
- Site: unilateral/bilateral, suprapubic, iliac fossa, back or perineal.
- Character: sharp, cramping, dull, burning or colicky.
- Radiation: shoulder tip (possible intraperitoneal blood), back, groin or thigh.
- Severity and functional impact.
- Timing: constant/intermittent; relation to menstruation, ovulation, intercourse, urination or defaecation.
- Aggravating/relieving factors and analgesia tried.

**Gynaecological and pregnancy history**
- Last menstrual period, cycle pattern, pregnancy possibility, contraception and pregnancy test history.
- Vaginal bleeding/discharge, dyspareunia, dysmenorrhoea, infertility.
- Previous ectopic pregnancy, miscarriage, PID/STIs, endometriosis, ovarian cysts, fibroids or pelvic surgery.

**Associated systems**
- Fever, vomiting, bowel change, urinary symptoms, haematuria, weight loss and appetite/early satiety.
- Sexual history, safeguarding and intimate partner violence where appropriate.
- Medication, anticoagulants and relevant comorbidities.

## 5. Examination

- **General:** appearance, hydration, pallor, fever, pulse, BP, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation and signs of shock.
- **Abdominal:** inspect scars/distension; palpate all quadrants for tenderness, guarding, rebound, masses and organomegaly; assess percussion and bowel sounds as indicated.
- **Speculum:** with consent, inspect bleeding source, discharge, cervix, lesions and foreign body.
- **Bimanual:** assess cervical motion tenderness, uterine size/position/tenderness, adnexal mass/tenderness and pouch of Douglas fullness.
- **Other systems:** renal angle, hernial orifices, spine, hips and pelvic floor as indicated.

Examination should be proportionate, explained and consented to; offer a chaperone. Avoid repeated or forceful pelvic examination in suspected ectopic pregnancy or acute severe pain.

## 6. Differential diagnosis by pattern

| Pattern | Important diagnoses |
|---|---|
| Sudden unilateral severe pain + vomiting | Ovarian torsion, ruptured cyst, ectopic pregnancy |
| Pain + missed period/bleeding | Ectopic pregnancy, miscarriage |
| Bilateral pain + discharge/fever | PID, tubo-ovarian abscess |
| Progressive cyclical pain + deep dyspareunia | Endometriosis |
| Painful heavy periods + enlarged tender uterus | Adenomyosis, fibroids |
| Mid-cycle unilateral brief pain | Mittelschmerz |
| Right iliac fossa pain with anorexia/vomiting | Appendicitis; also gynaecological causes |
| Colicky flank-to-groin pain | Ureteric stone |
| Chronic pain with bowel/bladder symptoms | Endometriosis, IBS, bladder pain syndrome, pelvic floor disorders |

## 7. Investigations

| Test | Why |
|---|---|
| Urine pregnancy test (or serum β-hCG) | Essential in reproductive-age acute pelvic pain |
| FBC | Anaemia, leukocytosis |
| CRP | Supports assessment of inflammation; non-specific |
| Urinalysis/culture | UTI, haematuria/stone clues |
| NAAT for chlamydia/gonorrhoea | If PID/cervicitis suspected |
| Pelvic ultrasound | First-line imaging for ectopic, adnexal mass, torsion support, fibroids and free fluid |
| Transvaginal ultrasound | Detailed pelvic assessment when appropriate |
| Group and crossmatch | Suspected haemorrhage/ectopic with instability |
| CT abdomen/pelvis | Selected non-gynaecological causes or diagnostic uncertainty; consider pregnancy/radiation |
| MRI | Selected chronic/deep endometriosis or complex pelvic pathology |

A normal ultrasound does not exclude early ectopic pregnancy, intermittent torsion or superficial endometriosis. Interpret imaging alongside clinical condition.

## 8. Management principles

1. **Stabilise first:** ABC, IV access, analgesia, fluids/blood if indicated, monitoring and senior review.
2. **Treat the cause:** ectopic pregnancy, torsion, PID/TOA, appendicitis, urinary disease, endometriosis or cyst pathology require different management.
3. **Analgesia:** paracetamol and/or NSAIDs when appropriate; consider contraindications and pregnancy.
4. **Antibiotics:** only when infection is suspected/diagnosed; PID requires broad-spectrum coverage according to local guideline.
5. **Surgery:** urgent for ruptured ectopic with instability, suspected ovarian torsion, ruptured TOA/peritonitis or other surgical emergencies.
6. **Chronic pain:** explain the possibility of multiple contributing mechanisms; use a biopsychosocial and multidisciplinary approach, treat endometriosis/adenomyosis/IBS/bladder/pelvic floor causes and avoid repeated investigations without a clinical question.

## 9. Important clinical conditions

**Ectopic pregnancy:** pain, amenorrhoea and vaginal bleeding; shoulder-tip pain, syncope or shock suggest rupture. Positive pregnancy test plus pain requires urgent assessment with ultrasound and β-hCG; instability demands resuscitation and emergency surgery.

**Ovarian torsion:** sudden unilateral pain, often nausea/vomiting; urgent surgical assessment. Doppler flow may be present and does not exclude torsion.

**PID/TOA:** lower abdominal pain, discharge, cervical motion/uterine/adnexal tenderness and sometimes fever. Treat empirically when clinical criteria support it; admit if severe, pregnant, TOA or unable to tolerate oral therapy.

**Endometriosis:** cyclical dysmenorrhoea, deep dyspareunia, dyschezia, chronic pelvic pain and infertility. Normal ultrasound does not exclude disease.

**Ruptured/haemorrhagic cyst:** sudden pain; assess for internal bleeding and haemodynamic instability.

## 10. CAT high-yield points

- Always exclude pregnancy in reproductive-age pelvic pain.
- Shock + pain + positive pregnancy test = ruptured ectopic until excluded.
- Sudden unilateral pain with vomiting = torsion until excluded.
- Fever/discharge/cervical motion tenderness = consider PID.
- Cyclical pain, deep dyspareunia and dyschezia = endometriosis.
- Pelvic ultrasound is first-line, but a normal scan does not exclude all emergencies.
- Resuscitation and urgent senior help take priority in unstable patients.
- Consider urinary and gastrointestinal causes; not all pelvic pain is gynaecological.

## 11. Tricky examiner questions

**Q1. What is the first investigation in a reproductive-age woman with acute pelvic pain?**  
**Answer:** A pregnancy test, while simultaneously assessing haemodynamic stability.

**Q2. Does normal Doppler flow rule out ovarian torsion?**  
**Answer:** No. Intermittent torsion and dual blood supply may preserve detectable flow.

**Q3. What does shoulder-tip pain suggest in a patient with pelvic pain and a positive pregnancy test?**  
**Answer:** Diaphragmatic irritation from intraperitoneal blood, raising concern for ruptured ectopic pregnancy.

**Q4. What is the significance of cervical motion tenderness?**  
**Answer:** It supports PID in the appropriate clinical context but is not specific; ectopic pregnancy and other pelvic pathology remain in the differential.

**Q5. Can normal ultrasound exclude endometriosis?**  
**Answer:** No. Superficial disease may not be visible.

## 12. Practice MCQs

### Question 1
A 25-year-old presents with sudden severe unilateral pelvic pain and vomiting. What must be urgently considered?

A. Ovarian torsion  
B. Primary amenorrhoea  
C. Chronic vaginitis  
D. Menopause

**Answer: A.** This is a typical torsion presentation and needs urgent surgical assessment.

### Question 2
A reproductive-age patient has pelvic pain, missed period and vaginal bleeding. Which diagnosis must be excluded urgently?

A. Ectopic pregnancy  
B. Fibrocystic breast disease  
C. Vulval dermatitis  
D. Primary dysmenorrhoea

**Answer: A.** Ectopic pregnancy can cause life-threatening internal haemorrhage.

### Question 3
Which symptom pattern is particularly suggestive of endometriosis?

A. Cyclical dysmenorrhoea and deep dyspareunia  
B. Painless jaundice  
C. Productive cough  
D. Acute painless haematuria only

**Answer: A.** Cyclical pelvic and sexual pain is characteristic.

### Question 4
A patient with pelvic pain is febrile and has cervical motion tenderness and mucopurulent discharge. Most likely diagnosis?

A. PID  
B. Ovarian failure  
C. Fibroid only  
D. Menopause

**Answer: A.** The combination supports ascending genital tract infection.

### Question 5
A patient with a positive pregnancy test, severe pain, hypotension and shoulder-tip pain requires:

A. Routine outpatient review  
B. Immediate resuscitation and emergency ectopic pregnancy management  
C. A menstrual diary  
D. Empirical antifungal treatment

**Answer: B.** These findings suggest ruptured ectopic pregnancy with haemorrhage.
