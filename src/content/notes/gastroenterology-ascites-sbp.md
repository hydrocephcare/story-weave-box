# Ascites and spontaneous bacterial peritonitis

*After reading this note you will be able to examine a patient with ascites at the bedside, work out the cause from the ascitic fluid, recognise and treat spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP), and manage the common complications. Check drug choices and local protocols against the Kenya MoH guideline and your hospital formulary.*

## Definition

- **Ascites**: pathological accumulation of free fluid in the peritoneal cavity. It is the commonest complication of cirrhosis.
- **Grading** (International Ascites Club): **grade 1** is seen only on ultrasound; **grade 2** is moderate, with symmetrical abdominal distension; **grade 3** is large or tense ascites.
- **Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP)**: infection of ascitic fluid with no surgically treatable source inside the abdomen. It is diagnosed when the ascitic **neutrophil (PMN) count is 250 cells/µL or more**, with or without a positive culture.
- **Look-alike variants**: *culture-negative neutrocytic ascites* (PMN 250 or more, culture negative, treated the same as SBP); *bacterascites* (positive culture, PMN below 250, treat if symptomatic, otherwise repeat the tap); *secondary peritonitis* (perforation or abscess, usually several organisms, needs surgery as well).

## Anatomy and physiology you need

- **Portal vein and hepatic sinusoids**: in cirrhosis, scarring raises resistance to portal flow, so portal pressure rises and fluid leaks out of the gut and liver surface into the peritoneum.
- **Splanchnic circulation**: nitric-oxide-driven vasodilation pools blood in the gut, so the *effective* arterial volume falls and the kidneys retain sodium and water through renin, angiotensin, aldosterone and ADH.
- **Albumin**: the failing liver makes less, so plasma oncotic pressure falls and fluid shifts out of vessels. This is why oedema and ascites come together.
- **Lymphatics**: the liver lymph flow rises and eventually overflows, so fluid weeps into the abdomen.
- **Gut wall**: portal hypertension and a leaky gut let bacteria cross into mesenteric lymph nodes (translocation), which is how ascitic fluid gets infected.

## Causes and risk factors

**High SAAG (1.1 g/dL or more): the fluid is leaking because of portal hypertension**
- Cirrhosis, the leading cause: hepatitis B (very common in Kenya), alcohol, hepatitis C, fatty liver. In the tropics also consider **schistosomiasis** (periportal fibrosis, the liver itself can look normal).
- Right heart failure and constrictive pericarditis, including **rheumatic heart disease** and TB pericarditis.
- Budd-Chiari syndrome (hepatic vein block) and portal vein thrombosis.
- Hepatocellular carcinoma or massive liver metastases.

**Low SAAG (below 1.1 g/dL): the peritoneum itself, the pancreas or protein loss**
- **Tuberculous peritonitis**, much more likely with HIV.
- Peritoneal carcinomatosis (ovary, stomach, colon).
- Pancreatic ascites (leak from a duct or pseudocyst).
- Nephrotic syndrome and severe malnutrition (low albumin, no portal hypertension).

**Common triggers of decompensation to ask about**: infection, GI bleeding, alcohol binge, stopping antivirals or diuretics, hepatocellular carcinoma, portal vein thrombosis, NSAIDs, a high-salt diet.

## Pathophysiology

- Cirrhosis raises portal pressure.
- Splanchnic arteries dilate, so effective arterial volume falls.
- Renin, aldosterone, sympathetic activity and ADH rise, so the kidneys keep sodium and water.
- The extra fluid cannot stay in the vessels (high portal pressure, low albumin), so it collects as ascites.
- The gut leaks bacteria into the nodes and the fluid, which causes SBP.
- Late in the course, kidney perfusion falls so far that **hepatorenal syndrome** appears.

## Clinical features

### History: what to ask, and why

- **Onset and speed of swelling**: gradual suggests chronic portal hypertension; rapid with pain or fever points to infection, bleeding into the fluid or a new portal vein clot.
- **Pain, fever, chills**: SBP can be silent in up to a third of patients, so a small change in confusion or kidney function should be enough to tap.
- **Cause**: hepatitis B or C, alcohol, transfusions, schistosomiasis exposure, heart disease, kidney disease, cancer symptoms, weight loss, night sweats, cough (TB).
- **HIV, TB and malaria history**: they change the differential and the drugs.
- **Decompensation clues**: vomiting blood or black stool (varices), drowsiness or day-night reversal (encephalopathy), reduced urine.
- **Drugs and herbal medicine**: NSAIDs, diuretics, traditional remedies, antiretroviral or anti-TB drugs (liver injury).
- **Recent procedures**: a recent paracentesis or endoscopy.

### Examination: how to do it at the bedside

1. **General and liver signs**: jaundice, spider naevi, palmar erythema, gynaecomastia, testicular atrophy, **asterixis**, clubbing, oedema.
2. **Inspection**: a distended abdomen with **bulging flanks**, an **everted umbilicus**, an umbilical hernia, tight shiny skin, dilated veins (*caput medusae*, flow away from the umbilicus), and scars.
3. **Percussion first, then confirm**:
   - **Flank dullness**: tympanic centre, dull flanks. If the flanks are not dull, large ascites is unlikely.
   - **Shifting dullness**: with the patient supine, mark the border of flank dullness, roll them onto one side and wait about a minute, then percuss again. The fluid falls to the dependent (lower) flank, so that flank is now dull and the upper flank, where the gas-filled bowel has floated up, becomes resonant. Dullness that shifts like this is the most useful single test.
   - **Fluid thrill** (needs an assistant to press the edge of a hand on the midline to stop skin transmission): tap one flank and feel for a wave on the other. It is quite specific but only becomes positive with a lot of fluid.
4. **Palpation**: tenderness and guarding (peritonitis), a *ballotable* liver or spleen (portal hypertension), masses, an enlarged hard nodular liver (carcinoma), a Sister Mary Joseph nodule at the umbilicus.
5. **Chest and heart**: a right pleural effusion (hepatic hydrothorax), raised JVP or murmurs (heart cause), and an enlarged left supraclavicular node.
6. **Vital signs and mental state**: fever, tachycardia, low blood pressure and confusion.

**Pitfalls**: massive ovarian cyst (dull centre, resonant flanks), obesity (resonant flanks, no shifting dullness), pregnancy, bowel obstruction and a full bladder can all mimic ascites. Ultrasound settles it.

## Differential diagnosis

| Condition | Clue | How to separate it |
|---|---|---|
| Heart failure or constrictive pericarditis | High JVP, leg oedema, hepatojugular reflux | Echocardiography, BNP; SAAG is high with a high ascitic protein |
| Nephrotic syndrome | Heavy proteinuria, periorbital oedema | Urine protein; low SAAG, no portal hypertension |
| Tuberculous peritonitis | Fever, night sweats, weight loss, HIV | Lymphocytic fluid, high ADA, TB culture or PCR; laparoscopy if unclear |
| Peritoneal carcinomatosis | Weight loss, known cancer, hard nodules | Cytology; CT shows nodules or an omental cake |
| Pancreatic ascites | Past pancreatitis, epigastric pain | Ascitic amylase very high |
| Budd-Chiari syndrome | Painful hepatomegaly, sudden ascites | Doppler ultrasound shows hepatic vein block |
| Schistosomiasis | Freshwater exposure, splenomegaly | Stool or urine ova, serology, periportal fibrosis |
| Massive ovarian cyst | Dull centre, resonant flanks | Pelvic ultrasound |

## Investigations

| Test | What it answers | Meaning of the result |
|---|---|---|
| **Diagnostic paracentesis** (cell count and differential, culture, albumin, total protein) | Is the fluid infected, and why is it there? | PMN 250 or more = SBP. Inoculate **blood culture bottles at the bedside** for the best yield |
| **SAAG** = serum albumin minus ascitic albumin (same day) | Portal hypertension or not | 1.1 g/dL or more: cirrhosis, heart failure, Budd-Chiari. Below 1.1: TB, cancer, pancreatic, nephrotic |
| Ascitic total protein | Infection risk and the cause | Below 1.5 g/dL raises the risk of SBP; above 2.5 g/dL with a high SAAG points to a heart or hepatic-vein cause |
| Ascitic ADA, lymphocyte count, TB culture | TB peritonitis | Lymphocytes and a high ADA support TB |
| Ascitic glucose, LDH, protein and amylase | Secondary peritonitis or pancreatic ascites | Low glucose with high LDH and high protein suggests a bowel perforation, not SBP |
| Ascitic cytology | Malignancy | Positive in carcinomatosis |
| Urea, creatinine, sodium, potassium, LFTs, INR, albumin, FBC | Severity, kidney injury, Child-Pugh and MELD | Guides diuretics, albumin and prognosis |
| Blood cultures, HIV, HBsAg, HCV | Bacteraemia and the cause | Positive HBsAg or HIV changes treatment |
| Ultrasound with Doppler | Fluid, liver shape, spleen, portal vein, masses | Confirms cirrhosis, thrombosis and hepatocellular carcinoma |
| Chest X-ray; echocardiography if heart failure suspected | Hepatic hydrothorax, heart disease | Right effusion with ascites suggests hydrothorax |
| Upper GI endoscopy (once stable) | Varices | Guides prophylaxis against bleeding |

**Where results can mislead**: antibiotics already given can make a culture negative, so count the cells first; a bloody tap (more than 10,000 red cells/µL) needs a correction of one PMN for every 250 red cells; serum and fluid albumin must be taken at the same time or SAAG is wrong; a person with cirrhosis *and* TB can have a high SAAG.

## Diagnosis and severity

- **Confirm ascites** by shifting dullness and ultrasound, then **tap it**. Cause: SAAG and protein. Infection: the PMN count.
- **SBP is diagnosed** when the PMN count is 250 cells/µL or more and imaging shows no surgical source, or when culture is positive with a compatible picture.
- **Treat on the count**, do not wait for culture. A tap that is clear but has a PMN of 250 or more is still SBP.
- **Features of severe disease** that need high-dependency care: low blood pressure, **acute kidney injury** (creatinine rise of 0.3 mg/dL or more), encephalopathy, jaundice with bilirubin above 4 mg/dL, or a bleed.
- **Stage the liver disease** with **Child-Pugh** (bilirubin, albumin, INR, ascites, encephalopathy) and **MELD** (bilirubin, INR, creatinine, sodium). Higher scores mean worse survival and move the patient toward transplant assessment or palliative care.

## Management

### Immediate: SBP

- **Resuscitate**: oxygen if saturation is below 94%, two cannulas, and be cautious with fluids; **albumin is the preferred fluid** because crystalloid in cirrhosis easily overloads the patient.
- **Diagnostic tap on admission** in every patient with ascites, and at once if there is fever, pain, encephalopathy, shock, GI bleeding or kidney injury. Do not delay it; a delayed tap is the commonest mistake.
- **Antibiotics immediately after the tap**: IV third-generation cephalosporin (cefotaxime or ceftriaxone). If the infection began in hospital, or the patient was on a quinolone, follow your local antibiogram (a broader agent such as piperacillin-tazobactam or a carbapenem may be needed). Usual course 5 to 7 days.
- **IV albumin**, 1.5 g/kg on day 1 and 1 g/kg on day 3. It cuts kidney failure and death, and matters most when creatinine is above 1 mg/dL or bilirubin above 4 mg/dL.
- **Stop** diuretics, beta-blockers if the blood pressure is low, and NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors and aminoglycosides.
- **Repeat the tap at 48 hours.** A fall of the PMN count by 25% or more means the antibiotic is working. If not, think of resistance or secondary peritonitis.

### Long term

- **Cause**: stop alcohol, treat hepatitis B or C, treat TB, manage heart failure, vaccinate against hepatitis A and B.
- **Salt**: about 2 g (88 mmol) of sodium a day. Do not restrict water unless sodium is below 125 mmol/L.
- **Diuretics**: spironolactone with furosemide in a **100 : 40** ratio, titrated every 3 to 7 days. Aim to lose about 0.5 kg a day (1 kg a day if there is leg oedema). Check sodium, potassium and creatinine often.
- **Refractory ascites**: repeated large-volume paracentesis **with albumin replacement** (about 6 to 8 g per litre removed when more than 5 L); consider **TIPS** in selected patients, and transplant assessment.
- **Prevent SBP**: after one episode, give long-term prophylaxis (norfloxacin, or ciprofloxacin or co-trimoxazole where norfloxacin is not available). Also consider it when the ascitic protein is low with kidney or liver failure, and give antibiotics to anyone with a variceal bleed.
- **Complications**: *hepatorenal syndrome (HRS-AKI)*: stop diuretics, give albumin, add a vasoconstrictor (terlipressin, or noradrenaline or midodrine with octreotide where terlipressin is unavailable) and involve a specialist; *encephalopathy*: treat the trigger, lactulose, rifaximin for recurrence; *varices*: beta-blocker or banding once the infection settles; *hepatic hydrothorax*: diuretics, and avoid a chest drain.

### Monitoring and safety

- Vital signs every 4 hours, strict fluid balance chart and daily weight.
- Creatinine and urea daily for the first 3 days, then every 48 hours; sodium and potassium daily on diuretics.
- Stop diuretics if sodium falls below 125 mmol/L, if there is kidney injury or encephalopathy, or if potassium is above 5.5 mmol/L.
- Albumin can overload the lungs: avoid it in pulmonary oedema.
- Watch for antibiotic-associated diarrhoea (*C. difficile*).

## Complications and prognosis

- **Hepatorenal syndrome and kidney injury**: a major cause of death in decompensated cirrhosis. Early albumin and a vasoconstrictor improve survival.
- **SBP recurrence**: about **40 to 70% within a year** without prophylaxis, and much lower with it.
- **Hepatic encephalopathy**: infection and bleeding precipitate it.
- **Variceal bleeding**: the risk rises during an SBP episode.
- **Refractory ascites and hepatic hydrothorax**: poor prognosis, a signal for transplant assessment.
- **Mortality**: SBP carries a hospital death rate of roughly 20 to 30% in older series, lower with early antibiotics and albumin. Once ascites appears in cirrhosis, about half of patients die within 2 years unless the liver is transplanted.

## High-yield exam points

- **SAAG** splits ascites into portal hypertension (1.1 g/dL or more) and the rest.
- **SBP = ascitic PMN 250 or more**; treat on the count; add albumin.
- **Antibiotic first line**: third-generation cephalosporin; **secondary prophylaxis** is long term, not 6 months.
- **Do not tap late**: tap every admitted patient with ascites.
- **TB peritonitis**: lymphocytes, high ADA, low SAAG (unless cirrhosis too).
- **Albumin** is given in SBP, with large-volume paracentesis, and in hepatorenal syndrome.
- **Avoid**: NSAIDs, aminoglycosides, ACE inhibitors, and sedatives in cirrhosis.

## Tricky questions examiners ask

**Q1.** A cirrhotic patient has a clear, straw-coloured tap with a PMN count of 300 cells/µL and a negative culture so far. Do you wait for the culture?
**Answer:** No. This is SBP (or culture-negative neutrocytic ascites). Start IV cefotaxime or ceftriaxone and albumin now; the diagnosis rests on the PMN count, and culture is often negative.

**Q2.** Why is albumin given with antibiotics in SBP?
**Answer:** It expands the effective arterial volume and binds inflammatory mediators, which cuts hepatorenal syndrome and death compared with antibiotics alone.

**Q3.** The ascitic albumin is 2.0 g/dL and the serum albumin is 3.0 g/dL. What is the SAAG, and what does it mean?
**Answer:** 1.0 g/dL, which is below 1.1, so the ascites is not due to portal hypertension. Think of TB, cancer, pancreatic disease or nephrotic syndrome.

**Q4.** How do you tell TB peritonitis from SBP on the ascitic fluid?
**Answer:** TB shows lymphocyte predominance and a high ADA, a low SAAG and a high protein; SBP shows a neutrophil rise and a low protein.

**Q5.** Creatinine rises from 1.1 to 2.0 mg/dL on day 3 of SBP treatment, urine output is falling. What now?
**Answer:** Think hepatorenal syndrome. Stop diuretics and nephrotoxins, treat infection, give albumin, add a vasoconstrictor (terlipressin, or the available alternative) and involve a specialist.

**Q6.** A patient has a high PMN count and the fluid glucose is low, LDH is high and protein is above 1 g/dL. What do you suspect?
**Answer:** Secondary peritonitis (perforation or abscess), not SBP. Image urgently, cover anaerobes and involve the surgeons.

**Q7.** Which patient with ascites needs a diagnostic tap on the day of admission?
**Answer:** All of them. Tap at once if there is fever, pain, encephalopathy, shock, kidney injury or a GI bleed.

## Clinical case

**Case**: A 48-year-old man with known alcoholic cirrhosis (Child-Pugh B) comes with fever 38.5 °C, diffuse abdominal pain and increasing abdominal girth over 2 days. On examination: tense ascites, shifting dullness, mild asterixis, BP 100/62 mmHg and pulse 112/min. A bedside tap gives cloudy fluid with a PMN count of 420 cells/µL. Culture is pending.

**Question 1.** What is the immediate management?
*Start IV cefotaxime or ceftriaxone, give albumin 1.5 g/kg, send bloods including creatinine, sodium and cultures, hold diuretics and avoid nephrotoxic drugs.*

**Question 2.** On day 2 the culture grows an *E. coli* that is resistant to cefotaxime and he is not improving. What is the next step?
*Switch to a carbapenem (for example meropenem) according to susceptibility, continue albumin, and repeat the tap to check the PMN fall.*

**Question 3.** What do you offer him before discharge?
*Long-term secondary prophylaxis with norfloxacin or co-trimoxazole, plus alcohol cessation, dietary salt advice, and review for transplant assessment.*

## Practice questions

### Question 1
A 55-year-old woman with hepatitis B cirrhosis presents with fever, abdominal tenderness and a new tense ascites. Paracentesis shows a PMN count of 300 cells/µL. What is the most appropriate next step?

A. Start oral ciprofloxacin only
B. Order CT abdomen before any treatment
C. Begin IV third-generation cephalosporin and give albumin
D. Increase the spironolactone dose

**Answer:** C. Begin IV third-generation cephalosporin and give albumin
**Explanation:** SBP is treated on the PMN count with prompt IV antibiotics, and albumin lowers the risk of kidney failure.

### Question 2
A patient with cirrhosis and SBP is improving on antibiotics, but his serum creatinine rises from 1.1 to 2.0 mg/dL on day 3. Which intervention is indicated?

A. Add terlipressin (or an available vasoconstrictor) and continue albumin
B. Stop albumin infusion
C. Switch to an NSAID for pain control
D. Increase the diuretic dose

**Answer:** A. Add terlipressin (or an available vasoconstrictor) and continue albumin
**Explanation:** A rising creatinine suggests hepatorenal syndrome; diuretics and nephrotoxins are stopped and a vasoconstrictor with albumin is the treatment.

### Question 3
Which ascitic fluid finding most strongly suggests tuberculous peritonitis rather than SBP?

A. Lymphocyte predominance with a high ADA
B. PMN count 250 cells/µL or more
C. Low total protein (below 1 g/dL)
D. A positive Gram stain

**Answer:** A. Lymphocyte predominance with a high ADA
**Explanation:** TB peritonitis gives a lymphocytic exudate with a high adenosine deaminase.

### Question 4
A 60-year-old man with decompensated cirrhosis has tense ascites that returns quickly despite maximal diuretics and salt restriction. What is the best next step for treatment?

A. Stop all salt and water for a week
B. Repeated large-volume paracentesis with albumin replacement
C. Large-volume paracentesis without albumin
D. High-dose oral steroids

**Answer:** B. Repeated large-volume paracentesis with albumin replacement
**Explanation:** For refractory ascites the standard treatment is repeated tapping with albumin; TIPS is a second-line option in selected patients and transplant assessment should follow.

### Question 5
A student finds bulging flanks and shifting dullness. Why does shifting dullness occur in ascites?

A. The raised abdominal pressure pushes the diaphragm upward
B. Hyperdynamic circulation makes the abdominal wall pulsate
C. The enlarged liver compresses the stomach
D. Free fluid settles to the lowest part and air-filled bowel floats on top, so the dull area changes when the patient rolls

**Answer:** D. Free fluid settles to the lowest part and air-filled bowel floats on top, so the dull area changes when the patient rolls
**Explanation:** Fluid is dull on percussion and gas in the floating bowel is resonant; rolling the patient moves the fluid and so moves the dullness.
