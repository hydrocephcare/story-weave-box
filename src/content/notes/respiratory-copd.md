# Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD)

*Orientation notes for study, based on GOLD and NICE. Check the Kenya MoH and your hospital guideline before prescribing.*


![Enlarged view of lung tissue showing the difference between healthy lung and COPD. Public domain](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/5/59/Copd_versus_healthy_lung.jpg)

## Definition

**COPD** is a **common, preventable and treatable disease** characterised by **persistent respiratory symptoms and airflow limitation that is not fully reversible**, caused by abnormalities of the airways (**chronic bronchitis, small airways disease**) and/or alveoli (**emphysema**) from significant exposure to noxious particles or gases.

## Causes and risk factors

- **Cigarette smoking** (commonest cause worldwide).
- **Biomass fuel smoke** (cooking with wood or charcoal in poorly ventilated kitchens): **a leading cause in Kenya, especially in women who never smoked**.
- **Occupational dusts and fumes** (mining, farming, cement, textile).
- **Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency** (young, lower-zone emphysema, family history, liver disease).
- **Post-TB lung damage**, recurrent childhood infections, HIV, poor lung growth.
- Ageing, air pollution.

## Pathophysiology

- **Chronic bronchitis:** goblet cell and mucous gland hypertrophy, airway inflammation (neutrophils, macrophages, CD8 T cells), luminal narrowing.
- **Emphysema:** **destruction of alveolar walls** by protease–antiprotease imbalance, with loss of elastic recoil, **air trapping** and hyperinflation. **Centriacinar** (smoking, upper zones), **panacinar** (alpha-1 antitrypsin, lower zones).
- Result: **airflow obstruction**, **V/Q mismatch** (hypoxaemia), **hypercapnia** in advanced disease, **pulmonary hypertension** and **cor pulmonale**.

## Clinical features

- **Symptoms:** **chronic cough**, **sputum**, **progressive exertional breathlessness** (grade with the **MRC dyspnoea scale**), **wheeze**, recurrent chest infections, weight loss and fatigue in severe disease.
- **Signs:** **barrel chest, hyperinflation, reduced cricosternal distance, pursed-lip breathing, accessory muscle use**, hyper-resonant percussion, **reduced cardiac dullness and liver displaced down**, **prolonged expiration**, wheeze, quiet breath sounds, **cyanosis**, **flapping tremor** and **bounding pulse** (CO2 retention), signs of **cor pulmonale** (raised JVP, ankle oedema, loud P2).
- **"Pink puffer"** (emphysema: breathless, thin, no cyanosis) versus **"blue bloater"** (chronic bronchitis: cyanosed, oedematous): a **descriptive** pair, with most patients having a mixture.

## Differential diagnosis and how to tell them apart  

| Condition | Key clinical clues pointing to it | Distinguishing features from COPD |
|---|---|---|
| Asthma (adult‑onset) | Episodic wheeze, nocturnal cough, triggers (allergens, exercise), rapid symptom onset, history of atopy | Reversible airflow obstruction (≥12 % and 200 mL increase in FEV₁ after bronchodilator), normal TLC at baseline, often normal DLCO; symptoms improve with inhaled corticosteroids |
| Bronchiectasis | Chronic productive cough with copious purulent sputum, recurrent haemoptysis, history of severe pneumonia or TB, clubbing | High‑resolution CT shows dilated airways; sputum cultures frequently grow Pseudomonas or H. influenzae; spirometry may show obstructive pattern but FEV₁/FVC often >0.7 after bronchodilator |
| Congestive heart failure (CHF) | Orthopnoea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea, peripheral oedema, elevated JVP, history of ischemic heart disease | Chest X‑ray shows cardiomegaly, pulmonary venous congestion, Kerley B lines; BNP markedly raised; spirometry may be normal or show restrictive pattern; response to diuretics |
| Interstitial lung disease (ILD) | Progressive dry cough, fine “velcro” crackles, digital clubbing, restrictive pattern on PFTs | Reduced total lung capacity and DLCO, HRCT shows reticulation or honey‑comb; FEV₁/FVC usually normal or increased; oxygen desaturation early on exertion |
| Pulmonary embolism (acute) | Sudden pleuritic chest pain, tachypnoea, tachycardia, risk factors (immobility, surgery, cancer) | CT pulmonary angiography positive; V/Q mismatch on perfusion scan; D‑dimer elevated; spirometry typically normal; ABG shows acute respiratory alkalosis with possible hypoxaemia |
| Tuberculosis (active) | Low‑grade fever, night sweats, weight loss, chronic cough with occasional haemoptysis, exposure history | Sputum acid‑fast bacilli positive, chest X‑ray shows upper‑lobe infiltrates or cavitation; response to anti‑TB therapy; may coexist with COPD but sputum smear distinguishes |
| Alpha‑1 antitrypsin deficiency‑related emphysema | Early‑onset (<45 y) emphysema, lower‑lobe predominant, family history, liver dysfunction | Serum A1AT level markedly reduced; genetic testing confirms Z allele; panacinar pattern on CT; unlike smoking‑related COPD, patients often non‑smokers or light smokers.

## Investigations

- **Spirometry (diagnostic): post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC below 0.7** with symptoms and exposure.
- **GOLD grade** (by FEV1 % predicted): **1 mild (80% or more), 2 moderate (50–79%), 3 severe (30–49%), 4 very severe (below 30%)**.
- **Chest X-ray:** hyperinflation, flattened diaphragms, **bullae**, enlarged hila; excludes lung cancer, pneumothorax, heart failure.
- **Full lung function:** raised **TLC and RV**, **reduced DLCO** in emphysema.
- **ABG/oximetry**, **FBC** (polycythaemia), **ECG/echo** (cor pulmonale), **alpha-1 antitrypsin level** in young or non-smoker patients, **sputum culture**, **HIV test and TB screen**.

## Management of stable COPD

1. **Stop smoking** (counselling and pharmacotherapy: the only measure that reduces decline); reduce **biomass exposure**.
2. **Vaccinations:** influenza yearly, pneumococcal, COVID-19.
3. **Pulmonary rehabilitation** and exercise; nutrition.
4. **Inhaled therapy (GOLD):**
   - **Group A (few symptoms):** a bronchodilator (short- or long-acting).
   - **Group B (more symptoms):** **LABA plus LAMA**.
   - **Group E (exacerbations):** **LABA plus LAMA**; add **ICS** if **blood eosinophils 300 or more/µL** (or 100 or more with frequent exacerbations).
   - **SABA or SAMA** as needed for relief.
   - Check **inhaler technique** and adherence.
5. **Other drugs** in selected patients: **roflumilast**, **azithromycin** (if frequent exacerbations in former smokers), mucolytics.
6. **Long-term oxygen therapy (LTOT):** for **PaO2 below 7.3 kPa (55 mmHg)**, **or 7.3–8 kPa with cor pulmonale, polycythaemia or pulmonary hypertension**, used **at least 15 hours a day**; patient must **not smoke**.
7. **Surgery:** bullectomy, lung volume reduction, **lung transplant** in selected cases.
8. **Palliative care** for end-stage disease.

## Acute exacerbation of COPD

- **Definition:** acute worsening of breathlessness, cough or sputum (volume or purulence), commonly **infective** (viral or bacterial).
- **Management:**
  - **Controlled oxygen: target SpO2 88–92%**, using a Venturi mask (24–28%). Too much oxygen worsens hypercapnia.
  - **Nebulised bronchodilators** (salbutamol and ipratropium), **driven by air** (not oxygen) if CO2 retention.
  - **Oral prednisolone 30–40 mg daily for 5 days.**
  - **Antibiotics if sputum is purulent** or pneumonia (amoxicillin, doxycycline or co-amoxiclav).
  - **Non-invasive ventilation (NIV)** if **respiratory acidosis persists (pH below 7.35 with PaCO2 above 6.5 kPa)** despite treatment; **intubation** if NIV fails.
  - **Check ABG** early and repeat; treat heart failure, PE, pneumothorax if present.
  - Decide **escalation and ceiling of care** with the patient.

## Complications

- **Respiratory failure** (type 2), **cor pulmonale**, **polycythaemia**, **pneumothorax** (bullae), recurrent infections, **lung cancer**, osteoporosis, depression, **malnutrition and cachexia**.

## Prognosis

- Worsened by **continued smoking**, low **FEV1**, frequent exacerbations, low BMI, hypoxaemia, cor pulmonale. **BODE index** (BMI, obstruction, dyspnoea, exercise capacity) predicts survival.

## Exam points

- **FEV1/FVC below 0.7 post-bronchodilator** diagnoses COPD.
- **Smoking cessation** and **LTOT (more than 15 hours a day)** are the only interventions that improve survival.
- **Oxygen target 88–92%** in an exacerbation.
- **Kenya:** **biomass smoke** and **post-TB lung disease** are important causes.

## Clinical case  

**Patient:** 58‑year‑old male, former tobacco smoker (30 pack‑years, quit 2 years ago), works as a charcoal‑burner.  
**Presentation:** 3‑month history of progressive dyspnoea on exertion (MRC grade 3), productive cough with white sputum in the mornings, occasional wheeze. No chest pain. He reports two recent episodes of worsening breathlessness lasting 3–4 days, treated with increased salbutamol use.  
**Key findings:**  
- Barrel‑shaped chest, use of accessory muscles, pursed‑lip breathing.  
- SpO₂ 90 % on room air, respiratory rate 22 /min, heart rate 96 /min.  
- Spirometry: post‑bronchodilator FEV₁/FVC 0.62, FEV₁ 45 % predicted.  
- Chest X‑ray: hyperinflated lungs, flattened diaphragms, increased retro‑sternal air‑space.  

### Questions  

1. **What is the most likely GOLD group for this patient and why?**  
   **Answer:** Group B – the patient is symptomatic (MRC ≥ 2) with an FEV₁ < 50 % predicted, but has had ≤ 1 exacerbation requiring oral steroids or antibiotics in the previous year. This places him in the “more symptoms, low risk” category, guiding the use of a LABA + LAMA combination.  

2. **Explain the pathophysiological mechanism that leads to the observed “pursed‑lip” breathing.**  
   **Answer:** Airway obstruction creates a prolonged expiratory phase with dynamic airway collapse. By exhaling through pursed lips, the patient generates a back‑pressure that splints the airways, maintaining patency, reducing air‑trapping, and improving alveolar ventilation.  

3. **During an acute exacerbation, why must oxygen be titrated to an SpO₂ of 88–92 % rather than given at high flow?**  
   **Answer:** In COPD with chronic CO₂ retention, high inspired oxygen can blunt hypoxic ventilatory drive and worsen V/Q mismatch, leading to a rise in PaCO₂ and respiratory acidosis. Targeting 88–92 % avoids suppressing the drive while still correcting hypoxaemia.

## Tricky questions examiners ask  

**Q1.** A patient with known COPD lies supine, SpO₂ 94 % on room air, RR 28 /min, pH 7.30, PaCO₂ 8 kPa. What is the next immediate step?  
**Answer:** Initiate non‑invasive ventilation (NIV). The combination of respiratory acidosis (pH < 7.35) with hypercapnia despite a relatively normal SpO₂ indicates impending type 2 respiratory failure; NIV corrects ventilation without the risks of intubation.  

**Q2.** Two patients present with chronic cough and sputum. One has a 20‑year smoking history, the other has never smoked but cooks with biomass. Which feature most reliably distinguishes COPD from asthma in these cases?  
**Answer:** Fixed airflow limitation on post‑bronchodilator spirometry (FEV₁/FVC < 0.7). Asthma typically shows reversible obstruction, whereas COPD obstruction is not fully reversible regardless of exposure history.  

**Q3.** A COPD patient is started on a long‑acting muscarinic antagonist (LAMA) but later develops urinary retention. Why might the examiner avoid switching to a long‑acting β₂‑agonist (LABA) alone?  
**Answer:** Monotherapy with a LABA does not address the cholinergic tone that contributes to airway narrowing in COPD and offers less protection against exacerbations; the preferred strategy is to add an inhaled corticosteroid if eosinophils are high, not to replace the LAMA.  

**Q4.** During an exacerbation, a clinician chooses to give high‑flow oxygen (FiO₂ 0.6) to a patient with known CO₂ retention. Why is this choice generally discouraged?  
**Answer:** High FiO₂ can abolish hypoxic drive, increase dead‑space ventilation, and precipitate CO₂ narcosis, worsening hypercapnia and potentially leading to respiratory arrest.  

**Q5.** A 65‑year‑old woman with COPD and a BMI of 18 kg/m² is evaluated for LTOT. Her PaO₂ is 7.0 kPa, but she is a current smoker. What factor precludes immediate LTOT prescription?  
**Answer:** Ongoing smoking is an absolute contraindication because supplemental oxygen can act as a fire hazard and because smoking negates the survival benefit of LTOT.  

**Q6.** In a patient with severe emphysema, why might lung volume reduction surgery be preferred over bullectomy?  
**Answer:** Lung volume reduction targets heterogeneous emphysema by resecting the most diseased portions, improving diaphragmatic mechanics and elastic recoil, whereas bullectomy only removes large bullae and may not provide functional benefit if the surrounding parenchyma remains hyperinflated.

## Pharmacology at a glance

Tap a drug name for its full card (how it works, adverse effects, cautions, dose), then use **Back to this note** to return to this spot. Condition guide: [COPD drugs](/pharmacology?tab=conditions&c=copd).

- **Salbutamol**: Short-acting β₂-agonist — Relief of breathlessness; nebulised in exacerbations — Tremor, palpitations, low potassium
- **Ipratropium**: Short-acting antimuscarinic — Nebulised with salbutamol in exacerbations — Dry mouth, urinary retention
- **Tiotropium**: Long-acting antimuscarinic (LAMA) — Maintenance bronchodilator — Dry mouth, urinary retention in older men
- **Budesonide–formoterol**: Inhaled steroid plus long-acting β₂-agonist — Add the steroid if frequent exacerbations and eosinophils 300/µL or more — Pneumonia risk with inhaled steroids, thrush
- **Prednisolone**: Systemic corticosteroid — Exacerbation: 30–40 mg for 5 days — High glucose, mood change
- **Doxycycline**: Tetracycline — Exacerbation with purulent sputum — Photosensitivity, oesophagitis (take upright with water)
- **Amoxicillin**: Penicillin — Alternative for a purulent exacerbation — Rash, diarrhoea, allergy
- **Co-amoxiclav**: Penicillin plus β-lactamase inhibitor — Resistant organisms or pneumonia — Diarrhoea, liver injury
- **Azithromycin**: Macrolide — Prevents exacerbations in former smokers with frequent attacks — QT prolongation, hearing loss
- **Roflumilast**: Phosphodiesterase-4 inhibitor — Severe COPD with chronic bronchitis and exacerbations — Weight loss, diarrhoea, low mood

**Oxygen is a drug too:** aim for 88–92% in an exacerbation; too much oxygen can worsen carbon dioxide retention.

## Practice questions

**1.** A 62-year-old smoker has breathlessness. Post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC 0.55 and FEV1 40% predicted. The GOLD grade is:
**A. 3** · B. 1 · C. 2 · D. 4 · E. Cannot be graded
*Answer: A (30–49%).*

**2.** A patient with COPD exacerbation has pH 7.28 and PaCO2 8 kPa on controlled oxygen with nebulisers and steroids. Next step:
A. Increase oxygen · B. **Non-invasive ventilation** · C. Discharge · D. Stop steroids · E. Sedation
*Answer: B.*

**3.** The oxygen saturation target in a COPD exacerbation is:
A. 100% · B. 94–98% · C. 80–85% · **D. 88–92%** · E. Above 98%
*Answer: D.*
