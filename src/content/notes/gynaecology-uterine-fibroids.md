# Uterine Fibroids (Leiomyomas)

*Year 4 MBChB | Obstetrics & Gynaecology | CAT revision*

## 1. Definition

Uterine fibroids (leiomyomas or myomas) are benign monoclonal tumours arising from the smooth muscle cells of the myometrium, with variable amounts of extracellular matrix. They are common during reproductive years, may be single or multiple, and vary greatly in size. Most are asymptomatic. Leiomyosarcoma is a rare malignant smooth-muscle tumour and is not simply the usual progression of a benign fibroid.

## 2. Aetiology and risk factors

The exact initiating cause is multifactorial. Ovarian steroid hormones, genetic changes and local growth factors influence growth.

**Risk factors/associations**
- Increasing reproductive age, especially the 30s and 40s.
- Family history and genetic susceptibility.
- African ancestry is associated with earlier onset, greater prevalence and more severe disease in many populations; this is an epidemiological association, not a diagnostic criterion.
- Early menarche, nulliparity and obesity are associated with increased risk.
- Fibroids commonly shrink after menopause when ovarian steroid levels fall.

Pregnancy may alter fibroid size and symptoms. Fibroids are not caused by infection.

## 3. Classification by anatomical location

Location is often more clinically important than diameter alone.

- **Submucosal:** beneath the endometrium, protruding into the uterine cavity. Strong association with heavy bleeding and impaired implantation.
- **Intramural:** within the myometrium. May enlarge the uterus and cause bleeding or pressure symptoms.
- **Subserosal:** project towards the outer uterine surface; more likely to cause pressure/bulk symptoms.
- **Pedunculated:** attached by a stalk, either submucosal or subserosal; may rarely undergo torsion.
- **Cervical/intraligamentary:** less common; may distort the cervix, urinary tract or pelvic anatomy.

### FIGO leiomyoma types (exam framework)

- Type 0: pedunculated intracavitary.
- Type 1: submucosal, less than 50% intramural.
- Type 2: submucosal, at least 50% intramural.
- Type 3: entirely intramural but contacts the endometrium.
- Type 4: entirely intramural.
- Type 5: subserosal, at least 50% intramural.
- Type 6: subserosal, less than 50% intramural.
- Type 7: pedunculated subserosal.
- Type 8: other sites, including cervical or parasitic fibroids.

Hybrid lesions may contact both endometrium and serosa and are described using two numbers.

## 4. Pathophysiology

A genetically altered myometrial cell proliferates and produces excess extracellular matrix. Oestrogen and progesterone signalling support growth. Fibroids may distort the endometrial cavity, alter uterine contractility and vascular architecture, and increase the endometrial surface area. These mechanisms contribute to heavy bleeding. Large lesions compress the bladder, rectum, ureters or pelvic veins. Cavity distortion and altered endometrial receptivity may affect fertility and pregnancy.

## 5. Clinical features

Many patients have no symptoms and fibroids are found incidentally.

**Bleeding**
- Heavy or prolonged menstrual bleeding.
- Iron-deficiency anaemia, fatigue, dizziness and reduced exercise tolerance.
- Less commonly, irregular bleeding; do not automatically attribute all bleeding to a fibroid.

**Pressure/bulk symptoms**
- Pelvic heaviness or abdominal enlargement.
- Urinary frequency, urgency or retention due to bladder compression.
- Constipation or difficulty defaecating due to rectal compression.
- Backache or pelvic discomfort.

**Pain**
- Dysmenorrhoea or chronic pelvic discomfort.
- Acute pain with red (carneous) degeneration, torsion of a pedunculated fibroid or prolapse of a submucosal fibroid.

**Reproductive and pregnancy effects**
- Subfertility, especially with cavity-distorting submucosal fibroids.
- Recurrent pregnancy loss may be associated with selected cavity-distorting lesions.
- Increased risk of malpresentation, preterm birth, placental problems, caesarean delivery and postpartum haemorrhage in selected patients; risk depends on number, size and location.

## 6. History and examination

Ask about menstrual pattern and flooding, clots, pain, pressure symptoms, urinary/bowel symptoms, anaemia symptoms, fertility goals, pregnancy history, previous treatment and rapid symptom progression. Ask about red flags such as postmenopausal growth/bleeding, unexplained weight loss or persistent systemic symptoms.

**Examination**
- Assess pallor, pulse, blood pressure and general condition.
- Abdominal examination may reveal a firm, irregular, midline pelvic mass continuous with the uterus; assess size, tenderness and mobility.
- Bimanual examination may reveal an enlarged, irregular, firm uterus. Distinguish it from an adnexal mass.
- Speculum examination is useful when bleeding source, cervical pathology or prolapsed fibroid is suspected.

## 7. Differential diagnosis

- Adenomyosis: often uniformly enlarged, tender/boggy uterus with dysmenorrhoea.
- Pregnancy: perform β-hCG; an enlarged uterus may be mistaken for fibroids.
- Ovarian tumour/cyst: adnexal rather than uterine mass.
- Endometrial polyp or hyperplasia: causes bleeding but not usually a large irregular uterine mass.
- Uterine malignancy: consider with postmenopausal bleeding, atypical imaging or concerning clinical course.

## 8. Investigations

| Test | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Pregnancy test | Excludes pregnancy in reproductive-age bleeding/pain |
| FBC and ferritin | Detects and characterises anaemia/iron deficiency |
| Pelvic ultrasound | First-line imaging; maps number, size, site and cavity distortion |
| Transvaginal ultrasound | Better detail for uterus/endometrium when appropriate |
| MRI pelvis | Selected complex cases, uncertain diagnosis or procedural planning |
| Hysteroscopy | Direct assessment of cavity and selected submucosal lesions |
| Endometrial sampling | If AUB age/risk/persistence indicates endometrial assessment |

Ultrasound is usually the first-line investigation. Imaging should describe dimensions, location, relationship to the endometrium and serosa, and any distortion of the cavity. A fibroid seen on ultrasound may be incidental; correlate it with the symptoms.

## 9. Management

Treatment is individualised according to symptoms, fibroid location/size, age, anaemia, fertility wishes, comorbidities and patient preference. **An asymptomatic fibroid does not automatically require treatment or hysterectomy.**

### A. Expectant management
Appropriate for asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic patients without concerning features. Explain symptoms that should prompt review and arrange follow-up based on clinical context.

### B. Medical management
Medical treatment controls bleeding or symptoms; it does not reliably remove the fibroid.
- Tranexamic acid during menstruation can reduce menstrual blood loss.
- NSAIDs may help dysmenorrhoea and reduce bleeding in some patients.
- LNG-IUS can reduce HMB when the uterine cavity is not significantly distorted and insertion is appropriate.
- Combined hormonal contraception or progestogens can regulate bleeding in selected patients.
- GnRH agonists/antagonists may temporarily suppress ovarian steroid stimulation and reduce fibroid volume/bleeding; consider adverse effects, bone loss and add-back therapy where indicated. Availability and protocols vary.
- Correct iron deficiency with iron replacement and dietary advice.

### C. Procedures and surgery
- **Hysteroscopic myomectomy:** selected submucosal fibroids (especially FIGO 0–2); preserves uterus.
- **Myomectomy:** removes fibroids while retaining uterus; often considered when fertility or uterine preservation is desired. Counsel about recurrence, adhesions, bleeding and future pregnancy implications.
- **Uterine artery embolisation (UAE):** reduces fibroid perfusion and volume; discuss post-embolisation pain, infection, ovarian reserve and uncertainty regarding future pregnancy compared with myomectomy.
- **Hysterectomy:** definitive treatment for patients who do not desire future pregnancy and choose definitive management after counselling.
- Other minimally invasive ablative approaches may be available in specialist centres.

### D. Acute complications
Severe haemorrhage: assess and resuscitate, correct anaemia and control bleeding. Acute severe pain requires assessment for degeneration, torsion, pregnancy-related pathology and other acute abdomen causes.

## 10. Complications

Iron-deficiency anaemia, pressure effects (urinary retention/hydronephrosis rarely), pain/degeneration, subfertility, miscarriage association in selected lesions, pregnancy and labour complications, postpartum haemorrhage and recurrence after uterus-preserving treatment.

## 11. CAT high-yield points

- Fibroids are benign tumours of **myometrium**.
- Submucosal fibroids are most strongly linked to heavy menstrual bleeding and cavity-related fertility problems.
- Intramural = within myometrium; subserosal = towards serosa.
- Ultrasound is first-line; MRI is useful for complex mapping/planning.
- Management depends on symptoms and reproductive goals—not size alone.
- Myomectomy preserves the uterus; hysterectomy is definitive and ends fertility.
- A rapidly enlarging mass is not proof of sarcoma, but postmenopausal growth or bleeding requires prompt assessment.
- Fibroids may be incidental; investigate other causes when symptoms do not fit.

## 12. Tricky examiner questions

**Q1. Which fibroid is most likely to cause heavy menstrual bleeding?**  
**Answer:** Submucosal, because it distorts the endometrial cavity.

**Q2. Which investigation is first-line?**  
**Answer:** Pelvic ultrasound, usually transvaginal when suitable.

**Q3. Does every fibroid require surgery?**  
**Answer:** No. Asymptomatic lesions may be observed; intervention is guided by symptoms, anatomy and patient goals.

**Q4. Which operation is preferred when a patient wants future pregnancy and has a suitable fibroid?**  
**Answer:** Myomectomy may be considered, with counselling about recurrence and obstetric implications.

**Q5. What is the definitive treatment for symptomatic fibroids when fertility is no longer desired?**  
**Answer:** Hysterectomy, if chosen after informed discussion of alternatives and risks.

## 13. Practice MCQs

### Question 1
A 34-year-old presents with heavy menstrual bleeding. Ultrasound shows a fibroid projecting into the uterine cavity. Which type is most likely?

A. Subserosal  
B. Submucosal  
C. Cervical  
D. Pedunculated subserosal

**Answer: B.** Submucosal fibroids distort the cavity and are strongly associated with HMB.

### Question 2
A woman with fibroids wishes to conceive. Which procedure removes fibroids while preserving the uterus?

A. Hysterectomy  
B. Myomectomy  
C. Endometrial ablation  
D. Bilateral oophorectomy

**Answer: B.** Myomectomy removes fibroids while retaining the uterus.

### Question 3
What is the usual first-line imaging investigation for suspected uterine fibroids?

A. CT brain  
B. Pelvic ultrasound  
C. Plain abdominal radiograph  
D. PET scan

**Answer: B.** Ultrasound assesses number, size, location and cavity distortion.

### Question 4
A patient has a large subserosal fibroid and urinary frequency. The most likely mechanism is:

A. Bladder compression  
B. Increased prolactin  
C. Cervical infection  
D. Ovarian failure

**Answer: A.** A large pelvic mass may compress the bladder and cause frequency.

### Question 5
Which statement is correct?

A. All fibroids are malignant  
B. All fibroids require hysterectomy  
C. Fibroid management should consider symptoms and fertility wishes  
D. Fibroids arise from the endometrium

**Answer: C.** Individualised management avoids unnecessary intervention and preserves fertility when desired.
