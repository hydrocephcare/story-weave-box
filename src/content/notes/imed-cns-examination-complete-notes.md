# 1. Preparation and general inspection

## A. Preparation

Before beginning:

1. Introduce yourself and confirm the patient's identity.
2. Explain the examination and obtain consent.
3. Wash your hands.
4. Ask about pain and position the patient comfortably.
5. Expose the relevant areas while maintaining dignity.
6. Ensure adequate lighting and have a reflex hammer, cotton wool, tuning fork and other necessary equipment available.

If the patient is unconscious or acutely deteriorating, assess ABCDE and call for urgent help before attempting a complete neurological examination.

## B. General inspection

Stand at the end of the bed and observe the patient before touching them.

| What to inspect | What it may reveal |
| --- | --- |
| Consciousness and behaviour | Encephalopathy, delirium, coma |
| Facial symmetry | Facial nerve palsy, stroke |
| Speech | Aphasia, dysarthria, reduced consciousness |
| Posture and limb position | Hemiplegia, abnormal posturing |
| Muscle bulk | Chronic denervation, disuse, neuromuscular disease |
| Involuntary movements | Tremor, chorea, myoclonus, fasciculations |
| Walking aids | Functional impairment or gait disorder |
| Scars or trauma | Previous surgery, head injury |
| Skin | Neurocutaneous disorders, rash suggesting infection |
| Hands and nails | Tremor, wasting, clubbing or signs of systemic disease |
| Face and eyes | Ptosis, unequal pupils, abnormal gaze |
| Speech and interaction | Confusion, language disorder, cognitive impairment |

### What do the common abnormal movements mean?

- **Tremor:** rhythmic oscillation of a body part.
- **Fasciculation:** visible spontaneous contraction of part of a muscle, potentially associated with lower motor neuron disease.
- **Chorea:** brief, irregular, unpredictable involuntary movements.
- **Myoclonus:** sudden, brief, shock-like muscle jerks.
- **Dystonia:** sustained muscle contractions producing abnormal postures.
- **Clonus:** repetitive rhythmic contractions triggered by sudden muscle stretch, often associated with an upper motor neuron lesion.

Do not diagnose a neurological disease from inspection alone. Use the findings to guide the detailed examination.

# 2. Level of consciousness

Consciousness has two important components:

- **Arousal:** whether the patient can be awakened.
- **Awareness:** whether the patient is conscious of themselves and their surroundings.

A patient can be awake but confused, or have severely reduced arousal and be difficult to awaken.

## A. Assessing the level of consciousness

| State | Description |
| --- | --- |
| Alert | Awake and responds appropriately |
| Drowsy | Sleepy but wakes to voice |
| Obtunded | Requires repeated stimulation and responds slowly |
| Stuporous | Responds minimally, usually to vigorous stimulation |
| Comatose | Cannot be awakened and has no purposeful response to stimulation |

These terms are descriptive; their exact definitions can vary between clinical settings.

## B. Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)

The GCS quantifies three types of response: eye opening, verbal response and motor response.

| Component | Response | Score |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Eye opening | Spontaneous | 4 |
| | To sound | 3 |
| | To pressure | 2 |
| | None | 1 |
| Verbal response | Oriented | 5 |
| | Confused | 4 |
| | Inappropriate words | 3 |
| | Incomprehensible sounds | 2 |
| | None | 1 |
| Motor response | Obeys commands | 6 |
| | Localizes pressure | 5 |
| | Normal flexion/withdrawal from pressure | 4 |
| | Abnormal flexion | 3 |
| | Extension | 2 |
| | None | 1 |

Maximum = 15; minimum = 3.

Example: E3 + V4 + M6 = GCS 13/15.

Always record the components individually. If a component cannot be assessed because of intubation, sedation or another limitation, document that limitation rather than assigning an ordinary score.

A GCS of 8 or less indicates severe impairment and should prompt urgent airway and neurological assessment. Airway management depends on the whole clinical picture, not the score alone.

## C. What else do you examine in an unconscious patient?

After ABCDE stabilization:

- Check capillary blood glucose immediately.
- Assess pupil size and reaction.
- Assess eye position and movements.
- Compare limb movement and motor responses.
- Look for trauma, fever, meningism, medication exposure or signs of poisoning.
- Consider seizures, stroke, intracranial haemorrhage and metabolic causes.

**Clinical pearl:** hypoglycaemia is a reversible cause of unconsciousness. Do not overlook it while investigating neurological causes.

# 3. Higher mental functions

Higher mental functions assess the cerebral cortex and connected networks responsible for attention, memory, language, recognition, planning and purposeful activity.

Examine these in an alert patient who can hear, understand and cooperate. Interpret results in light of language, education, vision, hearing and cultural background.

## A. Orientation

Ask:

- What is your name? Orientation to person.
- Where are we? Orientation to place.
- What day, month or year is it? Orientation to time.
- Why are you in hospital? Understanding of the current situation.

Disorientation can occur in delirium, dementia, metabolic encephalopathy and other disorders.

## B. Attention and concentration

### Serial 7s

Ask the patient to subtract 7 repeatedly from 100.

100 → 93 → 86 → 79 → 72 → 65.

Alternatively, ask the patient to recite the months backwards.

**Interpretation:** poor performance may reflect impaired attention, but can also be affected by education, anxiety, language or baseline numeracy.

## C. Memory

Memory is examined in three broad stages.

| Type | How to test | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Immediate registration | Give three words and ask the patient to repeat them immediately | "Apple, chair, coin" |
| Recent memory | Ask for the same words after a short delay | Recall after 3–5 minutes |
| Remote memory | Ask about established past events | Historical or personal information |

For delayed recall, use an intervening task rather than repeatedly rehearsing the words.

Poor recall does not automatically establish a primary memory disorder: inattention, poor hearing, language difficulties and reduced consciousness can also affect results.

## D. Calculation

Ask simple arithmetic questions appropriate to the patient's educational background.

Examples:

- What is 12 + 8?
- What is 20 − 3?
- If an item costs KSh 30 and you pay KSh 50, how much change should you receive?

Impaired calculation is termed **acalculia**, particularly when a previously learned ability is lost because of neurological dysfunction.

## E. Visuospatial perception

This assesses the ability to perceive spatial relationships and organize objects in space.

### How to test

Ask the patient to:

- Copy a geometric figure.
- Draw a clock and place the hands at a specified time.
- Arrange or reproduce a simple pattern.
- Describe the relative position of objects, where appropriate.

**Abnormal findings:** poor spatial organization, distorted copying or difficulty placing clock numbers correctly.

These may reflect parietal or other cortical network dysfunction, but vision, motor function, attention and education must be considered.

## F. Apraxia

Apraxia is an inability to perform a learned purposeful action despite adequate basic strength, comprehension and coordination.

### How to test

Ask the patient to:

- Demonstrate how they would brush their teeth.
- Pretend to use a comb.
- Show how they would salute.
- Imitate a meaningful gesture.

If the patient understands the instruction but cannot correctly perform a familiar learned action, consider apraxia after excluding weakness, sensory loss, poor comprehension and severe cognitive impairment.

It can occur with lesions involving dominant parietal and connected cortical networks.

## G. Agnosia

Agnosia is failure to recognize a familiar object or stimulus despite adequate basic sensation.

| Type | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Visual agnosia | Cannot recognize an object by sight despite adequate vision | Cannot identify a familiar object visually |
| Astereognosis | Cannot identify an object by touch despite adequate primary sensation | Cannot identify a key placed in the hand with eyes closed |
| Prosopagnosia | Difficulty recognizing familiar faces | Does not recognize familiar people by their faces |
| Auditory agnosia | Difficulty recognizing sounds despite adequate hearing | Cannot identify a familiar sound |

Agnosia must be distinguished from blindness, deafness, sensory loss, aphasia and inattention.

### Prosopagnosia

This is sometimes called face blindness. The patient may see a face clearly but fail to identify the person from facial appearance.

Recognition may be preserved using voice, clothing or other clues.

It is associated with dysfunction of face-processing networks, often involving the occipitotemporal region.

## H. Hemispatial neglect

Hemispatial neglect is failure to attend to one side of space, usually following a lesion of the right cerebral hemisphere, often involving the parietal attention network.

### How to test

- Ask the patient to copy a drawing.
- Ask them to mark the middle of a horizontal line.
- Present targets on the right and left sides.
- Observe whether they ignore food, objects or their own limbs on one side.

Example: a patient with right hemispheric stroke repeatedly ignores objects on the left side.

### Neglect versus visual field loss

- **Neglect:** the patient fails to attend to one side of space.
- **Hemianopia:** loss of one half of the visual field.

They can coexist. Visual field testing helps distinguish them, but formal assessment may be necessary.

## I. Frontal lobe functions

Assess planning, judgment, inhibition, verbal fluency and executive function.

Bedside tests include:

- Asking the patient to name as many animals as possible in one minute.
- Asking them to interpret a proverb.
- Observing impulsivity, inappropriate behaviour or difficulty following a sequence.
- Testing alternating hand movements or other structured tasks where appropriate.

Frontal dysfunction may cause disinhibition, apathy, impaired planning or reduced verbal fluency.

# 4. Speech and language

First establish whether the patient has a language disorder, a motor speech disorder or a voice disorder.

## A. Language examination

Test four principal components:

1. **Fluency:** ask the patient to speak spontaneously.
2. **Comprehension:** give commands of increasing complexity.
3. **Naming:** ask the patient to name a pen, watch or other familiar object.
4. **Repetition:** ask the patient to repeat a sentence.

Reading and writing can also be assessed when indicated.

## B. Types of aphasia

| Type | Fluency | Comprehension | Repetition |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Broca aphasia | Reduced, effortful | Relatively preserved | Impaired |
| Wernicke aphasia | Fluent, often nonsensical | Impaired | Impaired |
| Conduction aphasia | Fluent | Relatively preserved | Disproportionately impaired |
| Global aphasia | Severely impaired | Severely impaired | Severely impaired |

These are classical patterns; actual patients may have mixed features.

## C. Dysarthria

Dysarthria is impaired articulation due to dysfunction of the motor systems controlling speech.

The patient may know exactly what they want to say but produce slurred, slow, strained or poorly articulated speech.

Causes include stroke, Parkinsonism, cerebellar disease and neuromuscular disorders.

**Viva distinction:**

- Aphasia = impaired language processing.
- Dysarthria = impaired motor production of speech.
- Dysphonia = abnormal voice quality.
- Dysphasia is often used to mean language impairment; usage varies by region.

# 5. Cranial nerve examination

## CN I: Olfactory

**Function:** smell.

**Test:** test each nostril separately with a familiar, non-irritating scent when indicated.

**Abnormality:** anosmia or reduced smell. Causes include nasal disease, head trauma and neurological disease.

## CN II: Optic

CN II carries visual information from the retina.

### A. Visual acuity

Test each eye separately using a distance chart or near-vision chart, with usual corrective lenses if available.

Reduced acuity may result from refractive error, retinal disease, optic nerve disease or other ocular pathology.

### B. Visual fields

Perform confrontation testing:

1. Sit opposite the patient at eye level.
2. Ask them to cover one eye while you cover your opposite eye.
3. Ask them to fix their gaze on your open eye.
4. Bring a finger or suitable target into the visual field from the periphery.
5. Test all quadrants, comparing the patient's responses with your own.
6. Repeat for the other eye.

### Common visual field defects

| Defect | Possible localization |
| --- | --- |
| Monocular visual loss | Retina or optic nerve |
| Bitemporal hemianopia | Optic chiasm |
| Homonymous hemianopia | Contralateral retrochiasmal visual pathway |
| Quadrantanopia | May localize to optic radiations or visual cortex |

A homonymous defect affects the same side of the visual field in both eyes, not the same eye.

### C. Pupillary reflexes

Inspect pupil size, equality and shape.

Test direct and consensual light reflexes:

- Shine light into the right eye: the right pupil should constrict (direct response), and the left should also constrict (consensual response).
- Repeat on the left.

**Pathway:** retina → CN II → midbrain pretectal area → bilateral Edinger–Westphal nuclei → CN III → pupillary constriction.

A relative afferent pupillary defect may indicate asymmetric optic nerve or severe retinal dysfunction.

### D. Fundoscopy

Inspect the optic disc, retinal vessels and visible retina.

Look for:

- **Papilloedema:** optic disc swelling associated with raised intracranial pressure.
- **Optic atrophy:** pale optic disc, suggesting optic nerve damage.
- Retinal haemorrhages or vascular changes.
- Hypertensive or diabetic retinopathy where relevant.

Papilloedema is a significant finding, but its absence does not exclude raised intracranial pressure.

## CN III, IV and VI: Eye movements

Ask the patient to keep their head still and follow your finger through an H-shaped pattern.

Assess:

- Extraocular movements.
- Diplopia.
- Nystagmus.
- Ptosis.
- Pupil abnormalities.

| Nerve | Main action | Typical abnormality |
| --- | --- | --- |
| III, Oculomotor | Most extraocular movements, eyelid elevation, pupillary constriction | Ptosis, impaired adduction/elevation/depression; possible dilated pupil |
| IV, Trochlear | Superior oblique: helps depress the adducted eye | Vertical diplopia, especially looking down |
| VI, Abducens | Lateral rectus: abducts the eye | Inability to abduct the affected eye |

### Nystagmus

Nystagmus is involuntary rhythmic eye movement. It can be horizontal, vertical or torsional.

Assess its direction, whether it changes with gaze and whether it occurs spontaneously or with positional testing.

Possible causes include vestibular disease, cerebellar disease, drug toxicity and brainstem disorders.

Vertical or direction-changing gaze-evoked nystagmus can raise concern for a central cause, particularly when accompanied by other neurological signs.

## CN V: Trigeminal

### Functions

- Facial sensation: V1, V2 and V3.
- Motor supply to muscles of mastication: V3.

### Examination

**1. Sensory function.** Test light touch, and pain if indicated, over:

- V1: forehead.
- V2: cheek.
- V3: chin.

Compare corresponding areas on both sides.

**2. Motor function.** Ask the patient to clench their teeth. Palpate the masseter and temporalis muscles. Ask them to open their mouth against resistance if appropriate.

With a unilateral motor lesion, the jaw may deviate towards the weak side on opening.

**3. Corneal reflex.**

- Afferent limb: CN V1.
- Efferent limb: CN VII.

Test only when clinically indicated and using correct technique.

**4. Jaw jerk.** Place a finger on the patient's relaxed chin and tap gently with a reflex hammer.

A markedly brisk jaw jerk may indicate bilateral corticobulbar upper motor neuron dysfunction.

## CN VII: Facial

Ask the patient to:

- Raise their eyebrows.
- Close their eyes tightly.
- Show their teeth or smile.
- Puff out their cheeks.

### Upper versus lower motor neuron facial weakness

| Feature | UMN facial weakness | LMN facial weakness |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Forehead | Often relatively spared | Weak on the affected side |
| Lower face | Contralateral weakness | Ipsilateral weakness |
| Typical example | Cortical stroke | Bell palsy or facial nerve lesion |

This distinction is useful but not absolute in every clinical presentation.

## CN VIII: Vestibulocochlear

### Hearing

- Test hearing by conversation or whisper.
- If impaired, perform Rinne and Weber tests using a tuning fork.

**Rinne test.** Compare air conduction (AC) with bone conduction (BC).

- Normal: AC greater than BC.
- Conductive hearing loss: BC may exceed AC in the affected ear.

**Weber test.** Place a vibrating tuning fork on the midline of the forehead.

- Conductive loss: sound tends to lateralize to the affected ear.
- Sensorineural loss: sound tends to lateralize to the better ear.

### Vestibular function

Assess dizziness, balance and nystagmus. Further testing may be required depending on symptoms.

## CN IX and X: Glossopharyngeal and vagus

Assess:

- Voice quality.
- Swallowing history.
- Palatal elevation when the patient says "Ah".
- Cough strength where appropriate.

The gag reflex has CN IX as its main afferent limb and CN X as its efferent limb, but it may be absent in healthy individuals and is not a reliable stand-alone test of swallowing safety.

A unilateral vagal palsy may cause asymmetric palatal elevation, with the uvula often deviating away from the affected side.

## CN XI: Accessory

Ask the patient to shrug their shoulders against resistance and turn their head against resistance.

- Shoulder shrug tests the trapezius.
- Head rotation tests the sternocleidomastoid.

Look for weakness or wasting.

## CN XII: Hypoglossal

Inspect the tongue for:

- Wasting.
- Fasciculations.
- Asymmetry.

Ask the patient to protrude the tongue and move it from side to side.

A lower motor neuron lesion may cause deviation towards the affected side on protrusion, often with wasting or fasciculations.

# 6. Motor system examination

The motor examination covers inspection, tone, power, reflexes and the distribution of weakness.

## A. Inspection

Inspect the upper and lower limbs for:

- Muscle bulk and asymmetry.
- Fasciculations.
- Tremor and involuntary movements.
- Contractures or deformities.
- Abnormal posture.

## B. Tone

Explain that you are going to move the patient's limbs, then ask them to relax.

Passively move joints at different speeds, including the wrists, elbows, knees and ankles.

| Tone | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Spasticity | Velocity-dependent increase in tone, associated with UMN dysfunction |
| Rigidity | Relatively velocity-independent resistance, associated with disorders such as Parkinsonism |
| Hypotonia | Reduced resistance to passive movement |

Spasticity may show a clasp-knife phenomenon. Rigidity may be lead-pipe or cogwheel.

## C. Power

Use the MRC scale.

| Grade | Description |
| --- | --- |
| 0/5 | No contraction |
| 1/5 | Flicker of contraction |
| 2/5 | Movement with gravity eliminated |
| 3/5 | Movement against gravity |
| 4/5 | Movement against resistance, but weaker than normal |
| 5/5 | Normal power |

Test and document specific muscle groups.

| Upper limb | Lower limb |
| --- | --- |
| Shoulder abduction | Hip flexion |
| Elbow flexion | Hip extension |
| Elbow extension | Knee extension |
| Wrist extension | Knee flexion |
| Finger abduction | Ankle dorsiflexion |
| Grip | Ankle plantarflexion |
| Finger extension | Great toe extension |

Compare both sides and note whether weakness is proximal, distal, symmetrical or asymmetrical.

## D. UMN versus LMN lesions

| Feature | Upper motor neuron | Lower motor neuron |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Weakness | Present | Present |
| Tone | Usually increased | Usually decreased |
| Deep reflexes | Often brisk | Reduced or absent |
| Plantar response | May be extensor | Usually flexor or absent |
| Wasting | Often mild/disuse initially | May be marked |
| Fasciculations | Generally absent | May occur |

An acute UMN lesion can initially cause flaccidity and reduced reflexes before the classic signs develop.

# 7. Reflexes

Keep the patient relaxed and compare the two sides.

| Reflex | Root value | Technique |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Biceps | C5–C6 | Tap the biceps tendon |
| Supinator/brachioradialis | C5–C6 | Tap near the distal radius |
| Triceps | C7–C8, predominantly C7 | Tap the triceps tendon |
| Knee jerk | L3–L4, predominantly L4 | Tap the patellar tendon |
| Ankle jerk | S1–S2, predominantly S1 | Tap the Achilles tendon |

### Reflex grading

- 0: absent.
- 1+: reduced.
- 2+: normal.
- 3+: brisk.
- 4+: very brisk, often with clonus.

Interpret grades in context; brisk reflexes alone do not prove an UMN lesion.

## Plantar response

Stroke the lateral sole from the heel towards the forefoot and then curve medially.

- Normal adult response: toes flex downwards.
- Extensor plantar response: great toe extends, sometimes with fanning of the other toes.

A positive Babinski sign suggests corticospinal tract dysfunction.

## Clonus

Support the relaxed limb and suddenly stretch the relevant muscle, such as by dorsiflexing the ankle.

Repeated rhythmic contractions suggest clonus and may indicate an UMN lesion.

# 8. Sensory system examination

Explain each test. Ask the patient to close their eyes and compare symmetrical areas.

## A. Primary sensations

| Modality | Technique |
| --- | --- |
| Light touch | Cotton wool or a suitable light-touch stimulus |
| Pain | Appropriate safe sharp stimulus |
| Temperature | Warm and cool stimuli when indicated |
| Vibration | 128-Hz tuning fork over a bony prominence |
| Joint position | Move a distal digit slightly up or down while holding its sides |

For joint position, start distally and move proximally if the patient cannot detect the movement.

## B. Vibration sense and vitamin B12 deficiency

Vibration sense is commonly tested with a 128-Hz tuning fork at the distal interphalangeal joint of the great toe or another bony prominence.

If vibration is impaired, test more proximally.

Reduced vibration and joint position sense can occur in:

- Vitamin B12 deficiency.
- Peripheral neuropathy.
- Dorsal column disease.
- Tabes dorsalis and other neurological conditions.

Vitamin B12 deficiency can cause subacute combined degeneration, affecting the dorsal columns and corticospinal tracts. Patients may develop impaired vibration and position sense, sensory ataxia, weakness, brisk reflexes or extensor plantar responses. Peripheral neuropathy may coexist.

## C. Cortical sensation

Test these only after confirming that primary sensation is adequate.

- **Stereognosis:** identify a familiar object placed in the hand with eyes closed.
- **Graphesthesia:** identify a number or letter traced on the palm.
- **Sensory extinction:** with eyes closed, identify touch applied separately to each side, then simultaneously to both sides.

Failure can indicate dysfunction of cortical sensory processing, commonly involving parietal networks.

## D. Patterns of sensory loss

| Pattern | Possible localization |
| --- | --- |
| Dermatomal | Nerve root |
| Peripheral nerve distribution | Individual peripheral nerve |
| Glove-and-stocking | Length-dependent peripheral neuropathy |
| Hemisensory | Contralateral cerebral lesion or other sensory pathway lesion |
| Sensory level on trunk | Spinal cord lesion |
| Loss of vibration and position sense | Dorsal columns or large-fibre sensory pathways |

# 9. Coordination and cerebellar examination

The cerebellum coordinates the accuracy, timing and smoothness of movement.

## A. Finger-to-nose test

Ask the patient to touch their nose and then your finger repeatedly. Change the target position.

Look for:

- Dysmetria: overshooting or undershooting.
- Intention tremor.
- Decomposition of movement.

## B. Heel-to-shin test

Ask the patient to place one heel on the opposite knee and slide it down the shin.

Look for irregular, poorly coordinated movement.

## C. Rapid alternating movements

Ask the patient to alternate palm-up and palm-down movements rapidly.

Difficulty performing this smoothly is called **dysdiadochokinesia**.

## D. Rebound phenomenon

When appropriate and safe, assess the patient's ability to control movement after resistance is unexpectedly released.

## E. Cerebellar signs

- Ataxia.
- Dysmetria.
- Intention tremor.
- Dysdiadochokinesia.
- Nystagmus.
- Scanning or irregular dysarthria.
- Hypotonia in some cases.

Cerebellar lesions often cause ipsilateral limb incoordination. However, sensory and vestibular disorders can also produce ataxia.

# 10. Gait and balance

Only test gait when it is safe.

Observe:

- Normal walking and turning.
- Arm swing.
- Step length and base width.
- Heel walking and toe walking.
- Tandem gait, if appropriate.

| Gait | Typical association |
| --- | --- |
| Hemiplegic/circumduction | Unilateral UMN weakness |
| High-stepping | Foot drop |
| Shuffling | Parkinsonism |
| Broad-based ataxic | Cerebellar dysfunction |
| Sensory ataxic | Impaired proprioception |
| Scissoring | Bilateral spasticity |
| Waddling | Proximal muscle weakness |

## Romberg's test

Ask the patient to stand with feet together, arms by their sides, and eyes open. Guard them against falling. If safe, ask them to close their eyes.

- **Positive Romberg:** balance deteriorates substantially when the eyes close.
- This suggests impaired proprioceptive or vestibular input.
- It is not a specific test for cerebellar disease.

A patient with marked cerebellar ataxia may already be unstable with eyes open.

# 11. Meningeal signs

Consider these when the patient has headache, fever, photophobia, neck stiffness or suspected subarachnoid haemorrhage.

## Neck stiffness

Assess for resistance or pain during passive neck flexion.

## Kernig sign

With the hip flexed, attempt to extend the knee. Pain or resistance may be a positive sign.

## Brudzinski sign

Passive neck flexion causes involuntary flexion of the hips and knees.

These signs have limited sensitivity. Their absence does not exclude meningitis.

If meningitis is suspected, arrange urgent clinical assessment and treatment; do not delay care while completing the bedside examination.

# 12. Clinical localization: common IMED must-knows

| Finding | Possible localization |
| --- | --- |
| Contralateral face, arm and leg weakness | Cerebral hemisphere |
| Ipsilateral cranial nerve deficit with contralateral limb weakness | Brainstem |
| Bilateral spastic weakness with a sensory level | Spinal cord |
| Dermatomal pain with corresponding weakness | Nerve root |
| Distal wasting and reduced reflexes | Peripheral nerve or LMN disorder |
| Proximal weakness with preserved sensation | Myopathy or neuromuscular disorder |
| Fluctuating weakness and fatigability | Neuromuscular junction disorder |
| Ipsilateral limb dysmetria and ataxia | Cerebellar hemisphere |
| Loss of vibration and position sense | Dorsal column or large-fibre sensory pathway |
| Facial weakness with forehead sparing | Often a supranuclear lesion |
| Facial weakness including the forehead | Often a peripheral CN VII lesion |
| Extensor plantar response | Corticospinal tract dysfunction |
| New fixed dilated pupil with reduced consciousness | Possible brain compression/herniation; emergency |

Localization is a clinical hypothesis. Interpret findings together rather than relying on one sign alone.

# 13. How to present your findings to the examiner

A good presentation is structured, concise and based only on what you actually examined.

## Example: normal CNS examination

"On examination, the patient is conscious, alert and oriented to person, place and time. Higher mental functions and speech are intact. Cranial nerves are intact on examination. Muscle bulk, tone and power are normal in all four limbs, with power graded 5/5. Deep tendon reflexes are symmetrical and normal, and plantar responses are flexor bilaterally. Sensation is intact to the modalities tested. Coordination and gait are normal, and there are no clinical signs of meningeal irritation."

Do not state that all cranial nerves are normal if you did not examine them. Document any omitted or untestable components.

# 14. Common IMED bedside viva questions

| Question | Essential answer |
| --- | --- |
| What is the difference between aphasia and dysarthria? | Language impairment versus impaired motor speech production |
| What is apraxia? | Inability to perform a learned purposeful action despite adequate basic strength and comprehension |
| What is agnosia? | Failure to recognize a stimulus despite adequate basic sensation |
| What is prosopagnosia? | Difficulty recognizing familiar faces |
| What is hemispatial neglect? | Failure to attend to one side of space |
| What does a positive Romberg test suggest? | Proprioceptive or vestibular dysfunction |
| What is the afferent limb of the corneal reflex? | CN V1 |
| What is the efferent limb of the corneal reflex? | CN VII |
| What are the afferent and efferent limbs of the pupillary light reflex? | CN II and CN III, respectively |
| Which roots mediate the knee jerk? | L3–L4, predominantly L4 |
| Which root mediates the ankle jerk? | S1 predominantly |
| What does an extensor plantar response indicate? | Corticospinal tract dysfunction |
| What is the difference between UMN and LMN lesions? | Differences in tone, reflexes, plantar response, wasting and fasciculations |
| What does papilloedema suggest? | Raised intracranial pressure, although absence does not exclude it |
| Which pathway carries vibration sense? | Dorsal column–medial lemniscus pathway |
| What is subacute combined degeneration? | Neurological injury associated with B12 deficiency, particularly dorsal columns and corticospinal tracts |
| What does a markedly brisk jaw jerk suggest? | Possible bilateral corticobulbar dysfunction |
| What causes foot drop? | Weak dorsiflexion, commonly due to common fibular neuropathy or L5 radiculopathy |
| What is the difference between spasticity and rigidity? | Spasticity is velocity-dependent; rigidity is relatively velocity-independent |

## Final examination sequence to memorize

1. Preparation and general inspection.
2. Consciousness and higher mental functions.
3. Speech and language.
4. Cranial nerves.
5. Motor system: inspection, tone and power.
6. Reflexes and plantar responses.
7. Sensory system.
8. Coordination and cerebellar function.
9. Gait and balance.
10. Meningeal signs when indicated.
11. Summarize findings and localize the lesion.

For a routine examination, this sequence provides a comprehensive framework. In an unconscious or acutely unwell patient, stabilization takes priority.

## C. Extra bedside tests

- **Pronator drift:** arms outstretched with palms up and eyes closed. The arm that drifts down and turns inwards (pronates) suggests a mild upper motor neuron weakness on that side.
- **Hoffmann sign:** flick the nail of the middle finger. Flexion of the thumb and index finger suggests an upper motor neuron lesion in the cervical cord or above.
- **Lhermitte sign:** neck flexion produces an electric shock sensation down the spine; seen in cervical cord disease such as multiple sclerosis.
- **Tinel and Phalen tests:** tapping over the carpal tunnel or holding the wrists flexed reproduces tingling in carpal tunnel syndrome (median nerve).
- **Straight leg raise:** pain in the leg on raising the straight leg (about 30–70 degrees) suggests L5 or S1 nerve root irritation.
- **Gower sign:** a patient who rises from the floor by walking the hands up the legs has proximal weakness (myopathy).
- **Orbicularis oculi and ptosis fatigability:** ptosis that worsens on sustained upgaze suggests myasthenia gravis.

## D. Cranial nerve lesion patterns

| Finding | Likely lesion |
| --- | --- |
| Eye "down and out", ptosis, dilated pupil | CN III palsy (a dilated pupil suggests compression, such as a posterior communicating artery aneurysm) |
| Eye "down and out", ptosis, normal pupil | CN III palsy from small-vessel disease (diabetes, hypertension) |
| Cannot look down and in; head tilt | CN IV palsy |
| Cannot abduct; horizontal diplopia worse looking to the affected side | CN VI palsy (also a false localizing sign of raised intracranial pressure) |
| Forehead spared with lower-face weakness | UMN (supranuclear) lesion |
| Whole half of the face weak | LMN lesion (e.g. Bell palsy) |
| Tongue deviates towards the weak side | LMN hypoglossal lesion |

## F. Horner syndrome

Triad of **ptosis (partial), miosis and anhidrosis**, from interruption of the sympathetic pathway to the eye. Causes: lateral medullary stroke, Pancoast tumour, carotid dissection. Look for apical lung signs.

## G. Argyll Robertson pupil

Small, irregular pupils that **accommodate but do not react to light** ("prostitute's pupil"). Classic for neurosyphilis; also seen in diabetes.

## H. Lumbar puncture and CSF interpretation

**Before a lumbar puncture:** exclude raised intracranial pressure (papilloedema, focal signs, reduced consciousness), coagulopathy and local infection. Do not delay antibiotics for a suspected bacterial meningitis while waiting for imaging or the procedure.

| CSF finding | Normal | Bacterial | Viral | TB / fungal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Appearance | Clear | Turbid | Clear | Clear or slightly turbid |
| White cells | 0–5 per mm³ | High, mainly neutrophils | Mildly raised, mainly lymphocytes | Raised, mainly lymphocytes |
| Protein | 0.15–0.45 g/L | High | Normal or mildly raised | High |
| Glucose | At least two-thirds of blood glucose | Low | Normal | Low |

Opening pressure is raised in bacterial, TB and cryptococcal meningitis. In suspected cryptococcal disease, request the cryptococcal antigen and India ink.

## I. Brain herniation signs

- Decreasing GCS.
- New unilateral dilated, fixed pupil (uncal herniation compressing CN III).
- Cushing triad: hypertension, bradycardia and irregular breathing (late sign).
- Extensor (decerebrate) posturing.

This is an emergency: ABCDE, head up 30 degrees, and urgent senior and neurosurgical help.

## J. Decorticate versus decerebrate posturing

- **Decorticate:** arms flexed on the chest, legs extended. Lesion above the red nucleus.
- **Decerebrate:** arms and legs extended and internally rotated. Lesion in the brainstem; worse prognosis.

## K. Quick reference: NIHSS-style stroke screen

When a stroke is suspected, check face, arms and speech (FAST), and record the time of onset. Time of onset decides eligibility for thrombolysis. Check capillary glucose first, because hypoglycaemia can mimic a stroke.
