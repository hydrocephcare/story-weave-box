## Definition

**Asthma** is a **chronic inflammatory disorder of the airways** causing **recurrent episodes of wheeze, breathlessness, chest tightness and cough**, with **variable, reversible airflow obstruction** and **bronchial hyper-responsiveness**.

## Pathophysiology

- **Airway inflammation** (eosinophils, mast cells, Th2 lymphocytes, IgE) after exposure to a trigger.
- **Bronchoconstriction** (smooth muscle spasm), **mucosal oedema**, **mucus plugging**.
- Long term: **airway remodelling** (smooth muscle hypertrophy, subepithelial fibrosis) leading to partly **fixed obstruction**.
- **Atopy** (type 1 hypersensitivity) in most childhood and allergic asthma.

## Risk factors and triggers

- **Genetic:** family history, atopy (eczema, hay fever), male in childhood.
- **Triggers:** **allergens** (house dust mite, pollen, pets, moulds), **viral respiratory infections**, **exercise**, **cold air**, **smoke and biomass fuel**, **air pollution**, **NSAIDs/aspirin**, **beta-blockers**, **occupational agents**, stress, GORD.

## Clinical features

- **Symptoms:** **episodic wheeze, cough (often at night or early morning), breathlessness, chest tightness**; symptoms **vary with time**, **triggers** and **season**; improve with bronchodilators.
- **Signs:** may be normal between attacks; **polyphonic expiratory wheeze**, prolonged expiration, hyperinflation; in attacks, tachypnoea, tachycardia, accessory muscle use, **pulsus paradoxus**.

## Diagnosis

- **History** of variable symptoms and triggers.
- **Spirometry:** **FEV1/FVC below 0.7** with **reversibility: FEV1 rise of 12% and 200 mL or more** after a bronchodilator.
- **Peak expiratory flow:** **diurnal variability above 20%** over 2 weeks; low early morning "dips".
- **Other tests:** FeNO, skin-prick or specific IgE (allergy), blood eosinophils, bronchial provocation (methacholine), chest X-ray (to exclude other causes).
- **Differential:** COPD, heart failure ("cardiac asthma"), GORD, vocal cord dysfunction, bronchiectasis, foreign body, PE.

## Assessing severity of an acute attack

| Level | Features |
|---|---|
| **Moderate** | PEF 50–75% of best, speech normal, RR below 25 |
| **Acute severe** | PEF **33–50%**; **RR 25 or more; HR 110 or more; cannot complete sentences** |
| **Life-threatening** | PEF **below 33%**; **SpO2 below 92%**; **silent chest, cyanosis, poor respiratory effort, bradycardia or hypotension, exhaustion, confusion**, arrhythmia |
| **Near-fatal** | **Raised PaCO2** and/or need for ventilation |

## Investigations: what each test answers  

| Test | Clinical question it addresses | Interpretation relevant to asthma |
|---|---|---|
| **Spirometry with bronchodilator** | Is there airflow obstruction and is it reversible? | FEV₁/FVC < 0.7 with ≥12 % and ≥200 mL rise in FEV₁ after SABA → supports asthma; lack of reversibility suggests COPD or fixed disease |
| **Peak expiratory flow (PEF) diary** | Is there diurnal variability indicating uncontrolled disease? | >20 % variation between morning and evening readings over 2 weeks → characteristic of asthma |
| **Fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO)** | Is eosinophilic airway inflammation present? | Elevated FeNO (>35 ppb) suggests Th2‑high asthma and predicts steroid responsiveness |
| **Blood eosinophil count** | Is systemic eosinophilia a driver of disease? | Count >0.3 × 10⁹/L supports eosinophilic phenotype; guides biologic eligibility |
| **Allergy testing (skin‑prick or specific IgE)** | Are there identifiable IgE‑mediated triggers? | Positive test to aeroallergens → atopic asthma; informs avoidance and allergen‑immunotherapy |
| **Bronchial provocation (methacholine/ histamine)** | Does the airway exhibit hyper‑responsiveness when baseline spirometry is normal? | PC₂₀ < 8 mg/mL (or equivalent) indicates heightened reactivity, useful when diagnosis is uncertain |
| **Chest radiograph** | Are there alternative or complicating pathologies? | Usually normal in asthma; infiltrates, cardiomegaly, or hyperinflation point to infection, heart failure, or COPD |

*Exam tip:* When asked “what test would you order?”, state the specific question you need answered, then give the expected finding that would confirm or refute asthma. This demonstrates a logical, hypothesis‑driven approach.

## Management of acute severe asthma

1. **Oxygen** (target SpO2 94–98%).
2. **Salbutamol** 5 mg **nebulised** (repeat or continuous); add **ipratropium** 0.5 mg nebulised.
3. **Systemic steroid:** **prednisolone 40–50 mg** orally (or **IV hydrocortisone 100 mg**), continued for 5–7 days.
4. **IV magnesium sulfate** (1.2–2 g over 20 minutes) for severe or life-threatening attacks.
5. **Do not give sedatives.** Check **ABG** (a "normal" or rising PaCO2 is dangerous), U&E (potassium).
6. **Chest X-ray** if pneumothorax or pneumonia is suspected.
7. **Escalate:** IV aminophylline or salbutamol, **ICU**, NIV/intubation.
8. **Before discharge:** PEF above 75% of best, stable on inhalers for 24 hours; **inhaler technique** checked; **written action plan**; **GP or clinic review within 48 hours**; **steroid course**.

## Long-term management (GINA stepwise)

- **Aims:** control symptoms, prevent exacerbations, keep normal lung function and activity.
- **Preferred approach (GINA, track 1):** **low-dose ICS-formoterol** as the **reliever**, with the **same combination as maintenance** at higher steps (MART).
- **Alternative (track 2):** **ICS** (for example beclomethasone) maintenance plus **SABA** (salbutamol) reliever.
- **Step up** as needed: low-dose **ICS** → **ICS plus LABA** → medium or high-dose ICS-LABA → add **LAMA (tiotropium)**, **leukotriene receptor antagonist (montelukast)** → **biologics** (omalizumab, mepolizumab) in specialist care.
- **Avoid SABA-only treatment**; frequent reliever use signals poor control.
- **Review:** control, adherence, **inhaler technique**, triggers, comorbidity (rhinitis, GORD, obesity), step down after 3 months of good control.
- **Non-drug:** **trigger avoidance**, stop smoking, avoid biomass smoke, vaccinations (influenza, pneumococcal), **asthma education** and a **personal action plan**, regular exercise, weight loss.

## Complications

- Status asthmaticus, **respiratory failure**, **pneumothorax**, pneumonia, lung remodelling with fixed obstruction, **steroid side effects**, death.

## Exam points

- **Reversible** obstruction + **diurnal variation** + **atopy**.
- **Silent chest, normal or rising PaCO2** = life-threatening.
- Treat acute severe asthma with **oxygen, salbutamol, ipratropium, steroids, magnesium**.
- **ICS is the cornerstone of long-term control**; **LABA never used alone**.

## Differential diagnosis and how to tell them apart  

| Condition | Key clinical pointers | Distinguishing feature(s) |
|---|---|---|
| **COPD** | >40 y, long‑term smoking or biomass exposure; chronic productive cough, dyspnoea progressive, less diurnal variation | Fixed airflow obstruction (post‑bronchodilator FEV₁/FVC < 0.7) with < 12 % reversibility; emphysematous changes on CXR/CT |
| **Cardiac asthma (LVF)** | Orthopnoea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea, peripheral oedema, history of ischemic heart disease | Elevated BNP/NT‑proBNP, pulmonary venous congestion on CXR, normal bronchodilator response |
| **Vocal cord dysfunction (VCD)** | Inspiratory stridor, throat tightness, symptoms triggered by stress or exercise, little response to bronchodilators | Laryngoscopy shows paradoxical adduction of vocal cords; flow‑volume loop shows flattening of inspiratory limb |
| **Bronchiectasis** | Chronic wet cough, recurrent sputum‑producing infections, clubbing, localized crackles | High‑resolution CT shows dilated airways with signet‑ring appearance; sputum culture often positive |
| **Gastro‑oesophageal reflux disease (GORD)** | Cough or wheeze worse after meals or lying flat, heartburn, hoarseness | 24‑h pH monitoring or response to proton‑pump inhibitor trial; no reversible obstruction on spirometry |
| **Foreign body aspiration** | Sudden onset wheeze after choking episode, unilateral diminished breath sounds | Chest X‑ray may show air‑trapping on expiration; bronchoscopy confirms and removes obstruction |
| **Pulmonary embolism** | Acute pleuritic chest pain, tachycardia, hypoxia, risk factors (immobility, surgery) | CT pulmonary angiography positive; D‑dimer elevated; spirometry usually normal |

*Exam tip:* The examiner will probe the pattern of symptoms (chronic vs episodic), risk‑factor history, and response to bronchodilators. A focused physical exam (e.g., inspiratory stridor vs expiratory wheeze) and targeted imaging or functional test often clinch the diagnosis.

## Clinical case  

**Patient:** 12‑year‑old boy, previously well, presents to the emergency department with a 2‑hour history of progressive breathlessness, wheeze and chest tightness after a school PE session in cold weather. He reports a past history of intermittent nocturnal cough and occasional wheeze triggered by dust mites; his mother has allergic rhinitis.  

**Examination:**  
- Respiratory rate 28 /min, heart rate 110 /min, SpO₂ 94 % on room air.  
- Use of accessory muscles, prolonged expiratory phase, polyphonic wheeze audible bilaterally.  
- No cyanosis, no peripheral oedema.  

**Investigations (bedside):** Peak expiratory flow (PEF) 45 % of predicted, improves to 60 % after 5 min of nebulised salbutamol.  

### Questions  

1. **What is the most likely diagnosis and which feature in the history supports it?**  
   **Answer:** Acute severe asthma. The key supporting feature is a known atopic background with trigger‑related wheeze that has worsened after exercise in cold air, a classic bronchial hyper‑responsiveness scenario.  

2. **List the immediate management priorities in the correct order.**  
   **Answer:**  
   1. Administer high‑flow oxygen to maintain SpO₂ ≥ 94 %.  
   2. Give nebulised short‑acting β₂‑agonist (salbutamol) plus anticholinergic (ipratropium).  
   3. Start systemic corticosteroid (oral prednisolone or IV hydrocortisone).  
   4. Re‑assess response with repeat PEF and clinical observation; if still < 50 % or deteriorating, consider IV magnesium sulfate and escalation to intensive care.  

3. **What single bedside observation would alert you to an impending life‑threatening deterioration?**  
   **Answer:** A normal or rising arterial carbon dioxide tension (PaCO₂) on arterial blood gas despite worsening clinical signs. This indicates fatigue of the respiratory muscles and impending respiratory failure.

## Tricky questions examiners ask  

**Q1.** A patient with known asthma lies supine, is tachypnoeic, and has a “silent chest”. What is the next immediate step?  
**Answer:** Give high‑flow oxygen and start nebulised salbutamol + ipratropium; a silent chest denotes life‑threatening obstruction and requires rapid bronchodilation and oxygenation.  

**Q2.** Two patients have identical wheeze and dyspnoea, but one has a history of smoking and the other has allergic rhinitis. Which clinical test best separates asthma from COPD in this scenario?  
**Answer:** Spirometry with bronchodilator reversibility; a ≥12 % and 200 mL rise in FEV₁ after a short‑acting β₂‑agonist favours asthma, whereas COPD shows limited reversibility.  

**Q3.** Why is the use of a sedative (e.g., diazepam) contraindicated in an acute severe asthma attack?  
**Answer:** Sedatives depress the respiratory drive, risk worsening hypoventilation and can precipitate hypercapnic respiratory failure in an already compromised airway.  

**Q4.** A child presents with cough, wheeze and stridor that improves when lying prone. Which diagnosis does this pattern point to, and why is asthma less likely?  
**Answer:** Vocal cord dysfunction (paradoxical vocal fold motion). The positional improvement and presence of inspiratory stridor differentiate it from asthma, which typically shows expiratory wheeze and no positional relief.  

**Q5.** In a patient with frequent reliever use (> 2 puffs/day), why is stepping up to a fixed‑dose LABA + ICS regimen preferred over simply increasing SABA frequency?  
**Answer:** Frequent SABA use signals poor control and risk of exacerbation; adding a LABA to an inhaled corticosteroid provides anti‑inflammatory therapy and reduces β₂‑receptor tolerance, whereas increasing SABA alone does not address underlying inflammation.  

**Q6.** During an acute attack, a clinician notes a “normal” PaCO₂ on ABG. Why should this be interpreted as a warning sign rather than reassurance?  
**Answer:** In severe asthma, ventilation is already compromised; a normal PaCO₂ may represent a loss of the expected hypocapnia from hyperventilation and herald impending respiratory muscle fatigue and carbon dioxide retention.

| Budesonide–formoterol | Inhaled steroid plus long-acting β₂-agonist | Preventer and, in MART, the reliever too | Never use a long-acting β₂-agonist alone |
| Montelukast | Leukotriene antagonist | Add-on, especially with allergic rhinitis | Sleep and mood change |
| Tiotropium | Long-acting antimuscarinic | Add-on in severe asthma | Dry mouth |
| Prednisolone | Systemic corticosteroid | Acute attacks, 5–7 days | High glucose, mood change, stomach irritation |
| Magnesium sulfate | Smooth-muscle relaxant | IV in severe or life-threatening attacks | Flushing, low blood pressure; check reflexes |
| Aminophylline | Methylxanthine | Specialist use in a refractory attack | Arrhythmia, seizures; check the level |

**Avoid or take care:** beta-blockers (even eye drops) can trigger severe bronchospasm, and aspirin and other NSAIDs worsen asthma in aspirin-sensitive patients.

## Practice questions

**1.** A 19-year-old with asthma cannot complete sentences, RR 28, HR 118, PEF 40% of best. This is:
**A. Acute severe** · B. Mild · C. Moderate · D. Life-threatening · E. Near-fatal
*Answer: A.*

**2.** In a severe asthma attack the patient becomes drowsy with a silent chest and PaCO2 6.5 kPa. This indicates:
A. Improvement · B. **Impending respiratory failure (life-threatening)** · C. Anxiety · D. Pneumonia · E. Pulmonary embolism
*Answer: B.*

**3.** The most important drug for long-term control of persistent asthma is:
A. Oral salbutamol · B. Oral antihistamine · **C. Inhaled corticosteroid** · D. Theophylline alone · E. Ipratropium alone
*Answer: C.*
