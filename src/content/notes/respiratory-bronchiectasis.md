# Bronchiectasis — Year 4 Internal Medicine (Respiratory) Notes

*Orientation notes for study. Check local guidelines before prescribing.*


![Bronchiectasis. Public domain](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/65/Bronchiectasis_NHLBI.jpg)

## Definition

**Bronchiectasis** is **permanent, abnormal dilatation of the bronchi** with chronic inflammation and infection, leading to a clinical syndrome of **chronic productive cough, recurrent chest infections and sometimes haemoptysis**.

## Anatomy and physiology you need  

| Structure / Function | Why it matters in bronchiectasis |
|---|---|
| **Conducting airways (bronchi and bronchioles)** – cartilaginous support, smooth‑muscle tone, mucosal glands and ciliated epithelium | Loss of elastic recoil and cartilage integrity after chronic inflammation produces permanent dilatation; the widened lumen impairs laminar airflow and promotes turbulent eddies that trap secretions. |
| **Mucociliary clearance** – coordinated beating of cilia propels mucus toward the pharynx | Damage to cilia (by infection, smoking, or primary ciliary dyskinesia) reduces clearance, allowing mucus stasis, bacterial over‑growth and a self‑perpetuating “vicious cycle”. |
| **Bronchial wall components** – collagen, elastin, smooth‑muscle, submucosal glands | Inflammatory proteases (neutrophil elastase, matrix metalloproteinases) degrade elastin and collagen, thinning the wall and permitting outward expansion under airway pressure. |
| **Bronchial arterial supply** – systemic bronchial arteries run alongside bronchi | Chronic inflammation induces neovascularisation; fragile bronchial vessels are the source of haemoptysis when they rupture. |
| **Ventilation‑perfusion (V/Q) matching** – alveolar ventilation matched to pulmonary perfusion | Dilated, mucus‑filled bronchi cause regional hypoventilation, producing low V/Q units, hypoxaemia and dyspnoea that worsen with disease progression. |
| **Airway smooth‑muscle tone** – β₂‑adrenergic and cholinergic regulation | Bronchospasm can superimpose an obstructive component; bronchodilators improve airflow and facilitate chest‑physiotherapy‑mediated clearance. |
| **Lymphatic drainage of the lung** – interstitial lymphatics remove excess fluid and immune cells | Chronic infection overwhelms lymphatic capacity, leading to peribronchial oedema and further narrowing of the airway lumen. |
| **Systemic inflammatory response** – cytokines (IL‑8, TNF‑α) and acute‑phase reactants | Persistent systemic inflammation contributes to weight loss, anemia and, in severe cases, secondary amyloidosis. |

*Exam tip:* When asked why HRCT is the gold standard, cite that it visualises the **airway wall thickness, loss of tapering and the signet‑ring sign**, all direct consequences of the anatomical changes above.

---

## Pathogenesis

- The **"vicious cycle"**: an initial insult (infection, obstruction, or defective clearance) → **impaired mucociliary clearance** → mucus stasis and **bacterial colonisation** → **chronic inflammation** → **destruction of the bronchial wall** (elastin and muscle) → **dilatation** → worse clearance.

## Causes

| Group | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Post-infectious** (commonest, especially in Kenya) | **Tuberculosis**, severe pneumonia, **measles**, **whooping cough**, adenovirus |
| **Congenital and genetic** | **Cystic fibrosis**, **primary ciliary dyskinesia** (Kartagener: bronchiectasis, sinusitis, dextrocardia), alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, Young's syndrome |
| **Immune deficiency** | **HIV**, hypogammaglobulinaemia (common variable immunodeficiency) |
| **Obstruction** | **Foreign body**, tumour, enlarged nodes (compressing a bronchus), mucus plug |
| **Allergic** | **Allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA)** (central bronchiectasis in asthma) |
| **Inflammatory and autoimmune** | **Rheumatoid arthritis**, inflammatory bowel disease, Sjögren's |
| **Aspiration** | Recurrent aspiration, GORD |
| **Idiopathic** | A large fraction |

## Clinical features

- **Symptoms:** **chronic productive cough** with **copious purulent sputum** (daily, may separate into **three layers** on standing), **recurrent chest infections**, **haemoptysis** (from bronchial artery bleeding), dyspnoea, wheeze, **pleuritic pain**, halitosis, fatigue and weight loss.
- **Signs:** **finger clubbing**, **coarse crackles** (often **early to mid-inspiratory** and moving with cough), **wheeze**, rhonchi; signs of cause (sinusitis, dextrocardia, joint disease); in advanced disease cyanosis and **cor pulmonale**.

## Differential diagnosis and how to tell them apart  

| Condition | Key clinical / radiological clues | Distinguishing feature from bronchiectasis |
|---|---|---|
| **Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)** | Progressive dyspnoea, chronic cough, emphysematous changes on CT, pack‑year smoking history | Spirometry shows a fixed obstructive pattern with reduced FEV₁/FVC; CT lacks focal dilated bronchi with signet‑ring sign. |
| **Bronchiolitis obliterans** | Post‑viral or post‑transplant, dry cough, exertional dyspnoea, mosaic attenuation on CT | HRCT shows air‑trapping and bronchiole wall thickening without true bronchial dilatation; pulmonary function shows severe airflow limitation with normal diffusion. |
| **Pulmonary tuberculosis (active)** | Night sweats, weight loss, cavitary lesions in upper lobes, positive GeneXpert | Sputum AFB positive; cavities have thick walls and are not associated with diffuse bronchial dilatation. |
| **Cystic fibrosis** | Early‑onset recurrent infections, pancreatic insufficiency, salty skin, positive sweat test | Genetic testing/ sweat chloride confirms; bronchiectasis is usually diffuse and central, but the systemic features point to CF. |
| **Allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA)** | Asthma, fleeting pulmonary infiltrates, eosinophilia, high total IgE, Aspergillus‑specific IgE | Central bronchiectasis on CT plus immunologic markers; response to steroids distinguishes it. |
| **Pulmonary embolism with infarction** | Acute pleuritic chest pain, tachycardia, risk factors for thrombosis, wedge‑shaped opacity on CT | CT pulmonary angiography shows filling defect; no chronic mucus plugging or bronchial wall thickening. |
| **Lung cancer (central)** | Weight loss, hemoptysis, smoking history, mass lesion on imaging | Bronchoscopy reveals endobronchial tumour; CT shows a solid mass rather than uniform bronchial dilatation. |

*Exam strategy:* List the most likely alternative, point to a single discriminating investigation (e.g., sputum AFB for TB, sweat test for CF, eosinophil count/IgE for ABPA) and explain how its result would shift the diagnosis away from bronchiectasis.

## Investigations

- **HRCT chest (diagnostic):** **bronchial dilatation** with **internal diameter larger than the accompanying artery ("signet ring" sign)**, **lack of tapering ("tram-track")**, **bronchial wall thickening**, mucus plugging, "tree-in-bud".
- **Chest X-ray:** **tram-track lines, ring shadows**, cystic spaces (may be normal in early disease).
- **Sputum:** culture for *Haemophilus influenzae*, *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, *Staph aureus*; **AFB/GeneXpert** for TB and non-tuberculous mycobacteria; fungal culture.
- **Spirometry** (obstructive, sometimes mixed).
- **Cause-finding:** **HIV test**, **immunoglobulins**, **sweat chloride/CFTR** (cystic fibrosis), ciliary function and nasal nitric oxide, **Aspergillus IgE and precipitins**, rheumatoid factor, alpha-1 antitrypsin.
- **Bronchoscopy** if focal disease or obstruction is suspected.

## Management

1. **Treat the cause** where possible (immunoglobulin replacement, treat ABPA with steroids and itraconazole, remove obstruction).
2. **Airway clearance:** **chest physiotherapy** (active cycle of breathing, postural drainage, oscillating devices) **daily**; **mucolytics** or hypertonic saline in selected patients.
3. **Antibiotics for exacerbations:** sputum culture first, **treat for 10–14 days** (amoxicillin or doxycycline for *H. influenzae* and *S. pneumoniae*; **ciprofloxacin** for *Pseudomonas*).
4. **Long-term prophylaxis** in patients with **3 or more exacerbations a year:** **azithromycin** (macrolide), or **nebulised antibiotics** (colistin, gentamicin) for *Pseudomonas*.
5. **Bronchodilators** (and inhaled steroids if asthma or ABPA).
6. **Vaccination:** influenza and pneumococcal; **stop smoking**; nutrition.
7. **Surgery:** resection of a **localised** area, for **massive haemoptysis** or recurrent infections from a single lobe; **bronchial artery embolisation** for haemoptysis; **lung transplant** in end-stage disease.

## Complications

- **Recurrent infection**, **massive haemoptysis**, **empyema**, **lung abscess**, **pneumothorax**, **respiratory failure and cor pulmonale**, **amyloidosis**, **metastatic infection (brain abscess)**, sinusitis.

## Exam points

- **HRCT** is the diagnostic test (signet ring sign).
- **Post-TB and post-measles** bronchiectasis are common in Kenya.
- **Pseudomonas** colonisation signals worse disease and needs targeted antibiotics.
- **Daily airway clearance** is the foundation of management.
- **Kartagener syndrome:** bronchiectasis + sinusitis + situs inversus.

## Clinical case  

**Patient:** 28‑year‑old male, HIV‑negative, former smoker (5 pack‑years), works as a school teacher.  

**Presentation:** 6‑month history of daily copious purulent sputum that separates into three layers when the sputum is left to stand. He reports three episodes of worsening cough, fever > 38 °C and increased sputum volume in the past year, each requiring oral antibiotics. Over the past month he has noticed mild exertional dyspnoea (NYHA II) and occasional streaks of blood in his sputum. No weight loss, no chest pain.  

**Key findings on examination:**  
- Clubbing of the fingernails (grade 2).  
- Bilateral coarse crackles heard in the mid‑lung zones, moving with cough.  
- No wheeze, no peripheral oedema.  
- Sinus tenderness absent, cardiac exam normal.  

**Investigations (selected):**  
- Chest X‑ray: tram‑track shadows in the right lower zone.  
- HRCT chest: signet‑ring sign and lack of tapering of bronchi in the right lower lobe; mucus plugging.  
- Sputum culture: growth of *Haemophilus influenzae*; no *Pseudomonas*.  
- Spirometry: FEV₁ = 62 % predicted, FEV₁/FVC = 0.68 (obstructive pattern).  

### Exam‑style questions  

1. **What is the most likely underlying pathophysiological mechanism that explains this patient’s chronic productive cough?**  
   **Answer:** Impaired mucociliary clearance leading to mucus stasis, bacterial colonisation and a self‑perpetuating inflammatory cycle that destroys bronchial wall elastin, producing permanent dilatation and excess sputum production.  

2. **Which investigation is definitive for confirming the diagnosis and why?**  
   **Answer:** High‑resolution computed tomography (HRCT) of the chest because it visualises bronchial dilatation, the signet‑ring sign and loss of tapering—findings that are pathognomonic and not reliably seen on plain radiography.  

3. **What is the next best step in management after confirming bronchiectasis in this patient?**  
   **Answer:** Initiate a structured airway‑clearance programme (e.g., active cycle of breathing techniques with postural drainage) and treat the current exacerbation with an antibiotic directed at the cultured organism (*H. influenzae*), while arranging sputum surveillance for possible *Pseudomonas* colonisation.

## Tricky questions examiners ask  

**Q1.** A patient with known bronchiectasis presents with a new fever, increased sputum purulence and a rise in CRP. What is the first investigation you should order and why?  
**Answer:** Obtain a sputum sample for Gram stain and culture before starting antibiotics; this guides targeted therapy and avoids unnecessary broad‑spectrum use, which is crucial in bronchiectasis where resistant organisms are common.  

**Q2.** Two patients have chronic cough and sputum production. One has a “signet‑ring” sign on HRCT, the other has normal HRCT but a positive bronchodilator response on spirometry. Which finding separates bronchiectasis from asthma and what does it imply?  
**Answer:** The signet‑ring sign separates bronchiectasis; it indicates irreversible structural dilatation of bronchi, whereas asthma shows reversible airway obstruction without fixed bronchial changes.  

**Q3.** In a child with recurrent lower‑respiratory infections, a CT shows bronchial dilatation limited to the middle lobe and lingula. Why should you avoid labeling this as “post‑TB bronchiectasis” without further evidence?  
**Answer:** Post‑TB bronchiectasis typically involves the upper lobes; a focal distribution in the middle lobe/lingula suggests an obstructive cause (e.g., congenital anomaly) and mislabeling may miss the correct surgical or interventional treatment.  

**Q4.** A patient with bronchiectasis is being considered for long‑term macrolide prophylaxis. What key exclusion must you verify before starting azithromycin and why?  
**Answer:** Exclude active non‑tuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) infection because macrolides are a cornerstone of NTM therapy; using them as prophylaxis could induce macrolide resistance and compromise future treatment.  

**Q5.** During an acute exacerbation, a clinician chooses to give oral ciprofloxacin empirically. What clinical scenario would make this choice inappropriate?  
**Answer:** If the patient’s sputum culture has not shown *Pseudomonas* and there is no risk factor for gram‑negative infection (e.g., prior colonisation, severe COPD), using ciprofloxacin is unnecessary and promotes fluoroquinolone resistance.  

**Q6.** A patient with bronchiectasis and frequent haemoptysis is referred for bronchial artery embolisation. What alternative surgical option should be considered if the disease is limited to a single lobe, and why might it be preferred?  
**Answer:** Segmental or lobar resection (surgical excision) can be considered because it removes the source of bleeding and infected tissue definitively; it is preferred when the disease is localized and the patient has adequate pulmonary reserve, offering a potential cure rather than a temporising embolisation.

| Drug | Class | Role here | Watch for |

| Amoxicillin | Penicillin | Exacerbation with Haemophilus or pneumococcus (14 days) | Rash, diarrhoea |

| Ciprofloxacin | Fluoroquinolone | Pseudomonas exacerbations | Tendon injury, QT prolongation |
| Azithromycin | Macrolide | Long-term prophylaxis if 3 or more exacerbations a year | QT prolongation, hearing loss; exclude non-tuberculous mycobacteria first |
| Gentamicin | Aminoglycoside | Nebulised or IV for resistant Pseudomonas | Kidney and hearing damage; level monitoring |
| Salbutamol | Short-acting β₂-agonist | Bronchodilator before airway clearance | Tremor, palpitations |
| Prednisolone | Systemic corticosteroid | Allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis | High glucose, infection risk |

**Culture first, then treat:** short courses relapse, so a full 14 days is the usual duration.

## Practice questions

**1.** A 35-year-old man with daily large volumes of purulent sputum, clubbing and recurrent haemoptysis. The best diagnostic test is:
A. Chest X-ray · B. Spirometry · C. Bronchoscopy · D. Sputum cytology · **E. High-resolution CT chest**
*Answer: E.*

**2.** HRCT sign of bronchiectasis:
A. Ground glass · B. Honeycombing · C. Eggshell calcification · **D. Signet-ring sign (bronchus wider than its artery)** · E. Cavity with air-fluid level
*Answer: D.*

**3.** A man with bronchiectasis, chronic sinusitis and dextrocardia most likely has:
A. Cystic fibrosis · B. **Primary ciliary dyskinesia (Kartagener)** · C. Sarcoidosis · D. Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency · E. ABPA
*Answer: B.*
