# Menstrual Disorders: Amenorrhoea, Dysmenorrhoea and Cycle Abnormalities

*Year 4 MBChB | Obstetrics & Gynaecology | CAT revision*

## 1. Normal menstrual physiology

Menstruation is the cyclical shedding of the functional endometrial layer when pregnancy has not occurred. It is controlled by the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis.

1. Pulsatile hypothalamic GnRH stimulates pituitary FSH and LH.
2. FSH promotes ovarian follicular development and oestrogen production.
3. Oestrogen proliferates the endometrium (proliferative phase).
4. The LH surge triggers ovulation.
5. The corpus luteum produces progesterone, converting the endometrium to a secretory state.
6. If pregnancy does not occur, the corpus luteum regresses, progesterone/oestrogen fall and menstruation follows.

For adult reproductive-age patients, a typical cycle interval is approximately 24–38 days, with bleeding lasting up to 8 days. Adolescents may have more variable cycles during the first years after menarche.

## 2. Terminology

- **Amenorrhoea:** absence of menstruation.
- **Primary amenorrhoea:** menstruation has not started by the expected age; investigate if no menarche by age 15 with otherwise normal secondary sexual development, or by age 13 if there is no breast development, and earlier if concerning features exist.
- **Secondary amenorrhoea:** cessation of previously established menstruation for about 3 months in someone with previously regular cycles, or 6 months in someone with irregular cycles (definitions vary by guideline).
- **Oligomenorrhoea:** infrequent menstruation, commonly cycle intervals greater than 35–38 days.
- **Polymenorrhoea:** frequent menstruation, commonly cycle intervals shorter than 24 days.
- **Dysmenorrhoea:** painful menstruation.
- **Menorrhagia:** older term for heavy menstrual bleeding; current preferred term is HMB.
- **Metrorrhagia:** older term for bleeding between periods; use intermenstrual bleeding.
- **Anovulation:** absence of ovulation; may cause irregular bleeding or amenorrhoea.

## 3. Amenorrhoea

### A. Causes of primary amenorrhoea

Think anatomically and hormonally:

**Outflow tract/anatomical**
- Imperforate hymen: cyclical pain, haematocolpos/haematometra, primary amenorrhoea.
- Transverse vaginal septum or vaginal agenesis.
- Müllerian agenesis (MRKH): absent uterus/upper vagina, normal ovarian function and secondary sexual characteristics.
- Cervical agenesis/obstruction.

**Gonadal or chromosomal**
- Turner syndrome (45,X): streak ovaries, hypergonadotrophic hypogonadism, often short stature and absent/incomplete puberty.
- Gonadal dysgenesis.

**Hypothalamic/pituitary**
- Constitutional delay, low energy availability, excessive exercise, chronic illness, stress, congenital GnRH deficiency (e.g. Kallmann syndrome), pituitary lesions.

**Differences of sex development**
- Complete androgen insensitivity: 46,XY, testes, female external phenotype, absent uterus, breast development, sparse pubic/axillary hair.

### B. Causes of secondary amenorrhoea

- **Pregnancy** — always exclude first.
- PCOS and chronic anovulation.
- Functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea (weight loss, low energy availability, excessive exercise, stress).
- Hyperprolactinaemia (including prolactinoma or drug-related).
- Thyroid dysfunction.
- Premature ovarian insufficiency.
- Uterine adhesions (Asherman syndrome) after uterine instrumentation/infection.
- Menopause, medications and chronic systemic disease.

### C. Assessment of amenorrhoea

History: pregnancy possibility, previous cycle pattern, menarche, weight/exercise/stress, eating disorder symptoms, galactorrhoea, headaches/visual symptoms, hot flushes, acne/hirsutism, pelvic pain, uterine procedures and medications.

Examination: BMI, secondary sexual characteristics, thyroid, galactorrhoea, signs of androgen excess, visual fields if pituitary disease suspected, abdominal/pelvic examination as indicated.

**Investigations**
1. Pregnancy test first.
2. FSH/LH and oestradiol help distinguish ovarian failure from hypothalamic/pituitary suppression.
3. Prolactin and TSH according to initial assessment.
4. Androgen profile if hyperandrogenism.
5. Pelvic ultrasound to assess uterus, endometrium and ovaries.
6. Karyotype, MRI pituitary or further tests when indicated by clinical pattern.

**Interpretation**
- High FSH with low oestradiol: primary ovarian insufficiency/gonadal dysgenesis.
- Low/normal FSH/LH with low oestradiol: hypothalamic/pituitary cause.
- Hyperandrogenism with oligo/anovulation: consider PCOS after excluding mimics.
- Raised prolactin: repeat/assess causes, medication and pituitary pathology as appropriate.
- No uterus on imaging: consider Müllerian agenesis or androgen insensitivity; assess chromosomes/hormones with specialist input.

## 4. Dysmenorrhoea

### A. Primary dysmenorrhoea
Painful menstruation without identifiable pelvic pathology. It commonly begins within a few years of menarche, is cramping/suprapubic, begins shortly before or at onset of bleeding and lasts 1–3 days. It is caused largely by increased endometrial prostaglandins, producing uterine hypercontractility and vasoconstriction/uterine ischaemia.

**Management**
- Explain the mechanism and reassure after appropriate assessment.
- NSAIDs are first-line for many patients; start at onset of symptoms or shortly before predictable menstruation, if no contraindication.
- Hormonal contraception (combined or progestogen-only options) can suppress ovulation/endometrial proliferation and reduce pain when appropriate.
- Heat, exercise and supportive measures may help.
- Reassess if symptoms are severe, progressive or unresponsive.

### B. Secondary dysmenorrhoea
Painful menstruation due to pelvic pathology. Causes include endometriosis, adenomyosis, fibroids, PID, pelvic adhesions and congenital outflow obstruction. It may begin later in life, become progressively worse, occur outside menstruation or be associated with dyspareunia, infertility, abnormal bleeding or discharge.

Investigate according to symptoms; pelvic ultrasound is commonly used. Treat the underlying cause and refer when persistent or complex.

## 5. Oligomenorrhoea and anovulation

Infrequent menstruation often reflects infrequent ovulation. Common causes include PCOS, hypothalamic suppression, thyroid dysfunction, hyperprolactinaemia and ovarian insufficiency. Chronic anovulation may cause prolonged unopposed oestrogen exposure and endometrial hyperplasia risk.

Management depends on fertility goals:
- If pregnancy is desired: investigate cause and consider ovulation induction under an appropriate pathway.
- If pregnancy is not desired: cycle regulation and endometrial protection with appropriate hormonal therapy may be required.
- Address weight, nutrition, exercise, thyroid/prolactin disorders and other reversible causes.

## 6. Premenstrual syndrome (PMS) and premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD)

PMS is a recurrent pattern of physical, emotional and behavioural symptoms in the luteal phase that improve after menstruation begins. PMDD is a more severe form with prominent mood symptoms and significant functional impairment. A symptom diary over at least two cycles helps establish cyclical timing. Management includes education, lifestyle measures, psychological support/CBT and selected hormonal or SSRI treatment according to severity and local guidance.

## 7. Differential diagnosis and red flags

Do not label all pelvic pain as dysmenorrhoea. Exclude pregnancy/ectopic pregnancy, PID, ovarian torsion, endometriosis, adenomyosis and gastrointestinal/urinary causes. Red flags include severe acute pain, fever, syncope, positive pregnancy test, heavy bleeding with instability, galactorrhoea with headache/visual symptoms, virilisation or rapidly progressive androgen excess.

## 8. Management overview

| Disorder | Main approach |
|---|---|
| Primary amenorrhoea | Identify anatomical, gonadal or central cause; specialist evaluation |
| Secondary amenorrhoea | Exclude pregnancy, then investigate endocrine/ovarian/uterine causes |
| Oligomenorrhoea | Assess ovulation and cause; address endometrial protection/fertility goals |
| Primary dysmenorrhoea | NSAIDs, hormonal suppression if suitable, supportive measures |
| Secondary dysmenorrhoea | Investigate pelvic pathology and treat cause |
| PMS/PMDD | Symptom diary, lifestyle/psychological support and selected pharmacological therapy |

## 9. CAT high-yield points

- Pregnancy is the first diagnosis to exclude in secondary amenorrhoea.
- Primary amenorrhoea: assess age, breast development, uterus and outflow tract.
- Breast development indicates prior oestrogen exposure; absent uterus with normal breasts suggests MRKH or androgen insensitivity (differentiate by karyotype/hormones and hair pattern).
- High FSH + low oestradiol suggests ovarian failure; low gonadotrophins + low oestradiol suggests hypothalamic/pituitary suppression.
- PCOS is a common cause of oligo-ovulation.
- Primary dysmenorrhoea is prostaglandin-mediated; NSAIDs are first-line for many patients.
- Progressive pain, deep dyspareunia, dyschezia or infertility suggests endometriosis.
- Chronic anovulation can expose endometrium to unopposed oestrogen and increase hyperplasia risk.

## 10. Tricky examiner questions

**Q1. What is the first investigation in secondary amenorrhoea?**  
**Answer:** Pregnancy test.

**Q2. A young woman has primary amenorrhoea, normal breasts, absent uterus and normal pubic hair. What is likely?**  
**Answer:** Müllerian agenesis (MRKH); androgen insensitivity is another important differential, usually with sparse pubic/axillary hair and 46,XY karyotype.

**Q3. What does high FSH with low oestradiol suggest?**  
**Answer:** Hypergonadotrophic hypogonadism due to ovarian insufficiency or gonadal dysgenesis.

**Q4. What is the mechanism of primary dysmenorrhoea?**  
**Answer:** Increased endometrial prostaglandins cause uterine hypercontractility and vasoconstriction, leading to ischaemic cramping pain.

**Q5. Why is chronic anovulation clinically important?**  
**Answer:** Progesterone is absent or insufficient, leaving the endometrium exposed to unopposed oestrogen and increasing risk of hyperplasia.

## 11. Practice MCQs

### Question 1
A 19-year-old presents with secondary amenorrhoea for four months. What is the first test?

A. CA-125  
B. Pregnancy test  
C. Hysterectomy  
D. Cervical cytology

**Answer: B.** Pregnancy is the commonest and most important cause to exclude first.

### Question 2
A 16-year-old has primary amenorrhoea, normal breast development and no uterus on ultrasound. What is an important diagnosis?

A. MRKH syndrome  
B. PID  
C. Endometriosis as the only possibility  
D. Ovarian torsion

**Answer: A.** Müllerian agenesis causes absent uterus/upper vagina with usually normal ovarian function and secondary sexual development.

### Question 3
A 22-year-old has cramping pain during the first two days of menstruation, normal examination and no red flags. The likely mechanism is:

A. Prostaglandin-mediated uterine hypercontractility  
B. Tubal rupture  
C. Ovarian malignancy  
D. Cervical cancer

**Answer: A.** Primary dysmenorrhoea is largely mediated by endometrial prostaglandins.

### Question 4
A woman has oligomenorrhoea, acne and hirsutism. Which diagnosis is likely?

A. PCOS  
B. Acute PID  
C. Menopause  
D. Cervical polyp

**Answer: A.** PCOS commonly causes hyperandrogenism and oligo/anovulation.

### Question 5
A patient has progressively worsening dysmenorrhoea, deep dyspareunia and infertility. What should be suspected?

A. Endometriosis  
B. Physiological ovulation  
C. Vaginal candidiasis  
D. Simple cystitis

**Answer: A.** Progressive cyclical pain with deep dyspareunia and infertility suggests endometriosis.
