# Introduction to Psychopharmacology

## Key points

- **Psychopharmacology** is the study of drugs that affect **mood, thoughts, behaviour and sensation**, and their use in treating psychiatric disorders.
- **Pharmacokinetics** is what the body does to the drug (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion). **Pharmacodynamics** is what the drug does to the body (receptors, dose-response, therapeutic index).
- Four **dopamine pathways** explain both the symptoms and the side effects: mesolimbic, mesocortical, tuberoinfundibular and nigrostriatal.
- **Antidepressants** take about 2 to 4 weeks to work. **Lithium** has a narrow therapeutic index and needs blood levels. **Benzodiazepines** are for short-term use because of dependence.
- Check **special populations** (children, pregnancy and lactation, medical illness, substance use), **adherence**, **polypharmacy** and **stigma** before and during any prescription.

## 1. What is psychopharmacology?

**Psychopharmacology** is the study of how drugs act on the nervous system to change mood, thinking, behaviour and sensation, and how those drugs are used to treat psychiatric disorders.

**Principles of drugs acting on the nervous system**

- A drug must reach its target in the brain, so it has to cross the **blood-brain barrier** (lipid-soluble drugs cross easily).
- Most psychotropic drugs act on **neurotransmitter systems**: they change release, reuptake, breakdown or receptor activity.
- The **first effect on a receptor is quick**, but the **clinical benefit often takes weeks**, because the brain adapts.
- Every drug has **wanted and unwanted effects**. Side effects usually come from the same receptors, in other pathways.

## 2. Pharmacokinetics: what the body does to the drug

- **Absorption**: The drug enters the blood: oral, intramuscular, intravenous, or a long-acting depot injection. Oral drugs go through the liver first (first-pass metabolism). — Intramuscular and depot injections help in agitation and when adherence is poor.
- **Distribution**: The drug moves to tissues, bound partly to plasma proteins. Lipid-soluble drugs cross the blood-brain barrier and accumulate in fat. — Highly protein-bound drugs can interact; fat-soluble drugs (such as diazepam) act longer in older and obese patients.
- **Metabolism**: Mainly in the liver by **cytochrome P450 (CYP)** enzymes, such as CYP2D6, CYP3A4, CYP1A2 and CYP2C19. — Inhibitors raise drug levels (for example fluoxetine and paroxetine inhibit CYP2D6). Inducers lower them (for example carbamazepine, rifampicin). Smoking induces CYP1A2 and can lower olanzapine and clozapine levels.
- **Excretion**: Mainly by the kidney (lithium is excreted unchanged) or bile. — In kidney disease lithium accumulates and becomes toxic.

**Half-life** tells you how long a drug lasts. A drug reaches **steady state** after about 5 half-lives, so a change in dose shows its full effect only after that time.

## 3. Pharmacodynamics: what the drug does to the body

- **Agonist:** binds a receptor and activates it.
- **Antagonist:** binds a receptor and blocks it (haloperidol blocks dopamine D2 receptors).
- **Partial agonist:** activates the receptor, but less than the natural transmitter. Aripiprazole is a partial agonist at D2 receptors and buspirone is a partial agonist at 5-HT1A receptors.
- **Complete or full agonist:** produces the maximum response of the receptor.
- **Dose-response:** a larger dose gives a larger effect, up to a plateau, and more side effects.
- **Therapeutic index:** the gap between the effective dose and the toxic dose. A **narrow** index (lithium) needs blood level monitoring. A **wide** index (most SSRIs) is safer in overdose.

## 4. Neurobiology: neurons and synapses

**Structure of a neuron:** dendrites receive signals, the cell body (soma) integrates them, the axon carries them, and the axon terminal releases neurotransmitter.

**The synapse:**

1. An electrical signal reaches the axon terminal.
2. Neurotransmitter is released into the **synaptic cleft**.
3. It binds **receptors** on the next neuron and changes its activity.
4. The signal ends when the transmitter is taken back by **reuptake transporters**, or broken down by enzymes (such as monoamine oxidase, MAO).

Most psychotropic drugs work at this point: they **block reuptake** (SSRIs), **block receptors** (antipsychotics), **block breakdown** (MAO inhibitors) or **enhance receptor action** (benzodiazepines).

## 5. Neurotransmitters

- **Dopamine**: Reward, motivation, movement, thinking — Excess activity in some pathways is linked to psychosis. Antipsychotics block D2 receptors.
- **Serotonin (5-HT)**: Mood, sleep, appetite, anxiety — SSRIs raise synaptic serotonin. Many second-generation antipsychotics block 5-HT2A.
- **Norepinephrine**: Alertness, arousal, mood — SNRIs and atomoxetine raise it.
- **GABA**: Main inhibitory transmitter — Benzodiazepines enhance GABA-A receptor action: sedation, anxiolysis, anticonvulsant effect.
- **Glutamate**: Main excitatory transmitter — Involved in schizophrenia (NMDA receptor hypofunction) and in learning and memory.
- **Acetylcholine**: Memory, attention, autonomic function — **Nicotinic** receptors are ion channels; **muscarinic** receptors are G-protein coupled. Blocking muscarinic receptors causes dry mouth, constipation and blurred vision.
- **Histamine**: Wakefulness — Blocking H1 receptors causes sedation and weight gain.
- **Neuropeptides**: Modulate other transmitters (for example endorphins, oxytocin, substance P) — Involved in pain, stress and social behaviour.

## 6. The four dopamine pathways

- **Mesolimbic**: Reward and salience — Positive symptoms of psychosis (hallucinations, delusions) — Positive symptoms improve (the aim of treatment)
- **Mesocortical**: Cognition, motivation, emotion — Low activity is linked to negative and cognitive symptoms — Negative symptoms may not improve or may worsen
- **Tuberoinfundibular**: Dopamine inhibits prolactin release from the pituitary — **Hyperprolactinaemia** (galactorrhoea, menstrual disturbance, sexual dysfunction)
- **Nigrostriatal**: Control of movement — **Extrapyramidal side effects** (dystonia, akathisia, parkinsonism, tardive dyskinesia)

> Exam tip: link each pathway to one symptom and one side effect. Mesolimbic = positive symptoms; mesocortical = negative symptoms; tuberoinfundibular = prolactin; nigrostriatal = movement disorders.

## 7. Effects of psychotropic drugs: short term and long term

- **What happens**: Receptor effects begin within hours, with early side effects (sedation, nausea, agitation) — The brain adapts over weeks, and the clinical benefit appears
- **Examples**: SSRIs may cause anxiety and nausea in week 1; antipsychotics sedate and calm agitation quickly — Antidepressant response takes **about 2 to 4 weeks**; antipsychotics need weeks for psychotic symptoms; long-term risks are weight gain and metabolic syndrome, tardive dyskinesia, tolerance and dependence (benzodiazepines), and discontinuation symptoms

## 8. Antidepressants

**Theories.** The **monoamine hypothesis** says depression involves low serotonin, norepinephrine and dopamine function. The **inflammatory hypothesis** links depression with raised inflammatory markers and immune activation. Neither explains everything, and the delay before benefit suggests the brain adapts over time.

- **SSRIs**: Fluoxetine, sertraline, paroxetine, escitalopram — Block the serotonin transporter — First-line for depression and anxiety. Nausea, sexual dysfunction, insomnia or agitation at first, **hyponatraemia** (especially in older patients), increased bleeding risk. Paroxetine has the worst discontinuation symptoms. Fluoxetine has a long half-life.
- **SNRIs**: Venlafaxine, duloxetine — Block serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake — Can raise blood pressure (venlafaxine, dose-dependent). Nausea, sweating. Duloxetine also helps neuropathic pain.
- **Atypical antidepressants**: Bupropion — Blocks dopamine and norepinephrine reuptake — Lowers the seizure threshold; little sexual dysfunction; used in smoking cessation.
- Trazodone — 5-HT2A antagonist and weak reuptake blocker — Sedating; priapism is a rare risk.
- Mirtazapine — Blocks alpha-2, 5-HT2 and H1 receptors — Sedating, increases appetite and weight.
- Vortioxetine — Multimodal serotonergic action — Nausea is the commonest side effect.

**Safety points**

- **Serotonin syndrome:** agitation, sweating, tremor, clonus, hyperthermia. It follows combining serotonergic drugs (for example an SSRI with an MAO inhibitor, tramadol or linezolid).
- In young people, watch for **suicidal thoughts** when starting an antidepressant.
- Do not stop suddenly: taper, to avoid discontinuation symptoms.

## 9. Antipsychotics

**Underlying theory.** The **dopamine hypothesis** says excess dopamine activity in the mesolimbic pathway produces positive symptoms. The **glutamate hypothesis** says reduced NMDA receptor function contributes to positive, negative and cognitive symptoms.

### First-generation antipsychotics (FGAs)

- Examples: **haloperidol, fluphenazine, chlorpromazine.**
- Mechanism: block **D2 receptors**.
- High-potency drugs (haloperidol, fluphenazine) cause more **extrapyramidal side effects**. Low-potency chlorpromazine is more **sedating**, with **anticholinergic effects and postural hypotension**.
- Other effects: hyperprolactinaemia, QT prolongation, **neuroleptic malignant syndrome** (fever, rigidity, altered consciousness, raised creatine kinase), tardive dyskinesia with long use.
- Fluphenazine and haloperidol are available as long-acting depot injections.

### Second-generation antipsychotics (SGAs)

- Examples: **olanzapine, risperidone, quetiapine, lurasidone.**
- Mechanism: block **D2 and 5-HT2A receptors**.
- Fewer extrapyramidal effects at usual doses, but more **metabolic effects**.

- **Olanzapine**: Marked weight gain, diabetes and lipid changes, sedation
- **Risperidone**: Hyperprolactinaemia, extrapyramidal effects at higher doses
- **Quetiapine**: Sedation, postural hypotension, weight gain
- **Lurasidone**: Less weight gain; nausea and akathisia; take with food

**Monitor** weight, waist, blood pressure, glucose or HbA1c and lipids, and ask about movement symptoms.

## 10. Anxiolytics and hypnotics

- **Benzodiazepines** (diazepam, clonazepam, alprazolam) enhance GABA-A receptor action. They are fast acting and effective for acute anxiety, agitation, alcohol withdrawal, seizures and catatonia.
- Problems: sedation, falls, memory impairment, **tolerance, dependence and withdrawal** (including seizures after sudden stopping), and **respiratory depression with alcohol or opioids**. Use the lowest dose for the shortest time.
- **Non-benzodiazepine hypnotics** (zolpidem, zopiclone) act at the GABA-A receptor too and are for short-term insomnia. They can also cause dependence and complex sleep behaviours.
- **Buspirone** is a 5-HT1A partial agonist for generalised anxiety. It works slowly, over weeks, and does not cause dependence or sedation.

## 11. Mood stabilisers

### Lithium

- The classic mood stabiliser for bipolar disorder (prevention and mania).
- **Narrow therapeutic index**: measure blood levels about 12 hours after the last dose, and check kidney function, thyroid function and calcium.
- Side effects: tremor, thirst and polyuria, weight gain, hypothyroidism, kidney damage with long use.
- **Toxicity:** vomiting and diarrhoea, coarse tremor, ataxia, confusion, seizures.
- Levels rise with **dehydration, NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors and thiazide diuretics**.
- Risk in pregnancy (cardiac malformations).

### Anticonvulsants used as mood stabilisers

- **Valproic acid**: Effective in mania. **Teratogenic** (neural tube defects): avoid in women who can become pregnant. Weight gain, tremor, liver toxicity, pancreatitis.
- **Carbamazepine**: Mania and prevention. Strong CYP enzyme inducer, so it lowers levels of many drugs. Hyponatraemia, rash (HLA-B*1502 risk of Stevens-Johnson syndrome in some Asian populations), blood count changes.
- **Lamotrigine**: Prevents bipolar depression. **Titrate slowly**: rash can progress to Stevens-Johnson syndrome.

## 12. ADHD medication

- **Stimulants:** **methylphenidate** raises dopamine and norepinephrine by blocking their reuptake. Works within hours. Side effects: reduced appetite, insomnia, increased heart rate and blood pressure, slowed growth in children (monitor height and weight), potential for misuse.
- **Non-stimulants:** **atomoxetine** is a selective norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor. Takes weeks to work, with no misuse potential. Side effects: nausea, reduced appetite, sleepiness, and rarely liver injury. Watch for suicidal thoughts, especially in children and adolescents.

## 13. Special populations

- **Children and adolescents**: Few drugs are licensed for them; start low and go slow; monitor growth. Watch for suicidal thoughts when starting an antidepressant. Psychological therapy is often tried first.
- **Pregnancy and lactation**: Weigh the risk of untreated illness against the risk to the baby. **Avoid valproate.** Lithium and carbamazepine carry risks. Paroxetine is usually avoided in pregnancy. Benzodiazepines late in pregnancy can affect the newborn. Sertraline is commonly preferred during breastfeeding. Refer early for specialist advice.
- **Medically ill patients**: Adjust for kidney and liver disease; check heart disease and QT prolongation; consider interactions with other drugs.
- **Substance use disorder**: Benzodiazepines can be misused and are dangerous with alcohol and opioids. Bupropion lowers the seizure threshold, a problem in alcohol withdrawal. Treat withdrawal and intoxication first.

## 14. Cultural and ethnic considerations

- Explain the illness and the medicine in language the patient and family understand.
- Ask about **traditional or herbal remedies, faith-based care and substance use such as khat** that may affect treatment or interact with drugs.
- Beliefs about mental illness (for example spiritual causes) affect whether people accept and keep taking medicine. Respect them, and work with the family and, where appropriate, religious or traditional leaders.
- Enzyme activity differs between ethnic groups, so dose needs and side effects can differ.

## 15. Treatment adherence and compliance

- Many patients stop psychiatric drugs, because of side effects, poor insight, cost, a complicated regimen, stigma, or because they feel better.
- Improve adherence with simple regimens (once daily if possible), **psychoeducation** for the patient and family, early review of side effects, long-acting depot injections where suitable, and a good therapeutic relationship.
- Always ask about missed doses without blame.

## 16. Polypharmacy

- **Polypharmacy** is the use of more drugs than are clinically needed.
- It increases side effects, interactions, cost and non-adherence.
- Prefer **one drug at an adequate dose for an adequate time**; review every drug regularly and stop those without a clear benefit.

## 17. Stigma and patient education

- Stigma about "mental" drugs makes people hide treatment or stop it. Explain that these are **medicines for an illness**, like those for hypertension or diabetes.
- Teach the patient and family: **what the drug is for, how long it takes to work, the common side effects, what to do about missed doses, not to stop suddenly, and when to return**. Correct myths such as "all these drugs are addictive".

## Drugs at a glance

- **SSRIs**: Fluoxetine, sertraline, paroxetine, escitalopram — Hyponatraemia, serotonin syndrome, discontinuation
- **SNRIs**: Venlafaxine, duloxetine — Raised blood pressure
- **Atypical antidepressants**: Bupropion, trazodone, mirtazapine, vortioxetine — Seizures (bupropion), sedation and weight gain (mirtazapine)
- **First-generation antipsychotics**: Haloperidol, fluphenazine, chlorpromazine — Extrapyramidal effects, NMS
- **Second-generation antipsychotics**: Olanzapine, risperidone, quetiapine, lurasidone — Metabolic syndrome, prolactin (risperidone)
- **Benzodiazepines**: Diazepam, clonazepam, alprazolam — Dependence, respiratory depression
- **Non-benzodiazepine hypnotics, buspirone**: Zolpidem, zopiclone, buspirone — Short-term use; buspirone is slow
- **Mood stabilisers**: Lithium, valproic acid, carbamazepine, lamotrigine — Lithium toxicity, valproate teratogenicity, lamotrigine rash
- **ADHD drugs**: Methylphenidate, atomoxetine — Appetite and growth, misuse; suicidality (atomoxetine)

## Practice questions

**1. Define psychopharmacology.**
The study of drugs that affect mood, thoughts, behaviour and sensation, and their use in treating psychiatric disorders.

**2. What is the difference between pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics?**
Pharmacokinetics is what the body does to the drug: absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion. Pharmacodynamics is what the drug does to the body: receptor action, dose-response and therapeutic index.

**3. What is the therapeutic index, and which psychiatric drug has a narrow one?**
The gap between the effective dose and the toxic dose. Lithium has a narrow therapeutic index, so blood levels are monitored.

**4. Name the four dopamine pathways and one effect of each.**
Mesolimbic (positive symptoms of psychosis), mesocortical (negative and cognitive symptoms), tuberoinfundibular (blockade causes hyperprolactinaemia) and nigrostriatal (blockade causes extrapyramidal effects).

**5. How long do antidepressants take to work?**
Usually about 2 to 4 weeks, because the brain adapts to the drug over time.

**6. Give two examples each of SSRIs and SNRIs.**
SSRIs: fluoxetine, sertraline, paroxetine, escitalopram. SNRIs: venlafaxine and duloxetine.

**7. What is the main mechanism of action of benzodiazepines?**
They enhance GABA-A receptor action, increasing inhibition. This gives sedation, anxiolysis and an anticonvulsant effect.

**8. List three factors that raise lithium levels.**
Dehydration, NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors and thiazide diuretics (any three), and kidney disease.

**9. Which mood stabiliser should be avoided in women who can become pregnant, and why?**
Valproic acid, because it is teratogenic (neural tube defects).

**10. What is polypharmacy, and why is it a problem?**
Using more drugs than are clinically needed. It raises side effects, interactions, cost and non-adherence.

## Exam-style MCQs with answers and explanations

Try each question before you open the answer.

**1. Which dopamine pathway is responsible for hyperprolactinaemia when D2 receptors are blocked?**

A. Mesolimbic  
B. Mesocortical  
C. Tuberoinfundibular  
D. Nigrostriatal  

**Answer: C.** Dopamine normally inhibits prolactin release from the pituitary through the tuberoinfundibular pathway. Blocking D2 receptors there raises prolactin.

**2. Parkinsonism, dystonia and akathisia after starting haloperidol are caused by blockade in which pathway?**

A. Nigrostriatal  
B. Mesolimbic  
C. Tuberoinfundibular  
D. Mesocortical  

**Answer: A.** The nigrostriatal pathway controls movement. D2 blockade there causes extrapyramidal side effects.

**3. Which drug is a partial agonist at the 5-HT1A receptor and does not cause dependence?**

A. Diazepam  
B. Buspirone  
C. Zolpidem  
D. Alprazolam  

**Answer: B.** Buspirone is a 5-HT1A partial agonist that works slowly and does not cause dependence.

**4. A patient on lithium develops vomiting, diarrhoea, coarse tremor and ataxia after starting ibuprofen. What is the most likely explanation?**

A. Serotonin syndrome  
B. Neuroleptic malignant syndrome  
C. Lithium toxicity  
D. Tardive dyskinesia  

**Answer: C.** NSAIDs reduce the kidney clearance of lithium and raise its level. Vomiting, diarrhoea, coarse tremor and ataxia are signs of toxicity.

**5. Which antipsychotic is most associated with marked weight gain and metabolic syndrome?**

A. Haloperidol  
B. Lurasidone  
C. Olanzapine  
D. Fluphenazine  

**Answer: C.** Olanzapine causes the most weight gain and metabolic disturbance of this group. Monitor weight, glucose and lipids.

**6. Which mood stabiliser needs slow dose titration because of the risk of a serious rash?**

A. Lamotrigine  
B. Carbamazepine  
C. Valproic acid  
D. Lithium  

**Answer: A.** Lamotrigine rash can progress to Stevens-Johnson syndrome, so the dose is raised slowly.

**7. Which antidepressant lowers the seizure threshold and is also used for smoking cessation?**

A. Mirtazapine  
B. Bupropion  
C. Sertraline  
D. Trazodone  

**Answer: B.** Bupropion blocks dopamine and norepinephrine reuptake, lowers the seizure threshold and helps in smoking cessation.

**8. A woman with bipolar disorder who may become pregnant needs a mood stabiliser. Which should be avoided?**

A. Lamotrigine  
B. Valproic acid  
C. Quetiapine  
D. Olanzapine  

**Answer: B.** Valproic acid is teratogenic and is avoided in women who can become pregnant.

**9. Which of the following is the non-stimulant ADHD medicine?**

A. Methylphenidate  
B. Atomoxetine  
C. Lithium  
D. Buspirone  

**Answer: B.** Atomoxetine is a selective norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor. Methylphenidate is the stimulant.

**10. After how many half-lives does a drug reach steady state?**

A. 1  
B. 2  
C. 5  
D. 10  

**Answer: C.** A drug reaches steady state after about 5 half-lives, so the full effect of a dose change shows after that time.
