# Ectopic pregnancy — Year 4 Obstetrics and Gynaecology Notes

*Based on the Department of Obstetrics and Gynaecology lecture (Dr Ritho), reorganised for the ward. Ectopic pregnancy is a life-threatening emergency in any woman of reproductive age with pain, bleeding or collapse: think of it first, exclude it last.*

**The reasoning chain:** a woman of childbearing age with a missed period, bleeding and lower abdominal pain → is she stable or in shock → confirm pregnancy → find where it is (ultrasound plus hCG) → treat by stability and size: resuscitate and operate, or give methotrexate, or watch.

## 1. Definition and size of the problem

An **ectopic pregnancy** is one in which the fertilised ovum **implants and develops outside the normal endometrial cavity**. It occurs in about **1 in 80 spontaneously conceived pregnancies**. It remains a leading cause of **maternal death in the first trimester**.

## 2. Where it implants

| Site | Share |
|---|---|
| **Fallopian tube** (ampulla commonest, then isthmus, fimbria, interstitial) | **about 98%** |
| Ovary | about 0.5% |
| Abdominal and intraligamentous | rare |
| Cervix | about 0.4% |
| **Cornual / interstitial** | rare but bleeds torrentially |
| Caesarean scar | increasing with caesarean rates |

## 3. Risk factors: ask about each one

Anything that damages the tube or slows the egg's passage:

- **Previous pelvic inflammatory disease** (chlamydia and gonorrhoea), the commonest cause
- **Previous ectopic pregnancy**
- **Tubal surgery**, **tubal ligation** (a pregnancy after sterilisation is ectopic until proved otherwise), tubal reconstructive surgery
- **Contraceptive failure**, especially an **IUD** (it prevents womb pregnancies better than tubal ones) and progestogen-only pills
- **Infertility** and **assisted reproduction (IVF)**, especially with damaged tubes
- Previous **induced abortion** and **tubal endometriosis**
- **Smoking**

Roughly half of women with an ectopic pregnancy have **no risk factor**, so the absence of one never excludes it.

## 4. Natural history of a tubal pregnancy

The pregnancy ends in one of three ways: **tubal rupture** (heavy bleeding into the abdomen), **tubal abortion** into the peritoneal cavity (slower bleeding), or **spontaneous resolution** with reabsorption.

## 5. History: the classic triad

**Missed period (amenorrhoea of 6 to 8 weeks) + vaginal bleeding + lower abdominal pain.**

- The **bleeding** is usually **scanty and dark**, from the endometrium shedding because the pregnancy makes too little hormone to support it.
- The **pain** starts as a one-sided ache and may become sudden and severe on rupture. **Shoulder-tip pain** means blood under the diaphragm.
- Dizziness, fainting and collapse suggest rupture.
- Ask about every risk factor above and about contraception.

## 6. Examination

**General:** vital signs for **hypovolaemia and blood loss**. In a ruptured ectopic the patient may lie quietly, sweating and pale, with **severe pallor out of proportion to the visible bleeding**, a rapid feeble pulse, low blood pressure and cold clammy hands. Young women can keep their blood pressure until very late, so **tachycardia is the early sign**.

**Abdomen:** tense, tender lower abdomen, **guarding and rebound** if ruptured, **shifting dullness** from free blood, distended bowels. There is usually no mass.

**Pelvic (done gently):**

- **Cervical motion tenderness** ("chandelier sign") and **extreme tenderness in the fornices** (about 75%).
- A **tender adnexal mass**, a slightly enlarged **globular** uterus, a **full pouch of Douglas** and blanched vaginal mucosa.
- **Do not repeat bimanual examinations**: they can rupture a stable ectopic pregnancy.

## 7. Differential diagnosis

| Gynaecological | Non-gynaecological |
|---|---|
| Threatened, inevitable or incomplete miscarriage | Acute appendicitis |
| Ruptured corpus luteum cyst | Diverticulitis |
| Ovarian cyst accident (torsion, rupture) | Pyelonephritis, ureteric colic |
| Acute pelvic inflammatory disease | Perforated peptic ulcer |
| Red degeneration of a fibroid | Gastroenteritis |

## 8. Investigate with purpose

| Test | The question it answers |
|---|---|
| **Urine pregnancy test (positive)** | Is she pregnant? (Do this in every woman of reproductive age with abdominal pain.) |
| **Serum β-hCG** | Is the level rising normally? In a healthy early pregnancy it **roughly doubles every 48 hours**. A slow rise or plateau suggests ectopic or failing pregnancy |
| **Transvaginal ultrasound** | Is there a pregnancy in the uterus? **No intrauterine pregnancy** with a positive test, an **adnexal mass** (the "**blob sign**" or "**bagel sign**"), a "**double ring**" appearance, **free fluid in the pouch of Douglas** |
| **Full blood count, group and crossmatch, clotting screen** | How much blood has been lost, and is blood ready? |
| **Rhesus group** | **Give anti-D immunoglobulin to rhesus-negative women** |

**The discriminatory zone:** when the hCG is above about **1,500 to 2,000 IU/L**, a normal uterine pregnancy should be visible on transvaginal ultrasound. If nothing is seen in the uterus at that level, **ectopic pregnancy is likely**. Below that level, repeat the hCG in 48 hours.

![The "blob sign" of an ectopic pregnancy on transvaginal ultrasound. Mikael Häggström, CC0, Wikimedia Commons](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f0/Blob_sign_of_ectopic_pregnancy_%28hy%29.png/960px-Blob_sign_of_ectopic_pregnancy_%28hy%29.png)

**Where results can mislead:** a **negative urine test does not exclude a very early ectopic pregnancy** (rare but possible); a **"pseudosac"** in the uterus can fool you into thinking the pregnancy is intrauterine; hCG can be **lower than expected in an ectopic pregnancy and still rupture**. When in doubt, **keep the woman under review and do not discharge her without clear return advice.**

## 9. Management

### The woman is unstable or the ectopic has ruptured

This is **an emergency**:

1. **ABC.** Call for senior help, **two large-bore IV cannulae**, **crossmatch blood**, and give **IV fluids and blood**. Give **oxygen**.
2. **Emergency surgery.** In an **unstable** woman use **laparotomy** (the fastest way to reach and clamp the bleeding point). Do not delay surgery for further tests.
3. **Salpingectomy** (remove the tube) is usually done in the emergency.

### The woman is stable

Choose between **expectant, medical or surgical** care:

**Expectant** (watching): a small, falling hCG, no pain, and reliable follow-up.

**Medical: methotrexate** (a folate antagonist that stops the trophoblast dividing). Criteria:

- **hCG below about 5,000 IU/L** (some units use 3,000)
- **No yolk sac or fetal heart** on ultrasound, adnexal mass **under 3.5 cm**, no free fluid in the pouch of Douglas
- **Haemodynamically stable**, and **able to attend follow-up** (hCG on days 4 and 7, then weekly until negative)
- **No contraindications:** breastfeeding, immunodeficiency, peptic ulcer, active lung disease, kidney or liver impairment, a **viable intrauterine pregnancy**, methotrexate allergy

**Advantages:** avoids surgery and anaesthesia and **preserves the tube and fertility**. **Side effects:** abdominal pain in the first week (often "separation pain"; it can also be a rupture, so it needs review), nausea, stomatitis, conjunctivitis, dermatitis, hair loss, gastritis, pneumonitis, raised liver enzymes and bone marrow suppression. **Advise her to avoid NSAIDs, folic acid supplements, alcohol, sun, and sexual intercourse during treatment, and to avoid pregnancy for 3 months.**

**Surgical:** **laparoscopy** is the gold standard in a stable woman (less pain, shorter stay, faster recovery).

| Operation | When |
|---|---|
| **Salpingectomy** (remove the whole tube) | The default; the other tube is healthy; the tube is badly damaged or bleeding; the woman has completed her family |
| **Salpingotomy / salpingostomy** (open the tube, remove the pregnancy, leave the tube) | The other tube is damaged and she wants to conceive; **needs follow-up hCG** because trophoblast can persist |
| **Partial salpingectomy** | Rarely used |

## 10. Follow-up and counselling

- **Anti-D** to rhesus-negative women.
- **Follow hCG to zero** after any conservative treatment.
- **Fertility:** about **two-thirds** of women conceive again, but the **risk of another ectopic pregnancy rises** (about 10–15%). Advise an **early scan in the next pregnancy** (at 5 to 6 weeks).
- Treat **chlamydia** and trace partners. Counsel about contraception and the loss; offer psychological support.

## Practice questions

**1.** The commonest site of ectopic pregnancy is:
A. Ovary · B. Cervix · **C. Fallopian tube (ampulla)** · D. Abdominal cavity · E. Caesarean scar
*Answer: C. About 98% are tubal.*

**2.** A woman has a missed period, a positive pregnancy test, pelvic pain and no intrauterine pregnancy on ultrasound with hCG 4,000 IU/L. She is stable. Which treatment suits her best if she meets the other criteria?
A. Observation only · **B. Methotrexate** · C. Misoprostol · D. Laparotomy · E. Reassurance
*Answer: B.*

**3.** A woman with an ectopic pregnancy is rhesus negative. She needs:
A. Nothing · B. Iron · **C. Anti-D immunoglobulin** · D. Folic acid · E. Antibiotics
*Answer: C.*

**4.** Which finding is the most reliable sign of a ruptured ectopic pregnancy?
A. Scanty dark vaginal bleeding · **B. Shock with a tense tender lower abdomen and free fluid** · C. Positive pregnancy test · D. Breast tenderness · E. Nausea
*Answer: B.*

**5.** Which woman is at the HIGHEST risk of an ectopic pregnancy?
A. A woman on combined pills · **B. A woman with a previous ectopic pregnancy and a history of pelvic inflammatory disease** · C. A woman who has had three vaginal deliveries · D. A woman using condoms · E. A nulliparous woman
*Answer: B.*
