# Hepatitis B and the approach to hepatitis — Year 4 Internal Medicine (Gastroenterology) Notes

*Clinical reasoning notes. Treatment thresholds change with new guidance: check your current national guidelines before treating.*

**The reasoning chain:** what is the pattern of liver injury → what is the cause (virus, drug, autoimmune, alcohol) → which virus and which phase → does this patient need treatment → what could go wrong later (cirrhosis, liver cancer).

## 1. Presentation

Jaundice, fatigue, right upper quadrant pain, nausea and vomiting, fever, dark urine and pale stools. Many patients, especially with chronic hepatitis B, have **no symptoms** until the liver is badly scarred.

![Scleral icterus (yellow eyes), the commonest sign of jaundice. Sheila J. Toro, CC BY 4.0](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9c/Scleral_Icterus.jpg/960px-Scleral_Icterus.jpg)

## 2. Start with the pattern: liver function tests

| Pattern | What is raised | Think of |
|---|---|---|
| **Hepatocellular** | **ALT and AST** high | Viral hepatitis, drugs, alcohol, autoimmune hepatitis |
| **Cholestatic** | **ALP and GGT** high | Gallstones in the bile duct, tumour, primary biliary cholangitis |
| **Synthetic function** | **Low albumin, prolonged INR** | How badly the liver is failing (more serious than ALT) |

## 3. Find the cause

**Viral:** hepatitis **A, B, C and E**.

**Drugs and toxins:** **anti-TB drugs (isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide)**, co-amoxiclav, **paracetamol overdose**, **herbal remedies** and **alcohol**. Always take a drug history, including traditional medicines.

**Autoimmune:** **ANA**, **anti-smooth muscle antibody** and **anti-LKM1** for autoimmune hepatitis; **antimitochondrial antibody** for primary biliary cholangitis.

**Always do:** a toxicology and drug review, and **viral screening**.

## 4. Hepatitis B serology: read it like a story

| Marker | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| **HBsAg** | The patient has the virus now (acute or chronic). Positive for more than 6 months means chronic infection |
| **Anti-HBc IgM** | **Acute** (recent) infection |
| **Anti-HBc IgG** | Past or chronic infection |
| **Anti-HBs** | **Immunity**: from vaccination (anti-HBs alone) or after recovery (with anti-HBc IgG) |
| **HBeAg** | The virus is actively replicating: high infectivity |
| **HBV DNA (viral load)** | How much virus there is: the key number for the decision to treat |

![Hepatitis B virus particles under the electron microscope. Public domain](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/55/Hepatitis_B_virus_01.jpg/960px-Hepatitis_B_virus_01.jpg)

## 5. Chronic hepatitis B: who needs treatment?

Classic thresholds (a plain-language rule to remember):

- **HBeAg positive** with **HBV DNA above 20,000 IU/mL** and a raised ALT or significant fibrosis
- **HBeAg negative** with **HBV DNA above 2,000 IU/mL** and a raised ALT or significant fibrosis
- **Cirrhosis:** treat if the DNA is detectable at all

Newer WHO guidance has widened who is eligible for treatment, so check what is current in your country.

## 6. Drugs: why this one and not that one

| Drug | Role | The point to remember |
|---|---|---|
| **Tenofovir** | First line | Very high barrier to resistance; check kidney function and bone health |
| **Entecavir** | First line | High barrier to resistance |
| **Lamivudine** | Cheap but no longer first line | Resistance develops through the **YMDD mutation** in the viral reverse transcriptase |
| **Pegylated interferon-α** | A finite course in selected patients | Many side effects; not for cirrhosis with decompensation |
| **Ribavirin** | **Hepatitis C**, not hepatitis B | A classic exam trap: do not confuse the two viruses' drugs |

## 7. Supportive care, and why

- **Vomiting:** antiemetics (such as metoclopramide or ondansetron) and fluids.
- **Fever or pain:** **paracetamol is acceptable at a reduced dose**; the liver clears it more slowly.
- **Avoid NSAIDs such as diclofenac:** low clotting factors raise the risk of bleeding, they can bleed the stomach, and they injure the kidneys.
- **Avoid aspirin:** bleeding, and **Reye's syndrome in children**.
- Stop alcohol and any suspected hepatotoxic drug.

## 8. Complications and surveillance

**Cirrhosis, liver failure and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).** In chronic hepatitis B and in cirrhosis, screen for HCC with **ultrasound, with or without AFP, every 6 months**. A suspicious lesion is characterised with **triple-phase CT of the abdomen** (arterial enhancement then washout is typical).

## 9. Prevention

**Vaccinate** all infants and health workers, give **hepatitis B immunoglobulin plus vaccine** to babies of infected mothers, screen blood and contacts, and counsel on safe sex and needles.

## Practice questions

**1.** HBsAg negative, anti-HBs positive, anti-HBc negative. The patient is:
A. Acutely infected · B. Chronically infected · C. A carrier · **D. Immune through vaccination** · E. Immune after natural infection
*Answer: D. Anti-HBs alone means vaccine immunity.*

**2.** The mutation that makes hepatitis B resistant to lamivudine is:
A. T790M · B. V600E · **C. YMDD** · D. BRCA1 · E. K103N
*Answer: C.*

**3.** Which drug is used for hepatitis C but not hepatitis B?
A. Tenofovir · **B. Ribavirin** · C. Entecavir · D. Lamivudine · E. Pegylated interferon-α is used for neither
*Answer: B.*

**4.** The best way to screen a patient with cirrhosis for hepatocellular carcinoma is:
**A. Ultrasound with or without AFP every 6 months** · B. A liver biopsy every year · C. Chest X-ray · D. ALT every month · E. No screening is needed
*Answer: A.*
