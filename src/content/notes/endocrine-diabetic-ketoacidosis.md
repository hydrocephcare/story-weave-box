# Diabetic ketoacidosis and hyperglycaemic emergencies — Year 4 Internal Medicine (Endocrinology) Notes

*Clinical reasoning notes. Fluid, insulin and potassium regimens differ between hospitals: follow your local protocol and monitor closely.*

**The reasoning chain:** recognise the emergency → confirm the three diagnostic features → find the trigger → correct fluid first, then insulin, with potassium watched at every step → monitor for complications → prevent the next episode.

## 1. Definition: three things must be present

**Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)** needs all three:

1. **Hyperglycaemia:** blood glucose above about **11 mmol/L (200 mg/dL)**, or known diabetes (it can be lower in "euglycaemic" DKA, which is seen with SGLT2 inhibitors and in pregnancy).
2. **Ketosis:** blood ketones **3 mmol/L or more**, or **2+ or more on urine dipstick**.
3. **Acidosis:** **pH below 7.3 or bicarbonate below 15 mmol/L**.

**Severity (by pH):** mild 7.25 to 7.30, moderate 7.00 to 7.24, **severe below 7.00**, together with drowsiness or coma.

**Hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state (HHS)** is the other emergency: very high glucose (above about 30 mmol/L), **little or no ketosis**, severe dehydration and high osmolality, usually in older type 2 patients, with a gradual onset over days.

## 2. Why it happens

Without insulin the body cannot use glucose, so it **breaks down fat into ketone acids** (acetoacetate, β-hydroxybutyrate). The glucose spills into the urine and pulls water and electrolytes with it (**osmotic diuresis**), so the patient is **dry and short of potassium**, even if the blood potassium looks normal or high.

## 3. Find the trigger (the "I"s)

- **I**nfection (the commonest: pneumonia, urinary infection, sepsis, malaria, TB, **HIV**)
- **I**nsulin missed or stopped (including running out, or a faulty pen)
- **I**nfarction (a silent heart attack), **I**njury or surgery, **I**ntercurrent illness such as pancreatitis, stroke
- **I**nitial presentation of type 1 diabetes (a child with weight loss, thirst and breathing fast)
- **I**ncreased insulin needs: pregnancy, steroids
- **I**atrogenic drugs: steroids, thiazides, antipsychotics, SGLT2 inhibitors

## 4. Presentation

Polyuria, polydipsia, weight loss, **nausea, vomiting and abdominal pain** (can mimic an acute abdomen), **deep sighing "Kussmaul" breathing**, a **fruity (acetone) smell**, dehydration, tachycardia, low blood pressure, drowsiness or coma.

## 5. Investigate with purpose

| Test | The question it answers |
|---|---|
| **Capillary glucose and blood or urine ketones** | Is it DKA? Quick to do at the bedside |
| **Blood gas (arterial or venous)** | How acidotic? pH and bicarbonate stage the severity |
| **Urea, creatinine, electrolytes** | Kidney function; **potassium** decides the insulin plan; sodium (correct it for the glucose) |
| **Anion gap** | A raised gap confirms a ketoacid cause: gap = Na − (Cl + HCO₃) |
| **Full haemogram, CRP, blood cultures, urine culture, malaria test, chest X-ray** | Is there infection as the trigger? |
| **ECG** | Is there a silent infarct? What do the potassium changes look like? |
| **HbA1c** | How long has glucose been high? New diabetes or poorly controlled? |
| **Serum amylase or lipase** | Pancreatitis can cause DKA, and DKA can raise amylase |

**Where results can mislead:** the **serum potassium can be normal or high at presentation** although the body is very short of potassium (acidosis moves potassium out of the cells). It **falls fast once insulin starts**. The **measured sodium is falsely low** with very high glucose. The abdominal pain of DKA often **settles once treated**: do not rush to surgery.

## 6. Management: fluid first, then insulin, watch potassium

1. **Fluids:** rapid **isotonic saline** to restore circulation, then continue to correct the deficit over 24 to 48 hours. This alone lowers glucose and starts to clear ketones.
2. **Potassium (before insulin):** if potassium is **below 3.3 mmol/L**, replace it and **delay insulin** (insulin can drive potassium dangerously low and cause arrhythmias). If it is **above 5.3 mmol/L**, give no potassium and recheck. In between, add potassium to the fluids.
3. **Insulin:** a **fixed-rate IV insulin infusion** until ketones clear and the acidosis resolves. When glucose falls below about 14 mmol/L, **add dextrose** to the fluids and continue insulin until ketosis has cleared. Do not stop insulin because the glucose looks normal.
4. **Treat the trigger:** antibiotics for infection, antimalarials for malaria, and so on.
5. **Bicarbonate** is generally **not** given, except in very severe acidosis with shock.
6. **Monitor:** glucose hourly, ketones and gas every 2 hours, potassium regularly, urine output, mental state.

## 7. Complications to watch for

**Hypokalaemia and hypoglycaemia** from treatment, **cerebral oedema** (mainly in children, with headache, a falling level of consciousness and bradycardia: reduce the rate of fluids and give mannitol), **pulmonary oedema** from too much fluid, thromboembolism, aspiration, sepsis, acute kidney injury.

## 8. Before discharge

Restart **subcutaneous insulin** and overlap it with the infusion for at least 30 to 60 minutes. Educate on **sick-day rules** (never stop insulin, check ketones, drink fluids, seek help early), injection technique and access to insulin and supplies.

## Practice questions

**1.** A 19-year-old is drowsy with deep rapid breathing, glucose 28 mmol/L, ketones 5 mmol/L and pH 7.1. The first priority is:
A. Insulin bolus · **B. Rapid IV isotonic saline** · C. Sodium bicarbonate · D. Oral fluids · E. Subcutaneous insulin
*Answer: B. Fluids come first, then insulin once the potassium is known.*

**2.** The potassium is 2.9 mmol/L before treatment. The correct step is:
A. Start insulin at once · **B. Replace potassium and delay insulin** · C. Give bicarbonate · D. Stop fluids · E. Give no potassium
*Answer: B. Insulin would drive the potassium lower and risk arrhythmia.*

**3.** Which feature best separates HHS from DKA?
A. Dehydration · **B. Very high glucose with little or no ketosis** · C. Infection as a trigger · D. Altered consciousness · E. Hypokalaemia
*Answer: B.*

**4.** The most feared complication of DKA treatment in children is:
A. Hyperkalaemia · B. Hypernatraemia · **C. Cerebral oedema** · D. Pancreatitis · E. Optic neuritis
*Answer: C.*
