# Dementia and delirium  
*After studying this note you will be able to differentiate dementia from delirium, list the common local aetiologies, perform a focused mental status exam, interpret basic investigations and outline immediate and definitive management plans – remembering to verify doses and local protocols against the Kenya MoH guideline and your hospital formulary.*



## Definition  
**Dementia** – a progressive, usually irreversible decline in multiple cognitive domains (memory, language, executive function, visuospatial skills) that interferes with independence in daily activities and persists for ≥ 6 months.  

**Delirium** – an acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness, with a change in cognition (e.g., memory, disorientation) that develops over a short period (hours–days) and tends to fluctuate during the day.

## Anatomy and physiology you need  
- **Cerebral cortex** – especially temporal (memory) and frontal (executive) lobes; loss of neuronal networks underlies dementia.  
- **Reticular activating system (RAS)** – maintains arousal; disruption produces the inattention of delirium.  
- **Neurotransmitter balance** – acetylcholine deficiency and dopaminergic excess are central to delirium; amyloid‑β deposition, tau tangles, and loss of cholinergic neurons are key in Alzheimer’s dementia.  
- **Blood‑brain barrier (BBB)** – systemic inflammation or infection can increase permeability, precipitating delirium.  

## Causes and risk factors  

| Category | Common local aetiologies | Key risk factors |
|---|---|---|
| Neurodegenerative | Alzheimer’s disease, vascular dementia, Lewy‑body dementia, frontotemporal dementia | Age > 65, family history, hypertension, diabetes |
| Infectious | HIV‑associated neurocognitive disorder, tuberculous meningitis, cerebral malaria, neurosyphilis, bacterial meningitis | HIV positivity, TB exposure, recent malaria, immunosuppression |
| Metabolic / Toxic | Vitamin B12 deficiency, folate deficiency, chronic alcohol, lead exposure, anticholinergic burden | Poor nutrition, alcoholism, occupational exposure |
| Vascular / Cardiac | Multi‑infarct dementia, cardio‑embolic stroke, hypertension, atrial fibrillation | Uncontrolled BP, AF, prior stroke |
| Hematologic / Genetic | Sickle‑cell disease (cerebral infarcts), sickle‑cell trait, hereditary ataxias | Sickle‑cell disease, consanguinity |
| Autoimmune / Inflammatory | Systemic lupus erythematosus, vasculitis, rheumatic fever (Sydenham chorea) | Autoimmune disease, recent streptococcal infection |
| Medication‑induced | Benzodiazepines, anticholinergics, opioids, corticosteroids | Polypharmacy, high‑risk drugs |

## Pathophysiology  

**Dementia**  
Neuronal loss → synaptic dysfunction → cortical atrophy → progressive cognitive decline  

**Delirium**  
Systemic insult (infection, drugs, metabolic derangement) → inflammatory cytokines ↑ → BBB permeability ↑ → neurotransmitter imbalance (acetylcholine ↓, dopamine ↑) → impaired RAS function → fluctuating attention and cognition

## Clinical features  

| Domain | Key questions / findings | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Onset** | “When did the problem start?” “Was the change sudden (hours‑days) or gradual (months‑years)?” | Sudden onset favours delirium; insidious onset points to dementia. |
| **Course** | Fluctuation of symptoms (better in the morning, worse at night)? | Fluctuating consciousness is classic for delirium; dementia is usually progressive but may have day‑to‑day variability in later stages. |
| **Memory** | Recent memory loss (forgetting recent conversations, appointments)? Remote memory intact? | Early episodic memory loss is typical of Alzheimer’s dementia; delirium may have global attention deficit with impaired recall of recent events. |
| **Attention** | Ability to follow a conversation, count backwards, spell “world” backwards? | Impaired sustained attention is a core feature of delirium; attention is relatively preserved early in most dementias. |
| **Orientation** | “What is the date? Where are you? Who are you?” | Disorientation to time/place is common in delirium; in dementia it appears later. |
| **Behaviour / psychosis** | Hallucinations, delusions, agitation, aggression, sleep‑wake reversal? | Visual hallucinations are frequent in Lewy body dementia and delirium; acute agitation suggests delirium. |
| **Functional decline** | Ability to perform ADLs (bathing, dressing, cooking) before and now? | Decline in instrumental ADLs precedes basic ADLs in dementia; delirium can cause abrupt loss of all functions. |
| **Risk exposures** | Recent infection, fever, new medication, surgery, electrolyte imbalance, alcohol withdrawal, substance use, HIV status, TB treatment, malaria, sickle‑cell crisis? | Identifiable precipitants point to delirium; chronic infections (TB, HIV) can also cause dementia‑type syndromes. |
| **Past medical history** | Vascular disease, hypertension, diabetes, stroke, head injury, rheumatic fever (carditis), neuro‑degenerative disease in family? | Vascular risk factors predispose to vascular dementia; rheumatic fever can cause Sydenham chorea and later cognitive issues. |
| **Medication review** | Anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, opioids, steroids, antipsychotics, HIV antiretrovirals, anti‑TB drugs? | Many drugs are delirium precipitants; some (e.g., cholinesterase inhibitors) are used in dementia. |
| **Social / environmental** | Recent relocation, sensory deprivation, lack of caregiver support? | Environmental stressors precipitate delirium; social isolation worsens dementia progression. |
| **Physical symptoms** | Fever, headache, neck stiffness, focal weakness, seizures? | Suggests infectious or metabolic cause of delirium; focal deficits hint at stroke or space‑occupying lesion. |

**Examination findings**  

- **General** – fever, tachycardia, dehydration, signs of infection (e.g., pulmonary crackles, urinary tenderness). Fever or systemic illness supports delirium.  
- **Neurological** – level of consciousness (Glasgow), attention tests (e.g., digit span), orientation, language (naming, repetition), praxis, gait, reflexes, visual field testing. Fluctuating consciousness, impaired attention, disorganized thinking = delirium; focal deficits, extrapyramidal signs, primitive reflexes suggest specific dementias (vascular, Lewy body, Huntington).  
- **Cranial nerves** – visual hallucinations may accompany preserved visual fields (Lewy body) vs. visual field loss (stroke).  
- **Motor** – rigidity, tremor, myoclonus (possible Creutzfeldt‑Jakob, HIV‑associated neurocognitive disorder).  
- **Sensory** – hearing/vision impairment can masquerade as cognitive decline; correcting deficits may improve performance.

## Differential diagnosis  

| Condition | Points to it | How it separates |
|---|---|---|
| Delirium | Acute onset, fluctuating course, impaired attention, recent precipitant (infection, drugs) | Reversible, altered consciousness, often hyperactive or hypoactive; improves with treatment of cause |
| Alzheimer’s disease | Insidious memory loss, preserved attention early, ApoE4 risk, no focal deficits | Progressive, no fluctuation, typical neuroimaging pattern (hippocampal atrophy) |
| Vascular dementia | Stepwise decline, focal neurological signs, hypertension, diabetes | Imaging shows multiple infarcts; abrupt step changes after strokes |
| Lewy body dementia | Visual hallucinations, parkinsonism, REM sleep behavior disorder, fluctuating cognition | Sensitivity to antipsychotics, prominent autonomic dysfunction |
| Frontotemporal dementia | Early personality/behaviour change, language impairment, relative memory preservation | Age <65 common, focal frontal/temporal atrophy |
| HIV‑associated neurocognitive disorder | HIV positive, CD4 low, opportunistic infections, subcortical slowing | Improves with ART; CSF may show pleocytosis |
| TB meningitis / CNS TB | Fever, neck stiffness, focal deficits, TB exposure, immunosuppression | CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis, basal meningeal enhancement on MRI |
| Cerebral malaria | Recent travel to endemic area, fever, seizures, anemia | Peripheral smear positive for Plasmodium falciparum |
| Sickle‑cell cerebral infarcts | Sickle‑cell disease, recurrent pain crises, focal deficits | MRI shows silent infarcts; history of vaso‑occlusive events |
| Metabolic encephalopathy (e.g., uremia, hepatic) | Organ failure, electrolyte disturbances, toxin exposure | Reversible with correction of metabolic derangement |
| Normal pressure hydrocephalus | Gait disturbance, urinary incontinence, dementia triad | Enlarged ventricles on imaging, CSF tap test improves gait |

## Investigations  

| Test | What it answers | Meaning of result |
|---|---|---|
| Full blood count, ESR/CRP | Infection, anemia, inflammation | Elevated CRP/ESR → possible infection precipitating delirium |
| Electrolytes, renal & liver panel, glucose | Metabolic derangements | Hyponatremia, hyperglycemia, hepatic failure → reversible delirium causes |
| Thyroid function (TSH, free T4) | Thyroid dysfunction | Hypo‑ or hyper‑thyroidism can mimic cognitive decline |
| HIV serology + CD4 count + viral load | HIV status, immune competence | Low CD4 with high viral load → risk for HIV‑associated dementia |
| TB work‑up (sputum GeneXpert, chest X‑ray) | Active TB | Positive GeneXpert → possible CNS TB contribution |
| Malaria rapid test / thick smear | Recent malaria infection | Positive → consider cerebral malaria if neurologic signs |
| Serum vitamin B12, folate, thiamine | Nutritional deficiencies | Low B12 → reversible cognitive impairment |
| Urinalysis & culture | Urinary infection | Significant bacteriuria → common delirium trigger |
| Brain CT (non‑contrast) | Structural lesions, bleed, hydrocephalus | Acute bleed → delirium; cortical atrophy → dementia |
| Brain MRI (if available) | Detailed parenchymal changes, infarcts, white‑matter disease | Multiple infarcts → vascular dementia; hippocampal atrophy → Alzheimer’s |
| EEG | Diffuse slowing vs. epileptiform activity | Diffuse slowing → metabolic or delirium; periodic sharp waves → CJD |
| CSF analysis (cell count, protein, glucose, cultures, PCR for TB, cryptococcus) | CNS infection, inflammatory disease | Lymphocytic pleocytosis + low glucose → TB meningitis |
| Neuropsychological testing (MMSE, MoCA) | Baseline cognitive profile, severity | Scores <24 suggest moderate‑severe dementia; rapid drop suggests delirium |

## Diagnosis and severity  

1. **Determine the primary syndrome**  
   - **Delirium**: meets DSM‑5 criteria – acute onset, fluctuating course, impaired attention, and either disorganized thinking or altered level of consciousness, with evidence of a physiological trigger.  
   - **Dementia**: chronic progressive decline in two or more cognitive domains (memory, executive, language, visuospatial, praxis) that interferes with independence in daily life, not better explained by delirium.  

2. **Classify type of dementia** using history, exam, and imaging (Alzheimer’s, vascular, Lewy body, frontotemporal, HIV‑associated, etc.).  

3. **Severity grading** (use validated scales)  
   - **Mild**: MMSE 21‑26, able to perform most ADLs, some instrumental ADL loss.  
   - **Moderate**: MMSE 10‑20, needs assistance with instrumental ADLs, some basic ADL difficulty.  
   - **Severe**: MMSE <10, dependent for basic ADLs, possible incontinence, bedridden.  

4. **Delirium severity** can be staged with the Confusion Assessment Method (CAM) severity score or the Delirium Rating Scale – higher scores indicate more severe agitation, disorientation, and physiological disturbance.  

5. **Document precipitating factors** (infection, drugs, metabolic derangement) and underlying dementia subtype; this guides immediate versus long‑term management.

## Management  

**Immediate stabilisation**  
- **Airway, Breathing, Circulation** – ensure patient is not hypoxic or hypotensive; treat fever, pain, dehydration promptly.  
- **Identify and treat precipitants** – infection (urinary, respiratory, TB, malaria), metabolic derangements (electrolytes, glucose), drug toxicity or withdrawal (benzodiazepines, anticholinergics, opioids).  
- **Environment** – quiet, well‑lit room, clocks/calendars, orientation cues; remove restraints if possible.  
- **Safety** – low‑lying bed, padded rails, bedside commode, sitter if agitation threatens self‑harm or falls.  

**Definitive treatment**  

| Condition | First‑line pharmacologic class | Rationale | Non‑pharmacologic adjunct |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alzheimer’s disease | Acetylcholinesterase inhibitor (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) | Improves cortical cholinergic transmission, modestly slows cognitive decline | Cognitive stimulation, regular exercise, caregiver education |
| Vascular dementia | Control of vascular risk (antihypertensives, statins, antiplatelets) | Prevents further infarcts | Physical therapy, gait training |
| Lewy‑body dementia | Antipsychotic avoidance; consider low‑dose atypical only if severe psychosis | High sensitivity to neuroleptics → severe parkinsonism | Structured routine, visual cues |
| Frontotemporal dementia | No disease‑modifying drugs; manage behaviour with selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) for disinhibition | Targets serotonergic dysregulation | Speech therapy, occupational therapy |
| Delirium (hyperactive) | Low‑dose antipsychotic (haloperidol or atypical) only after non‑pharm measures fail | Reduces agitation, prevents injury | Reorientation, sleep‑wake cycle optimisation, early mobilisation |
| Delirium (hypoactive) | Treat underlying cause; consider low‑dose stimulant (e.g., methylphenidate) if severe lethargy | Improves alertness without excessive sedation | Mobilise, ensure adequate nutrition & hydration |

**Monitoring & follow‑up**  
- Re‑assess mental status every 4–6 h in acute delirium (CAM or 4AT).  
- Daily vitals, fluid balance, pain score, and review of medication list for anticholinergic load.  
- After discharge, schedule outpatient review at 4–6 weeks, then 3‑monthly for progressive dementias.  
- Use standardized scales (MMSE, MoCA, CDR) to track progression.  

**Contra‑indications & safety**  
- Avoid high‑potency anticholinergics, benzodiazepines (except for alcohol/benzodiazepine withdrawal), and typical antipsychotics in Lewy‑body disease.  
- Do not use physical restraints as first resort; they increase mortality.  
- Caution with haloperidol in patients with QT prolongation or Parkinsonian features.

## Complications and prognosis  

- **Falls & fractures** – due to gait instability, orthostatic hypotension, or agitation.  
- **Pressure ulcers** – especially in bedridden delirium.  
- **Infections** – aspiration pneumonia, urinary tract infection from catheter use.  
- **Nutritional deficiency** – poor intake, dysphagia.  
- **Accelerated cognitive decline** – each episode of delirium adds to long‑term impairment.  
- **Institutionalisation** – up to 50 % of moderate‑to‑severe dementia patients require nursing home care within 5 years.  
- **Mortality** – delirium doubles in‑hospital mortality; advanced dementias carry a median survival of 3–7 years depending on aetiology (vascular < Alzheimer’s).

## High‑yield exam points  

- **Look‑alike conditions**: delirium vs. depression (pseudodementia), vs. normal ageing, vs. dementia with superimposed delirium.  
- **Commonest wrong first step**: ordering a CT scan before treating reversible causes of delirium.  
- **Examiner bedside question**: “What three environmental modifications would you implement right now for this confused patient?” (Answer: orienting clock, reduce noise, ensure adequate lighting).  
- **Key differentiator**: fluctuating consciousness and inattention → delirium; insidious, progressive loss of memory → dementia.  
- **Drug safety**: “Why is haloperidol preferred over olanzapine in a patient with Parkinsonism?” (Answer: lower anticholinergic burden, less worsening of motor symptoms).

## Clinical case  

**Case**: A 68‑year‑old man with known hypertension and type 2 diabetes is brought from the ward after a 2‑day history of increasing confusion, visual hallucinations, and a fever of 38.5 °C. He is disoriented to time, has a tremulous hand, and a urinary catheter in situ.  

**Questions**  

1. **What is the most likely diagnosis?**  
   *Answer*: Hyperactive delirium secondary to a urinary tract infection.  

2. **List the immediate three management steps.**  
   *Answer*: (1) Obtain vitals, start IV fluids and treat fever; (2) Send urine for microscopy, culture and start empiric antibiotics covering Gram‑negative organisms; (3) Reorient the patient, provide a calm environment, and discontinue any anticholinergic drugs.  

3. **Which long‑term medication should be reviewed to prevent recurrence?**  
   *Answer*: Review and stop any anticholinergic or sedative agents (e.g., diphenhydramine, benzodiazepines) and assess the need for the urinary catheter.

## Practice questions  

### Question 1  
A 75‑year‑old woman with known Alzheimer’s disease is brought from her home because she has become increasingly agitated, pulling at her IV line and shouting. Her temperature is 37 °C, BP 130/80 mmHg, pulse 88 bpm. What is the most appropriate next step?  

A. Give intramuscular haloperidol immediately  
B. Remove the IV line and call a sitter  
C. Perform a rapid bedside assessment for reversible causes (infection, pain, constipation)  
D. Transfer to ICU for sedation  

**Answer**: C. Perform a rapid bedside assessment for reversible causes (infection, pain, constipation)  
**Explanation**: Delirium is often precipitated; treating the underlying trigger is priority before antipsychotics.

### Question 2  
A 60‑year‑old man with sickle cell disease presents with confusion, low grade fever, and a new focal seizure. MRI shows multiple small cortical infarcts. Which dementia subtype is most likely?  

A. Alzheimer’s disease  
B. Vascular dementia  
C. Frontotemporal dementia  
D. Lewy‑body dementia  

**Answer**: B. Vascular dementia  
**Explanation**: Recurrent cerebral infarcts in a young patient with sickle cell point to a vascular aetiology.

### Question 3  
A patient with known Parkinson’s disease develops visual hallucinations and fluctuating cognition. Which medication should be avoided?  

A. Rivastigmine  
B. Donepezil  
C. Haloperidol  
D. Memantine  

**Answer**: C. Haloperidol  
**Explanation**: Typical antipsychotics can precipitate severe parkinsonism and worsen cognition in Lewy‑body or Parkinson‑related dementia.

### Question 4  
During a ward round you encounter an elderly patient who is quiet, sleepy, and has a CAM score of 0 but a MMSE of 20/30. What is the most likely explanation?  

A. Severe delirium  
B. Pseudodementia  
C. Normal ageing with mild cognitive impairment  
D. Depression  

**Answer**: C. Normal ageing with mild cognitive impairment  
**Explanation**: CAM negative indicates no delirium; MMSE 20 suggests mild impairment, not severe dementia.

### Question 5  
A 45‑year‑old woman with HIV on antiretroviral therapy presents with progressive memory loss over 12 months, normal CT brain, and a CD4 count of 350 cells/µL. Which investigation is most useful next?  

A. Lumbar puncture for cryptococcal antigen  
B. Serum vitamin B12 level  
C. Neuropsychological testing (MoCA)  
D. Repeat MRI with contrast  

**Answer**: C. Neuropsychological testing (MoCA)  
**Explanation**: In the absence of opportunistic infection, formal cognitive testing helps stage HIV‑associated neurocognitive disorder.
