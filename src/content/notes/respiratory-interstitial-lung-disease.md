# Interstitial Lung Diseases — Year 4 Internal Medicine (Respiratory) Notes

*Orientation notes for study. Specialist diagnosis uses a multidisciplinary team; check local guidelines before prescribing.*


![Interstitial Lung Diseases. Public domain](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/eb/End-stage_interstitial_lung_disease_%28honeycomb_lung%29.jpg)

## Definition

**Interstitial lung diseases (ILD)**, or **diffuse parenchymal lung diseases**, are a **heterogeneous group of more than 200 disorders** that cause **inflammation and/or fibrosis of the lung interstitium** (alveolar walls and the tissue around airways and vessels). They produce a **restrictive** pattern of lung function and **impaired gas exchange**.

## Anatomy and physiology you need  

| Structure / Function | Why it matters for ILD |
|---|---|
| **Alveolar‑capillary membrane** – thin type I pneumocytes, basement membrane, capillary endothelium | The primary site of gas exchange; inflammation or fibrosis thickens this barrier → ↓ diffusion capacity (low DLCO) and hypoxaemia on exertion. |
| **Interstitial compartment** – connective tissue surrounding alveoli, bronchioles, vessels, lymphatics | ILD is defined by pathology in this zone. Expansion by oedema, cellular infiltrates or collagen replaces normal elastic recoil, producing a **restrictive** ventilatory pattern (reduced TLC, FVC). |
| **Pulmonary surfactant system** – type II pneumocytes produce phospholipid film | Damage or loss of type II cells reduces surfactant → alveolar collapse (atelectasis) and contributes to stiff lungs; also releases profibrotic cytokines (e.g., TGF‑β). |
| **Lymphatic drainage of the lung** – peribronchovascular and subpleural lymphatics | Impaired drainage from chronic inflammation leads to interstitial oedema and facilitates deposition of fibrotic tissue; also a route for antigen presentation in hypersensitivity pneumonitis. |
| **Pulmonary vasculature (arterioles & veins)** – thin‑walled muscular vessels | Fibrotic distortion narrows vessels, raising pulmonary vascular resistance → secondary pulmonary hypertension and right‑heart strain (cor pulmonale). |
| **Chest wall & diaphragm mechanics** – rib cage, intercostals, diaphragm | Restriction from stiff lungs limits tidal volume; the diaphragm must generate higher pressures, which is why patients develop rapid, shallow breathing and early fatigue. |
| **Neuro‑humoral control of ventilation** – chemoreceptors, mechanoreceptors | Progressive hypoxaemia stimulates peripheral chemoreceptors → tachypnoea; however, fibrotic lungs blunt stretch‑receptor feedback, reducing the normal drive to increase tidal volume. |

## Pathophysiology  

**Inhaled/ systemic insult → immune activation → cytokine cascade → fibroblast proliferation → extracellular matrix deposition → architectural distortion → clinical syndrome**

```
Environmental/occupational antigen, drug, auto‑immune trigger, or idiopathic injury
      ↓
Alveolar epithelial injury (type I & II pneumocyte apoptosis) + endothelial damage
      ↓
Release of alarmins (HMGB1, IL‑33) → activation of alveolar macrophages & dendritic cells
      ↓
Th1/Th17 (HP, sarcoid) or Th2 (eosinophilic) skewing → cytokines (TNF‑α, IFN‑γ, IL‑1β, IL‑6)
      ↓
Transforming growth factor‑β (TGF‑β) and platelet‑derived growth factor (PDGF) drive fibroblast recruitment
      ↓
Myofibroblast differentiation → excess collagen I/III, fibronectin, elastin deposition
      ↓
Loss of normal alveolar architecture → honey‑comb cyst formation, traction bronchiectasis
      ↓
Reduced lung compliance → restrictive spirometry, ↓ TLC, ↓ DLCO
      ↓
Ventilation‑perfusion mismatch → exertional desaturation, dyspnoea
      ↓
Pulmonary vascular remodeling → pulmonary hypertension → right‑sided heart failure
```

## Classification

| Group | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Idiopathic interstitial pneumonias** | **Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF)**, nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP), cryptogenic organising pneumonia (COP), desquamative interstitial pneumonia |
| **Known cause** | **Occupational** (silica, asbestos, coal), **drugs** (**amiodarone, methotrexate, nitrofurantoin, bleomycin**), **radiation**, **hypersensitivity pneumonitis** (farmer's lung, bird fancier's lung) |
| **Connective tissue disease–related** | **Rheumatoid arthritis, systemic sclerosis, SLE, myositis, Sjögren's** |
| **Granulomatous** | **Sarcoidosis** |
| **Rare** | Eosinophilic pneumonia, **pulmonary alveolar proteinosis**, LAM, Langerhans cell histiocytosis, vasculitis |

## Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (the commonest idiopathic ILD)

- **Who:** **over 50 years, male**, **smokers**; chronic, **progressive** fibrosis with **usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP)** pattern; unknown cause.
- **Clinical:** **progressive dry cough and exertional breathlessness** over months to years; **fine "velcro" end-inspiratory crackles at the bases**, **finger clubbing**; late: cyanosis and cor pulmonale.
- **HRCT:** **subpleural, basal, reticular opacities with honeycombing** and traction bronchiectasis (diagnostic without biopsy in the right clinical setting).
- **Treatment:** **antifibrotics (nintedanib, pirfenidone)** slow decline; **oxygen**, pulmonary rehabilitation, treat reflux and cough, **vaccinations**; **lung transplant** in selected patients; **palliative care**. **Steroids do not help** (and can harm) in IPF.
- **Prognosis:** poor; median survival about **3–5 years** from diagnosis; **acute exacerbations** are often fatal.

## Other important ILDs

- **Sarcoidosis:** **non-caseating granulomas**; young adults; **bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy** with or without parenchymal disease; **erythema nodosum, arthralgia, uveitis, hypercalcaemia, raised ACE**; **Löfgren syndrome** (bilateral hilar nodes + erythema nodosum + arthralgia, good prognosis). Treat with **steroids** if symptomatic or organ-threatening. In Kenya, **TB is the main differential**.
- **Hypersensitivity pneumonitis:** immune reaction to **inhaled organic antigens** (mouldy hay: **farmer's lung**; birds: **bird fancier's lung**); acute (flu-like 4–8 hours after exposure) or chronic fibrosis. **Remove the exposure**; steroids.
- **CTD-ILD:** commonly **systemic sclerosis** (NSIP), **rheumatoid arthritis** (UIP); treat with **immunosuppression**.
- **Drug-induced:** **stop the drug**; steroids if severe.
- **Cryptogenic organising pneumonia:** subacute, patchy consolidation not responding to antibiotics; **excellent response to steroids**.

## Clinical approach

- **History:** onset and course, **occupation and hobbies** (birds, mould, dusts), **drugs**, smoking, **family history**, symptoms of CTD (joint pain, Raynaud's, rash, dry eyes), previous radiation or TB.
- **Examination:** **clubbing**, **fine bibasal crackles**, skin or joint signs of CTD, signs of pulmonary hypertension and right heart failure.

## Differential diagnosis and how to tell them apart  

| Condition | Key clinical / radiological clues | Distinguishing feature from other ILDs |
|---|---|---|
| **Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF)** | Age > 50, male, smoker; progressive dry cough, basal “velcro” crackles, clubbing; HRCT – subpleural basal reticulation, honey‑comb, traction bronchiectasis (UIP). | UIP pattern on HRCT (basal, subpleural, honey‑comb) without significant ground‑glass; lack of exposure history; steroids ineffective. |
| **Nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP)** | Often CTD‑related or drug‑induced; dyspnoea over months, fine crackles; HRCT – homogeneous ground‑glass with basal predominance, minimal honey‑comb. | Predominant ground‑glass with traction bronchiectasis but **absence of honey‑comb**; responds to steroids/immunosuppression. |
| **Cryptogenic organising pneumonia (COP)** | Subacute fever, cough, dyspnoea; patchy consolidations on HRCT, often peripheral; bronchoscopy shows organising plugs. | **Consolidative, migratory opacities** on imaging; dramatic response to steroids within weeks. |
| **Sarcoidosis** | Young adults, African descent; systemic signs (erythema nodosum, uveitis); bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy, upper‑lobe nodules on HRCT; non‑caseating granulomas on biopsy. | **Mediastinal/hilar lymphadenopathy** and **upper‑lobe predominance**; serum ACE ↑; granulomas on biopsy. |
| **Hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP) – chronic** | History of bird, mould, or farm exposure; flu‑like episodes; HRCT – diffuse ground‑glass, centrilobular nodules, mosaic attenuation, early fibrosis. | **Exposure history** plus BAL lymphocytosis > 30 %; **ill‑defined centrilobular nodules** and air‑trapping on expiratory CT. |
| **Drug‑induced ILD** | Temporal relation to offending drug (e.g., amiodarone, bleomycin); may mimic UIP or NSIP; improvement after drug cessation. | **Clear temporal association**; often reversible with drug withdrawal; may show mixed patterns. |
| **Connective‑tissue disease‑associated ILD** | Joint pain, Raynaud’s, skin thickening, mechanic’s hands; HRCT – NSIP pattern common in systemic sclerosis, UIP in rheumatoid arthritis. | **Extra‑pulmonary CTD features**; serology (ANA, RF, anti‑CCP, ENA) positive; pattern may overlap but clinical context guides. |

*In an exam, the examiner expects you to link the dominant radiological pattern (UIP vs NSIP vs organising) with the clinical context (age, exposure, systemic signs) to narrow the diagnosis.*

## Investigations

- **Chest X-ray:** **reticular or reticulonodular shadowing**, **reduced lung volumes**, **honeycombing**; may be normal early.
- **HRCT chest:** the **key test** (UIP pattern, NSIP pattern, nodules, ground glass).
- **Lung function:** **restrictive pattern**: **reduced FVC, FEV1/FVC normal or raised, reduced TLC and DLCO (transfer factor)**.
- **ABG/oximetry:** hypoxaemia, desaturation on **exercise** (6-minute walk test).
- **Blood tests:** FBC, **ESR/CRP**, **ANA, RF, anti-CCP, ENA, ANCA**, myositis panel, ACE and calcium (sarcoid), precipitins.
- **Bronchoalveolar lavage** (lymphocytosis in sarcoid and hypersensitivity pneumonitis), **transbronchial or surgical lung biopsy** when HRCT is not diagnostic.
- **Echocardiography** for pulmonary hypertension; **HIV and TB screening** in Kenya.

## Management principles

1. **Remove the cause:** stop the drug, avoid the antigen, treat the underlying CTD.
2. **Inflammatory ILD** (NSIP, COP, sarcoid, HP, CTD-ILD): **corticosteroids** and **steroid-sparing agents** (azathioprine, mycophenolate, methotrexate, rituximab).
3. **IPF:** **antifibrotics**; avoid steroids.
4. **Supportive:** **oxygen** (resting or exertional hypoxaemia), **pulmonary rehabilitation**, **vaccines** (influenza, pneumococcal, COVID-19), **stop smoking**, treat **reflux**, cough and pulmonary hypertension.
5. **Lung transplantation** referral; **palliative care** early.

## Complications

- **Respiratory failure**, **pulmonary hypertension and cor pulmonale**, **acute exacerbation**, **lung cancer** (IPF raises the risk), pneumothorax, infection (**PCP** on immunosuppression), **weight loss**.

## Exam points

- **Dry cough + exertional dyspnoea + fine "velcro" crackles + clubbing** = think **IPF**.
- **HRCT** is the key investigation; **restrictive pattern with reduced DLCO** on lung function.
- **Ask about drugs and occupation.**
- **Sarcoid:** bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy; non-caseating granulomas.
- **IPF treatment: antifibrotics, not steroids.**

## Clinical case  

**Patient:** 58‑year‑old male, former smoker (20 pack‑years), works as a stone‑cutter.  

**Presentation:** 8‑month history of progressive dyspnoea on exertion (NYHA II → III) and a dry, non‑productive cough. No fever, weight loss or chest pain.  

**Key findings**  

| Finding | Relevance |
|---|---|
| Bibasal “velcro” crackles on auscultation | Typical of a fibrotic interstitial process |
| Digital clubbing (grade 2) | Suggests chronic hypoxaemia and fibrosis |
| HRCT: subpleural basal reticulation with honey‑comb cysts and traction bronchiectasis | Usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP) pattern – diagnostic of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis in the appropriate clinical context |
| Spirometry: FVC 58 % predicted, FEV₁/FVC = 0.82, TLC reduced, DLCO 45 % predicted | Restrictive physiology with impaired gas transfer |
| No occupational exposure to asbestos, no recent drug changes, ANA negative | Helps exclude known‑cause ILD |

### Questions  

1. **What is the most likely diagnosis and why?**  
   **Answer:** Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF). The patient fits the classic demographic (male >50 yr, smoking history), has a progressive dry cough, exertional dyspnoea, bibasal velcro crackles, clubbing, and HRCT shows a definitive UIP pattern. The restrictive physiology with markedly reduced DLCO supports a fibrotic process.  

2. **Which investigation would you request next to assess a common complication of this disease, and what finding would you look for?**  
   **Answer:** Transthoracic echocardiography to screen for pulmonary hypertension. On Doppler, an elevated estimated systolic pulmonary artery pressure (>35 mm Hg) or right‑ventricular systolic pressure elevation would indicate secondary pulmonary hypertension, a frequent complication of advanced IPF.  

3. **Outline the first‑line pharmacologic management for this patient, including non‑pharmacologic measures.**  
   **Answer:** Initiate an antifibrotic agent (either a tyrosine‑kinase inhibitor or a pyridine‑derivative) as first‑line disease‑modifying therapy; avoid systemic steroids. Provide supplemental oxygen if resting or exertional hypoxaemia is documented, enrol the patient in a pulmonary rehabilitation programme, vaccinate against influenza, pneumococcus and COVID‑19, advise smoking cessation, and treat gastro‑oesophageal reflux to minimise micro‑aspiration.

## Tricky questions examiners ask  

**Q1.** A 45‑year‑old woman with systemic sclerosis presents with dry cough and bibasal crackles. HRCT shows ground‑glass opacities with subpleural sparing. What is the most likely ILD pattern and why?  
**Answer:** Nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP). NSIP in connective‑tissue disease characteristically shows ground‑glass attenuation with relative subpleural sparing, unlike the basal subpleural predominance of UIP.  

**Q2.** A patient with suspected hypersensitivity pneumonitis has a BAL lymphocyte count of 12 %. Does this support the diagnosis?  
**Answer:** No. Lymphocytosis >20 % is typical for HP; a count of 12 % is low and makes HP less likely, prompting search for alternative causes.  

**Q3.** In a patient with sarcoidosis and bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy, why would you avoid starting high‑dose steroids immediately?  
**Answer:** Most sarcoidosis cases are self‑limiting; steroids are reserved for organ‑threatening disease. Unnecessary high‑dose steroids expose the patient to infection, hyperglycaemia and bone loss without benefit.  

**Q4.** A 70‑year‑old ex‑smoker with a UIP pattern on HRCT is started on systemic steroids. What is the main reason this choice is discouraged?  
**Answer:** Steroids have no proven benefit in IPF and may increase the risk of infection and accelerate decline; antifibrotic therapy is the evidence‑based first‑line treatment.  

**Q5.** Two patients present with restrictive spirometry and reduced DLCO. One has a history of amiodarone use, the other works in a bird‑cage facility. Which clinical clue helps differentiate drug‑induced ILD from chronic hypersensitivity pneumonitis?  
**Answer:** Temporal relationship to drug exposure (symptom onset after initiating amiodarone) versus chronic antigen exposure (bird droppings). Drug‑induced ILD often improves rapidly after drug cessation, whereas HP requires antigen avoidance and may show BAL lymphocytosis.  

**Q6.** A patient with confirmed IPF develops an acute worsening of dyspnoea, new bilateral ground‑glass opacities on CT and no infection on cultures. What is the appropriate next step?  
**Answer:** Diagnose an acute exacerbation of IPF and initiate high‑flow oxygen, consider broad‑spectrum antibiotics empirically, and discuss eligibility for intensive care or lung transplantation; steroids may be used despite limited evidence, but the key is rapid supportive care.

| Drug | Class | Role here | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nintedanib | Tyrosine kinase inhibitor (antifibrotic) | IPF and other progressive fibrosing ILD | Diarrhoea, liver tests, bleeding |
| Pirfenidone | Antifibrotic | IPF | Nausea, photosensitivity rash |
| Prednisolone | Systemic corticosteroid | Inflammatory ILD (sarcoid, organising pneumonia, hypersensitivity pneumonitis); not for IPF | High glucose, infection, bone loss |
| Methotrexate | Antimetabolite | Steroid-sparing agent in CTD-ILD, but can itself cause pneumonitis | Lung toxicity, liver, blood count |
| Rituximab | Anti-CD20 antibody | Refractory connective-tissue-disease ILD | Infusion reactions, infection |
| Amiodarone | Antiarrhythmic | A common drug cause of pulmonary fibrosis | Thyroid, liver, lung and eye toxicity |
| Nitrofurantoin | Urinary antibiotic | Drug cause of acute and chronic lung disease | Pulmonary reactions with long use |
| Bleomycin | Cytotoxic antibiotic | Chemotherapy cause of pulmonary fibrosis | Dose-related lung toxicity |

**Always ask about drugs:** stopping the cause is the first treatment in drug-induced lung disease.

## Practice questions

**1.** A 65-year-old man has 18 months of dry cough and breathlessness, clubbing and bibasal fine end-inspiratory crackles. HRCT shows subpleural basal honeycombing. The treatment that slows progression is:
A. Prednisolone · B. **Nintedanib or pirfenidone** · C. Antibiotics · D. Salbutamol · E. Methotrexate
*Answer: B.*

**2.** The pattern of lung function in ILD is:
**A. Restrictive with reduced DLCO** · B. Obstructive with high TLC · C. Normal · D. Isolated high FEV1/FVC with high TLC · E. Obstructive with raised DLCO
*Answer: A.*

**3.** A young woman has erythema nodosum, arthralgia and bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy. The most likely diagnosis is:
A. Tuberculosis · B. Lymphoma · C. **Sarcoidosis (Löfgren syndrome)** · D. IPF · E. Silicosis
*Answer: C.*
