# Mood Disorders

**MBChB Year 4 · Psychiatry · Comprehensive examination and clinical revision**

Mood disorders are conditions characterised by persistent or recurrent disturbances of mood, accompanied by changes in energy, activity, cognition, sleep, appetite, behaviour and functioning. This note covers depressive disorders, bipolar disorders, perinatal mood disorders, suicide assessment, clinical evaluation and management.

> **Clinical safety:** These notes support learning, not independent prescribing. Assess immediate risk, consult current Kenya Ministry of Health guidance and local protocols, and involve senior clinicians for severe illness, pregnancy, psychosis or suicidality.

![Mood spectrum](https://res.cloudinary.com/l4wbzpfr/image/upload/v1793772407/ompathstudy/year-4/psychiatry/mood-disorders/mood-spectrum.svg)

## 1. Foundations

### 1.1 Definition and classification
- **Mood** is a sustained emotional state that colours a person's experience of the world.
- A **mood disorder** is a clinically significant disturbance of mood associated with distress, impaired functioning or risk.
- **Depressive pole:** low mood and/or loss of interest or pleasure, often with reduced energy.
- **Elevated pole:** abnormally elevated, expansive or irritable mood with increased energy or activity.
- Broad groups:
  - **Depressive disorders:** major depressive disorder (MDD), persistent depressive disorder (PDD) and other specified depressive disorders.
  - **Bipolar and related disorders:** bipolar I, bipolar II, cyclothymic disorder and related conditions.
- A history of **mania** establishes bipolar I disorder. Always screen for previous mania/hypomania before diagnosing unipolar depression.

### 1.2 Mood versus affect
- **Mood:** patient's subjective, sustained emotional state, elicited by asking how they have been feeling.
- **Affect:** clinician's observation of emotional expression during the interview.
- Describe affect by **range** (full/restricted/blunted/flat), **intensity**, **reactivity**, **mobility** (stable/labile) and **congruence** with stated mood/thought content.
- Example: the patient reports depression (mood) and appears tearful with reduced facial expression (affect).

### 1.3 Normal variation versus disorder
Sadness after bereavement, happiness after success and temporary irritability are normal. Consider a disorder when the change is persistent/recurrent, disproportionate or out of context, represents a change from baseline, clusters with other symptoms, and causes distress, impairment or risk. A psychosocial trigger does **not** exclude a depressive episode.

### 1.4 Biopsychosocial model
![Biopsychosocial model](https://res.cloudinary.com/l4wbzpfr/image/upload/v1793772417/ompathstudy/year-4/psychiatry/mood-disorders/biopsychosocial-model.svg)

- **Biological:** genetic/familial vulnerability; neurotransmitter and neural-circuit changes; HPA-axis and stress-response dysregulation; circadian/sleep disruption; hormonal changes; medical illness; medication and substance effects.
- **Psychological:** negative cognitive schemas, low self-esteem, learned helplessness, trauma, maladaptive coping and personality vulnerabilities.
- **Social:** bereavement, relationship conflict, unemployment, poverty, isolation, violence and chronic adversity.
- **4Ps formulation:** Predisposing (vulnerabilities); Precipitating (triggers); Perpetuating (maintaining factors); Protective (strengths/resources).

### 1.5 Neurobiology
- **Serotonin (5-HT):** mood, anxiety, sleep and appetite; targeted by SSRIs.
- **Noradrenaline:** alertness, attention and energy; targeted by SNRIs and other agents.
- **Dopamine:** reward, motivation and goal-directed behaviour; altered reward signalling may contribute to anhedonia, while increased activity is implicated in mania/psychosis.
- **HPA axis:** hypothalamic CRH → pituitary ACTH → adrenal cortisol. Chronic stress and altered cortisol regulation are implicated in some depressive states.
- Circadian rhythm, sleep and neuroplasticity also contribute.
- Depression is **not simply a serotonin deficiency**; it is a complex interaction of genes, neural networks, stress systems, sleep and environment.

## 2. Major depressive disorder (MDD)

### 2.1 Definition and risk factors
MDD is characterised by one or more major depressive episodes and **no history of mania or hypomania**. Risk factors include previous depression, family history, childhood adversity/trauma, bereavement, relationship or financial stress, social isolation, chronic illness (e.g. diabetes, HIV, cancer, cardiovascular or neurological disease), pregnancy/postpartum state, sleep disruption, alcohol/substance use and certain medications (including corticosteroids).

### 2.2 Clinical features
- **Emotional:** persistent sadness, emptiness, hopelessness, anhedonia, guilt, worthlessness, irritability, anxiety and thoughts of death.
- **Cognitive:** poor concentration, impaired attention, indecisiveness, negative views of self/world/future; severe illness may include guilt or nihilistic delusions.
- **Somatic:** insomnia or hypersomnia, appetite/weight change, fatigue, low energy, reduced libido, psychomotor retardation/agitation, constipation and non-specific pain.
- **Behavioural:** social withdrawal, reduced communication, poor academic/occupational performance, reduced self-care and loss of usual activities.

### 2.3 DSM-5-TR major depressive episode: nine symptoms
At least **five of nine** symptoms during the same **two-week period**, representing a change from baseline, including depressed mood or loss of interest/pleasure. Symptoms cause clinically significant distress or impairment.

![Nine symptoms of major depression](https://res.cloudinary.com/l4wbzpfr/image/upload/v1793772412/ompathstudy/year-4/psychiatry/mood-disorders/mdd-nine-symptoms.svg)

1. Depressed mood most of the day, nearly every day (may be irritability in children/adolescents).
2. Markedly diminished interest or pleasure (**anhedonia**).
3. Significant weight or appetite change.
4. Insomnia or hypersomnia.
5. Observable psychomotor agitation or retardation.
6. Fatigue or loss of energy.
7. Worthlessness or excessive/inappropriate guilt.
8. Poor concentration or indecisiveness.
9. Recurrent thoughts of death, suicidal ideation, attempt or specific plan.

**SIGECAPS:** Sleep, Interest, Guilt, Energy, Concentration, Appetite, Psychomotor change, Suicidality. Depressed mood is assessed alongside these.

Exclude substance/medication effects and medical causes; assess past mania/hypomania and whether another psychiatric disorder better explains the symptoms. Bereavement or a stressor does not automatically rule out MDD.

### 2.4 Severity and specifiers
Severity depends on symptom number/intensity, distress, functional impairment, psychosis and risk.
- **Mild:** just above diagnostic threshold; functioning largely maintained.
- **Moderate:** appreciable impairment in social, academic or occupational functioning.
- **Severe:** numerous/intense symptoms, marked impairment, inability to maintain self-care, severe psychomotor change or high suicide risk.
- **Melancholic features:** profound anhedonia/lack of mood reactivity; early morning awakening; worse in the morning; marked psychomotor change; reduced appetite/weight; excessive guilt.
- **Atypical features:** mood reactivity; increased appetite/weight; hypersomnia; leaden paralysis; longstanding rejection sensitivity.
- **Seasonal pattern:** recurrent seasonal relationship, often reduced daylight; hypersomnia, increased appetite and low energy may occur.
- **Anxious distress:** tension, restlessness, poor concentration due to worry, fear of losing control or that something terrible may happen.
- **Peripartum onset:** DSM-5-TR onset during pregnancy or within four weeks after delivery; clinical services recognise postpartum depression over a broader period.
- **Psychotic features:** mood-congruent or mood-incongruent delusions/hallucinations may occur in severe depression.

### 2.5 Differential diagnosis
- Bipolar depression: ask about past mania/hypomania.
- PDD, adjustment disorder with depressed mood, grief/bereavement.
- Schizoaffective disorder, schizophrenia and other psychotic disorders.
- Substance/medication-induced depression; anxiety disorders and PTSD.
- Medical causes: hypothyroidism, anaemia, B12/folate deficiency, HIV, chronic infection, Parkinson disease, stroke and other neurological illness.

### 2.6 Investigations
MDD is a **clinical diagnosis**. Investigate according to history, examination, comorbidity and treatment choice—not a mandatory fixed panel for everyone.
- FBC (anaemia); TSH/thyroid function; renal function/electrolytes; LFTs.
- Glucose/HbA1c and lipids where indicated.
- B12/folate if deficiency suspected.
- HIV/syphilis testing when clinically indicated, with consent and local protocols.
- Toxicology if substance use suspected; pregnancy test where relevant to treatment.
- ECG or neuroimaging only when indicated (cardiac risk/medication choice, neurological signs, atypical onset or suspected intracranial pathology).

### 2.7 Management
Individualise according to severity, risk, previous response, comorbidity, preference and access to care.

**Psychological/social**
- **CBT:** identifies and modifies unhelpful thoughts and behaviours.
- **Interpersonal therapy (IPT):** grief, role transitions, interpersonal conflict and isolation.
- **Behavioural activation:** gradual return to meaningful/rewarding activities.
- Problem-solving therapy, supportive counselling and family psychoeducation.
- Address sleep, physical activity, substance use, social support, financial and occupational stressors.

**Antidepressants**
- Consider especially in moderate-to-severe, recurrent illness, patient preference or inadequate response to psychological treatment.
- **SSRIs:** fluoxetine, sertraline, escitalopram, citalopram, paroxetine; inhibit SERT.
  - Adverse effects: nausea, diarrhoea, headache, sleep disturbance, initial activation/anxiety, sexual dysfunction and hyponatraemia (especially older adults).
  - Increased bleeding risk with NSAIDs/anticoagulants; consider serotonin syndrome with interacting serotonergic drugs.
  - Monitor for worsening agitation or suicidal thoughts, particularly early in treatment and after dose changes.
- **SNRIs:** venlafaxine, duloxetine; inhibit serotonin/noradrenaline reuptake. Nausea, sweating, sexual dysfunction, possible BP increase (notably venlafaxine), discontinuation symptoms.
- **TCAs:** amitriptyline, imipramine, clomipramine, nortriptyline. Anticholinergic effects, sedation, postural hypotension and cardiac conduction toxicity; overdose can be fatal.
- **Other agents:** mirtazapine (sedation, increased appetite/weight); bupropion (noradrenaline/dopamine reuptake inhibition, insomnia, lower seizure threshold); trazodone (sedation, postural hypotension, rare priapism).
- Explain delayed benefit, adherence, adverse effects, interactions and not stopping abruptly. Review response and tolerability.

**ECT**
- Consider when rapid response is required: life-threatening refusal of food/fluids, severe psychotic depression, catatonia, high suicide risk or failure of appropriate treatments.
- Delivered under general anaesthesia with muscle relaxation.
- Adverse effects: transient confusion, headache, short-term memory impairment; some report persistent autobiographical memory difficulties.

**Continuation/relapse prevention**
- After remission, continue effective antidepressant for at least **six months** for many patients; longer for recurrent, severe or high-risk illness.
- Taper gradually; maintain psychological support, address residual symptoms and develop a relapse plan.

### 2.8 Complications
Suicide/attempts, self-neglect, malnutrition/dehydration, substance misuse, worsening physical comorbidity, relationship breakdown, isolation, impaired study/work, recurrent episodes and chronic functional impairment.

## 3. Persistent depressive disorder (PDD; dysthymia)

### 3.1 Definition and criteria
Chronic depressed mood for most of the day, more days than not, for at least **two years in adults** and **one year in children/adolescents**. At least two of:
- Poor appetite or overeating.
- Insomnia or hypersomnia.
- Low energy/fatigue.
- Low self-esteem.
- Poor concentration or difficulty deciding.
- Hopelessness.

During the required period, the person has not been without symptoms for more than two months at a time. Symptoms cause distress/impairment; no history of mania/hypomania; not better explained by another condition.

### 3.2 Features and management
- Chronic low mood; patient may say they have “always been this way”.
- Pessimism, hopelessness, low self-esteem, fatigue, poor concentration, sleep/appetite disturbance and long-term functional impairment.
- CBT/other psychotherapy; SSRIs or SNRIs when indicated; combined treatment may help.
- Treat comorbid anxiety, substance use and medical illness; provide long-term follow-up.

**Double depression:** a patient with PDD develops a superimposed major depressive episode.

| Feature | MDD | PDD |
|---|---|---|
| Minimum duration | 2 weeks | 2 years adult; 1 year young people |
| Course | Often episodic | Chronic |
| Intensity | May be severe | Often less intense, can be severe |
| Symptom-free interval | Variable | No more than 2 months during required period |
| Mania/hypomania | Excludes unipolar MDD | Excludes PDD |

## 4. Bipolar and related disorders

### 4.1 Classification
- **Bipolar I:** at least one manic episode; depression is common but not required.
- **Bipolar II:** at least one hypomanic episode and one major depressive episode; **no history of mania**.
- **Cyclothymic disorder:** chronic fluctuating subthreshold hypomanic and depressive symptoms.
- Substance/medication-induced bipolar disorder; bipolar disorder due to another medical condition; other specified/unspecified bipolar disorders.

**Bipolar II is not simply mild bipolar:** depressive morbidity, suicide risk and impairment can be substantial.

![Bipolar episode course](https://res.cloudinary.com/l4wbzpfr/image/upload/v1793772422/ompathstudy/year-4/psychiatry/mood-disorders/bipolar-episode-course.svg)

### 4.2 Aetiology and features
- Strong genetic/familial contribution; altered neurotransmission, reward processing, intracellular signalling and neural plasticity.
- Circadian and sleep-wake disruption; stress, substances and routine disruption may precipitate episodes.
- During elevated episodes: elevated/irritable mood, increased energy, reduced need for sleep, pressured speech, racing thoughts, grandiosity, distractibility, increased activity, spending/sexual risk-taking.
- During depression: low mood, anhedonia, fatigue, sleep/appetite change, guilt, poor concentration and suicidal thoughts.

### 4.3 Cyclothymic disorder
- At least two years in adults (one year in children/adolescents) of numerous periods of hypomanic symptoms and depressive symptoms that do not meet full episode criteria.
- Symptoms present at least half the time; no symptom-free interval longer than two months.
- No prior full manic, hypomanic or major depressive episode during the specified period.
- Causes distress/impairment. Management: psychoeducation, regular sleep/routine, psychotherapy, substance avoidance and specialist review; avoid reflexive antidepressant monotherapy.

## 5. Mania and hypomania

### 5.1 Mania: definition and criteria
Distinct period of abnormally and persistently elevated, expansive or irritable mood **and increased energy/goal-directed activity**, lasting at least **one week**, most of the day nearly every day, or any duration if hospitalisation is necessary.

At least **three** associated symptoms (or **four** if mood is only irritable):
1. Inflated self-esteem/grandiosity.
2. Decreased need for sleep (rested after very little sleep).
3. More talkative than usual/pressured speech.
4. Flight of ideas/racing thoughts.
5. Distractibility.
6. Increased goal-directed activity or psychomotor agitation.
7. Excessive involvement in activities with high potential for painful consequences (spending, sexual indiscretion, reckless driving, risky investments).

There is marked social/occupational impairment, hospitalisation to prevent harm, or psychotic features. Exclude substance and medical causes.

### 5.2 Clinical features/MSE
- Mood: euphoric, expansive or irritable; labile.
- Speech: loud, rapid, pressured, difficult to interrupt.
- Thought form: flight of ideas, racing thoughts, distractibility.
- Thought content: grandiosity, unrealistic plans, possible delusions.
- Behaviour: overactivity, disinhibition, excessive spending, reckless driving, increased sexual activity, multiple projects or aggression.
- Sleep: reduced need without expected tiredness.
- Insight/judgement often impaired.

### 5.3 Hypomania
- Similar mood/energy change for at least **four consecutive days**.
- Unequivocal change from baseline, observable by others.
- Not severe enough for marked impairment or hospitalisation; no psychosis.
- May present as increased confidence, sociability, productivity, talking, activity and reduced need for sleep.
- Psychosis during an elevated episode means the episode is mania, not hypomania.

| Feature | Mania | Hypomania |
|---|---|---|
| Duration | ≥1 week, or any duration if hospitalised | ≥4 consecutive days |
| Function | Marked impairment | Observable change, no marked impairment |
| Hospitalisation | May be necessary | Not required |
| Psychosis | May occur | Absent |
| Diagnosis | Establishes bipolar I | With MDE and no mania, supports bipolar II |

**Memory anchor:** mania = elevated/irritable mood + increased energy + marked impairment, hospitalisation or psychosis. Hypomania = similar change without these severe features.

### 5.4 Acute mania management
- Assess danger to patient/others, suicide risk, aggression, capacity, self-care, sleep, hydration and nutrition.
- Urgent admission where risk, severe impairment, psychosis or inability to care for self is present.
- Calm, low-stimulation environment; obtain collateral history.
- Assess alcohol, khat, stimulants and other substances; review prescribed/non-prescribed medicines.
- Treat dehydration, injury, intoxication, withdrawal or medical complications.
- Specialist-directed pharmacotherapy may include an antipsychotic (e.g. olanzapine, risperidone, quetiapine, haloperidol or aripiprazole) and/or mood stabiliser (lithium or selected alternatives). Severe mania may require combination treatment.
- Short-term benzodiazepine may be used under supervision for severe agitation/insomnia.
- **Valproate:** major teratogenic/neurodevelopmental risks; avoid in pregnancy and follow stringent current reproductive safety restrictions.
- **Lithium:** serum-level, renal and thyroid monitoring; pregnancy/breastfeeding require specialist assessment.

### 5.5 Hypomania management
Assess risk and progression, review antidepressants/stimulants and other triggers, consider bipolar diagnosis and specialist treatment, restore sleep/routine, educate patient/family and arrange close follow-up.

## 6. Mixed features
- Mixed features: an episode of one mood polarity accompanied by several symptoms of the opposite polarity.
- Example: depression and suicidal thoughts with racing thoughts, increased energy, reduced need for sleep and agitation.
- DSM-5-TR generally requires at least **three** opposite-pole symptoms during the majority of days of the episode.
- In depression: elevated/expansive mood, grandiosity, increased talkativeness, racing thoughts, increased energy/activity, risky behaviour or decreased need for sleep.
- In mania/hypomania: depressed mood, anhedonia, psychomotor slowing, fatigue, worthlessness/excessive guilt or recurrent thoughts of death/suicide.
- Mixed presentations may involve impulsivity and increased suicide risk. Assess safety urgently; consider admission; establish bipolar diagnosis; use specialist-directed mood stabiliser/antipsychotic treatment; generally avoid antidepressant monotherapy when bipolar mixed features are suspected.

## 7. Postpartum mood disorders

![Postpartum blues, depression and psychosis](https://res.cloudinary.com/l4wbzpfr/image/upload/v1793772427/ompathstudy/year-4/psychiatry/mood-disorders/postpartum-spectrum.svg)

### 7.1 Postpartum blues
- Common, mild, transient emotional lability after childbirth; not itself MDD.
- Usually begins in first few days, peaks around days 3–5 and resolves within **two weeks**.
- Tearfulness, irritability, anxiety, mood swings, feeling overwhelmed and mild sleep disturbance.
- Insight and basic functioning generally preserved; psychosis absent.
- Reassurance, rest, practical/emotional support and monitoring. Persistent, severe or worsening symptoms need reassessment.

### 7.2 Postpartum depression
- Depressive episode during pregnancy or after childbirth, with distress and impairment.
- DSM-5-TR peripartum specifier: onset during pregnancy or within four weeks postpartum; clinical services assess a broader postpartum period.
- Risks: previous depression/postpartum depression, bipolar disorder, trauma, sleep deprivation, poor support, domestic violence, relationship/financial stress and obstetric complications.
- Features: persistent low mood, anhedonia, guilt, feeling inadequate as a mother, anxiety, appetite/sleep change, fatigue, poor concentration, withdrawal and suicidal thoughts.
- Ask about intrusive thoughts of harm to infant: establish whether unwanted/distressing with preserved insight, or associated with delusions, hallucinations, intent or impaired control.
- **EPDS:** 10-item screening tool, not diagnostic. Any positive self-harm response requires prompt direct assessment.
- Management: assess safety/severity; CBT/IPT/support; practical infant-care and sleep support; antidepressants for moderate-severe illness when indicated. Consider previous response, breastfeeding, infant exposure and maternal preference. Sertraline is often considered when starting an SSRI during breastfeeding because infant exposure is generally low.

### 7.3 Postpartum psychosis
<aside>**Psychiatric emergency:** acute psychosis, severe mood disturbance or disorganisation after childbirth requires urgent specialist assessment and usually admission.</aside>

- Often begins within days and commonly within the first two weeks.
- Risks: previous postpartum psychosis, bipolar disorder, severe mood episodes, family history; sleep loss may contribute.
- Features: severe insomnia; mania, depression or mixed symptoms; delusions, hallucinations, confusion, disorganisation, bizarre behaviour, agitation, refusal to eat or inability to care safely for infant.
- Exclude postpartum infection/sepsis, eclampsia, thyroid/metabolic disease, intoxication/withdrawal, seizures and (when indicated) autoimmune encephalitis.
- Tests guided by presentation: FBC, electrolytes/renal function, LFTs, glucose, thyroid function, calcium, toxicology and infection work-up; neuroimaging/EEG if neurological signs, seizures, atypical features or altered consciousness.
- **Immediate management:** urgent psychiatric and obstetric/medical review; admission, ideally mother-and-baby unit where available and safe; appropriate observation; assess risk to mother/infant; arrange safe infant care; assess capacity/legal safeguards.
- Specialist treatment may include antipsychotics, lithium or other mood stabilisers, short-term benzodiazepines for severe agitation/insomnia, and ECT when rapid response is required or illness is severe/refractory. Lithium requires serum, renal and thyroid monitoring and specialist breastfeeding advice.
- Plan relapse prevention and future-pregnancy care.

| Feature | Blues | Depression | Psychosis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Onset | First few days | Pregnancy/postpartum | Often days to first 2 weeks |
| Duration | Resolves within 2 weeks | Persistent episode | Acute, variable |
| Severity | Mild | Moderate-severe | Severe |
| Psychosis | Absent | Not typical | Present |
| Function | Largely preserved | Impaired | Often severely impaired |
| Response | Support/monitor | Psychological ± medication | Emergency admission |

## 8. Suicide and self-harm

### 8.1 Terminology
- **Suicidal ideation:** thoughts about ending one's life.
- **Suicide plan:** formulated method, time or place.
- **Suicide attempt:** non-fatal self-directed behaviour with at least some intent to die.
- **Self-harm:** intentional self-poisoning or injury irrespective of apparent purpose; do not automatically label it a suicide attempt.
- **Suicide death:** death resulting from intentional self-inflicted injury.

### 8.2 Risk and protective factors
**Risk:** previous attempt/self-harm; depression, bipolar disorder, psychosis, substance use, severe anxiety, personality disorder, insomnia; hopelessness, agitation, impulsivity, command hallucinations, mixed symptoms; family history, trauma, psychiatric admission, chronic illness; bereavement, violence, relationship/financial/legal problems, unemployment, isolation; access to pesticides, toxic medicines or firearms.

**Protective:** supportive relationships, accessible mental healthcare, coping skills, willingness to seek help, future goals, meaningful responsibilities and cultural/religious/community support when meaningful. Protective factors do not eliminate risk.

### 8.3 Structured assessment
Ask directly, calmly and without judgement. Asking about suicide does not cause suicidal behaviour.
1. **Thoughts:** “Have you thought about ending your life?” “Have you wished you were dead?”
2. **Plan:** method, timing, place, preparation and how developed.
3. **Intent:** expectation/intention to act; what has stopped them.
4. **Means:** access to the method and preparatory actions.
5. **Previous behaviour:** attempts/self-harm, timing, circumstances, medical severity and intent.
6. **Mental state:** hopelessness, depression, mania/mixed state, psychosis, command hallucinations, agitation, impulsivity, intoxication and cognition.
7. **Context:** losses, violence, social stressors, support and foreseeable changes.
8. **Protective factors:** people, beliefs, goals and willingness to collaborate.

![Suicide risk assessment](https://res.cloudinary.com/l4wbzpfr/image/upload/v1793772433/ompathstudy/year-4/psychiatry/mood-disorders/suicide-assessment.svg)

### 8.4 Risk formulation/documentation
Do not rely on a score alone. Formulate baseline vulnerabilities, current thoughts/intent/plan/means/mental and medical state, precipitating events, protective factors and foreseeable changes (intoxication, discharge, loss of support, worsening illness). Document patient statements, actions, contacts, rationale for disposition and follow-up. Risk is dynamic; reassess.

### 8.5 Immediate management and safety planning
- Treat overdose, poisoning, injury, intoxication or medical instability first.
- If imminent risk, do not leave patient without appropriate supervision; arrange urgent emergency/psychiatric assessment.
- Restrict access to lethal means collaboratively; involve trusted supports appropriately.
- Consider admission when risk cannot be managed safely in the community, or with psychosis, severe mood illness, impaired capacity or inability to maintain safety.
- Treat underlying illness and address substance use, agitation, insomnia and social crisis.
- In Kenya, consider access to agricultural pesticides and local referral pathways; emergency medical stabilisation takes priority in poisoning.
- Collaborative safety plan: personal warning signs; internal coping strategies; safe people/places for distraction; trusted people to contact; professional/emergency contacts; steps to reduce access to lethal means; timely follow-up.
- A safety plan is **not** a promise or “no-suicide contract”; contracts do not replace assessment, means safety, treatment or follow-up.

## 9. Other mood-related conditions

### 9.1 Premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD)
- Severe cyclical emotional/physical symptoms in luteal phase, improving shortly after menstruation begins.
- Occurs in most cycles, prominent in final week before menses, improves within days of onset and minimal/absent in following week.
- Mood lability, irritability, depressed mood, anxiety, hopelessness, interpersonal conflict, breast tenderness, bloating, headache, fatigue, appetite/sleep change.
- Causes clinically significant distress/impairment. Prospective daily symptom recording over at least two cycles helps confirm.
- Management: psychoeducation, lifestyle, CBT, SSRIs continuously or in luteal phase, selected hormonal treatment.

### 9.2 Substance/medication-induced mood disorders
- Depressive symptoms: alcohol, opioids, sedatives or stimulant withdrawal.
- Manic-like symptoms: cocaine, amphetamines and other stimulants; corticosteroids, antidepressants and dopaminergic drugs may precipitate elevation.
- Establish timing relative to exposure, dose changes and withdrawal; exclude primary bipolar and medical causes.
- Treat acute risk, manage intoxication/withdrawal safely and review causative medicines with prescriber.

### 9.3 Mood disorder due to another medical condition
Consider especially with late/abrupt onset, atypical features, cognitive change, neurological signs or systemic illness.

| Condition | Possible presentation |
|---|---|
| Hypothyroidism | Depression, fatigue, slowing |
| Hyperthyroidism | Anxiety, agitation, occasionally manic-like symptoms |
| Cushing syndrome | Depression/mood changes |
| Parkinson disease | Depression |
| Stroke | Post-stroke depression/disinhibition |
| Multiple sclerosis | Depression/emotional changes |
| Brain tumour/neurological disease | New mood/personality change |
| SLE/encephalitis | Neuropsychiatric manifestations |

### 9.4 Rapid-cycling bipolar disorder
- Course specifier: **four or more** distinct mood episodes within 12 months.
- Episodes may be manic, hypomanic or major depressive and must meet episode criteria, separated by remission or switch in polarity as defined by criteria.
- Associated with frequent relapse, impairment and treatment complexity.
- Review adherence, substances, thyroid function and antidepressant exposure; specialist maintenance planning.

### 9.5 Bipolar disorder with psychotic features
Delusions/hallucinations may occur during severe manic or depressive episodes. Mania may involve grandiose, religious or persecutory delusions; depression may involve guilt, poverty, punishment or nihilistic beliefs. Psychosis confined to mood episodes supports mood disorder with psychotic features. Psychosis persisting outside major mood episodes requires consideration of schizoaffective and schizophrenia-spectrum disorders.

## 10. Mood stabilisers and key pharmacology

![Mood stabiliser monitoring](https://res.cloudinary.com/l4wbzpfr/image/upload/v1793772442/ompathstudy/year-4/psychiatry/mood-disorders/mood-stabiliser-monitoring.svg)

### 10.1 Lithium
- **Mechanism:** affects intracellular signalling and neurotransmission; full mechanism not completely understood.
- **Uses:** acute mania, bipolar maintenance and prevention of recurrence; evidence supports reduced suicide risk in bipolar disorder.
- **Adverse effects:** fine tremor, nausea/diarrhoea, weight gain, polyuria/polydipsia, hypothyroidism, hyperparathyroidism/hypercalcaemia and renal impairment.
- **Monitoring:** serum concentration at appropriate times, renal function/eGFR, thyroid function, calcium, weight and clinical adverse effects; ECG when indicated.
- **Toxicity:** coarse tremor, vomiting/diarrhoea, ataxia, dysarthria, confusion and seizures in severe toxicity.
- Dehydration, sodium depletion, renal impairment and interacting drugs (notably NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors/ARBs and thiazide diuretics) can raise lithium levels. Suspected toxicity needs urgent assessment and withholding further doses pending specialist advice.

### 10.2 Sodium valproate
- Enhances GABAergic neurotransmission and affects voltage-gated ion channels.
- Used for acute mania and selected maintenance cases.
- Adverse effects: weight gain, tremor, GI upset, thrombocytopenia, hepatotoxicity and pancreatitis.
- Major teratogenic and fetal neurodevelopmental risks. Avoid in pregnancy for bipolar disorder and follow current national/regulatory restrictions and stringent reproductive safeguards for anyone who could become pregnant.

### 10.3 Carbamazepine
- Blocks voltage-gated sodium channels; selected use in acute mania/maintenance.
- Adverse effects: dizziness, diplopia, ataxia, hyponatraemia, bone marrow suppression, hepatotoxicity and serious skin reactions including SJS.
- Enzyme induction causes important drug interactions; consider ancestry-related HLA risk testing where recommended by local guidance.

### 10.4 Lamotrigine
- Blocks voltage-gated sodium channels and reduces glutamate release.
- Useful in bipolar maintenance, particularly prevention of depressive episodes; **not** an acute antimanic treatment.
- Adverse effects: headache, dizziness, nausea and rash; rare SJS.
- Start low and titrate slowly; rapid escalation increases rash risk.

### 10.5 Antidepressants in bipolar disorder
Antidepressants may precipitate mood elevation. Screen for past mania/hypomania before prescribing for depression. Antidepressant monotherapy is generally avoided in bipolar I and suspected mixed states. If mania emerges, arrange urgent review; medication changes must be supervised and an antimanic plan instituted.

## 11. Clinical assessment

### 11.1 History
- Presenting symptoms/duration: low mood, anhedonia, sleep, energy, appetite, concentration, elevated mood, irritability, activity and risk behaviour.
- Onset, course, severity, triggers, baseline functioning and impact on study/work/relationships/self-care.
- Previous depressive, manic, hypomanic or psychotic episodes; treatment, response, adherence and adverse effects.
- Direct suicide/self-harm assessment and history of violence/risk to others.
- Substance use: alcohol, cannabis, khat, cocaine, amphetamines and prescribed/non-prescribed drugs.
- Family history: depression, bipolar disorder, psychosis, suicide and substance use.
- Medical/neurological history, head injury, medication and pregnancy/perinatal history where relevant.
- Personal/social history: development, trauma, relationships, education, work, finances, housing and support.

### 11.2 Mental state examination

| Domain | Assess |
|---|---|
| Appearance | Grooming, hygiene, dress |
| Behaviour | Agitation, retardation, cooperation, disinhibition |
| Speech | Rate, volume, quantity, pressure |
| Mood | Patient's subjective emotional state |
| Affect | Range, intensity, reactivity, mobility, congruence |
| Thought form | Flight of ideas, slowing, loosening |
| Thought content | Guilt, hopelessness, grandiosity, delusions, suicide |
| Perception | Hallucinations |
| Cognition | Orientation, attention, concentration, memory |
| Insight | Awareness of illness and need for care |
| Judgement | Ability to make safe, reasoned decisions |

### 11.3 Examination and investigations
Vital signs, hydration, nutrition, general and focused neurological examination. Tests answer a clinical question: exclude secondary causes, assess comorbidity or establish treatment baseline. Consider FBC, electrolytes/renal function, LFTs, thyroid function, glucose/HbA1c, calcium, pregnancy test and toxicology as indicated. In first mania with atypical features or later-life onset, exclude neurological, endocrine, infectious, medication and substance causes.

## 12. Differential diagnosis

- **Bipolar vs unipolar depression:** ask about reduced need for sleep without fatigue, increased energy/talkativeness/activity, grandiosity, risk-taking, antidepressant-associated elevation and family history. Early onset, recurrent episodes, psychotic depression and atypical features raise suspicion but do not independently diagnose bipolar disorder.
- **Mania vs ADHD:** mania is episodic, a clear change from baseline, with abnormal mood and reduced need for sleep; ADHD usually begins in childhood and is persistent across settings.
- **Mania vs borderline personality disorder:** mania is a sustained episode with increased energy/activity and characteristic sleep/speech/thought changes; borderline affective shifts are often brief and interpersonal-reactive. They may coexist.
- **Bipolar vs schizoaffective:** bipolar psychosis occurs during mood episodes. Schizoaffective disorder requires at least two weeks of psychosis without a major mood episode, with major mood episodes present for most of the total illness duration.
- **Depression vs adjustment disorder:** adjustment symptoms follow a stressor and do not meet criteria for another mental disorder. A stressor does not exclude MDD.
- **Depression vs schizophrenia-spectrum:** establish whether psychosis persists outside mood episodes and assess formal thought disorder, negative symptoms and longitudinal course.

## 13. Course, prognosis and relapse prevention
- Depression may be single episode, recurrent, chronic, partially remitted or fully remitted.
- Bipolar disorder is commonly recurrent, with episodes and periods of remission; depressive morbidity may dominate.
- Better outcomes: early recognition, appropriate treatment, adherence, social support and reduced substance use.
- Poorer outcomes: recurrent severe episodes, psychosis, previous suicide attempts, substance misuse, medical comorbidity and poor support.
- Maintain appropriate medication, regular sleep/routine, avoid alcohol/recreational drugs, recognise early warning signs and attend follow-up.
- Teach families to recognise reduced sleep, increased talking, irritability, overactivity and impulsive spending as possible early mania signs.
- Develop an individualised relapse plan, including who to contact if symptoms recur.

## 14. High-yield comparisons

### Depression versus mania

| Feature | Depression | Mania |
|---|---|---|
| Mood | Low | Elevated, expansive or irritable |
| Energy | Reduced | Increased |
| Sleep | Insomnia/hypersomnia; often tired | Reduced need; feels rested |
| Speech | Often reduced/slowed | Pressured/increased |
| Thought | Hopeless, guilty, slowed | Racing, flight of ideas, grandiosity |
| Activity | Reduced | Increased |
| Self-esteem | Often low | May be inflated |
| Risk | Suicide/self-neglect | Impulsivity/unsafe behaviour |
| Psychosis | Possible when severe | Possible when severe |

### Bipolar I versus bipolar II

| Feature | Bipolar I | Bipolar II |
|---|---|---|
| Defining elevated episode | Mania | Hypomania |
| Major depression | Not required | Required |
| Full mania ever | Yes | No |
| Psychosis during elevated episode | Possible | Not during hypomania |
| Severity | Mania causes marked impairment | Depression may cause major impairment |

### Insomnia versus reduced need for sleep
- **Insomnia:** wants to sleep but cannot; usually tired next day.
- **Mania:** sleeps very little but feels rested and energetic.

### Diagnostic durations

| Condition | Duration |
|---|---|
| Major depressive episode | ≥2 weeks |
| Mania | ≥1 week, or any duration if hospitalised |
| Hypomania | ≥4 consecutive days |
| PDD (adult) | ≥2 years |
| PDD (child/adolescent) | ≥1 year |
| Cyclothymic disorder (adult) | ≥2 years |
| Cyclothymic disorder (child/adolescent) | ≥1 year |
| Rapid cycling | ≥4 episodes in 12 months |
| Postpartum blues | Usually resolves within 2 weeks |

## 15. Clinical application
1. **24-year-old student:** low mood, anhedonia, fatigue, insomnia, poor concentration and worthlessness for three weeks with missed lectures. Assess nine symptoms, impairment, suicide risk, medical causes and any history of hypomania before diagnosing MDD.
2. **30-year-old man:** nine days of increased energy, pressured speech, grandiosity, reduced need for sleep and excessive spending with major occupational impairment. Consistent with mania; assess safety, substances and medical causes urgently.
3. **26-year-old woman:** major depressive episodes and a previous four-day period of increased energy, reduced need for sleep and unusual productivity, without mania. Consider bipolar II after confirming full episode criteria and excluding alternatives.
4. **Woman on day three postpartum:** tearful and emotionally labile but retains insight/function; symptoms resolve. Consistent with postpartum blues.
5. **Woman five days postpartum:** confusion, severe insomnia, bizarre behaviour and delusions involving newborn. Suspect postpartum psychosis: urgent admission, specialist assessment and immediate mother/infant safety.
6. **Patient with three years of chronic depressive symptoms** develops a full major depressive episode. This is a major depressive episode superimposed on PDD (“double depression”).
