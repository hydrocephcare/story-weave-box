# Nephrotic and nephritic syndromes

*Clinical reasoning notes. The two syndromes are the classic exam confusion: learn the pattern, then the causes.*

**The reasoning chain:** look at the urine and the blood pressure → name the syndrome (nephrotic or nephritic) → find the cause (primary or secondary, with local causes) → confirm with serology and, if needed, biopsy → treat the cause, control oedema, protect the kidney, prevent complications.


![Histopathological image of diabetic glomerulosclerosis with nephrotic syndrome. KGH, CC BY-SA 3.0](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/ea/Diabetic_glomerulosclerosis_%281%29_HE.jpg)

## 1. The core difference

- **Main problem**: Heavy **protein** loss through a leaky filter — **Inflammation** of the glomerulus
- **Urine**: **Protein above 3.5 g/day** (a protein:creatinine ratio above 300 mg/mmol); frothy — **Blood** (red cell casts, dysmorphic red cells), modest protein
- **Blood**: **Low albumin** (below 25–30 g/L), **high cholesterol** — Raised urea and creatinine
- **Oedema**: **Gross** (face, legs, ascites, effusions) — Mild
- **Blood pressure**: Normal or low — **High**
- **Urine volume**: Normal — **Reduced (oliguria)**
- **Typical**: Child: minimal change disease — Child after throat infection: PSGN

**Mnemonic:** nephrotic = **protein** (tonnes of it); nephritic = **blood and pressure**.

## Anatomy and physiology you need  
- **Glomerular filtration barrier (fenestrated endothelium → glomerular basement membrane → podocyte slit diaphragm)**: Loss of selective permeability explains proteinuria in nephrotic syndrome and hematuria in nephritic syndrome.
- **Podocyte foot processes**: Effacement (minimal change disease) or segmental sclerosis (FSGS) directly reduces filtration surface area, driving protein loss.
- **Complement regulatory proteins (factor H, CD46)**: Deficiency or autoantibody‑mediated inhibition leads to uncontrolled C3 activation, precipitating post‑streptococcal or lupus nephritis.
- **Renal autoregulation (myogenic response, tubuloglomerular feedback)**: Failure in nephritic disease (e.g., crescentic GN) causes intraglomerular hypertension, raising systemic BP and reducing urine output.
- **Renin‑angiotensin‑aldosterone system (RAAS)**: Proteinuria induces intrarenal RAAS activation, worsening edema and accelerating progression; ACE‑I/ARB therapy exploits this mechanism.
- **Antithrombin III and protein C/S**: Urinary loss of these anticoagulants in nephrotic syndrome explains the hypercoagulable state and risk of venous thromboembolism.
- **Lipid metabolism in the liver**: Hypoalbuminaemia stimulates hepatic lipoprotein synthesis, causing hypercholesterolaemia and atherogenic risk.

## Pathophysiology  
**Nephrotic syndrome**  
```
Podocyte injury (e.g., effacement, sclerosis) → ↓ filtration barrier integrity
↓ ↓
↑ glomerular permeability to albumin → massive proteinuria
↓ ↓
↓ plasma oncotic pressure → interstitial fluid shift → edema
↓ ↓
↑ hepatic lipoprotein synthesis → hyperlipidaemia
↓ ↓
↑ urinary loss of antithrombin III/protein C → hypercoagulability
```

**Nephritic syndrome**  
```
Immune complex deposition or anti‑GBM antibodies → complement activation (C3/C5 convertase)
↓ ↓
Inflammatory cell influx → glomerular capillary wall damage
↓ ↓
Red cell casts, dysmorphic erythrocytes → hematuria
↓ ↓
Capillary leakage and interstitial edema → hypertension
↓ ↓
Crescent formation (if severe) → rapid loss of filtration surface → oliguria, rising creatinine
```

## 2. Causes of nephrotic syndrome

- **Children:** **minimal change disease** (about 90% under 10 years; responds to steroids; normal light microscopy).
- **Adults:** **focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS)**, **membranous nephropathy**, minimal change.
- **Secondary causes:** **diabetes mellitus** (the commonest worldwide), **HIV-associated nephropathy**, **hepatitis B** and **C**, **malaria (quartan)**, **schistosomiasis**, **SLE**, **amyloidosis**, drugs (NSAIDs, gold, penicillamine), cancers (membranous nephropathy with solid tumours; minimal change with Hodgkin lymphoma).

## 3. Causes of nephritic syndrome

- **Post-streptococcal GN (PSGN)**: 1–3 weeks after a throat or skin infection; **low C3, raised ASO**; usually self-limiting in children
- **IgA nephropathy**: Haematuria **during or just after** an upper respiratory infection; normal complement
- **Rapidly progressive GN (RPGN)**: Creatinine rises over days to weeks; **crescents** on biopsy; causes: anti-GBM (Goodpasture), ANCA vasculitis, immune complex disease
- **Lupus nephritis**: Young woman, arthralgia, rash; **low C3 and C4, anti-dsDNA**
- **Infective endocarditis, hepatitis B/C, HIV, shunt nephritis**: Think of the infection that drives it

## Differential diagnosis and how to tell them apart  

| Condition | Key points that point to it | Features that separate it from nephrotic/nephritic syndromes |
|---|---|---|
| **Minimal change disease** | Children <10 yrs, sudden onset of 4+ protein, normal BP, normal serum creatinine | No hematuria, no crescents, normal light microscopy |
| **Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis** | Adults, heavy proteinuria, hypertension, often steroid‑resistant | Segmental sclerosis on biopsy, normal complement |
| **Membranous nephropathy** | Adults, nephrotic, subnephrotic proteinuria, hypercholesterolaemia, often hepatitis B/C | Thickened GBM on EM, subepithelial spikes |
| **Post‑streptococcal GN** | 1–3 wk after throat/skin infection, low C3, ASO ↑, dysmorphic RBCs | Transient, self‑limited, no proteinuria >3.5 g/day |
| **IgA nephropathy** | Hematuria concurrent with respiratory infection, normal C3 | Mild proteinuria, no hypertension in most |
| **Rapidly progressive GN (anti‑GBM)** | Rapid rise in creatinine, crescents on biopsy, anti‑GBM antibodies | Severe hypertension, oliguria, no significant proteinuria |
| **Lupus nephritis** | Young woman, rash, arthralgia, low C3/C4, anti‑dsDNA ↑ | Multiorgan involvement, serology positive |
| **Diabetic nephropathy** | Long‑standing diabetes, gradual rise in creatinine, microalbuminuria → macroalbuminuria | Hyperglycaemia, retinopathy, no hematuria |
| **Malaria‑associated nephropathy** | Fever, hemolysis, acute kidney injury, proteinuria <3.5 g/day | Parasitemia on peripheral smear, no hypertension |
| **Schistosomiasis‑associated GN** | Exposure to freshwater, eosinophilia, hematuria | Chronic exposure, eosinophilia, no proteinuria >3.5 g/day |

*Exam‑style tip:* When asked to differentiate, focus on the triad of **proteinuria, hematuria, and hypertension** and the **complement profile**. A low C3 with hematuria and hypertension points to post‑streptococcal or lupus; normal C3 with hematuria suggests IgA. Heavy proteinuria with hypoalbuminaemia and hypercholesterolaemia, without hematuria or hypertension, is classic nephrotic.

## 4. Investigate with purpose

- **Urinalysis and microscopy**: Protein (nephrotic) or red cell casts (nephritic)?
- **Urine protein:creatinine ratio, 24-hour protein**: How heavy is the proteinuria?
- **Albumin, cholesterol, creatinine, electrolytes**: Severity of nephrotic syndrome; kidney function
- **Complement C3 and C4**: Low in PSGN, lupus, and some membranoproliferative disease
- **ASO titre, anti-DNase B, throat or skin swab**: Streptococcal link
- **ANA, anti-dsDNA, ANCA, anti-GBM**: Autoimmune cause
- **HIV, hepatitis B and C, malaria tests, blood glucose**: Secondary causes
- **Kidney ultrasound**: Size; obstruction; chronic disease
- **Renal biopsy**: Adults with nephrotic syndrome, any RPGN, steroid-resistant disease, children older than 10

**Where results can mislead:** a **normal creatinine does not exclude significant disease**; **ASO may be normal in skin-related PSGN** (use anti-DNase B); in a child, **do not biopsy first**: treat with steroids and biopsy if there is no response.

## 5. Management

**Nephrotic syndrome**
1. **Oedema:** salt restriction and a loop diuretic. Albumin infusion only for severe, resistant oedema.
2. **Reduce proteinuria and protect the kidney:** an **ACE inhibitor or ARB**.
3. **Treat the cause:** **corticosteroids** for minimal change disease (children respond in most cases), other immunosuppression for FSGS and membranous nephropathy; treat HIV, hepatitis and diabetes.
4. **Prevent complications:** **infection** (pneumococcal vaccine, prompt antibiotics for peritonitis), **venous thrombosis** (loss of antithrombin in the urine: consider anticoagulation in high-risk patients), **hyperlipidaemia** (statin), **acute kidney injury**.

**Nephritic syndrome**
1. **Treat hypertension and fluid overload** (salt and fluid restriction, diuretics, calcium channel blockers).
2. **PSGN:** supportive care and a course of penicillin for the infection; most children recover.
3. **RPGN and lupus nephritis:** an **emergency**: high-dose steroids, cyclophosphamide, plasma exchange for anti-GBM, and renal support. Delay costs kidney function.
4. **Monitor** creatinine, urine output and weight; refer early for dialysis if red flags appear (see the note on acute kidney injury).

## Complications and prognosis  

- **Thrombo‑embolism**: Urinary loss of antithrombin III, protein C, protein S and fibrinogen; hyper‑coagulable plasma viscosity from hypo‑albuminaemia — Deep‑vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolus, renal vein thrombosis — Often within the first 6 months of heavy proteinuria
- **Infectious peritonitis**: Depleted complement factors (especially C3) and immunoglobulins in the urine; ascites provides a culture medium — Fever, abdominal pain, cloudy ascitic fluid; high mortality if delayed — Can occur at any stage, but risk peaks when oedema is severe
- **Acute kidney injury (AKI)**: Intrarenal hypovolaemia from aggressive diuresis, interstitial edema compressing tubules, or superimposed nephritic flare — Rising creatinine, oliguria, need for renal replacement therapy — Usually precipitated by infection, NSAID use or rapid diuresis
- **Progressive chronic kidney disease (CKD)**: Persistent podocyte injury (FSGS, membranous) → glomerulosclerosis; ongoing hypertension and proteinuria accelerate nephron loss — Declining eGFR, eventual dialysis or transplant — Variable; median 5–10 years in untreated membranous, longer in steroid‑responsive minimal change
- **Hyperlipidaemia‑related atherosclerosis**: Hepatic up‑regulation of VLDL synthesis secondary to hypo‑albuminaemia; oxidative modification of lipids — Premature cardiovascular disease, especially in adults with long‑standing nephrosis — Develops over years; risk amplified by steroid therapy
- **Hypovolaemic shock**: Over‑diuresis or albumin loss leading to intravascular depletion despite peripheral oedema — Tachycardia, hypotension, renal hypoperfusion — Acute, often after aggressive diuretic regimens
- **Electrolyte disturbances**: Sodium‑water shifts, loss of potassium and magnesium in urine; ACE‑I/ARB‑induced hyperkalaemia — Muscle weakness, arrhythmias — Can appear early when diuretics are introduced

**Prognostic pearls for the examiner**  
- **Minimal change disease**: excellent prognosis if steroid‑responsive; relapse common but renal function usually preserved.  
- **FSGS**: poorer outcome; >50 % progress to CKD within 5 years if proteinuria persists.  
- **Membranous nephropathy**: 30 % achieve remission with immunosuppression; the remainder have a gradual decline in GFR.  
- **Nephritic entities**: PSGN in children is self‑limited with >90 % full recovery; RPGN carries a high risk of ESRD unless rapid immunosuppression and plasma exchange are instituted.  

When marking, examiners look for: identification of the mechanistic link (e.g., loss of antithrombin → thrombosis), temporal relationship (early AKI vs late CKD), and appropriate risk‑stratification (steroid‑responsive vs steroid‑resistant disease).

## Clinical case  

**Case vignette**  
A 7‑year‑old boy is brought to Kijabe Hospital with a 2‑week history of periorbital puffiness, swollen ankles and a “foamy” urine. He has no fever, cough or sore throat. Blood pressure is 100/60 mmHg. Urinalysis shows 4+ protein, no red cells, and a urine protein‑creatinine ratio of 350 mg/mmol. Serum albumin is 22 g/L, cholesterol 7 mmol/L, creatinine 70 µmol/L. No recent infections are reported.

### Questions  

1. **What is the most likely aetiology of his renal disease and why?**  
   *Model answer*: The presentation fits a primary nephrotic syndrome in a child. The classic cause in this age group is **minimal change disease** – characterised by massive selective proteinuria, hypo‑albuminaemia, hyperlipidaemia, normal blood pressure and preserved renal function. The absence of haematuria or hypertension makes secondary causes (e.g., diabetic nephropathy, lupus) unlikely.

2. **Outline the initial management plan, emphasizing the rationale behind each step.**  
   *Model answer*:  
   - **Corticosteroid therapy** (prednisone) – first‑line for minimal change; induces remission in >80 % of children.  
   - **Salt restriction (<2 g/day) and a loop diuretic** – to control oedema while avoiding intravascular depletion.  
   - **ACE inhibitor or ARB** – reduces intraglomerular pressure, diminishes proteinuria and offers renal protection.  
   - **Vaccination and infection prophylaxis** – pneumococcal vaccine and prompt treatment of any peritoneal infection because of urinary loss of immunoglobulins and complement.  
   - **Monitoring** – daily weight, urine output, serum albumin and creatinine; assess response after 2 weeks of steroids.  

3. **What complications should the examiner expect you to discuss, and how would you mitigate them in this patient?**  
   *Model answer*:  
   - **Thrombo‑embolism** – loss of antithrombin III; mitigate by maintaining serum albumin >25 g/L, early mobilisation, and consider prophylactic low‑molecular‑weight heparin if oedema is severe.  
   - **Infectious peritonitis** – due to complement loss; ensure pneumococcal vaccination, educate caregivers on early signs of abdominal pain, and treat any abdominal infection promptly.  
   - **Steroid‑related side effects** – growth suppression, hyperglycaemia; use the lowest effective dose, supplement calcium/vitamin D, and monitor blood glucose.  

*Key examiner cue*: Demonstrate that you can link the clinical picture to a pathophysiological mechanism, choose the evidence‑based first‑line therapy, and anticipate the most relevant complications with appropriate preventive measures.

## Practice questions

**1.** A 6-year-old has facial puffiness, 4+ protein, albumin 18 g/L and normal blood pressure. The first treatment is:
A. Renal biopsy · B. Plasma exchange · C. Antibiotics only · **D. Corticosteroids** · E. Dialysis
*Answer: D. Minimal change disease is the most likely cause.*

**2.** A child with haematuria, hypertension and low C3 two weeks after a skin infection most likely has:
**A. Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis** · B. IgA nephropathy · C. Minimal change disease · D. Anti-GBM disease · E. FSGS
*Answer: A.*

**3.** Which feature points to nephrotic rather than nephritic syndrome?
A. Hypertension · B. Red cell casts · **C. Heavy proteinuria with hypoalbuminaemia and hypercholesterolaemia** · D. Oliguria · E. Low C3
*Answer: C.*

**4.** Crescents on renal biopsy are the hallmark of:
A. Minimal change disease · **B. Rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis** · C. Membranous nephropathy · D. Diabetic nephropathy · E. Amyloidosis
