# Clinical Clarifications: JVP, Oedema and Heart Murmurs

These notes explain common bedside concepts from the beginning. The aim is to understand *why* a sign occurs, not just memorise a definition.

## 1. Raised JVP: what is the jugular venous pressure?

### Start with the route blood takes

After delivering oxygen to the body, blood returns through veins:

- The upper body drains mainly into the **superior vena cava (SVC)**.
- The lower body drains mainly into the **inferior vena cava (IVC)**.
- Both empty into the **right atrium**, the upper-right chamber of the heart.
- Blood then passes through the tricuspid valve into the right ventricle.

The right ventricle pumps blood to the lungs to receive oxygen.

### Why can the neck veins tell us about the right heart?

The internal jugular veins in the neck are connected, through the large veins, to the right atrium. Pressure changes in the right atrium can therefore be transmitted backwards into the jugular veins.

**JVP means the pressure in the venous system as reflected by the jugular veins.** It is not a pressure caused by the neck veins squeezing or constricting.

Imagine a sink draining through a pipe. If the outlet is blocked or the pump downstream cannot move fluid forward, pressure builds up behind the problem. The fluid does not need to reverse direction for the pressure to rise.

Similarly, if the right side of the heart cannot accept or pump blood forward effectively, blood can accumulate in the veins returning to it. This is **venous congestion**. The jugular veins may become more distended, producing a raised JVP.

### Causes of raised JVP

Common clinical causes include:

- Right-sided heart failure.
- Fluid overload.
- Pulmonary hypertension or severe lung disease causing strain on the right ventricle.
- Tricuspid valve disease.
- Cardiac tamponade or constrictive pericarditis, which can impair filling of the heart.

Raised JVP suggests increased right atrial pressure, but it does not by itself identify the cause.

**Remember:** blood normally flows toward the heart. Raised JVP usually means pressure is backing up, not that all the blood is flowing backwards.

## 2. Oedema: why does fluid collect in tissues?

**Oedema** is excess fluid in the tissues outside blood vessels.

Normally, some fluid moves from tiny blood vessels called capillaries into the surrounding tissues. Much of it returns to the circulation, and the lymphatic system collects and returns excess fluid.

Oedema develops when too much fluid leaves the capillaries, too little returns, or lymphatic drainage cannot remove the excess.

### The main mechanisms

1. **Increased hydrostatic pressure:** pressure inside capillaries pushes more fluid outward. This occurs with venous congestion, including heart failure.
2. **Reduced plasma oncotic pressure:** blood proteins, especially albumin, help keep water within the circulation. Low albumin can allow more fluid to remain in tissues.
3. **Increased capillary permeability:** inflammation or injury makes vessels leakier.
4. **Lymphatic obstruction or failure:** fluid cannot drain away properly.
5. **Sodium and water retention:** the kidneys retain extra salt and water, expanding fluid volume and potentially worsening oedema.

### Is this blood flowing backwards?

Not necessarily. In heart failure, the main issue may be a **backlog of pressure** in the veins. That elevated pressure is transmitted to capillaries and pushes more fluid out into the tissues. The fluid that causes oedema is mainly fluid from the blood plasma, not whole red blood cells leaking out.

If fluid leaves the capillaries faster than the lymphatics can return it, fluid accumulates in the tissues.

### Pitting versus non-pitting oedema

**Pitting oedema:** press a finger over a swollen area, often the shin, for several seconds. A dent remains temporarily after you lift your finger. This often reflects relatively mobile excess interstitial fluid.

Possible causes:
- Heart failure and venous congestion.
- Kidney disease or salt-and-water retention.
- Liver disease or low albumin.
- Chronic venous insufficiency.

**Non-pitting oedema:** pressing does not leave a persistent dent. This may occur when tissue contains substances or structural changes that hold fluid in place, or when lymphatic drainage is impaired.

Possible causes:
- Lymphoedema.
- Myxoedema associated with hypothyroidism.
- Pretibial myxoedema associated with Graves disease.

These are clues, not absolute rules. Early or mixed forms can behave differently.

## 3. What is myxoedema?

The term can be confusing because it is used in two related but distinct settings.

### Generalised myxoedema in severe hypothyroidism

The thyroid gland produces hormones that help regulate metabolism. In longstanding or severe hypothyroidism, certain complex sugars called **glycosaminoglycans** accumulate in the skin and other tissues. They attract and hold water.

This produces firm, doughy swelling that is often **non-pitting**. Other features of hypothyroidism may include feeling unusually cold, constipation, dry skin, slowed thinking, a slow pulse and weight gain.

### Pretibial myxoedema in Graves disease

This is a different condition, often associated with Graves disease. It produces thickened skin, especially over the shins, due to immune-related tissue changes. A person can have pretibial myxoedema in Graves disease even though Graves commonly causes an overactive rather than underactive thyroid.

**Key distinction:** ordinary pitting oedema often reflects excess mobile fluid; myxoedema involves material accumulating in the tissue that holds water, so the swelling may not pit.

## 4. Heart murmurs: understand the timing first

A **murmur** is an extra sound produced by turbulent blood flow. It may occur when blood passes through a narrowed valve, leaks through a valve that should be closed, or flows through an abnormal opening.

### The two phases of the heartbeat

- **Systole:** the ventricles squeeze and pump blood out.
- **Diastole:** the ventricles relax and fill with blood.

The normal heart sounds help mark the phases:

- **S1** is mainly closure of the mitral and tricuspid valves. It marks the beginning of ventricular systole.
- **S2** is mainly closure of the aortic and pulmonary valves. It marks the end of systole and the beginning of diastole.

During systole, the mitral and tricuspid valves should be closed, while the aortic and pulmonary valves open for ejection.

During diastole, the aortic and pulmonary valves should be closed, while the mitral and tricuspid valves open for filling.

### Two terms that explain many murmurs

- **Stenosis:** a valve is narrowed, making forward flow difficult.
- **Regurgitation:** a valve fails to close properly, allowing blood to leak backwards.

A murmur's timing helps you work out which valve or abnormal opening might be involved.

### Common murmur patterns

**Ejection systolic (midsystolic):** occurs during the period when blood is being pumped out through the aortic or pulmonary valve. A narrowed outflow valve creates turbulent flow. It often gets louder and then softer (crescendo–decrescendo). Classic example: **aortic stenosis**.

**Pansystolic (holosystolic):** occurs throughout systole, from S1 to S2. Examples include **mitral regurgitation**, **tricuspid regurgitation** and a **ventricular septal defect (VSD)**. In mitral regurgitation, the valve should be closed during systole but leaks, so the murmur continues through the squeeze.

**Late systolic:** occurs toward the end of systole. One classic association is **mitral valve prolapse**, where the valve bulges backwards and may leak late in systole.

**Mid-diastolic:** occurs while the ventricles are filling. A narrowed mitral or tricuspid valve makes blood flow noisily through the opening. Example: **mitral stenosis**, often described as a low-pitched rumble.

**Early diastolic:** occurs just after S2, when the ventricles are relaxing. If the aortic valve does not close properly, blood leaks from the aorta back into the left ventricle. This produces the classic blowing, decrescendo murmur of **aortic regurgitation**. Pulmonary regurgitation can produce a similar timing on the right side.

### A simple timeline

- **S1 → systole → S2:** murmurs here are systolic.
- **S2 → diastole → next S1:** murmurs here are diastolic.

Stenosis and regurgitation are useful patterns, but not every murmur follows a single textbook pattern. Always combine timing with location, radiation, pitch, intensity, manoeuvres and the patient's symptoms.

## 5. Bedside reasoning: connect the signs

A patient with raised JVP and bilateral pitting ankle oedema may have systemic venous congestion, such as right-sided heart failure. Raised venous pressure promotes fluid movement out of capillaries into tissues.

However, oedema alone does not prove heart failure, and a raised JVP alone does not establish the exact diagnosis. Examine the patient as a whole and use investigations guided by the clinical picture.

### Quick recap

- **Raised JVP:** elevated venous pressure reflected in the neck veins; often a sign of raised right atrial pressure.
- **Pitting oedema:** often excess mobile fluid in tissues.
- **Myxoedema:** tissue changes involving glycosaminoglycans that bind water; often non-pitting in hypothyroidism.
- **Systole:** ventricles squeeze.
- **Diastole:** ventricles relax and fill.
- **Stenosis:** narrowed valve.
- **Regurgitation:** leaking valve.
- **Pansystolic murmur:** lasts through systole.
- **Early diastolic murmur:** begins just after S2.

*These notes support learning and do not replace assessment of an actual patient by the treating clinical team.*
