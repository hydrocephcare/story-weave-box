# Acute rheumatic fever and rheumatic heart disease — Year 4 Internal Medicine (Cardiovascular) Notes

*Clinical reasoning notes. Rheumatic heart disease is the commonest cause of valve disease in young people across much of Africa and Asia.*

**The reasoning chain:** was there a recent group A streptococcal infection → do the Jones criteria fit → how badly is the heart involved → treat the attack, then prevent the next one → follow the valves for life.

## 1. What it is

**Acute rheumatic fever (ARF)** is an autoimmune reaction, 2 to 4 weeks after a **group A streptococcal throat infection** (and in some settings skin infection), mostly in children 5 to 15 years old. Antibodies against the streptococcus cross-react with heart, joint, brain and skin tissue. **Repeated attacks scar the heart valves** and cause **rheumatic heart disease (RHD)**, most often **mitral stenosis**, then mitral regurgitation and aortic valve disease.

## Anatomy and physiology you need  

| Structure / Physiology | Why it matters for ARF / RHD | Clinical implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Mucosal immunity of the oropharynx** – tonsillar crypts, secretory IgA, M‑cell sampling | First exposure to *Streptococcus pyogenes* occurs here; inadequate clearance allows a robust humoral response that later cross‑reacts with host tissues. | History of sore throat or scarlet fever; importance of prompt antibiotic therapy. |
| **M‑protein of GAS** – surface fibrillar protein with epitopes that mimic cardiac myosin, laminin, and valve‑specific proteins (e.g., α‑actinin). | Molecular mimicry drives production of cross‑reactive IgG that binds to endocardial endothelium and myocardial tissue. | Basis of Jones criteria (carditis, chorea). |
| **Valvular architecture** – mitral valve leaflets (fibrosa, atrialis, ventricularis) rich in collagen and elastin; aortic valve similar but less exposed to high pressure gradients in children. | Auto‑antibodies and T‑cell mediated inflammation preferentially target the mitral valve’s connective tissue, leading to Aschoff bodies, fibrosis, and eventual stenosis. | Murmur of mitral stenosis (diastolic rumble) is the classic late finding. |
| **Cardiac conduction system** – AV node and proximal His‑Purkinje network. | Inflammatory infiltrates can prolong the PR interval (first‑degree AV block) without overt heart failure. | PR prolongation counts as a minor criterion; ECG helps detect silent carditis. |
| **Synovial membrane of large joints** – lined by type‑B synoviocytes, rich in fibrinous exudate. | Immune complexes deposit in the synovium, causing a transient, migratory polyarthritis that resolves within weeks. | Joint swelling is a major criterion; rapid response to aspirin may mask it. |
| **Basal ganglia (caudate/putamen)** – dopaminergic pathways modulated by GABAergic interneurons. | Anti‑neuronal antibodies (e.g., anti‑lysoganglioside) disrupt basal ganglia signaling, producing Sydenham chorea. | Chorea may appear months after the initial infection; important for delayed diagnosis. |
| **Skin microvasculature** – dermal capillaries with perivascular connective tissue. | Immune‑mediated vasculitis produces erythema marginatum, a fleeting rash that spares the face. | Recognising the rash helps fulfill major criteria, especially in high‑risk settings. |

## Pathophysiology  

**Group A Streptococcus infection** → **M‑protein‑specific B‑cell activation** → **Molecular mimicry → cross‑reactive IgG & CD4⁺ Th1 cells** → **Immune complex deposition & cytokine storm (TNF‑α, IFN‑γ, IL‑1β)** → **Endothelial activation in heart, joints, brain, skin** → **Aschoff bodies (granulomatous inflammation) in myocardium** → **Valve leaflet edema → fibrin deposition → fibrosis & commissural fusion** → **Clinical manifestations:**  

- **Carditis** (murmur, PR prolongation, heart failure)  
- **Migratory polyarthritis** (large joints, transient)  
- **Sydenham chorea** (hyperkinetic movements)  
- **Erythema marginatum** (annular rash)  
- **Subcutaneous nodules** (granulomatous lesions)  

Repeated episodes → progressive **valvular scarring** → **mitral stenosis** (diastolic rumble, left atrial enlargement) → **atrial fibrillation** → **thrombo‑embolic stroke**.

## 2. Jones criteria (2015) for the first attack

**Evidence of a preceding group A streptococcal infection** (raised **ASO or anti-DNase B**, positive throat culture or rapid antigen test, or recent scarlet fever) **plus**:

- **2 major criteria**, or
- **1 major and 2 minor criteria**.

| Major criteria | Minor criteria |
|---|---|
| **Carditis** (including silent carditis seen on echocardiography) | **Fever** |
| **Arthritis** (a migratory polyarthritis of large joints) | **Arthralgia** (joint pain without swelling) |
| **Sydenham chorea** (jerky, purposeless movements) | **Raised ESR or CRP** |
| **Erythema marginatum** (pink rings on the trunk, not the face) | **Prolonged PR interval** on ECG |
| **Subcutaneous nodules** (painless, over bony prominences) | |

In **high-risk populations** the thresholds for fever, joint involvement and inflammatory markers are lower, and **subclinical carditis on echocardiography counts** as a major criterion.

## Differential diagnosis and how to tell them apart  

| Condition | Points that favour it | Key feature that separates it from ARF |
|---|---|---|
| **Post‑streptococcal glomerulonephritis** | Recent GAS infection, hematuria, edema, hypertension | Renal involvement dominates; no migratory arthritis or chorea; low ASO may be present but urine shows RBC casts. |
| **Septic arthritis** | Acute mono‑articular pain, fever, elevated WBC, positive joint aspirate | Joint fluid is purulent with high neutrophils; arthritis does **not** migrate and does not respond rapidly to aspirin. |
| **Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)** | Polyarthritis, fever, rash, positive ANA, low complement | Rash is malar or discoid, not annular marginatum; serology (ANA, anti‑dsDNA) positive; no preceding streptococcal infection. |
| **Juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA)** | Persistent arthritis >6 weeks, morning stiffness, ANA may be positive | Arthritis is chronic, not migratory; no carditis, chorea, or elevated streptococcal titres. |
| **Infective endocarditis** | Fever, new murmur, positive blood cultures, embolic phenomena | Persistent bacteremia, vegetations on echo, usually no migratory arthritis or chorea; ASO may be normal. |
| **Kawasaki disease** | Fever >5 days, conjunctival injection, mucosal changes, cervical lymphadenopathy | Coronary artery aneurysms on echo; rash is polymorphous, not marginatum; occurs in <5 yr age group. |
| **Sydenham’s chorea alone (isolated)** | Isolated choreiform movements, history of GAS infection months earlier | Absence of carditis, arthritis, rash, or subcutaneous nodules; EEG may show basal ganglia involvement. |

## 3. Investigate with purpose

| Test | The question it answers |
|---|---|
| **ASO titre, anti-DNase B** | Was there a recent streptococcal infection? |
| **ESR and CRP** | Is there systemic inflammation (a minor criterion)? |
| **ECG** | Is the PR interval prolonged? Any heart block? |
| **Echocardiography** | Is there valve disease or silent carditis? How severe is it? |
| **Blood cultures** | Is this endocarditis instead? |
| **Throat swab** | Is the organism still there? |

## 4. Treatment

1. **Rest** while there is carditis, and **treat heart failure** if present.
2. **Eradicate the streptococcus** with a course of **penicillin** (a macrolide if penicillin-allergic).
3. **Anti-inflammatory therapy:** aspirin or another anti-inflammatory for arthritis; **corticosteroids** for severe carditis.
4. **Chorea:** reassurance and, if severe, a drug such as valproate or carbamazepine.

## 5. Secondary prevention: the part that saves hearts

**Long-term penicillin prophylaxis** (a monthly injection of benzathine penicillin, or oral penicillin) prevents recurrences and further valve damage. The duration depends on carditis and valve disease: at least 5 years or until age 21 for those without carditis, longer if the heart is involved, and often for life in severe disease.

**Primary prevention:** treat streptococcal sore throats properly.

## 6. Complications

Heart failure, **mitral stenosis** with atrial fibrillation and **stroke**, infective endocarditis, and in pregnancy a risk of decompensation. Severe valve disease needs **balloon valvotomy or valve surgery**.

## 7. Where tests can mislead

- A **normal ASO does not exclude ARF** (it may be low late in the illness or after starting antibiotics): repeat it, and check anti-DNase B.
- **Arthritis improves within hours of aspirin**, which can hide the diagnosis if given before you have seen the joints.
- Silent carditis can only be found by echocardiography.

## Clinical case

A 12‑year‑old boy from a rural Kenyan village presents with a 4‑day history of fever, sore throat, and a sudden swelling of the right knee and left ankle. He reports a “stiffness” in his fingers that started the previous night. On examination he has a temperature of 38.8 °C, a faint erythematous rash over the trunk, a holosystolic murmur best heard at the apex, and a soft, non‑tender subcutaneous nodule over the left clavicle. His throat swab is negative for GAS, but ASO titre is 400 IU/mL and anti‑DNase B is 650 IU/mL. ECG shows a PR interval of 200 ms. Transthoracic echocardiography reveals mild mitral regurgitation and a small pericardial effusion.

### Questions and model answers

| # | Question | Model answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in the management of this patient? | **Initiate a full course of intramuscular benzathine penicillin G and start high‑dose aspirin for the arthritis, while arranging a repeat echocardiogram in 6 weeks to assess progression of the mitral regurgitation.** |
| 2 | Explain the immunopathogenic mechanism that leads from a GAS throat infection to the cardiac manifestations seen in this case. | **Molecular mimicry: antibodies raised against the M‑protein of GAS cross‑react with cardiac myosin and endothelial antigens, activating complement and recruiting neutrophils, which cause myofibrillar necrosis and valvular scarring.** |
| 3 | In this patient, which of the Jones criteria is fulfilled by the presence of a PR interval of 200 ms? | **Prolonged PR interval is a minor criterion (electrocardiographic evidence of atrioventricular block).**

## Practice questions

**1.** A 9-year-old has a migrating polyarthritis, fever and a raised ASO two weeks after a sore throat. The diagnosis needs a second major criterion. Which is one?
A. Raised ESR · **B. Carditis** · C. Arthralgia · D. Fever · E. Prolonged PR interval
*Answer: B. ESR, arthralgia, fever and a long PR interval are minor criteria.*

**2.** The valve most often damaged in rheumatic heart disease is:
A. Tricuspid · B. Pulmonary · C. Aortic · D. Eustachian · **E. Mitral**
*Answer: E.*

**3.** The aim of long-term penicillin prophylaxis after rheumatic fever is to:
A. Treat chorea · B. Reduce fever · C. Treat arthritis · **D. Prevent recurrent attacks and further valve damage** · E. Cure existing valve disease
*Answer: D.*
