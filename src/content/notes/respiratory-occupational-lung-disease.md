# Occupational Lung Disease — Year 4 Internal Medicine (Respiratory) Notes

*Orientation notes for study. Check Kenyan occupational health law and local guidelines for notification and compensation.*

## Definition

**Occupational lung diseases** are lung conditions **caused or made worse by exposure to dusts, fumes, gases, vapours or biological agents at work**. They are **largely preventable** and are often **missed** because nobody asks about work.

## Anatomy and physiology you need  

| Structure / Function | Why it matters for occupational lung disease |
|---|---|
| **Alveolar‑capillary membrane** – thin type I pneumocytes, basement membrane, capillary endothelium | Site of gas exchange; fibrosis or inflammation (silicosis, asbestosis, HP) thickens this barrier → ↓ diffusion, hypoxaemia, exertional dyspnoea. |
| **Terminal bronchioles and respiratory bronchioles** – smooth‑muscle‑rich airways without cartilage | Early target of inhaled irritants (isocyanates, wood dust). Hyper‑reactivity here produces occupational asthma and chronic bronchitis. |
| **Macrophage‑mediated clearance** – alveolar macrophages ingest particles, present antigens, release cytokines | Inorganic dusts that are **non‑soluble** (silica, asbestos) resist clearance → macrophage activation → release of TNF‑α, IL‑1, growth factors → fibroblast proliferation and progressive fibrosis. |
| **Lymphatic drainage of the pleura** – stomata on parietal pleura, subpleural lymphatics | Asbestos fibres migrate to pleural space, provoke chronic inflammation → pleural plaques, diffuse thickening, and mesothelioma. |
| **Pulmonary surfactant system** – type II pneumocytes produce phospholipid film | Toxic gases (chlorine, ammonia) denature surfactant → alveolar collapse, acute respiratory distress, and later fibrosis if exposure persists. |
| **Immune complex formation in the interstitium** – Th1‑mediated granulomatous response | Organic antigens (mould spores, bird proteins) trigger type III/IV hypersensitivity → hypersensitivity pneumonitis with lymphocytic alveolitis, granulomas, and eventual fibrosis. |
| **Ventilation‑perfusion (V/Q) matching** – regional differences in perfusion and ventilation | Upper‑zone predominance of silicosis reflects higher ventilation relative to perfusion in apices, allowing more particle deposition; lower‑zone asbestosis mirrors greater perfusion and particle settling in bases. |
| **Airway smooth‑muscle tone regulation** – cholinergic, histaminergic, leukotriene pathways | Occupational asthmatic agents provoke bronchoconstriction via mast‑cell degranulation; serial peak‑flow variation reflects reversible airway obstruction. |

## Pathophysiology  

**Inorganic dust (silica, asbestos, coal)** → inhalation → deposition in distal airways → **macrophage activation** → release of **pro‑fibrotic cytokines (TNF‑α, TGF‑β, PDGF)** → **fibroblast proliferation & collagen deposition** → **interstitial fibrosis** → ↓ lung compliance, restrictive spirometry, impaired DLCO → **dyspnoea, crackles, progressive massive fibrosis**.  

**Asbestos fibres** → trans‑pleural migration → chronic pleural inflammation → **fibroblast activation** → **pleural plaques / diffuse thickening** → restrictive defect + risk of **mesothelioma** (malignant transformation of mesothelial cells).  

**Organic antigens (mould, bird proteins)** → inhalation → **immune complex (type III) formation** → complement activation → neutrophil influx → alveolar wall injury → **lymphocytic alveolitis (type IV)** → granuloma formation → **fibrosis** if exposure persists → **HP** (acute flu‑like illness → chronic interstitial disease).  

**Sensitising low‑molecular‑weight chemicals (isocyanates, latex)** → hapten formation → **IgE‑mediated (type I) mast‑cell degranulation** → bronchoconstriction, airway hyper‑responsiveness → **occupational asthma** (reversible obstruction, diurnal/weekly variation).  

**Toxic gases (chlorine, ammonia)** → direct epithelial injury → loss of surfactant → **alveolar collapse** → acute inflammatory exudate → **chemical pneumonitis** → possible progression to **fibrosis** if repeated exposure.  

**Silica + TB** → silica impairs macrophage killing → **facilitates Mycobacterium tuberculosis replication** → synergistic increase in TB incidence (silico‑tuberculosis).

## Main groups

1. **Pneumoconioses:** fibrosis from **inorganic dust** (silica, asbestos, coal).
2. **Occupational asthma:** asthma **caused by a workplace agent**.
3. **Hypersensitivity pneumonitis:** immune reaction to **organic dusts** (see also the ILD note).
4. **Occupational COPD and chronic bronchitis:** dusts and fumes.
5. **Occupational cancers:** lung cancer and **mesothelioma**.
6. **Acute toxic inhalation:** chlorine, ammonia, smoke, nitrogen oxides.
7. **Infection risk:** TB in miners and health workers, anthrax and brucellosis (animal workers).

## The pneumoconioses

| Disease | Agent | Typical workers | Features |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Silicosis** | **Silica (quartz) dust** | **Miners, quarry and stone workers, sandblasters, tunnellers, ceramics, masons** | **Upper-zone nodules**, **eggshell calcification** of hilar nodes, progressive massive fibrosis; **high risk of TB (silico-tuberculosis)**, lung cancer, COPD, autoimmune disease |
| **Asbestosis** | **Asbestos fibres** (roofing, insulation, brakes, shipbuilding, demolition) | Construction and building workers, mechanics, insulators | **Lower-zone fibrosis**, **pleural plaques** (calcified, marker of exposure), **diffuse pleural thickening**, **benign pleural effusion**; risk of **mesothelioma** and **lung cancer** (especially with smoking) |
| **Coal worker's pneumoconiosis** | Coal dust | Coal miners | Small upper-zone nodules, **progressive massive fibrosis**, **Caplan syndrome** (rheumatoid arthritis + nodules) |
| **Berylliosis** | Beryllium | Aerospace, electronics, nuclear | **Granulomas** that look like sarcoid |
| **Siderosis, stannosis, baritosis** | Iron, tin, barium | Welders, miners | Radiological change with little functional impairment |

## Occupational asthma

- **Agents:** **isocyanates** (spray painting), **flour and grain dust** (bakers), **latex** (health workers), **wood dust**, **solder flux**, **animal proteins**, **enzymes**, **pesticides**.
- **Clue:** symptoms **improve at weekends and holidays and worsen at work**; **serial peak flow** records at work and off work show the pattern.
- **Management:** **early diagnosis, remove from exposure** (earlier removal gives a better outcome), usual asthma treatment.

## Organic dust diseases

- **Byssinosis:** **cotton, flax, hemp** workers; **"Monday morning" chest tightness** that eases later in the week; progresses to chronic obstruction.
- **Farmer's lung:** **mouldy hay** (*Saccharopolyspora rectivirgula*, *Thermoactinomyces*); acute flu-like illness 4–8 hours after exposure, chronic fibrosis.
- **Bagassosis:** mouldy **sugar cane waste** (relevant to **sugar-belt** workers in western Kenya).
- **Bird fancier's lung, mushroom worker's lung, malt worker's lung.**
- **Grain and cotton dust** also cause **chronic bronchitis** and **organic dust toxic syndrome** (non-immune fever).

## Occupational cancers

- **Asbestos → mesothelioma** (latency 20–40 years, pleural, poor prognosis), lung cancer.
- **Silica, arsenic, chromium, nickel, radon, diesel fumes** → lung cancer.

## Clinical approach

- **Always take an occupational history:** every job ever held, duties, **dust and fume exposure, protection used**, **latency** (often **many years**), symptoms relating to work, **coworkers affected**, hobbies.
- **Examination:** clubbing, crackles, wheeze, pleural signs.
- **Investigations:** **chest X-ray** (use ILO classification for pneumoconioses), **HRCT**, **spirometry and DLCO**, **serial peak flow** (occupational asthma), **TB screening** in silica-exposed workers, **specific IgE or challenge tests**, **biopsy** if needed, **mineral analysis**.

## Investigations: what each test answers  

| Test | Question it answers | Interpretation relevant to occupational lung disease |
|---|---|---|
| Chest X‑ray (PA & lateral) | Is there radiographic evidence of dust‑related change? | Upper‑zone nodules → silicosis; lower‑zone reticular pattern → asbestosis; pleural plaques → asbestos exposure |
| High‑resolution CT (HRCT) | What is the pattern and distribution of parenchymal disease? | Centrilobular nodules & emphysema → coal; subpleural fibrosis & honey‑comb → asbestosis; mosaic attenuation & ground‑glass → HP |
| Spirometry with bronchodilator | Is the defect obstructive, restrictive, or mixed? | Restrictive (↓FVC, normal/↑FEV₁/FVC) → pneumoconiosis; reversible obstruction → occupational asthma |
| Diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO) | Is gas transfer impaired? | Reduced DLCO → interstitial fibrosis (silicosis, asbestosis, HP); normal DLCO in pure airway disease |
| Broncho‑alveolar lavage (BAL) cell count | What is the inflammatory profile? | Lymphocytosis > 30 % → HP; neutrophilia → toxic inhalation; macrophage predominance → simple dust exposure |
| Serum precipitating antibodies (precipitins) | Is there an immune response to specific organic antigens? | Positive precipitin to *Thermoactinomyces* → farmer’s lung; bird antigen → bird‑fancier’s lung |
| Serial peak‑flow monitoring (work vs off‑work) | Does airway calibre vary with exposure? | ≥ 20 % fall during work days → occupational asthma |
| Occupational exposure assessment (air sampling, personal dosimetry) | What is the quantitative level of hazardous particles? | Confirms exceeding of permissible exposure limits; guides compensation claims |
| Tuberculin skin test / Interferon‑γ release assay | Is latent or active TB present in silica‑exposed workers? | Positive test + compatible CXR → silico‑TB; warrants chemoprophylaxis |
| Pleural fluid cytology | Is pleural effusion malignant? | Malignant mesothelial cells → mesothelioma; otherwise benign exudate in asbestosis |
| Lung biopsy (VATS) | What is the definitive histopathology? | Silicotic nodules with birefringent silica; asbestos bodies (Ferruginous) in tissue; non‑caseating granulomas in HP. |
| Pulmonary function trend (serial spirometry) | Is disease progressing despite exposure control? | Decline in FVC/DLCO over 6‑12 months signals progressive fibrosis; informs prognosis and work‑restriction decisions. |

## Management and prevention

- **No cure for established pneumoconiosis:** **stop or reduce exposure**, **stop smoking**, **treat complications** (TB, COPD, cancer), **vaccinations**, **oxygen** and rehabilitation, palliative care.
- **Prevention is the key:**
  - **Hierarchy of control:** eliminate or substitute the hazard → **engineering** controls (**wet drilling, dust extraction, enclosure, ventilation**) → administrative controls (limit exposure time) → **personal protective equipment** (respirators/masks) last.
  - **Pre-employment and regular medical surveillance**, **air monitoring**, worker education.
  - **Ban or restrict** hazardous substances (asbestos).
- **Notification and compensation** (in Kenya, the **Occupational Safety and Health Act 2007** and the **Work Injury Benefits Act 2007** provide for notification of occupational disease and compensation).

## Exam points

- **Always ask about work.**
- **Silica = upper zones, eggshell calcification, TB risk; asbestos = lower zones, pleural plaques, mesothelioma.**
- **Occupational asthma:** better on days off.
- **Prevention is by controlling exposure at source.**
- **Silicosis and TB** often occur together in miners.

## Differential diagnosis and how to tell them apart  

| Condition | Key clinical / exposure clues | Distinguishing feature |
|---|---|---|
| Silicosis | Upper‑zone nodules, stone‑quarry/mining, long latency, eggshell hilar calcification | **Eggshell calcification** of hilar nodes; high TB risk |
| Asbestosis | Lower‑zone fibrosis, shipyard/insulation work, pleural plaques | **Calcified pleural plaques** on CT; risk of mesothelioma |
| Coal‑worker’s pneumoconiosis | Upper‑zone small nodules, coal mining, possible massive fibrosis | **Caplan syndrome** (RA + nodules) if present |
| Chronic hypersensitivity pneumonitis | Farm/bird exposure, acute flu‑like episodes 4‑8 h after exposure, granulomas | **Lymphocytosis > 30 % on BAL**; precipitating antibodies |
| Occupational asthma | Variable wheeze, improves on weekends/holidays, isocyanate/flour exposure | **Serial peak‑flow variability** > 20 % between work/off days |
| COPD from dust/fumes | Chronic productive cough, > 10 y smoking + dust, fixed obstruction | **Post‑bronchodilator FEV₁/FVC < 0.70** with minimal reversibility |
| Tuberculosis (silico‑TB) | Fever, night sweats, weight loss, positive Mantoux, silica exposure | **Positive sputum AFB**; cavitary lesions on CXR, not just nodules |
| Mesothelioma | Pleural effusion, asbestos exposure, latency > 20 y | **Pleural thickening encasing lung** on CT; malignant cells in pleural fluid |

## Complications and prognosis  

| Complication | Pathophysiology | Clinical impact | Typical time‑frame |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Progressive massive fibrosis (PMF)** | Coalescence of nodules → dense collagenous mass, usually in upper zones (silica, coal) or lower zones (asbestos) | Severe restrictive defect, hypoxaemia, right‑heart strain, high mortality | 5–15 yr after radiographic onset; accelerated if exposure continues |
| **Silico‑tuberculosis** | Silica impairs macrophage killing, facilitates *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* replication | Chronic cough, weight loss, cavitary disease; synergistic increase in mortality | Often within 2–10 yr of silicosis diagnosis |
| **Pulmonary hypertension** | Chronic hypoxic vasoconstriction + loss of capillary bed from fibrosis | Exertional dyspnoea out of proportion to spirometry, right‑sided edema | Late stage, 10–20 yr after disease onset |
| **Lung cancer** | Carcinogenic dust (silica, asbestos, diesel) + chronic inflammation | New infiltrate, weight loss, haemoptysis; prognosis poorer than idiopathic lung cancer | 15–30 yr latency; risk proportional to cumulative exposure |
| **Malignant mesothelioma** | Asbestos fibres provoke chronic pleural inflammation → mesothelial transformation | Pleural effusion, chest pain, weight loss; median survival < 12 months | 20–40 yr after asbestos exposure |
| **Autoimmune disease** (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis, systemic sclerosis) | Silica acts as adjuvant, breaking tolerance | Polyarthritis, skin thickening; may worsen pulmonary fibrosis | Can appear years after exposure; prevalence higher in silicosis |
| **Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)** | Dust‑induced airway inflammation + smoking synergy | Fixed airflow obstruction, frequent exacerbations | Often concurrent; worsens functional reserve |

**Prognostic pearls for the examiner**  

* The dominant determinant of survival is **extent of fibrosis** on HRCT (PMF = worst).  
* **Continued exposure** after diagnosis shortens survival by 2–3 years on average.  
* Presence of **TB** or **malignancy** overrides the natural history of the pneumoconiosis – the examiner expects you to prioritize treatment of these complications.  
* **DLCO** falling below 40 % predicted is a red flag for impending respiratory failure and is often asked as a trigger for referral for lung transplantation (rare in Kenya but conceptually important).

## Clinical case  

**Case vignette**  
A 48‑year‑old male stone‑cutting supervisor presents with a 9‑month history of progressive exertional dyspnoea and a dry cough. He reports that his symptoms are *worse* after a week of intensive drilling and improve during a two‑week holiday. He quit smoking 5 years ago (10 pack‑years). Examination shows digital clubbing, fine inspiratory crackles at the lung bases, and reduced breath sounds in the right lower zone. Chest X‑ray reveals bilateral lower‑zone reticular opacities with pleural plaques. HRCT confirms subpleural fibrosis and calcified pleural plaques. Spirometry: FVC 62 % predicted, FEV₁/FVC 0.78, DLCO 48 % predicted.  

### Questions  

1. **What is the most likely occupational lung disease and why?**  
   *Answer:* Asbestosis. The occupational history (stone‑cutting with use of asbestos‑containing cutting fluids), lower‑zone fibrosis, and characteristic pleural plaques point to asbestos exposure.  

2. **Identify two major complications this patient is at risk of developing and the investigations you would use to screen for them.**  
   *Answer:* (a) Malignant mesothelioma – screen with contrast‑enhanced CT of the thorax and periodic pleural fluid cytology if effusion appears. (b) Tuberculosis – perform interferon‑γ release assay (IGRA) and sputum microscopy/culture because silica‑containing dust often co‑exists with asbestos and raises TB risk.  

3. **Outline the immediate management steps, emphasizing both disease‑specific and general measures.**  
   *Answer:*  
   * Stop further asbestos exposure (relocate away from cutting work, ensure proper wet‑cutting and ventilation).  
   * Counsel on smoking abstinence and vaccinate against influenza and pneumococcus.  
   * Initiate pulmonary rehabilitation and prescribe long‑term oxygen if PaO₂ < 55 mmHg.  
   * Arrange baseline HRCT and serial DLCO to monitor progression.  
   * Discuss surveillance for mesothelioma (annual low‑dose CT) and TB screening.

## Tricky questions examiners ask  

**Q1.** A miner with upper‑zone nodules on CXR presents with a chronic cough but normal spirometry. What is the next investigative step and why?  
**Answer:** Perform a high‑resolution CT scan. HRCT is more sensitive than plain radiography for early interstitial change and can detect subtle fibrosis or PMF that may not yet affect spirometric indices.

**Q2.** Two workers present with similar restrictive patterns; one has a history of sandblasting, the other of shipyard insulation work. Which radiographic feature helps you separate silicosis from asbestosis?  
**Answer:** Egg‑shell calcification of hilar lymph nodes favors silicosis, whereas pleural plaques (especially calcified) are characteristic of asbestosis.

**Q3.** A patient with known asbestos exposure develops a unilateral pleural effusion. Why should you avoid immediately labeling it as “benign asbestos effusion”?  
**Answer:** Because malignant mesothelioma can initially present with a small effusion; cytological analysis and imaging are required to exclude malignancy before assuming a benign cause.

**Q4.** In a worker with occupational asthma, why is a specific IgE test sometimes less useful than serial peak‑flow monitoring?  
**Answer:** Many occupational asthmatic agents (e.g., isocyanates) act via non‑IgE mechanisms; serial peak‑flow records demonstrate the work‑related pattern directly, whereas IgE may be negative.

**Q5.** A coal miner with a small nodule on chest X‑ray is being evaluated for compensation. Which classification system must you use and what is its purpose?  
**Answer:** The International Labour Organization (ILO) classification for pneumoconioses. It standardises radiographic grading, allowing objective comparison across cases for legal and compensation purposes.

**Q6.** A patient with silicosis is started on anti‑TB therapy after a positive sputum smear. Why should you be cautious about drug‑induced hepatotoxicity in this context?  
**Answer:** Silicosis patients often have concomitant alcohol use or malnutrition, both of which increase the risk of isoniazid‑ or rifampicin‑related liver injury; baseline liver function tests and close monitoring are essential.

## Practice questions

**1.** A 55-year-old retired stone-quarry worker has upper-zone nodules and eggshell calcification of hilar nodes on chest X-ray. The disease with the highest associated risk of infection is:
**A. Tuberculosis** · B. Pneumococcal pneumonia · C. Candida · D. PCP · E. Histoplasmosis
*Answer: A.*

**2.** A bakery worker wheezes at work, improving on holidays. The most likely cause is:
A. COPD · B. **Occupational asthma (flour dust)** · C. Silicosis · D. Asbestosis · E. Heart failure
*Answer: B.*

**3.** Calcified pleural plaques on the chest X-ray of a retired roofer suggest exposure to:
A. Silica · B. Coal · C. Cotton · D. Beryllium · **E. Asbestos**
*Answer: E.*
