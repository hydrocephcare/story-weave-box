# Endometriosis

*Year 4 MBChB | Obstetrics & Gynaecology | CAT revision*

## 1. Definition

**Endometriosis** is a chronic, oestrogen-dependent inflammatory condition in which tissue similar to endometrial glands and stroma occurs outside the uterine cavity, commonly on the ovaries, pelvic peritoneum and uterosacral ligaments. It may cause dysmenorrhoea, chronic pelvic pain, deep dyspareunia, cyclical bowel/bladder symptoms and infertility. Symptom severity does not reliably correlate with anatomical stage.

- **Superficial peritoneal disease:** implants on pelvic peritoneum.
- **Ovarian endometrioma:** cyst containing old altered blood ("chocolate cyst").
- **Deep endometriosis:** lesions extending beneath the peritoneal surface, potentially involving uterosacral ligaments, rectovaginal septum, bowel, bladder or ureter.
- Extrapelvic disease is less common.

Common sites include ovaries, pouch of Douglas, uterosacral ligaments, broad ligament and pelvic peritoneum.

## 3. Aetiology and pathophysiology

The exact cause is not fully established. Proposed mechanisms include retrograde menstruation with implantation of endometrial cells, coelomic metaplasia, lymphatic/haematogenous spread and genetic/immune influences. Retrograde menstruation alone cannot explain all cases.

Ectopic endometrial-like tissue responds to ovarian hormones, undergoes cyclic inflammation/bleeding and releases inflammatory mediators. Repeated inflammation produces fibrosis, adhesions and distorted pelvic anatomy. Ovarian endometriomas may damage ovarian tissue and reduce ovarian reserve, particularly after repeated surgery.

**Why pain occurs:** inflammatory mediators, local nerve sensitisation, fibrosis, adhesions and deep organ involvement contribute. Pain may become chronic and persist beyond menstruation.

**Why infertility occurs:** adhesions distort tubo-ovarian anatomy; inflammation may impair oocyte pickup, fertilisation and implantation; ovarian endometriomas and their treatment may reduce ovarian reserve.

## 4. Risk factors and associations

- First-degree relative with endometriosis.
- Early menarche, short cycles or prolonged/heavy menstruation may be associated.
- Nulliparity is associated epidemiologically.
- Outflow obstruction may increase risk in selected congenital anomalies.
- Endometriosis can occur in adolescents and in people with apparently normal menstrual flow.

## 5. Clinical features

The classic pattern is **cyclical pain**, but symptoms may be non-cyclical.

- Secondary dysmenorrhoea, often progressively worsening and interfering with daily activities.
- Chronic pelvic pain.
- Deep dyspareunia (pain during or after deep intercourse).
- Infertility or subfertility.
- Dyschezia (painful defaecation), especially during menstruation.
- Cyclical rectal bleeding, dysuria or haematuria when bowel/bladder involvement occurs (uncommon; investigate carefully).
- Fatigue, bloating and bowel symptoms that fluctuate with the cycle.

Some patients are asymptomatic; others have severe pain with limited visible disease.

## 6. History and examination

Ask about onset, timing in relation to menstruation, progression, analgesic use, effect on school/work, deep dyspareunia, bowel/bladder symptoms, fertility goals, previous surgery and family history. A pain and symptom diary can help demonstrate cyclical patterns.

**Examination**
- Abdominal examination: tenderness, mass or scars.
- Pelvic examination with consent and chaperone where appropriate: reduced uterine/adnexal mobility, fixed retroverted uterus, tender nodules in posterior fornix/uterosacral ligaments, adnexal mass/endometrioma or visible vaginal lesions.
- A normal pelvic examination does not exclude endometriosis.

## 7. Differential diagnosis

- Adenomyosis: painful heavy periods, tender/boggy enlarged uterus.
- PID: discharge, fever, STI risk, cervical motion tenderness.
- Fibroids: heavy bleeding, enlarged irregular uterus.
- Ovarian cyst/torsion.
- IBS and inflammatory bowel disease.
- Interstitial cystitis/urinary tract pathology.
- Ectopic pregnancy in acute pain/bleeding.
- Primary dysmenorrhoea (usually begins soon after menarche, without pelvic pathology or progressive symptoms).

## 8. Investigations

| Investigation | Role and limitation |
|---|---|
| Pregnancy test | Excludes pregnancy/ectopic pregnancy when relevant |
| Transvaginal ultrasound | First-line imaging for endometrioma and deep disease; also detects alternative pathology |
| Transabdominal ultrasound | Alternative if transvaginal scan is declined or unsuitable |
| Specialist ultrasound/MRI | Maps deep disease, bowel/bladder/ureter involvement and extent before surgery |
| Laparoscopy | Consider when suspicion persists, imaging is negative but symptoms remain, or operative treatment is planned |
| Histology | Biopsy can confirm lesions; negative histology does not always exclude disease |

**Important examination trap:** a normal ultrasound does not rule out superficial peritoneal endometriosis or all deep disease. CA-125 is not recommended as a diagnostic test for endometriosis.

## 9. Management

Management is individualised by pain, disease extent, fertility goals, age, previous treatment and patient preference. Explain that endometriosis is chronic and recurrence can occur.

### A. Initial and supportive care
- Validate symptoms and discuss impact on daily life.
- Pain diary, education, supportive counselling and multidisciplinary pain care when needed.
- NSAIDs or other analgesia if appropriate and not contraindicated.
- Consider hormonal treatment when pregnancy is not being attempted immediately.

### B. Hormonal treatment
- **Combined hormonal contraception:** suppresses ovulation and menstruation; may be used continuously or cyclically depending on symptoms and contraindications.
- **Progestogens:** oral, injectable or intrauterine options can suppress endometrial-like tissue activity and pain.
- **GnRH agonists/antagonists:** suppress ovarian hormone production; generally specialist second-line options because of hypo-oestrogenic effects and bone loss. Add-back therapy may be required.
- Treatment controls symptoms but does not permanently eradicate disease. Hormonal suppression is not a fertility treatment and should not be used to improve spontaneous pregnancy rates while actively trying to conceive.

### C. Surgery
Laparoscopic excision or ablation of lesions and adhesiolysis may improve pain in selected patients. Endometrioma surgery requires careful consideration of ovarian reserve; cystectomy can reduce recurrence but may remove healthy ovarian tissue. Deep disease involving bowel, bladder or ureter should be managed by an experienced multidisciplinary specialist team. Hysterectomy with or without oophorectomy is considered only in selected patients with completed childbearing and persistent symptoms after counselling; it does not guarantee cure if extrauterine disease remains.

### D. When fertility is a priority
Refer for fertility assessment and individualised planning. Options may include conservative surgery in selected patients, expectant conception, ovulation/assisted reproduction depending on other factors, or IVF. Weigh potential benefit of endometrioma surgery against loss of ovarian reserve, especially with bilateral or repeat procedures.

## 10. Complications

Chronic pelvic pain, dyspareunia, adhesions, bowel/bladder/ureteric complications in deep disease, endometrioma, reduced ovarian reserve and infertility. Psychological distress and reduced quality of life are important.

## 11. CAT high-yield points

- Endometriosis = endometrial-like glands/stroma **outside the uterine cavity**.
- Common symptoms: secondary dysmenorrhoea, deep dyspareunia, chronic pelvic pain and infertility.
- Cyclical dyschezia or urinary symptoms suggest bowel/bladder involvement.
- Endometrioma contains altered old blood ("chocolate cyst").
- Normal ultrasound does not exclude endometriosis.
- CA-125 is not a diagnostic test.
- Medical hormonal suppression treats pain but does not improve fertility while a patient is trying to conceive.
- Surgery may improve pain/fertility in selected patients but ovarian surgery can reduce ovarian reserve.
- Refer suspected deep disease involving bowel, bladder or ureter to specialist care.

## 12. Tricky examiner questions

**Q1. What is an endometrioma?**  
**Answer:** An ovarian cyst formed by endometriosis and containing thick altered old blood, classically called a chocolate cyst.

**Q2. Does a normal ultrasound exclude endometriosis?**  
**Answer:** No. Superficial peritoneal disease may not be visible, and persistent suggestive symptoms warrant further assessment.

**Q3. Why can endometriosis cause infertility?**  
**Answer:** Inflammation, adhesions, distorted tubo-ovarian anatomy and ovarian reserve damage can impair conception.

**Q4. Why is repeated ovarian endometrioma surgery approached cautiously?**  
**Answer:** Cystectomy may remove normal ovarian cortex and reduce ovarian reserve.

**Q5. Does hormonal suppression improve spontaneous conception while the patient is trying to become pregnant?**  
**Answer:** No. It suppresses disease activity/pain but prevents or delays attempts at conception and is not a fertility-enhancing treatment.

## 13. Practice MCQs

### Question 1
A 27-year-old has progressively worsening dysmenorrhoea, deep dyspareunia and painful defaecation during menstruation. What is the most likely diagnosis?

A. Endometriosis  
B. Acute cystitis  
C. Cervical ectropion  
D. Stress incontinence

**Answer: A.** Progressive cyclical pelvic, sexual and bowel pain is suggestive of endometriosis.

### Question 2
A woman with suspected endometriosis has a normal pelvic ultrasound. What is the correct interpretation?

A. Endometriosis is excluded  
B. Superficial disease may still be present  
C. She must have ovarian cancer  
D. CA-125 confirms the diagnosis

**Answer: B.** Normal imaging does not exclude endometriosis.

### Question 3
An ovarian endometrioma is being considered for surgery in a patient who wants future pregnancy. Which issue is particularly important?

A. Risk of reducing ovarian reserve  
B. Risk of causing diabetes  
C. Guaranteed menopause  
D. Increased sperm production

**Answer: A.** Ovarian cystectomy can remove healthy cortex and reduce reserve.

### Question 4
Which marker should not be used to diagnose endometriosis?

A. CA-125  
B. Pregnancy test when indicated  
C. Pelvic ultrasound  
D. Specialist MRI for deep disease mapping

**Answer: A.** CA-125 is non-specific and is not a diagnostic test for endometriosis.

### Question 5
Which symptom raises suspicion of deep bowel endometriosis?

A. Cyclical painful defaecation  
B. Painless jaundice  
C. Productive cough  
D. Polyuria after meals

**Answer: A.** Cyclical dyschezia is a recognised symptom of bowel involvement.
