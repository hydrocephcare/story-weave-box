# Sadness and Depression

**MBChB Year 4 · Psychiatry · Clinical and examination notes**

Sadness is a normal human emotion. Depression is a clinical syndrome or disorder in which depressed mood and/or loss of interest occurs with a cluster of cognitive, behavioural and physical symptoms. The distinction depends on duration, pattern, associated symptoms, change from baseline, functional impairment and risk—not simply how sad someone feels.

> **Clinical safety:** Educational notes only. Assess immediate safety, ask directly about suicide, screen for previous mania/hypomania and involve a senior clinician for severe illness, psychosis, pregnancy-related illness or significant risk. Use current Kenyan guidance and local protocols.

![Mood spectrum](https://res.cloudinary.com/l4wbzpfr/image/upload/v1793772407/ompathstudy/year-4/psychiatry/mood-disorders/mood-spectrum.svg)

## 1. Emotion, mood and affect
- **Emotion:** a relatively brief response to an event or thought.
- **Mood:** a more sustained emotional state that colours a person's experience; usually described by the patient.
- **Affect:** emotional expression observed by the clinician during the interview: facial expression, tone, gestures and responsiveness.
- **Depressed mood:** sustained sadness, emptiness or hopelessness.
- **Anhedonia:** markedly reduced interest or ability to experience pleasure in previously enjoyable activities.

In the MSE, record mood in the patient's own words and describe affect objectively. Example: “Mood: ‘low and empty’; affect: constricted, tearful and congruent.”

## 2. Normal sadness and grief
Sadness is a normal response to disappointment, loss, rejection, failure, illness or other difficult experiences. It is not, by itself, a psychiatric diagnosis.

**Typical features**
- Often follows an identifiable event, although sadness can occur without an obvious trigger.
- May come in waves and fluctuate with reminders or supportive experiences.
- The person can usually still experience moments of pleasure, connection or hope.
- Self-esteem is generally preserved.
- Daily functioning may be temporarily affected, but essential responsibilities are usually retained.
- Intensity often reduces with time, adjustment and support.

**Example:** A student fails an examination, feels disappointed and cries for a few days, but can still enjoy time with friends and gradually resumes studying. This may be a normal emotional response.

**Bereavement and grief**
- Grief follows the death of someone or another significant loss. It may include yearning, sadness, anger, disbelief, guilt and temporary difficulty concentrating.
- Grief often comes in waves linked to reminders; positive emotions and self-esteem may remain accessible.
- Major depression can also occur after bereavement. A loss or stressor does **not** automatically exclude a depressive episode.
- Assess the whole syndrome, duration, impairment, cultural context and safety. Do not diagnose a depressive disorder from sadness alone.

## 3. When does low mood become a clinical problem?
Consider these dimensions together:
1. **Duration:** how long have symptoms been present?
2. **Persistence:** most of the day, nearly every day, or fluctuating?
3. **Context/proportionality:** understandable in context, or overwhelming and pervasive?
4. **Change from baseline:** clearly different from usual mood, energy and functioning?
5. **Symptom clustering:** sleep, appetite, energy, concentration, self-worth, movement or pleasure also affected?
6. **Distress/impairment:** education, work, relationships or self-care affected?
7. **Risk:** thoughts of death, self-harm, suicide, self-neglect or inability to maintain food and fluids?
8. **Alternative explanations:** medical illness, medication, substance/withdrawal, bipolar disorder or another psychiatric condition?

A psychosocial trigger can precipitate a depressive episode; its presence does not make symptoms “just sadness”.

## 4. What is depression?
Depression refers to disorders characterised by persistent or recurrent disturbance of mood, loss of interest or pleasure, or both, accompanied by cognitive, behavioural and physical symptoms that cause clinically significant distress or functional impairment.

- **Emotional:** sadness, emptiness, hopelessness, irritability, reduced emotional responsiveness.
- **Cognitive:** poor concentration, indecisiveness, negative thinking, guilt, worthlessness and thoughts of death.
- **Behavioural:** withdrawal, reduced activity, loss of motivation and neglect of responsibilities.
- **Physical:** fatigue, sleep disturbance, appetite/weight change, reduced libido, psychomotor slowing or agitation, and sometimes non-specific aches or pain.

Depression is not a character weakness, laziness or failure of willpower. It is a multifactorial health condition.

## 5. Major depressive episode: DSM-5-TR
A **major depressive episode (MDE)** requires **at least five of nine symptoms** during the same **two-week period**. Symptoms represent a change from previous functioning, and at least one must be:
- Depressed mood; **or**
- Markedly diminished interest or pleasure (anhedonia).

Symptoms cause clinically significant distress or impairment and are not better explained by a substance, medication, medical condition or a history of mania/hypomania.

![Nine symptoms of major depression](https://res.cloudinary.com/l4wbzpfr/image/upload/v1793772412/ompathstudy/year-4/psychiatry/mood-disorders/mdd-nine-symptoms.svg)

### SIGECAPS: the nine symptom domains
1. **S — Sleep:** insomnia (difficulty falling asleep, waking during the night or early-morning awakening) or hypersomnia.
2. **I — Interest:** markedly reduced interest or pleasure in almost all activities (anhedonia).
3. **G — Guilt:** excessive or inappropriate guilt, worthlessness or self-blame.
4. **E — Energy:** fatigue or loss of energy nearly every day.
5. **C — Concentration:** reduced ability to think, concentrate or make decisions.
6. **A — Appetite:** reduced or increased appetite, sometimes with significant weight change.
7. **P — Psychomotor change:** observable psychomotor agitation or retardation.
8. **S — Suicidality:** recurrent thoughts of death, suicidal ideation, a suicide plan or attempt.
9. **Depressed mood:** sadness, emptiness or (in children/adolescents) sometimes prominent irritability.

**Memory point:** SIGECAPS is a mnemonic; the diagnostic threshold remains five of nine, including depressed mood or anhedonia.

### Clinical interpretation
- **Terminal insomnia:** waking earlier than intended and being unable to return to sleep. It can occur in melancholic depression but is not diagnostic alone.
- **Psychomotor retardation:** objectively slowed speech, movement, responses and spontaneous activity.
- **Psychomotor agitation:** observable pacing, hand-wringing, restlessness or inability to remain seated.
- **Mood-congruent guilt:** beliefs of having caused terrible harm or committed an unforgivable act may become delusional in severe psychotic depression.
- Somatic symptoms such as fatigue, pain, constipation and reduced libido may accompany depression but are non-specific.

## 6. Severity and specifiers
Severity depends on symptom number and intensity, functional impairment, psychotic symptoms and risk—not duration alone.
- **Mild:** symptoms are distressing but relatively limited; most essential functioning is maintained.
- **Moderate:** appreciable symptoms and impairment in education, work or relationships.
- **Severe:** numerous/intense symptoms, marked impairment, major self-care difficulty, psychosis, catatonia or significant suicide risk may be present.

**Melancholic features:** markedly reduced pleasure or mood reactivity; morning worsening; early-morning awakening; marked psychomotor change; significant appetite/weight loss or excessive guilt.

**Atypical features:** mood reactivity; increased appetite/weight; hypersomnia; leaden paralysis; longstanding rejection sensitivity.

**Psychotic depression:** severe depression accompanied by delusions and/or hallucinations. Delusions may involve guilt, poverty, deserved punishment, bodily disease or nihilism. Psychotic content may be mood-congruent or mood-incongruent. Requires urgent specialist assessment.

Other specifiers include anxious distress and seasonal pattern. These are clinical descriptors, not separate symptom-count rules.

## 7. Other depressive disorders

### A. Persistent depressive disorder (PDD; dysthymia)
Chronic depressed mood for most of the day, more days than not, for at least **two years in adults** or **one year in children/adolescents**, plus at least two of:
- Poor appetite or overeating.
- Insomnia or hypersomnia.
- Low energy/fatigue.
- Low self-esteem.
- Poor concentration or difficulty deciding.
- Hopelessness.

The person has not been without symptoms for more than two months at a time during the required period. Symptoms cause distress or impairment.

**Clinical picture:** “I have always been a low person.” The patient may continue working or studying but experience persistent pessimism, fatigue, low self-worth and reduced enjoyment.

**Double depression:** a major depressive episode occurs on top of pre-existing PDD.

### B. Premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD)
A severe cyclical mood disturbance in the late luteal phase, improving shortly after menstruation begins. Features include mood swings, irritability, depressed mood, anxiety, reduced interest and difficulty concentrating. Prospective daily symptom recording over at least two cycles helps confirm the pattern.

### C. Substance/medication-induced depressive disorder
Symptoms arise during or soon after intoxication, withdrawal or exposure to a medicine capable of producing them. Establish timing and consider other causes rather than assuming causation from exposure alone.

### D. Depressive disorder due to another medical condition
A depressive presentation judged to be a direct physiological consequence of a medical condition. Consider endocrine, neurological and systemic illness.

### E. Peripartum depression
Depression may begin during pregnancy or after delivery. Assess maternal safety, functioning, bonding, infant safety and support. **Postpartum psychosis is a psychiatric emergency**, not simply a more severe form of ordinary “baby blues”.

## 8. Aetiology: biopsychosocial model
Depression is multifactorial: biological vulnerability interacts with psychological processes and social circumstances.

**Biological factors**
- **Genetics:** depression is polygenic; many variants each contribute small effects. Family history increases risk but does not determine outcome.
- **Neurobiology:** altered serotonergic, noradrenergic and dopaminergic signalling may contribute to mood, energy, attention and reward processing.
- **Stress systems:** altered hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis activity and cortisol regulation are implicated in some patients.
- **Neural circuits:** changes involving prefrontal regions, amygdala, hippocampus and reward networks have been associated with depression.
- **Circadian regulation:** disturbed sleep and biological rhythms can contribute.
- **Medical illness and medicines:** chronic pain, endocrine/neurological disease and some medicines may contribute.

**Important:** depression is not simply caused by “low serotonin”. Neurotransmitters are relevant, but depression involves interacting neural circuits, stress systems, genetics, sleep, cognition and environment.

**Psychological factors:** childhood adversity, neglect, abuse or trauma; low self-esteem; negative cognitive schemas; learned helplessness; rumination; perfectionism and maladaptive coping.

**Social factors:** bereavement, relationship breakdown, poverty, unemployment, financial insecurity, social isolation, discrimination, violence, academic/occupational stress and limited support.

### Beck's cognitive triad
1. **Negative view of self:** “I am useless.”
2. **Negative view of the world:** “Everything is against me.”
3. **Negative view of the future:** “Nothing will ever improve.”

These recurring negative thoughts can reinforce hopelessness, withdrawal and reduced activity. This is a psychological model, not a diagnostic test.

## 9. Clinical assessment
Depression is diagnosed clinically. A structured psychiatric history, MSE, risk assessment and targeted physical assessment are essential.

**History of presenting illness:** clarify onset (sudden/gradual), duration, course (continuous/fluctuating/recurrent/seasonal), depressed mood, anhedonia, sleep, appetite, weight, energy, concentration, guilt, psychomotor change, libido, precipitating factors and functional impact on attendance, performance, relationships, hygiene, eating and self-care.

**Previous psychiatric history:** ask about previous depressive episodes, treatment response, admissions, self-harm or suicide attempts, psychotic symptoms, previous mania/hypomania, antidepressant exposure and possible mood switching.

**Always screen for bipolar disorder before diagnosing unipolar depression or starting antidepressant monotherapy.** Ask about past periods of unusually elevated or irritable mood, increased energy, reduced need for sleep, pressured speech, racing thoughts, increased activity, grandiosity or risky behaviour.

**Family, medical and substance history:** family history of depression, bipolar disorder, psychosis, suicide or substance-use disorders; medical illness, pain, endocrine or neurological symptoms; prescribed, over-the-counter and traditional medicines; alcohol, khat, cannabis, stimulants and other substances; pregnancy/recent delivery where relevant.

**Personal and social history:** developmental history, trauma, education, occupation, relationships, housing, finances, support, cultural understanding of distress, strengths and coping strategies.

## 10. Mental state examination in depression

| MSE domain | Possible findings |
|---|---|
| Appearance | Poor grooming, neglected hygiene, weight change or tired appearance |
| Behaviour | Reduced eye contact, withdrawal, slowed movement or agitation |
| Speech | Quiet, slow, reduced quantity, increased response latency |
| Mood | Patient reports sadness, emptiness or hopelessness |
| Affect | Constricted, blunted or tearful; often congruent |
| Thought form | Usually coherent; thinking may be slowed |
| Thought content | Guilt, worthlessness, hopelessness, self-blame, suicidal ideas; delusions in psychotic depression |
| Perception | Usually no hallucinations; may occur in psychotic depression |
| Cognition | Poor concentration, attention and decision-making |
| Insight/judgment | Variable; judgment may be impaired in severe illness |

Not every depressed patient is slowed, tearful or poorly groomed. Some appear agitated, irritable or outwardly composed.

## 11. Suicide and self-harm risk assessment
Suicide assessment is essential, particularly with hopelessness, psychosis, severe agitation, substance use or previous self-harm. Ask directly, calmly and without judgement.

Ask about:
1. **Passive death wish:** “Have you wished you could go to sleep and not wake up?”
2. **Suicidal thoughts:** “Have you thought about ending your life?”
3. **Intent:** “Do you feel you might act on these thoughts?”
4. **Plan:** method, timing, location and preparation.
5. **Access to means:** can the patient access the planned method?
6. **Past behaviour:** previous attempts, aborted attempts and non-suicidal self-injury.
7. **Current drivers:** hopelessness, agitation, psychosis, intoxication, shame, pain or acute loss.
8. **Protective factors:** supportive relationships, reasons for living, future plans, willingness to accept help and access to care.

A previous attempt, current intent, a specific plan, access to lethal means, severe agitation, psychosis or inability to maintain safety are particularly concerning.

**If risk is imminent**
- Do not leave the patient alone while urgent help is arranged.
- Arrange urgent psychiatric/emergency assessment.
- Reduce access to potential means of harm and use an appropriate observation level.
- Assess and treat intoxication, overdose, injury, dehydration or other medical emergencies.
- Involve supportive family/carers when appropriate, considering confidentiality and safety.
- Consider admission when required for safety and treatment.

Protective factors do not cancel active intent or a feasible plan. No risk score reliably predicts individual suicide; document the clinical formulation, actions and follow-up.

## 12. Differential diagnosis

| Differential | Clues |
|---|---|
| Normal sadness/grief | Understandable response to loss; waves of emotion; positive feelings may remain; depression can coexist |
| Adjustment disorder with depressed mood | Symptoms follow an identifiable stressor but do not meet criteria for another disorder |
| Bipolar depression | Previous mania/hypomania; ask about episodic increased energy and reduced need for sleep |
| Persistent depressive disorder | Chronic low mood for at least two years in adults |
| Anxiety disorders/PTSD | Prominent worry, fear, hyperarousal, avoidance or trauma symptoms |
| Schizoaffective disorder/schizophrenia | Psychosis and its longitudinal relationship to mood episodes |
| Hypothyroidism | Cold intolerance, constipation, weight gain, dry skin and fatigue |
| Anaemia | Fatigue, pallor, exertional dyspnoea and relevant blood-loss/nutritional history |
| Substance/medication-induced symptoms | Temporal relationship to intoxication, withdrawal or medication exposure |
| Delirium | Acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness |

## 13. Investigations
There is **no laboratory test that confirms MDD**. Investigations are guided by history, physical examination, age, comorbidity and treatment planning.

| Investigation | Reason when indicated |
|---|---|
| Full blood count | Anaemia or other haematological cause of fatigue |
| TSH/thyroid function | Hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism |
| Urea, electrolytes, creatinine | Renal disease, electrolyte disturbance and medication baseline |
| Liver function tests | Liver disease and medication planning |
| Glucose/HbA1c | Diabetes and metabolic health |
| Vitamin B12/folate | Suspected deficiency |
| HIV/syphilis testing | When clinically indicated, with consent and local protocols |
| Urine toxicology | Suspected substance-related presentation |
| Pregnancy test | When relevant to diagnosis or treatment choice |
| ECG | Cardiac risk, overdose or medicines with conduction/QT effects |
| Neuroimaging | Focal neurological signs, atypical presentation, head injury or suspected intracranial pathology |

Do not order every test routinely for every patient. Treat identified medical contributors and review medicines that may worsen mood.

## 14. Management
Treatment is individualised according to severity, suicide risk, psychosis, previous response, comorbidity, patient preference, pregnancy status and available services.

![Depression treatment pathway](https://res.cloudinary.com/l4wbzpfr/image/upload/v1793772438/ompathstudy/year-4/psychiatry/mood-disorders/depression-treatment.svg)

### A. General principles
- Establish a therapeutic, non-judgemental relationship.
- Explain the diagnosis and treatment choices clearly.
- Assess and manage suicide risk.
- Address sleep, nutrition, physical activity, substance use and social stressors.
- Agree on follow-up and monitor symptoms, functioning, adherence and adverse effects.
- Treat coexisting medical and psychiatric disorders.

### B. Psychological therapies
**Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT):** identifies negative automatic thoughts and unhelpful behaviours; tests thoughts such as “I failed once, so I am a complete failure”; develops balanced thinking, coping skills and problem-solving; often includes behavioural activation and relapse-prevention skills.

**Interpersonal therapy (IPT):** focuses on grief, interpersonal disputes, role transitions and social isolation.

**Behavioural activation:** gradually reintroduces meaningful activities, breaking the cycle of low mood → withdrawal → fewer rewarding experiences → worsening low mood.

Other approaches include problem-solving therapy, supportive psychotherapy and family psychoeducation.

### C. Antidepressant pharmacology
Antidepressants are considered particularly in moderate-to-severe depression, recurrent illness, patient preference or inadequate response to psychological treatment. Choice depends on clinical picture, interactions, comorbidity, overdose risk and availability.

**1. SSRIs**
- Examples: sertraline, fluoxetine, escitalopram, citalopram and paroxetine.
- Mechanism: inhibit the serotonin transporter (SERT), reducing neuronal reuptake of serotonin.
- Adverse effects: nausea, diarrhoea, headache, sleep disturbance, initial activation/anxiety and sexual dysfunction.
- Cautions: hyponatraemia (especially older adults), bleeding risk with NSAIDs/anticoagulants, QT prolongation with some agents and serotonin syndrome with interacting serotonergic medicines.

**2. SNRIs**
- Examples: venlafaxine and duloxetine.
- Mechanism: inhibit serotonin and noradrenaline reuptake.
- Adverse effects: nausea, sweating, sexual dysfunction, sleep disturbance and discontinuation symptoms; venlafaxine can increase blood pressure.

**3. Tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs)**
- Examples: amitriptyline, imipramine, clomipramine and nortriptyline.
- Mechanism: inhibit noradrenaline and serotonin reuptake, with additional receptor-blocking effects.
- Adverse effects: anticholinergic effects (dry mouth, constipation, blurred vision, urinary retention), sedation and postural hypotension.
- **Overdose can be fatal** due to cardiac conduction abnormalities and arrhythmias; use particular caution where suicide risk is present.

**4. Other antidepressants**
- **Mirtazapine:** alpha-2 adrenergic antagonism and serotonergic modulation; commonly causes sedation and increased appetite/weight.
- **Bupropion:** noradrenaline/dopamine reuptake inhibition; may cause insomnia and lowers seizure threshold.
- **Trazodone:** serotonergic antagonist/reuptake inhibitor; sedation, postural hypotension and rare priapism.

**Prescribing principles**
- Screen for past mania/hypomania and bipolar disorder before antidepressant monotherapy.
- Explain that benefit is delayed and may take several weeks; adverse effects can appear earlier.
- Review adherence, tolerability, interactions, response and suicide risk, particularly early in treatment and after dose changes.
- Do not stop abruptly; taper gradually with clinical guidance.
- If response is inadequate, reassess diagnosis, adherence, dose, duration, comorbidity and psychosocial factors.
- Prescribing and dose require an individual clinical decision; verify current Kenyan guidance and the BNF/formulary.

### D. Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT)
ECT uses a controlled electrical stimulus to induce a therapeutic seizure under general anaesthesia and muscle relaxation.

Consider when severe depression requires rapid response, there is life-threatening refusal of food/fluids, severe psychotic depression or catatonia, suicide risk is high, or other appropriate treatments have failed/cannot be used.

Adverse effects include transient confusion, headache, muscle aches and memory disturbance; some patients report more persistent autobiographical memory difficulties. Specialist oversight, anaesthetic assessment and informed consent are required.

### E. Social support and relapse prevention
- Involve family/carers with the patient's consent.
- Address housing, financial, relationship and academic/occupational problems.
- Encourage gradual return to routine, meaningful activity and physical activity as tolerated.
- Treat alcohol or other substance-use disorders.
- Continue effective treatment after remission to reduce relapse. For many patients after a first episode, antidepressant treatment continues for at least six months after remission; longer may be appropriate after recurrent, severe or high-risk episodes.
- Review residual symptoms, adherence and adverse effects; taper medication gradually when stopping is appropriate.
- Develop an early-warning-sign and help-seeking plan.

## 15. Complications and prognosis
**Complications:** suicide/self-harm, self-neglect, dehydration, malnutrition, poor academic/occupational performance, relationship breakdown, social isolation, substance misuse, worsening physical illness and recurrent/chronic episodes.

Many patients improve with appropriate treatment and support. Recurrence is possible. A less favourable course is associated with severe or recurrent episodes, chronic/residual symptoms, comorbid substance use or psychiatric illness, poor support and inadequate treatment. Follow-up and relapse prevention are important.

## 16. Sadness versus major depression: examination comparison

| Feature | Normal sadness/grief | Major depressive episode |
|---|---|---|
| Context | Often follows loss/disappointment | May follow stressor or occur without obvious trigger |
| Pattern | Often comes in waves | More persistent or pervasive |
| Pleasure | Moments of pleasure may remain | Marked loss of interest/pleasure may occur |
| Self-esteem | Usually preserved | Worthlessness or excessive guilt may occur |
| Thoughts | Often focused on the loss/event | May include global hopelessness and self-condemnation |
| Function | Variable; often gradually adapts | Often significant impairment |
| Diagnosis | Not a disorder by itself | Requires symptom, duration, impairment and exclusion criteria |

This is a clinical guide, not an absolute rule. Grief and depression can coexist, and suicidal thoughts can occur in bereavement as well as depressive illness.

## 17. Clinical case
A 22-year-old university student presents with three weeks of persistent low mood. They have stopped attending lectures and no longer enjoy football or spending time with friends. They wake at 04:00 and cannot return to sleep, have reduced appetite, feel worthless and struggle to concentrate. They say that sometimes they wish they would not wake up.

**Approach**
1. Establish depressed mood or anhedonia and count the nine symptoms over the same two-week period.
2. Assess impairment in education, relationships and self-care.
3. Ask directly about suicidal thoughts, intent, plan, access to means and previous attempts; arrange urgent safety measures if needed.
4. Ask about previous mania/hypomania, psychosis, substance use, medication exposure and medical symptoms.
5. Perform an MSE and physical assessment; request targeted investigations where indicated.
6. If criteria and exclusions are satisfied, diagnose a major depressive episode and determine whether it is unipolar or part of bipolar disorder.
7. Agree on a treatment plan based on severity and risk, with close follow-up.

**Learning points:** loss of interest in football is anhedonia; early-morning awakening is a sleep symptom but not diagnostic alone; worthlessness and poor concentration are additional symptoms; a passive death wish still requires direct suicide assessment; academic stress does not rule out a depressive episode.

## 18. Examination quick review
- Sadness is an emotion; depression is a clinical syndrome/disorder.
- Mood is subjective and sustained; affect is observed emotional expression.
- Anhedonia means reduced interest or pleasure.
- DSM-5-TR MDE: **5 of 9 symptoms in 2 weeks**, including depressed mood or anhedonia.
- Persistent depressive disorder: **2 years in adults; 1 year in children/adolescents**.
- Always screen for past mania/hypomania before diagnosing unipolar depression.
- Depression is a clinical diagnosis; investigations are targeted.
- Suicide assessment is essential: ideation, intent, plan, means, past attempts and protective factors.
- CBT, IPT and behavioural activation are important psychological treatments.
- SSRIs are commonly used antidepressants; monitor adverse effects, response and suicide risk.
- ECT is important when severe depression requires rapid response or is life-threatening.
- Grief and depression can coexist; bereavement does not automatically exclude MDD.

## 19. Practice questions

**1.** A 20-year-old student feels sad for three days after failing an examination. They continue to enjoy time with friends and resume studying. What is the most appropriate interpretation?

A. Major depressive disorder · B. Persistent depressive disorder · C. Normal sadness in response to a stressor · D. Bipolar II disorder · E. Psychotic depression

**Answer: C.** Brief, context-linked sadness with preserved pleasure and functioning is more consistent with a normal emotional response.

**2.** Which is required as one of the symptoms in a DSM-5-TR major depressive episode?

A. Hallucinations · B. Depressed mood or anhedonia · C. Weight loss · D. Early-morning awakening · E. Suicidal intent

**Answer: B.** At least one of depressed mood or markedly diminished interest/pleasure is required.

**3.** An adult reports low mood for three years, low self-esteem, fatigue and hopelessness, without symptom-free periods longer than two months. Which diagnosis should be considered?

A. Brief depressive episode · B. Persistent depressive disorder · C. Mania · D. Adjustment disorder only · E. Delirium

**Answer: B.** The chronicity and associated symptoms are consistent with PDD, provided exclusions are met.

**4.** What history is especially important before prescribing antidepressant monotherapy?

A. Childhood vaccination history · B. Previous episodes of mania or hypomania · C. Blood group · D. Hand dominance · E. Family size

**Answer: B.** Antidepressant monotherapy may destabilise bipolar illness; screen for past mania/hypomania.

**5.** A depressed patient reports a specific suicide plan and access to the intended means. What is the immediate priority?

A. Reassure and discharge · B. Wait for the next routine clinic · C. Arrange urgent safety measures and psychiatric/emergency assessment · D. Start an antidepressant and review in one month · E. Avoid discussing suicide

**Answer: C.** A specific plan and access to means require urgent assessment and protection.

**6.** Which antidepressant group is particularly dangerous in overdose because of cardiac conduction toxicity?

A. SSRIs · B. TCAs · C. SNRIs · D. Mirtazapine · E. Bupropion

**Answer: B.** Tricyclic antidepressants can cause life-threatening arrhythmias and conduction abnormalities in overdose.
