# Approach to Cardiovascular Symptoms and Examination

*Clinical method notes · Start with the patient, then use the findings to narrow the diagnosis.*

The purpose of cardiovascular assessment is to answer four questions:

1. Is the patient unstable?
2. Which cardiovascular syndrome best explains the presentation?
3. What is the likely cause or trigger?
4. Which investigations will change management?

## 1. Recognise urgent presentations first

Urgent assessment is required for severe breathlessness or pulmonary oedema, ongoing chest pain suggestive of acute coronary syndrome, syncope with haemodynamic instability, a new focal neurological deficit, shock, suspected aortic dissection, or a tachyarrhythmia/bradyarrhythmia causing instability.

Use an ABCDE approach, call for senior/emergency help early, monitor vital signs, obtain IV access and an ECG when indicated. Give oxygen for hypoxaemia, not routinely to every patient. Do not delay stabilisation while taking a long history.

## Anatomy and physiology you need  

- **Myocardial fibres (subendocardial vs subepicardial)**: Subendocardial layers are most vulnerable to ischaemia because they receive blood during diastole; loss of contractility here produces early systolic dysfunction and can manifest as exertional dyspnoea before overt chest pain.
- **Coronary arterial tree (left main, LAD, circumflex, RCA)**: Occlusion of the left main or proximal LAD rapidly compromises >70 % of left‑ventricular mass → acute coronary syndrome; a right‑sided lesion may present with isolated inferior ST changes and bradyarrhythmia.
- **Aortic wall layers (intima, media, adventitia) and vasa vasorum**: A tear in the intima with propagation through the media creates a false lumen (dissection). The presence of aortic media cystic necrosis (e.g., in hypertension) predisposes to this, explaining tearing chest pain radiating to the back and pulse deficits.
- **Cardiac conduction system (SA node, AV node, His‑Purkinje)**: Ischaemia or fibrosis here produces arrhythmias that can cause palpitations, syncope or haemodynamic collapse; recognising a regular narrow‑complex tachycardia versus an irregular irregular rhythm guides immediate management.
- **Venous return pathways (jugular veins, superior/inferior vena cava, hepatic veins)**: Elevated right‑atrial pressure transmits back to the jugular veins (raised JVP) and hepatic veins (congestive hepatomegaly). The examiner assesses JVP height and hepato‑jugular reflux to infer right‑sided failure or tamponade.
- **Pulmonary capillary bed and alveolar fluid dynamics**: Increased left‑atrial pressure → pulmonary venous hypertension → transudation of fluid into interstitium and alveoli, producing crackles and orthopnoea. Understanding Starling forces helps the examiner link dyspnoea to left‑sided failure.
- **Peripheral arterial tree (radial, femoral, popliteal)**: Pulse deficits or radio‑femoral delay suggest large‑vessel obstruction (e.g., coarctation, dissection). The examiner compares pulse timing and amplitude to localise the lesion.
- **Valvular anatomy (leaflet motion, annulus, chordae tendineae)**: Stenosis creates a pressure gradient that can be heard as a systolic ejection murmur; regurgitation produces a holosystolic murmur radiating to the apex or back. The examiner correlates murmur timing, location and radiation with the underlying valve lesion.

## Pathophysiology  

**Ischaemic chest pain →** coronary plaque rupture → platelet aggregation → thrombus formation → lumen occlusion → ↓ myocardial O₂ delivery → subendocardial necrosis → release of troponin → impaired systolic shortening → reduced stroke volume → reflex tachycardia → chest pressure, diaphoresis, dyspnoea.  

**Aortic dissection →** hypertension‑induced medial degeneration → intimal tear → blood enters media → false lumen expands → compression of branch vessels → pulse asymmetry, organ ischaemia → severe tearing pain radiating along the dissected plane; if the dissection involves the pericardial sac → cardiac tamponade → Beck’s triad (hypotension, muffled heart sounds, raised JVP).  

**Heart failure (systolic) →** chronic pressure/volume overload (e.g., hypertension, valvular disease) → ventricular remodelling → ↓ ejection fraction → ↑ left‑atrial pressure → pulmonary venous congestion → interstitial oedema → orthopnoea, PND, basal crackles.  

**Heart failure (diastolic) →** concentric hypertrophy → impaired ventricular relaxation → ↑ left‑ventricular end‑diastolic pressure at normal EF → pulmonary congestion with preserved contractility; exam shows S4 gallop.  

**Arrhythmia‑induced syncope →** rapid ventricular response (e.g., SVT) → ↓ diastolic filling time → ↓ cardiac output → cerebral hypoperfusion → transient loss of consciousness; termination of arrhythmia restores perfusion.  

**Peripheral venous insufficiency →** valve incompetence in deep veins → reflux → increased hydrostatic pressure → transudation into interstitium → dependent oedema, calf pain, skin changes; distinguishes from cardiac oedema by unilateral distribution and absence of raised JVP.

## 2. Focused cardiovascular history

### Chest pain
Ask about onset, site, character, radiation, severity, duration, triggers, relieving factors and associated symptoms. Exertional pressure relieved by rest suggests angina; persistent pressure with sweating or nausea raises concern for acute coronary syndrome. Sudden tearing pain radiating to the back, syncope or pulse/BP differences raises concern for aortic dissection. Pleuritic pain may be pulmonary or pericardial rather than myocardial.

### Breathlessness
Ask about exertional dyspnoea, orthopnoea (number of pillows), paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea, reduced exercise tolerance, cough and leg swelling. These can suggest heart failure, but respiratory disease, anaemia and pulmonary embolism are important alternatives.

### Palpitations
Clarify sudden or gradual onset, duration, regularity, frequency, triggers, and whether there is chest pain, dyspnoea, dizziness or syncope. Ask about caffeine, alcohol, medicines and stimulant use.

### Syncope or dizziness
Establish what the patient was doing, warning symptoms, duration, recovery, witnessed movements and post-event confusion. Exertional syncope, syncope while supine, known structural heart disease or a family history of sudden death are concerning features.

### Swelling and vascular symptoms
Ask about bilateral ankle swelling, rapid weight gain, abdominal distension, calf pain/swelling, cold or painful limbs, ulcers and walking distance before leg pain begins.

### Background and risk
Ask about hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia, kidney disease, HIV where appropriate, rheumatic fever, previous cardiac disease, pregnancy-related hypertension, smoking, family history of premature cardiovascular disease, and all prescribed, over-the-counter and herbal medicines. Ask about adherence and recent changes.

## 3. Cardiovascular examination

Explain the examination, obtain consent, wash hands and position the patient appropriately. Expose the chest respectfully and examine the patient from the end of the bed before focusing on the precordium.

### General inspection and observations
Look for distress, cyanosis, pallor, sweating, cachexia, oedema, scars, oxygen use and monitoring equipment. Record pulse rate and rhythm, blood pressure, respiratory rate, temperature and oxygen saturation. Assess capillary refill and peripheral perfusion.

### Hands and pulse
Assess temperature, capillary refill, clubbing, peripheral cyanosis and signs of infective endocarditis when clinically relevant. Check pulse rate, rhythm, volume and character; compare radial pulses if indicated. Radio-femoral delay or unequal pulses may suggest vascular disease. Measure blood pressure with the correct cuff.

### Face and neck
Look for pallor, central cyanosis and xanthelasma when relevant. Assess the jugular venous pressure with the patient at about 45 degrees. Inspect for raised JVP and hepatojugular reflux when appropriate. Examine the carotid pulse carefully; listen for bruits when indicated and never palpate both carotids simultaneously.

### Precordium
Inspect for scars, deformity and visible pulsations. Palpate the apex beat for location and character; assess for heaves and thrills. Auscultate systematically at the aortic, pulmonary, tricuspid and mitral areas. Identify S1 and S2, then listen for added sounds, murmurs and pericardial rub. If a murmur is heard, describe timing, location, radiation, intensity and response to manoeuvres when appropriate.

### Lungs, abdomen and legs
Listen for basal crackles or other signs of pulmonary congestion. Examine the abdomen for hepatomegaly, ascites and pulsatile masses where appropriate. Check for peripheral oedema and assess peripheral pulses. A cardiovascular examination is incomplete without looking for evidence of heart failure and peripheral vascular disease.

## Differential diagnosis and how to tell them apart  

| Condition | Key clinical pointers | Distinguishing feature(s) |
|---|---|---|
| Acute coronary syndrome | Central pressure‑like chest pain, radiation to left arm/jaw, diaphoresis, exertional onset, troponin rise, ST changes | Pain relief with nitrates, ECG evolution, cardiac biomarkers |
| Aortic dissection | Sudden tearing back pain, pulse deficit, BP differential, widened mediastinum on CXR | Marfanoid habitus, hypertension, “ripping” quality, CT angiography shows intimal flap |
| Pulmonary embolism | Pleuritic chest pain, tachypnoea, pleuritic rub, risk factors (immobility, cancer), sinus tachycardia, D‑dimer ↑ | V/Q scan or CT pulmonary angiogram shows filling defect; right‑heart strain on ECG |
| Pericarditis | Sharp retro‑sternal pain improved by sitting forward, pericardial rub, diffuse ST‑elevation | Friction rub on auscultation, low‑voltage ECG, effusion on echo |
| Congestive heart failure (LV) | Orthopnoea, PND, basal crackles, displaced apex, S3 gallop, elevated BNP | Reduced EF on echo, pulmonary congestion, response to diuretics |
| Hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy | Exertional dyspnoea, systolic murmur that increases with Valsalva, family history | Asymmetric septal hypertrophy on echo, SAM of mitral valve |
| Valvular regurgitation (e.g., MR) | Holosystolic murmur radiating to axilla, bounding pulses, pulmonary oedema if severe | Echo shows regurgitant jet, LV dilation, pansystolic murmur intensity unchanged with respiration |

## 4. First-line investigations: ask what each test answers

- **12-lead ECG:** rhythm, conduction, ischaemia/infarction, chamber strain and other electrical abnormalities. A normal ECG does not exclude acute coronary syndrome.
- **Chest X-ray:** cardiomegaly, pulmonary congestion, pleural effusions and alternative thoracic diagnoses.
- **Echocardiography:** ventricular function, chamber size, valves, pericardial fluid and structural disease.
- **Troponin:** myocardial injury; interpret serial values and the clinical picture, because elevation is not specific to coronary thrombosis.
- **FBC:** anaemia or infection that may cause or worsen cardiovascular symptoms.
- **Urea, creatinine and electrolytes:** renal function and electrolyte abnormalities, especially before or during diuretic and renin–angiotensin system treatment.
- **Glucose/HbA1c and lipid profile:** cardiovascular risk assessment.
- **BNP/NT-proBNP:** supports assessment of suspected heart failure; interpret alongside clinical findings and renal function.
- **Additional tests:** D-dimer, CT pulmonary angiography, blood cultures, thyroid tests or other tests only when the differential diagnosis and pre-test probability justify them.

## 5. Build a problem representation

Instead of listing every symptom, summarise the case in one sentence.

**Example:** “A 68-year-old with long-standing hypertension presents with progressive exertional dyspnoea, orthopnoea, bilateral ankle oedema, raised JVP and basal crackles, suggesting congestive heart failure.”

Then ask:
- Is the patient stable or unstable?
- Is this mainly heart failure, ischaemia, arrhythmia, valvular disease, pericardial disease or vascular disease?
- What could have precipitated it?
- What dangerous alternative must be excluded?

## 6. Clinical cases

### Case 1 — Breathlessness and swelling
A 65-year-old with hypertension reports worsening breathlessness, needing three pillows to sleep, and bilateral ankle swelling. Examination reveals raised JVP and bilateral basal crackles.

**Most likely syndrome:** heart failure with congestion.

**Next steps:** assess ABCDE and oxygen saturation; obtain ECG, chest X-ray, renal function/electrolytes and natriuretic peptide if available; arrange echocardiography. Look for triggers such as myocardial ischaemia, arrhythmia, infection, uncontrolled BP, missed medication or renal deterioration. Treat acute congestion according to severity and local protocol.

Related reading: [Heart failure](/topic/heart-failure).

### Case 2 — Chest pain
A 54-year-old with diabetes develops central chest pressure while walking, radiating to the left arm, with sweating and nausea. The pain persists at rest.

**Working diagnosis:** acute coronary syndrome until excluded.

**Immediate approach:** urgent ECG, serial troponins, monitoring and senior/emergency assessment. Give antiplatelet and other ACS treatment according to contraindications and local protocol. Do not dismiss the case because the first ECG is non-diagnostic.

Related reading: [Acute coronary syndrome](/topic/acute-coronary-syndrome).

### Case 3 — Fever and a new murmur
A patient with fever has a new murmur and small painless lesions on the palms.

**Consider:** infective endocarditis.

**Approach:** obtain multiple blood-culture sets before antibiotics when this can be done without dangerous delay; arrange echocardiography and urgent specialist review. In an unstable patient, do not delay life-saving treatment for investigations.

Related reading: [Infective endocarditis](/topic/infective-endocarditis).

## 7. Exam checklist

When asked to assess a cardiovascular patient, state:
1. Immediate stability assessment and vital signs.
2. Focused history, including red flags and risk factors.
3. General, peripheral, neck, precordial, lung, abdominal and leg examination.
4. A concise problem representation and ranked differential diagnoses.
5. Investigations with a reason for each.
6. Initial stabilisation, cause-directed management, monitoring and reassessment.

## Complications and prognosis  

- **Acute coronary syndrome**: Ventricular fibrillation, cardiogenic shock, papillary‑muscle rupture, free‑wall rupture, acute mitral regurgitation — Ischaemic cardiomyopathy, heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, ventricular aneurysm, arrhythmogenic scar — Infarct size (peak troponin, ECG changes), reperfusion delay, left‑ventricular ejection fraction, presence of diabetes or chronic kidney disease
- **Acute decompensated heart failure**: Pulmonary oedema, severe hypoxaemia, arrhythmia, renal hypoperfusion (cardiorenal syndrome), hepatic congestion — Progressive systolic/diastolic dysfunction, atrial fibrillation, cachexia, thrombo‑embolism from stasis — NYHA class at discharge, natriuretic peptide level, renal function, adherence to guideline‑directed medical therapy
- **Aortic dissection (type A)**: Cardiac tamponade, aortic regurgitation, myocardial ischaemia, stroke, renal malperfusion — Chronic aortic aneurysm, recurrent dissection, hypertension‑related aortopathy — Extent of dissection, involvement of coronary or cerebral vessels, time to surgical repair, pre‑existing connective‑tissue disease
- **Pulmonary embolism**: Right‑ventricular failure, haemodynamic collapse, infarction‑related pleuritic pain, haemoptysis — Chronic thrombo‑embolic pulmonary hypertension, recurrent emboli — RV/LV diameter ratio on CT, troponin/BNP elevation, underlying malignancy or thrombophilia
- **Pericardial tamponade**: Pulsus paradoxus, electrical alternans, shock — Constrictive pericarditis (if chronic effusion), recurrent effusion — Rate of fluid accumulation, volume of effusion, underlying cause (malignancy, uremia, infection)
- **Valvular disease (e.g., severe aortic stenosis)**: Syncope, acute decompensation, sudden death — Progressive LV hypertrophy, heart failure, atrial fibrillation — Valve area/gradient, symptom burden, LV systolic function, presence of coronary disease

**Mechanistic insight for the examiner** – When you hear a murmur or see a raised JVP, think of the underlying pressure‑volume relationship. For example, a rapid‑onset pericardial effusion raises intrapericardial pressure, equalising diastolic pressures across chambers and precipitating tamponade. In chronic aortic stenosis, the LV faces a fixed afterload; concentric hypertrophy maintains wall stress but eventually leads to diastolic dysfunction and subendocardial ischaemia, explaining exertional syncope. Examiners expect you to link the bedside finding to the pathophysiology and then to the most important prognostic marker (e.g., EF in MI, RV dysfunction in PE).

## Tricky questions examiners ask  

**Q1.** A patient lies supine, breathless, with a blood pressure of 85/50 mmHg, a narrow pulse pressure and distant heart sounds. What is the next immediate step?  
**Answer:** Perform an urgent bedside echocardiogram or focused cardiac ultrasound to assess for pericardial effusion/tamponade because the haemodynamic picture (pulsus paradoxus, hypotension, muffled sounds) points to tamponade, which requires rapid drainage.

**Q2.** Two patients present with exertional dyspnoea and a systolic murmur radiating to the carotids. One has a soft S2, the other a loud S2. Which finding separates severe aortic stenosis from hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy?  
**Answer:** A soft or absent S2 indicates severe aortic stenosis (the aortic component is diminished by fixed obstruction), whereas a loud S2 is typical of hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy where the aortic valve closes normally.

**Q3.** A 70‑year‑old with known coronary artery disease develops sudden chest pain, hypotension and a new systolic murmur at the left sternal border. Why should you avoid attributing the murmur to mitral regurgitation without further assessment?  
**Answer:** The murmur is more likely a new aortic regurgitation murmur caused by an acute aortic dissection extending into the aortic root; assuming mitral regurgitation could delay life‑saving imaging (CT aortogram) and surgery.

**Q4.** In a patient with acute pulmonary oedema, you note bibasal crackles, a third heart sound and a rapid, irregular pulse. Which underlying mechanism is the examiner expecting you to identify as the driver of the current decompensation?  
**Answer:** The irregular rapid pulse suggests atrial fibrillation with loss of atrial kick, reducing LV filling and precipitating acute decompensated heart failure; rate control is therefore a priority.

**Q5.** A young adult presents with syncope while standing, a low‑volume pulse and a systolic murmur that increases on standing. What pathophysiological concept does this illustrate, and what is the most appropriate bedside test?  
**Answer:** This illustrates dynamic left‑ventricular outflow obstruction (e.g., hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy) where decreased preload augments the gradient; the Valsalva maneuver or standing‑to‑squatting manoeuvre while auscultating the murmur helps confirm the diagnosis.

**Q6.** A patient with chronic kidney disease and hypertension has peripheral oedema, a displaced apex, and an S3. The examiner asks why a high BNP alone would not be sufficient to stage heart failure in this patient.  
**Answer:** Renal impairment reduces BNP clearance, leading to falsely elevated levels; therefore, BNP must be interpreted alongside clinical signs, echocardiographic assessment of ejection fraction, and renal function to accurately stage heart failure.

## CVS bedside viva: examiner questions with answers

Use this as active recall: read the question, answer aloud, then select **Click to view answer**. These are revision answers for a Year 4 IMED bedside examination; interpret findings in the context of the patient.

### A. Cardiovascular history

<details><summary><strong>1. What are the main symptoms of cardiovascular disease?</strong></summary>

Chest pain, dyspnoea, orthopnoea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea (PND), palpitations, syncope or presyncope, fatigue, and peripheral oedema. Ask about onset, duration, progression, triggers, associated symptoms and relevant risk factors.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>2. How do you assess chest pain?</strong></summary>

Clarify site, onset, character, radiation, severity, duration, aggravating and relieving factors, and associated symptoms. Ask about exertion, sweating, nausea, dyspnoea and syncope. Acute severe or ongoing pain may represent an emergency.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>3. Differentiate orthopnoea from PND.</strong></summary>

Orthopnoea is breathlessness soon after lying flat, relieved by sitting or standing. PND is an episode of breathlessness that wakes the patient from sleep, typically after one or more hours, and improves on sitting upright. Both can occur in heart failure.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>4. What is the NYHA classification?</strong></summary>

Class I: no limitation of ordinary physical activity. Class II: slight limitation; ordinary activity causes symptoms. Class III: marked limitation; less-than-ordinary activity causes symptoms. Class IV: symptoms at rest or inability to perform physical activity without discomfort.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>5. Which cardiovascular risk factors should you ask about?</strong></summary>

Hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia, smoking, obesity, physical inactivity, kidney disease, family history of premature cardiovascular disease, previous cardiovascular events and relevant alcohol or drug use. Ask about medication adherence.
</details>

### B. General examination, pulse and blood pressure

<details><summary><strong>6. What do you inspect from the end of the bed?</strong></summary>

Assess general appearance, respiratory distress, position, consciousness, nutritional state, visible cyanosis or pallor, oxygen delivery, monitoring equipment and signs of fluid overload. Check observations and assess stability before proceeding.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>7. What are the characteristics of the pulse?</strong></summary>

Rate, rhythm, volume, character, equality between sides, and vessel-wall condition. Also assess radio-radial and radio-femoral delays when indicated.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>8. What is a normal adult resting pulse rate?</strong></summary>

Usually 60–100 beats/min in a resting adult. Interpret it alongside symptoms, rhythm, medications, fitness, temperature and haemodynamic status.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>9. What is a collapsing pulse, and what can cause it?</strong></summary>

A pulse that rises briskly and then collapses rapidly. It may occur with aortic regurgitation and other high-output or wide-pulse-pressure states, such as a patent ductus arteriosus. Confirm the finding and interpret it with the rest of the examination.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>10. What is a slow-rising pulse associated with?</strong></summary>

A delayed, small-volume carotid upstroke is classically associated with significant aortic stenosis because blood leaves the left ventricle through a narrowed valve.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>11. What does an irregularly irregular pulse suggest?</strong></summary>

A rhythm with no predictable pattern, classically atrial fibrillation. Confirm with a 12-lead ECG; do not diagnose the rhythm from pulse examination alone.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>12. What is pulse deficit?</strong></summary>

A difference between the apical heart rate and the peripheral pulse rate because some cardiac contractions do not generate a palpable peripheral pulse. It may occur in atrial fibrillation or frequent ectopic beats.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>13. What is radio-femoral delay?</strong></summary>

The femoral pulse is delayed relative to the radial pulse. It suggests aortic coarctation or another cause of impaired flow between the upper and lower body; assess blood pressure in both arms and legs where appropriate.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>14. What is pulse pressure, and what affects it?</strong></summary>

Pulse pressure = systolic blood pressure minus diastolic blood pressure. A wide pulse pressure may occur in aortic regurgitation or arterial stiffness; a narrow pulse pressure may occur with reduced stroke volume, including severe heart failure or shock. Interpret it with the full clinical picture.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>15. How do you assess postural hypotension?</strong></summary>

Measure blood pressure and pulse after the patient has rested supine, then repeat after standing when safe, commonly at 1 and 3 minutes. A fall of at least 20 mmHg systolic or 10 mmHg diastolic within 3 minutes supports orthostatic hypotension. Assist patients at risk of falling.
</details>

### C. JVP and venous pressure

<details><summary><strong>16. What is the JVP?</strong></summary>

The jugular venous pressure is a bedside estimate of right atrial pressure, assessed from the internal jugular venous pulsation. It helps assess systemic venous congestion and cardiac filling pressures.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>17. How do you examine the JVP?</strong></summary>

Position the patient at about 30–45 degrees and adjust the angle until the venous pulsation is visible. Use tangential light, identify the highest point of the internal jugular venous pulsation, and measure its vertical height above the sternal angle. Add approximately 5 cm to estimate pressure above the right atrium.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>18. How do you distinguish the JVP from the carotid pulse?</strong></summary>

The JVP is usually not palpable, has a biphasic waveform, varies with posture and respiration, and can be altered by gentle abdominal pressure. The carotid pulse is palpable, has a single brisk outward impulse and is less affected by posture. Never press on both carotid arteries.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>19. What causes a raised JVP?</strong></summary>

Right-sided heart failure, fluid overload, pulmonary hypertension, tricuspid valve disease, cardiac tamponade and constrictive pericarditis are important causes. A raised JVP indicates elevated right atrial pressure, not one specific diagnosis.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>20. What is hepatojugular reflux?</strong></summary>

A sustained rise in JVP during firm, sustained pressure over the abdomen, usually the right upper quadrant. It suggests limited right-heart ability to accommodate increased venous return and may support right-sided heart failure.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>21. What is Kussmaul's sign?</strong></summary>

A rise or failure of the JVP to fall during inspiration. It can occur in constrictive pericarditis, restrictive cardiomyopathy and right ventricular infarction, among other conditions.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>22. What do prominent V waves in the JVP suggest?</strong></summary>

Large systolic V waves classically suggest tricuspid regurgitation, because blood flows back into the right atrium during right ventricular systole.
</details>

### D. Precordium and heart sounds

<details><summary><strong>23. Where is the normal adult apex beat?</strong></summary>

Usually in the left 5th intercostal space near the midclavicular line. The position can vary with body habitus, pregnancy and thoracic anatomy.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>24. What does a displaced apex beat suggest?</strong></summary>

A laterally and/or inferiorly displaced apex may indicate left ventricular enlargement, often due to chronic volume or pressure overload. Consider other causes such as mediastinal shift.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>25. What is a heaving apex beat?</strong></summary>

A sustained, forceful impulse that may suggest left ventricular pressure overload and hypertrophy, for example from longstanding hypertension or aortic stenosis.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>26. What is a parasternal heave?</strong></summary>

A palpable lift at the left sternal edge, often indicating right ventricular hypertrophy or enlargement, as may occur with pulmonary hypertension.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>27. What is a thrill?</strong></summary>

A palpable vibration caused by turbulent blood flow, often associated with a loud murmur. Describe its location and correlate it with auscultation.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>28. What are S1 and S2?</strong></summary>

S1 is mainly closure of the mitral and tricuspid valves and marks the beginning of ventricular systole. S2 is closure of the aortic and pulmonary valves and marks the end of systole.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>29. What are S3 and S4?</strong></summary>

S3 occurs early in diastole during rapid ventricular filling; in adults it may indicate volume overload or heart failure, although it can be physiological in younger people. S4 occurs late in diastole due to atrial contraction against a stiff ventricle; it may occur with ventricular hypertrophy or ischaemia and is absent in atrial fibrillation.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>30. How do you describe a murmur?</strong></summary>

State timing (systolic, diastolic or continuous), site of maximum intensity, radiation, intensity/grade, pitch, quality, shape, and response to posture or manoeuvres. Also describe associated heart sounds and peripheral signs.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>31. What are the four main cardiac auscultation areas?</strong></summary>

Aortic: 2nd right intercostal space at the sternal edge. Pulmonary: 2nd left intercostal space. Tricuspid: lower left sternal edge. Mitral: apex, usually near the 5th left intercostal space at the midclavicular line. These are listening areas, not exact surface projections of every valve.
</details>

### E. Common valvular lesions

<details><summary><strong>32. Describe the murmur of aortic stenosis.</strong></summary>

A harsh crescendo–decrescendo ejection systolic murmur, loudest at the right 2nd intercostal space and often radiating to the carotids. A slow-rising carotid pulse and reduced A2 may accompany severe disease.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>33. Describe the murmur of mitral regurgitation.</strong></summary>

A high-pitched pansystolic murmur loudest at the apex and typically radiating to the axilla. The apex may be displaced and hyperdynamic if there is left ventricular volume overload.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>34. Describe the murmur of mitral stenosis.</strong></summary>

A low-pitched mid-diastolic rumble best heard at the apex with the bell, with the patient in the left lateral position. An opening snap may be present when the valve remains mobile. Rheumatic heart disease is an important cause in Kenya.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>35. Describe the murmur of aortic regurgitation.</strong></summary>

An early diastolic, high-pitched, decrescendo murmur, often best heard along the left sternal edge with the patient sitting forward at end-expiration. A wide pulse pressure or collapsing pulse may coexist.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>36. What is a pericardial friction rub?</strong></summary>

A superficial, scratchy or grating sound caused by inflamed pericardial layers rubbing together. It may have up to three components and is often best heard with the patient leaning forward. Its presence supports pericarditis, but absence does not exclude it.
</details>

### F. Peripheral signs and clinical cases

<details><summary><strong>37. Why do you examine the lungs in a CVS examination?</strong></summary>

Left-sided heart failure can raise pulmonary venous pressure, causing basal crackles, and in more severe cases pulmonary oedema. Reduced breath sounds may indicate pleural effusions. Lung signs should be interpreted with the history and other findings.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>38. Why examine the abdomen and lower limbs?</strong></summary>

Hepatomegaly, ascites and dependent oedema may indicate systemic venous congestion from right-sided heart failure. Peripheral pulses and limb temperature help assess arterial perfusion and peripheral vascular disease.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>39. A patient has orthopnoea, raised JVP, bilateral ankle oedema and bibasal crackles. What is your working diagnosis?</strong></summary>

Heart failure with systemic and pulmonary congestion is a leading diagnosis. Assess haemodynamic stability, oxygen saturation and precipitating causes, then investigate with ECG, chest radiograph, blood tests and echocardiography as available.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>40. A patient has exertional syncope, chest pain and a systolic murmur radiating to the carotids. What is likely?</strong></summary>

Aortic stenosis is a key diagnosis. Urgently assess the patient, obtain ECG and echocardiography, and seek senior review. Exertional syncope in suspected severe aortic stenosis is a red flag.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>41. A patient has fever, a new murmur and splinter haemorrhages. What should you suspect?</strong></summary>

Suspect infective endocarditis. Obtain multiple sets of blood cultures before antibiotics when this can be done promptly and safely, arrange echocardiography and urgent senior assessment. Do not delay emergency treatment in an unstable patient.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>42. What is the difference between Osler nodes and Janeway lesions?</strong></summary>

Osler nodes are classically tender nodules on fingers or toes; Janeway lesions are classically painless erythematous or haemorrhagic macules on palms or soles. Both may occur in infective endocarditis, but neither sign alone confirms the diagnosis.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>43. How would you summarise a CVS examination to the examiner?</strong></summary>

Give a concise synthesis: general condition and observations; pulse and blood pressure; JVP; apex and other precordial findings; heart sounds and murmur characteristics; lung bases; abdominal and peripheral signs. Then state the leading diagnosis, supporting findings, important differentials and investigations needed.
</details>

<details><summary><strong>44. What investigations commonly support a cardiovascular assessment?</strong></summary>

Choose tests based on the clinical question: 12-lead ECG, echocardiography, chest radiograph, full blood count, renal function and electrolytes, glucose/lipids where relevant, troponin for suspected acute myocardial injury, and natriuretic peptides where available for suspected heart failure. Suspected endocarditis requires blood cultures and echocardiography.

### Normal values: quick recall

- **Resting adult pulse:** usually 60–100 beats/min.
- **JVP:** commonly considered normal when the top of the venous column is no more than about 3–4 cm above the sternal angle, with technique and reference convention stated.
- **Apex beat:** usually 5th left intercostal space near the midclavicular line.
- **Pulse pressure:** systolic minus diastolic blood pressure.
- **Orthostatic hypotension:** sustained fall of at least 20 mmHg systolic or 10 mmHg diastolic within 3 minutes of standing.
