# Pneumothorax

## Definition

A **pneumothorax** is **air in the pleural space**, which allows the lung to **collapse** away from the chest wall.

## Anatomy and physiology you need  

- **Parietal pleura (costal, diaphragmatic, mediastinal) – thin serous membrane attached to chest wall, diaphragm and mediastinum.**: Forms the outer layer of the pleural cavity; its adherence to the endothoracic fascia creates a potential space that can fill with air. — Loss of the negative pressure between parietal and visceral pleura allows the lung to collapse; the parietal pleura transmits transmitted sounds (eg, bronchial breath sounds) that disappear when it is separated from the visceral layer.
- **Visceral pleura – delicate membrane covering the lung surface, continuous with the parietal pleura at the pleural recesses.**: Provides the suction that keeps the lung expanded (‑5 cm H₂O intrapleural pressure at rest). — When air enters the pleural space, this suction is abolished, so the lung recoils and collapses; the loss of visceral‑parietal apposition explains the hyper‑resonant percussion and absent breath sounds.
- **Pleural cavity (potential space) – normally contains a thin film of fluid that permits gliding of the two pleural layers.**: The cavity is a “vacuum” that can be breached by air from the lung (ruptured bleb) or from the outside (trauma). — Air in this space creates a true pneumothorax; the size of the air pocket determines the degree of lung collapse and the shift of mediastinal structures in tension.
- **Intercostal muscles & ribs – generate the negative intrathoracic pressure during inspiration.**: Their contraction expands the thoracic volume, pulling the pleural surfaces apart. — In a tension pneumothorax the one‑way valve effect means each inspiratory effort adds more air, rapidly raising intrapleural pressure and compromising venous return.
- **Mediastinum (great vessels, heart, trachea) – suspended by the pericardial and pleural reflections.**: Sensitive to changes in intrathoracic pressure; a rapid rise pushes the mediastinum away from the affected side. — Tracheal deviation, hypotension and distended neck veins are hallmarks of tension physiology and guide the examiner to immediate decompression.
- **Alveolar‑capillary interface – site of gas exchange.**: When a subpleural bleb ruptures, air escapes directly from the alveolar space into the pleural cavity. — The sudden loss of functional alveolar surface produces pleuritic pain (visceral pleura irritation) and acute dyspnoea, which the examiner will ask you to describe.
- **Pulmonary vasculature (pulmonary veins & arteries) – low‑pressure system that drains into the left atrium and right ventricle.**: Elevated intrapleural pressure in tension compresses pulmonary vessels, reducing preload and cardiac output. — The examiner may test your understanding of why hypotension and tachycardia occur before any obvious respiratory failure.

## Pathophysiology  

**Spontaneous/Traumatic breach → Air entry → Loss of negative intrapleural pressure → Lung recoil → Collapse → Decreased ventilation‑perfusion matching → Hypoxaemia**  

**Tension mechanism:**  

1. **One‑way valve formation** (ruptured bleb, chest‑wall wound) →  
2. **Air enters pleural space on inspiration but cannot exit on expiration** →  
3. **Progressive rise in intrapleural pressure** →  
4. **Compression of ipsilateral lung → further collapse** →  
5. **Mediastinal shift to contralateral side** →  
6. **Compression of superior/inferior vena cava → ↓ venous return** →  
7. **Reduced cardiac output → hypotension, tachycardia** →  
8. **Compensatory sympathetic surge → tachypnoea, cyanosis**  

**Re‑expansion pulmonary oedema (large, rapid drainage):**  

Air removal → sudden increase in pulmonary blood flow to previously collapsed alveoli → capillary leak → alveolar flooding → dyspnoea, pink frothy sputum.

## Types and causes

- **Primary spontaneous**: No underlying lung disease: **tall, thin young men, smokers**, rupture of subpleural **blebs**
- **Secondary spontaneous**: Underlying lung disease: **COPD** (commonest), **TB**, **PCP** (HIV), cystic fibrosis, asthma, lung cancer, ILD, Marfan syndrome, catamenial (menstrual)
- **Traumatic**: Penetrating or blunt chest injury, rib fracture
- **Iatrogenic**: Central line insertion, lung biopsy, pleural aspiration, mechanical ventilation (barotrauma)
- **Tension**: One-way valve: air enters but cannot leave, pressure builds (see below)

## Clinical features

- **Symptoms:** **sudden-onset pleuritic chest pain** (often unilateral) and **breathlessness**; may be minimal in a small primary pneumothorax; more severe in secondary pneumothorax.
- **Signs:** **reduced chest expansion**, **hyper-resonant percussion**, **reduced or absent breath sounds** and **reduced vocal resonance** on the affected side.
- **Tension pneumothorax (a clinical emergency):** **severe respiratory distress, tachycardia, hypotension, distended neck veins, tracheal deviation AWAY from the affected side**, cyanosis, hyper-resonant hemithorax with absent breath sounds. **Do not wait for an X-ray.**

## Differential diagnosis and how to tell them apart  

| Condition | Key clinical / bedside clues | Distinguishing feature from pneumothorax |
|---|---|---|
| **Pleural effusion** | Dullness to percussion, decreased tactile fremitus, fluid level on upright X‑ray | Dull, not resonant; breath sounds may be bronchial over fluid but not absent |
| **Pulmonary embolism** | Sudden pleuritic chest pain, tachycardia, risk factors, normal chest expansion, may have clear lungs on X‑ray | No unilateral hyper‑resonance; breath sounds usually present; CT pulmonary angiogram needed |
| **Acute pericarditis** | Central chest pain improving when leaning forward, pericardial rub, normal lung fields | Pain is retrosternal, not pleuritic; percussion normal; ECG shows diffuse ST elevation |
| **Pneumonia (lobar)** | Fever, productive cough, crackles, dullness over consolidation | Auscultation shows bronchial breath sounds and crackles; X‑ray shows infiltrate, not absent lung markings |
| **Bronchial asthma exacerbation** | Wheeze, diffuse hyper‑inflation, reversible obstruction, no unilateral findings | Bilateral wheeze, normal percussion; chest X‑ray shows hyperinflated lungs, not a visible pleural line |
| **Mediastinal mass** | Persistent cough, dysphagia, possible tracheal deviation but no respiratory distress | No hyper‑resonance; imaging shows soft‑tissue density, not air in pleural space |
| **Chest wall subcutaneous emphysema** | Crepitus on palpation, may follow trauma or ventilation | Crepitus felt in soft tissue, not a collapsed lung; breath sounds usually preserved |

## Investigations

- **Erect PA chest X-ray:** a **visible lung edge** with absent lung markings beyond it. In tension, the mediastinum is shifted and the diaphragm is flattened.
- **Size** (BTS): **large if a rim of 2 cm or more** between the lung edge and chest wall at the level of the hilum; **small if below 2 cm**.
- **Ultrasound** (absent lung sliding) and **CT** if unclear (blebs, underlying disease).
- **ABG** or oximetry if the patient is breathless.

## Management

**Tension pneumothorax:** **immediate needle decompression** (large-bore cannula, 2nd intercostal space mid-clavicular line or 4th–5th space anterior axillary line), then a **chest drain**. Give **high-flow oxygen**.

**Primary spontaneous (BTS approach):**
- **Small and not breathless:** **observe** (and review as an outpatient); oxygen if needed.
- **Large or breathless:** **needle aspiration** (16–18G cannula, up to 2.5 L); if it fails, a **small-bore chest drain**.

**Secondary spontaneous:**
- **Large (2 cm or more) or breathless:** **chest drain** (admit).
- **1–2 cm:** needle aspiration, admit and observe.
- **Under 1 cm:** high-flow **oxygen** and observe, admitted.

**Chest drain care:** insert in the **"safe triangle"** (above the 5th intercostal space, anterior to the mid-axillary line, behind the lateral border of pectoralis major); connect to an **underwater seal**; bubbling means an air leak, **swinging** shows the drain is in the pleural space; **never clamp a bubbling drain**; remove when the lung is up and the leak has stopped.

**Persistent air leak or recurrence:** **surgery** (VATS bullectomy and pleurectomy or **pleurodesis**).

**Advice at discharge:** **stop smoking** (reduces recurrence); **do not fly** until the X-ray has cleared and for **1 week** after resolution; **never scuba dive** unless definitive surgery has been performed.

## Complications

- **Tension pneumothorax and cardiac arrest**, persistent air leak (**bronchopleural fistula**), **re-expansion pulmonary oedema**, infection (empyema), **recurrence** (about 30% after a first primary episode), **haemopneumothorax**, surgical emphysema.

## Exam points

- **Tension pneumothorax is a clinical diagnosis: treat first.**
- **Hyper-resonant percussion + absent breath sounds** = pneumothorax.
- **2 cm rim** is the cut-off for "large".
- **COPD, TB and PCP** cause secondary pneumothorax: **a pneumothorax is more dangerous in lung disease.**
- Advice about **flying and diving**.

## Clinical case  

**Case:**  
A 28‑year‑old male construction worker, 180 cm, 65 kg, presents to the emergency department after a sudden “sharp” left‑sided chest pain while lifting a brick. The pain is pleuritic, worsens on deep inspiration, and is accompanied by mild dyspnoea. He is a current smoker (10 cig/day) and has no known lung disease.  

**Examination:**  
- Respiratory rate 22 /min, SpO₂ 96 % on room air, heart rate 98 bpm, BP 122/78 mmHg.  
- Left hemithorax: reduced expansion, hyper‑resonant percussion, absent breath sounds, and diminished vocal fremitus. No tracheal deviation, neck veins not distended.  

**Investigations:**  
Erect PA chest X‑ray shows a peripheral lucency with a visible visceral pleural line; the distance between the lung edge and chest wall at the level of the hilum is 2.5 cm.  

**Management performed:**  
Needle aspiration with a 16 G cannula yields immediate symptom relief; a repeat X‑ray confirms re‑expansion. The patient is observed for 4 h, given high‑flow oxygen, and discharged with smoking‑cessation advice.  

### Questions  

1. **What radiological feature distinguishes a “large” primary spontaneous pneumothorax from a “small” one?**  
   **Answer:** A rim of air ≥ 2 cm between the visceral pleural line and the chest wall at the level of the hilum on an erect PA film defines a large pneumothorax. This measurement guides the decision to intervene (aspiration or chest drain) rather than observe.  

2. **Why is needle aspiration preferred over immediate chest‑tube insertion in this patient?**  
   **Answer:** He is haemodynamically stable, has a moderate‑size pneumothorax, and is not markedly dyspnoeic. Needle aspiration is less invasive, has comparable success for primary spontaneous pneumothorax ≤ 3 cm, reduces hospital stay, and avoids the complications of a chest tube (infection, pain, prolonged air leak).  

3. **If the same patient had presented with hypotension, tachycardia, and tracheal deviation to the right, what would be the next immediate step and why?**  
   **Answer:** Immediate needle decompression (large‑bore cannula in the 2nd intercostal space, mid‑clavicular line) is required because these signs indicate tension pneumothorax, a life‑threatening condition where a one‑way valve creates rising intrapleural pressure, compromising venous return and cardiac output. Delay for imaging would be fatal.

## Tricky questions examiners ask  

**Q1.** A patient lying supine after a fall has unilateral absent breath sounds but no tracheal deviation. What is the next immediate investigation?  
**Answer:** Perform a bedside thoracic ultrasound looking for absent lung sliding; this is rapid, radiation‑free, and can detect a pneumothorax even when the X‑ray is obscured by supine positioning.  

**Q2.** In a ventilated ICU patient you note sudden hypotension, tachycardia, and a “flat” diaphragm on the chest X‑ray. Which condition must be excluded first and why?  
**Answer:** Tension pneumothorax must be excluded because positive pressure ventilation can rapidly convert a simple pneumothorax into tension, causing mediastinal shift, impaired venous return, and cardiovascular collapse. Immediate decompression supersedes further diagnostics.  

**Q3.** Two patients have identical chest X‑rays showing a 1 cm rim of air. One is a tall, thin 22‑year‑old male; the other is a 68‑year‑old woman with severe COPD. What clinical factor separates their management pathways?  
**Answer:** Underlying lung pathology. The COPD patient has a secondary spontaneous pneumothorax, which warrants chest‑tube drainage even for a small‑to‑moderate size because compromised lung compliance predisposes to rapid decompensation. The young primary case may be observed.  

**Q4.** A trainee proposes clamping a bubbling chest drain to stop an air leak. Why is this choice avoided?  
**Answer:** Clamping a chest drain can convert a simple air leak into a tension pneumothorax by preventing egress of intrapleural air, risking hemodynamic compromise. The correct approach is to leave the drain open to an underwater seal and monitor.  

**Q5.** After successful needle aspiration, a patient’s repeat X‑ray still shows a 1 cm rim of air. Should a chest tube be placed? Explain.  
**Answer:** Not necessarily. Persistent small residual air is common; if the patient is clinically stable, asymptomatic, and the lung remains expanded, continued observation is acceptable. Chest‑tube insertion is reserved for ongoing air leak, enlarging pneumothorax, or clinical deterioration.  

**Q6.** Why is high‑flow oxygen given to a patient with a small primary pneumothorax, and what physiological principle underlies this therapy?  
**Answer:** High‑flow oxygen creates a nitrogen wash‑out gradient, accelerating the diffusion of intrapleural air (mostly nitrogen) into the alveoli and thus hastening reabsorption of the pneumothorax. This reduces the time to radiographic resolution without invasive intervention.

## Practice questions

**1.** A 22-year-old tall, thin smoker has sudden pleuritic chest pain and mild breathlessness. Chest X-ray: 1 cm rim of air; he is comfortable. Best management:
A. Chest drain · B. **Observation with outpatient review** · C. Pleurodesis · D. Thoracotomy · E. Needle decompression
*Answer: B.*

**2.** A man after a road traffic crash has severe dyspnoea, BP 80/50, distended neck veins, absent breath sounds on the right and tracheal deviation to the left. Immediate management:
A. Chest X-ray · B. CT chest · C. Intubation first · **D. Needle decompression** · E. IV antibiotics
*Answer: D.*

**3.** A man with COPD has a 3 cm pneumothorax and is breathless. Best management:
**A. Chest drain** · B. Observation · C. Discharge · D. Needle decompression only · E. Steroids
*Answer: A. Secondary pneumothorax with breathlessness.*
