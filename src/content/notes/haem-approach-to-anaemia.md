# Approach to anaemia

*Clinical reasoning notes. Anaemia is a sign, not a diagnosis: always ask why.*

**The reasoning chain:** confirm anaemia → classify by red cell size (MCV) → decide whether the marrow is making too few cells or the body is losing or destroying them → find the cause (including the local ones: malaria, hookworm, HIV, sickle cell, TB) → treat the cause and replace what is missing.

## 1. Define

Anaemia is a **haemoglobin below normal for age and sex**: roughly below **13 g/dL in men, 12 g/dL in women, 11 g/dL in pregnancy and young children** (WHO). Symptoms: tiredness, breathlessness, palpitations, headache, pallor, and angina or heart failure when severe.

## Anatomy and physiology you need  

- **Erythropoietic niche (bone‑marrow sinusoids, stromal cells, macrophages)**: Provides the micro‑environment for erythroid progenitors, supplies iron via transferrin‑bound uptake, and clears expelled nuclei. Disruption (fibrosis, infiltration, aplasia) limits red‑cell output → low reticulocyte count. — A low retic with normal iron studies points to marrow failure; a marrow biopsy will show hypocellularity or dysplasia.
- **Renal peritubular fibroblasts (EPO‑producing cells)**: Synthesize erythropoietin in response to hypoxia via HIF‑2α signalling. Chronic kidney disease reduces EPO → normocytic, low‑retic anaemia. — In CKD the appropriate next step is to assess renal function and consider erythropoiesis‑stimulating agents.
- **Hepatic hepcidin regulation**: Hepcidin binds ferroportin on enterocytes and macrophages, blocking iron export. In inflammation (IL‑6 driven) hepcidin rises → iron sequestration → anaemia of chronic disease. — High ferritin with low transferrin saturation suggests functional iron deficiency; the examiner may ask how to differentiate from true iron deficiency.
- **Spleen (red‑pulp macrophages)**: Removes senescent or defective erythrocytes; also a site of extravascular haemolysis. Splenomegaly signals increased clearance. — Elevated LDH, indirect bilirubin, low haptoglobin plus splenomegaly points to haemolysis; the examiner may probe the distinction between intravascular vs. extravascular haemolysis.
- **Intestinal iron absorption (duodenal enterocytes)**: DMT1 transports Fe²⁺ across the apical membrane; ferroportin exports it to plasma where it binds transferrin. Absorption is up‑regulated by low hepcidin. — In iron‑deficiency anaemia, ferritin is low and transferrin saturation falls; the student should explain why oral iron works only when hepcidin is suppressed.
- **Vitamin B12‑intrinsic factor complex (terminal ileum)**: B12 is absorbed after binding intrinsic factor; it is required for methylmalonyl‑CoA mutase and methionine synthase, crucial for DNA synthesis in rapidly dividing erythroid precursors. Deficiency arrests nuclear maturation → macrocytosis and megaloblastic changes. — Presence of hypersegmented neutrophils on the film and elevated methylmalonic acid helps the examiner differentiate B12 from folate deficiency.
- **Folate metabolism (enterocytes, liver)**: Folate is reduced to tetrahydrofolate, a one‑carbon donor for thymidylate synthesis. Deficiency also produces megaloblastic anaemia but without neurologic sequelae. — The examiner may ask why giving folate alone can mask B12 deficiency – it improves the haematologic picture while neurological damage progresses.

## 2. Classify by MCV, then by mechanism

- **Microcytic**: below 80 fL — **Iron deficiency** (commonest), **thalassaemia**, anaemia of chronic disease, sideroblastic anaemia, lead
- **Normocytic**: 80–100 fL — Anaemia of chronic disease, **acute blood loss**, **haemolysis**, **chronic kidney disease**, **marrow failure**, mixed deficiencies
- **Macrocytic**: above 100 fL — **Vitamin B12 or folate deficiency**, alcohol, hypothyroidism, liver disease, drugs (zidovudine, methotrexate, hydroxycarbamide), myelodysplasia

**Then ask: is the reticulocyte count high or low?**

- **High:** the marrow is responding: **blood loss or haemolysis**.
- **Low:** the marrow is **not making enough**: deficiency, chronic disease, marrow failure or infiltration.

## Differential diagnosis and how to tell them apart  

| Condition | Key pointers (clinical / lab) | Distinguishing feature |
|---|---|---|
| Iron‑deficiency anaemia | Microcytosis, low ferritin, high TIBC, low transferrin saturation, positive occult blood or heavy menses. | Ferritin < 30 µg/L is highly specific; response to oral iron within 2 weeks. |
| Anaemia of chronic disease (ACD) | Normocytic or mildly microcytic, normal/high ferritin, low TIBC, elevated CRP/ESR, underlying infection, rheumatologic disease or malignancy. | Ferritin normal/high with low iron and low TIBC; hepcidin‑mediated sequestration. |
| Thalassaemia trait | Microcytosis with normal/high ferritin, target cells, elevated HbA₂ (β‑trait) or HbF (α‑trait), family history. | Iron studies normal; haemoglobin electrophoresis shows characteristic pattern. |
| Sideroblastic anaemia | Microcytic, high ferritin, normal/high iron, ring sideroblasts on marrow stain, often alcohol or drug related. | Presence of ≥ 15 % ring sideroblasts; serum iron markedly elevated. |
| Haemolytic anaemia (immune) | Elevated retic, indirect bilirubin, LDH, low haptoglobin, positive direct Coombs, possible splenomegaly. | Direct antiglobulin test positive distinguishes immune from non‑immune haemolysis. |
| Haemolytic anaemia (non‑immune, e.g., G6PD) | Episodic haemoglobin drop after oxidative stress, bite cells, Heinz bodies, normal Coombs. | History of trigger (primaquine, fava beans) and specific smear findings. |
| Megaloblastic anaemia (B12 deficiency) | Macrocytosis, hypersegmented neutrophils, low B12, elevated methylmalonic acid, neurologic signs. | Neurologic deficits and raised methylmalonic acid differentiate from folate deficiency. |
| Megaloblastic anaemia (folate deficiency) | Macrocytosis, low folate, normal B12, normal methylmalonic acid, no neurologic signs. | Normal neurologic exam and methylmalonic acid help exclude B12 deficiency. |
| Chronic kidney disease anaemia | Normocytic, low retic, low serum EPO, elevated creatinine, often accompanied by hypertension or proteinuria. | Low EPO level measured; improvement with erythropoiesis‑stimulating agents. |
| Aplastic anaemia | Pancytopenia, low retic, hypocellular marrow, no splenomegaly, possible exposure to benzene or radiation. | Bone‑marrow biopsy shows empty marrow; distinguishes from peripheral destruction. |

## 3. Investigate with purpose

- **Full haemogram with MCV and red cell indices**: Is it anaemia, and what type? Are other lines affected?
- **Blood film**: Hypochromic cells (iron deficiency), target cells (thalassaemia), sickle cells, spherocytes, schistocytes, parasites, blasts
- **Reticulocyte count**: Is the marrow responding?
- **Ferritin, iron studies**: Iron deficiency (low ferritin); a **raised ferritin can hide deficiency** in inflammation
- **B12 and folate**: Macrocytic anaemia
- **Bilirubin, LDH, haptoglobin, direct Coombs test**: Haemolysis? Autoimmune?
- **Haemoglobin electrophoresis**: Sickle cell disease, thalassaemia
- **Malaria test, HIV test, stool for ova (hookworm)**: Local causes
- **Urea, creatinine, liver and thyroid tests**: Chronic kidney, liver or thyroid disease
- **Bone marrow aspirate and biopsy**: Marrow failure, infiltration, myelodysplasia, leukaemia, myeloma
- **Endoscopy, colonoscopy, or stool occult blood**: **In an adult with iron deficiency, find the bleeding site** until proved otherwise

**Where results can mislead:** **ferritin is an acute-phase reactant**, so a "normal" level in an inflamed patient may still mean deficiency (use transferrin saturation); the **haemoglobin falls late after an acute bleed** (the concentration is unchanged at first); **mixed deficiencies** (iron plus B12) can give a normal MCV.

## 4. Causes by mechanism: the local list

- **Blood loss:** hookworm and schistosomiasis, peptic ulcer, heavy menstrual bleeding, postpartum haemorrhage, colorectal cancer.
- **Haemolysis:** **malaria** (including blackwater fever), **sickle cell disease**, G6PD deficiency (after drugs such as primaquine, dapsone or fava beans), autoimmune haemolysis, thalassaemia.
- **Under-production:** iron, folate or B12 deficiency, **HIV**, TB, chronic kidney disease, malignancy, aplastic anaemia.

## 5. Management

1. **Treat the cause** (deworm, stop the bleeding, treat malaria and infection, start ART, treat the underlying disease).
2. **Replace what is missing:**
   - **Iron deficiency:** oral iron (continue 3 months after the haemoglobin normalises, to refill stores); IV iron when oral iron fails or is not tolerated, in pregnancy, or in kidney disease.
   - **B12 deficiency:** B12 injections (oral for dietary lack only). **Give folate only if B12 is replaced or absent**, or neurological damage can worsen.
   - **Folate deficiency:** folic acid.
3. **Transfuse only when needed:** severe anaemia with **heart failure, shock, ongoing bleeding, or very low haemoglobin with symptoms**. **In chronic anaemia, transfuse slowly** (with a diuretic) to avoid overload.
4. **Special groups:** sickle cell disease (hydroxycarbamide, folate, vaccines, prompt treatment of crises), pregnancy (iron and folate supplements), chronic kidney disease (erythropoietin).

## 6. When to worry

Anaemia **with fever, bleeding, bruising or abnormal white cells or platelets** suggests **leukaemia or marrow failure**: get a film and urgent haematology help.

## Complications and prognosis

- **Cardiovascular strain**: Chronic anaemia reduces oxygen delivery → compensatory tachycardia, left‑ventricular hypertrophy, and eventual heart failure. — Exertional dyspnoea, arrhythmias, congestive heart failure. — Poorer survival in patients with heart disease; early transfusion or erythropoietin improves outcomes.
- **Iron overload**: Repeated transfusions or ineffective erythropoiesis with iron‑recycling → hepatic, cardiac, endocrine deposition. — Hepatomegaly, cirrhosis, cardiomyopathy, diabetes, hypogonadism. — Mortality rises with hepatic failure; chelation therapy mitigates risk.
- **Infection risk**: Anemia of chronic disease or marrow suppression impairs neutrophil function; iron overload favours bacterial growth. — Sepsis, osteomyelitis, invasive fungal disease. — Higher morbidity; early antimicrobial coverage essential.
- **Cognitive and neuro‑psychiatric sequelae**: Severe hypoxia in children → impaired neurodevelopment; B12 deficiency → subacute combined degeneration. — Learning difficulties, ataxia, neuropathy. — Long‑term disability if untreated; early correction restores function.
- **Pregnancy‑related complications**: Maternal anaemia → placental hypoxia → intra‑uterine growth restriction, pre‑eclampsia, perinatal death. — Low birth weight, neonatal anaemia. — Risk stratified by Hb <10 g/dL; aggressive iron/folate therapy improves neonatal outcomes.
- **Marrow failure progression**: Aplastic anaemia or myelodysplasia may evolve to acute leukaemia. — Cytopenias, bleeding, infection. — Prognosis depends on cytogenetics; allogeneic transplant offers cure in fit patients.

**Prognosis** is largely determined by the underlying cause, the degree of haemoglobin reduction, and the presence of comorbidities.  
- *Iron‑deficiency anaemia* in otherwise healthy adults has an excellent prognosis once the source of bleeding is controlled.  
- *Anaemia of chronic disease* carries a guarded outlook; it reflects the severity of the inflammatory or malignant process.  
- *Marrow failure syndromes* have a variable course; early recognition and referral for haematology input are critical.

## Clinical case

**Patient:** 48‑year‑old woman, G2P1, presents with fatigue, dizziness, and a 2‑month history of intermittent bright‑red rectal bleeding. She reports a 4‑kg weight loss over the past month. No history of NSAID use or known gastrointestinal disease.  

**Examination:** Pale conjunctivae, mild tachycardia (HR 102 bpm), blood pressure 110/70 mmHg. Abdomen soft, no organomegaly. Rectal examination reveals a small, friable mass 3 cm from the anal verge.  

**Investigations:**  
- Hb 7.8 g/dL, MCV 78 fL, reticulocyte count 1.2 % (low).  
- Ferritin 12 ng/mL, transferrin saturation 12 %.  
- Normal B12 and folate.  
- Colonoscopy confirms a 3 cm ulcerated polypoid lesion at 12 cm from the anal verge; biopsy shows moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma.  
- CT chest/abdomen/pelvis: no distant metastases.  

**Management plan:**  
1. Discuss surgical options (low anterior resection) with oncology.  
2. Pre‑operative optimisation: transfuse 2 units PRBCs to raise Hb >9 g/dL, start oral iron to rebuild stores.  
3. Post‑operative staging and adjuvant chemotherapy per NCCN guidelines.  

### Questions

1. **Why is the reticulocyte count low in this patient despite significant haemoglobin loss?**  
   *Answer:* The marrow is not responding adequately because iron stores are depleted (low ferritin, low transferrin saturation). Iron‑deficiency anaemia impairs erythropoiesis, leading to a low reticulocyte index even in the setting of chronic blood loss.

2. **What is the most appropriate next step in the management of this patient’s anaemia?**  
   *Answer:* Transfuse packed red blood cells to raise haemoglobin above 9 g/dL, then initiate oral iron therapy to replenish depleted stores. This stabilises the patient for definitive oncological treatment.

3. **Explain the rationale for withholding folate supplementation until B12 status is confirmed.**  
   *Answer:* In B12 deficiency, folate can mask the haematological picture (normalise Hb and MCV) while allowing irreversible neuro‑psychiatric damage to progress. Therefore, folate is withheld until B12 levels are ruled out or B12 therapy is started.
