## 1. Understanding psychosis

### 1.1 Definition

**Psychosis** is a clinical syndrome in which a person has significant impairment in reality testing. The person may interpret, perceive or organise experiences in ways that are not consistent with shared reality.

Psychosis is **a syndrome, not a single diagnosis**. It can occur in primary psychotic disorders, mood disorders, substance-related conditions and medical or neurological illnesses.

A useful clinical starting point is to ask whether the patient has abnormalities in:

- **Belief:** delusions.
- **Perception:** hallucinations.
- **Thinking and communication:** disorganised thought or speech.
- **Behaviour and motor activity:** disorganisation or catatonia.

Do not diagnose schizophrenia merely because a patient reports hearing voices. Establish the duration, context, functional effect, associated mood symptoms, substance exposure and possible medical causes.

### 1.2 Delusions

A **delusion** is a fixed belief that is not readily changed in response to clear evidence, is not ordinarily accepted by the person's cultural or religious community, and is held with a degree of conviction that is out of keeping with the available evidence.

Assess the belief in its cultural context. A culturally shared religious or spiritual belief is not automatically a delusion.

**Common types**

- **Persecutory:** belief that one is being followed, watched, poisoned, threatened or conspired against.
- **Grandiose:** belief that one has exceptional power, wealth, status, knowledge or identity.
- **Referential:** belief that neutral events, gestures, media reports or conversations are specifically directed at oneself.
- **Somatic:** belief that one has a disease, infestation, deformity or abnormal bodily condition.
- **Erotomanic:** belief that another person, often someone of higher status, is in love with the patient.
- **Jealous:** fixed belief that a partner is unfaithful without adequate evidence.
- **Nihilistic:** belief that oneself, one's organs or the world does not exist, is dead or has ceased to function.
- **Guilt or poverty:** excessive, fixed conviction of having committed an unforgivable wrong or having lost all resources despite evidence to the contrary.

**Clinical assessment of a delusion**

Ask what the patient believes, how certain they are, when the belief began, what evidence they use, whether it is shared by their community, how it affects behaviour and whether it creates risk. Avoid arguing aggressively or reinforcing the belief.

### 1.3 Hallucinations

A **hallucination** is a perception-like experience occurring without an external stimulus and experienced with the quality of a real perception.

- **Auditory:** hearing voices, music, sounds or noises when no corresponding sound is present. Voices may comment, converse or issue commands.
- **Visual:** seeing people, animals, shapes, lights or objects that are not present.
- **Olfactory:** smelling an odour without an external source.
- **Gustatory:** experiencing a taste without a corresponding stimulus.
- **Tactile:** feeling touch, crawling or insects on the skin.
- **Somatic:** experiencing abnormal sensations arising from within the body.

Auditory hallucinations are common in schizophrenia. Prominent visual, olfactory or tactile hallucinations should increase suspicion of delirium, intoxication or withdrawal, epilepsy, neurological disease or another medical cause; they do not, by themselves, exclude a primary psychotic disorder.

**Command hallucinations** require specific risk assessment. Establish what the voice is instructing, whether the patient feels compelled to obey, whether they have obeyed previously and whether the command involves self-harm or harm to another person.

### 1.4 Hallucinations, illusions and intrusive thoughts

- **Hallucination:** perception without an external stimulus.
- **Illusion:** misinterpretation of a real external stimulus; for example, mistaking a coat hanging in a dark room for a person.
- **Intrusive thought:** an unwanted thought recognised as arising from one's own mind; common in obsessive-compulsive disorder and not equivalent to thought insertion.
- **Hypnagogic or hypnopompic experience:** perception occurring while falling asleep or waking. These can occur in otherwise healthy people and should be interpreted in context.

### 1.5 Disorders of thought form and speech

Thought form is assessed by listening to how the patient connects and communicates ideas.

- **Circumstantiality:** excessive detail and unnecessary digressions, but the patient eventually answers the question.
- **Tangentiality:** the patient moves away from the question and does not return to it.
- **Flight of ideas:** rapid movement between ideas, with understandable links; classically associated with mania.
- **Loosening of associations:** weak or absent logical connections between ideas.
- **Derailment:** the patient shifts from one topic to another with little connection.
- **Thought blocking:** sudden interruption of speech or thought, with the patient unable to continue.
- **Neologism:** a newly created word or unusual use of a word with a private meaning.
- **Clang associations:** words linked by sound or rhyme rather than meaning.
- **Perseveration:** inappropriate repetition of a response, word or idea.
- **Incoherence or word salad:** speech becomes so disorganised that its meaning cannot be followed.

Differentiate flight of ideas in mania from loosening of associations in schizophrenia: flight of ideas is usually rapid and has identifiable links, whereas loosening of associations involves impaired logical connections.

### 1.6 Disorders of thought possession and control

These are experiences in which the patient believes that thoughts are being controlled or interfered with by an external agent.

- **Thought insertion:** the patient believes thoughts have been placed into their mind by an outside force.
- **Thought withdrawal:** the patient believes thoughts are being removed from their mind.
- **Thought broadcasting:** the patient believes other people can hear or access their thoughts.
- **Delusion of control:** the patient believes their actions, impulses or bodily movements are controlled by an external force.
- **Made feelings or impulses:** the patient experiences feelings or urges as being imposed by an outside agent.

These are traditionally described among Schneiderian first-rank symptoms. They can support a clinical formulation but are **not specific to schizophrenia** and should not be used as a stand-alone diagnostic test.

### 1.7 Positive, negative, cognitive and affective symptoms

![Positive psychotic symptoms: examples of delusions and disorganised thinking](https://res.cloudinary.com/l4wbzpfr/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto,w_1000/v1793772140/ompathstudy/year-4/psychiatry/psychotic-disorders/positive-psychotic-symptoms.png)

**Positive symptoms** are additions or distortions of normal experience:

- Delusions.
- Hallucinations.
- Disorganised speech.
- Grossly disorganised behaviour.
- Catatonic features.

**Negative symptoms** are reductions in normal emotional, motivational or social functioning:

- **Avolition:** reduced motivation and difficulty initiating or sustaining purposeful activities.
- **Alogia:** reduced quantity or spontaneity of speech.
- **Anhedonia:** reduced capacity to experience pleasure.
- **Asociality:** reduced interest in social interaction.
- **Blunted or flat affect:** reduced outward expression of emotion.

Negative symptoms may be primary to schizophrenia or secondary to depression, medication adverse effects, social deprivation, substance use or persistent positive symptoms. Always consider these secondary causes before concluding that negative symptoms are intrinsic to schizophrenia.

**Cognitive symptoms** include impaired attention, working memory, processing speed, verbal learning, planning and executive function.

**Affective symptoms** include depression, anxiety, irritability and mood instability. Mood symptoms may coexist with psychosis and are important for differential diagnosis and suicide-risk assessment.

## 2. Schizophrenia

### 2.1 Definition and overview

**Schizophrenia** is a primary psychotic disorder characterised by disturbances in thought, perception, behaviour, affect and cognition, with a prolonged course and clinically significant effects on functioning.

It is not the same as dissociative identity disorder or “split personality”. The term refers historically to a disruption in the integration of mental functions.

The illness is heterogeneous. Some patients have recurrent episodes with substantial recovery; others experience persistent symptoms and functional impairment.

### 2.2 Epidemiology and usual age of onset

- Schizophrenia occurs across populations and socioeconomic groups.
- Onset is commonly in late adolescence or early adulthood.
- On average, onset is earlier in men than in women.
- Childhood-onset schizophrenia is rare.
- A later onset is possible, so age alone should not be used to exclude the diagnosis.

### 2.3 Aetiology and risk factors

Schizophrenia is understood through a **biopsychosocial and neurodevelopmental model**. No single factor explains every case.

**Genetic vulnerability**

- Schizophrenia is more common among biological relatives of affected people.
- Risk increases with the degree of genetic relatedness.
- Genetic susceptibility is polygenic: many genetic variants contribute small effects.
- A family history increases risk but does not make the illness inevitable.

**Neurodevelopmental and environmental factors**

- Prenatal or perinatal complications.
- Maternal infection or significant nutritional problems during pregnancy.
- Childhood adversity and trauma.
- Social isolation and severe psychosocial stress.
- Urban upbringing and migration-related adversity in some populations.
- Cannabis exposure, particularly frequent use of high-potency cannabis, is associated with increased risk in vulnerable individuals.

**Substances and medicines**

- Cannabis, amphetamines, cocaine and hallucinogens can precipitate psychotic symptoms.
- Dopaminergic medicines and corticosteroids can cause psychosis in some patients.
- Always establish the timing of symptoms in relation to substance or medication exposure.

### 2.4 Neurobiology and dopamine pathways

The dopamine hypothesis proposes that altered dopamine signalling contributes to psychotic symptoms. It is a useful explanatory model, but schizophrenia is not simply “too much dopamine” throughout the brain.

![Four major dopamine pathways and their anatomical projections](https://res.cloudinary.com/l4wbzpfr/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto,w_1000/v1793772134/ompathstudy/year-4/psychiatry/psychotic-disorders/four-dopamine-pathways.png)

**The four major dopamine pathways**

1. **Mesolimbic pathway**
   - Projects from the ventral tegmental area to limbic structures, including the nucleus accumbens.
   - Increased dopamine signalling in this pathway is associated with positive psychotic symptoms.
   - D2 receptor blockade here contributes to the antipsychotic effect.

2. **Mesocortical pathway**
   - Projects from the ventral tegmental area to the prefrontal cortex.
   - Reduced or dysregulated dopamine signalling is associated with negative and cognitive symptoms.
   - Excessive dopamine blockade may worsen secondary negative symptoms in some patients.

3. **Nigrostriatal pathway**
   - Projects from the substantia nigra to the dorsal striatum.
   - Important in motor control.
   - D2 blockade can produce extrapyramidal symptoms: dystonia, akathisia and parkinsonism; longer exposure may cause tardive dyskinesia.

4. **Tuberoinfundibular pathway**
   - Dopamine from the hypothalamus inhibits prolactin release from the anterior pituitary.
   - D2 blockade removes this inhibition and may cause hyperprolactinaemia, galactorrhoea, amenorrhoea, sexual dysfunction and reduced bone density over time.

**Other neurobiological systems**

- Glutamate, especially NMDA receptor signalling, is implicated in cognitive and psychotic symptoms.
- GABAergic inhibitory signalling and serotonergic systems also contribute.
- Structural and functional brain findings have been reported at group level, but no scan can confirm schizophrenia in an individual patient.

### 2.5 Clinical presentation and course

Symptoms may develop gradually or acutely. A patient may present with:

- Suspiciousness, social withdrawal or unusual beliefs.
- Hearing voices or experiencing other hallucinations.
- Fixed delusions.
- Disorganised speech or behaviour.
- Declining academic, occupational or social functioning.
- Reduced motivation, emotional expression or self-care.
- Cognitive difficulties.

**Common clinical phases**

- **Premorbid period:** before clear illness, some individuals may have subtle social, academic or developmental difficulties. These are not diagnostic.
- **Prodromal period:** emerging changes such as withdrawal, reduced concentration, sleep disturbance, declining function or suspiciousness before a clear psychotic episode.
- **Acute phase:** prominent delusions, hallucinations, disorganisation or catatonia.
- **Residual or recovery phase:** acute symptoms reduce, but negative, cognitive or attenuated psychotic symptoms may remain.

Not every patient follows these phases in a fixed sequence.

### 2.6 Diagnosis of schizophrenia

Diagnosis is clinical. Use the diagnostic system specified by the examination or clinical setting.

**DSM-5-TR criteria — key requirements**

**A. Characteristic symptoms:** at least two of the following must be present for a significant portion of a one-month period (or less if successfully treated):

1. Delusions.
2. Hallucinations.
3. Disorganised speech.
4. Grossly disorganised or catatonic behaviour.
5. Negative symptoms.

At least one symptom must be delusions, hallucinations or disorganised speech.

**B. Functional impairment:** functioning in work, interpersonal relationships or self-care is markedly below the previous level, or the expected level has not been achieved when onset is in childhood or adolescence.

**C. Duration:** continuous signs of disturbance persist for at least six months. This includes at least one month of active-phase symptoms, or less if successfully treated; the remaining time may include prodromal or residual symptoms.

**D. Mood-disorder exclusion:** schizoaffective disorder and depressive or bipolar disorder with psychotic features must be excluded.

**E. Substance and medical exclusion:** symptoms are not attributable to a substance, medication or another medical condition.

**F. Developmental conditions:** if there is a history of autism spectrum disorder or a childhood-onset communication disorder, prominent delusions or hallucinations must also be present for at least one month (or less if successfully treated).

**ICD-11 distinction**

ICD-11 uses a different duration threshold for schizophrenia: characteristic symptoms are generally present for at least one month. Do not mix the DSM-5-TR six-month rule with the ICD-11 one-month rule in the same diagnostic answer.

### 2.7 Investigations

There is no blood test, scan or psychological test that independently confirms schizophrenia. Investigations are selected to exclude secondary causes, assess physical health and establish baseline monitoring before antipsychotic treatment.

**Initial assessment may include:**

- Full blood count.
- Urea, electrolytes and creatinine.
- Liver function tests.
- Blood glucose or HbA1c.
- Lipid profile.
- Thyroid function tests when indicated.
- Urine toxicology when substance exposure is suspected and testing is clinically appropriate.
- Pregnancy testing where relevant.
- ECG when indicated by cardiac history, symptoms, risk factors or the selected antipsychotic.

**Additional investigations guided by presentation**

- HIV and syphilis testing according to consent, local protocols and clinical indication.
- Vitamin B12, folate and other nutritional tests if deficiency is suspected.
- Brain CT or MRI for focal neurological signs, head injury, seizures, atypical presentation or other red flags.
- EEG when seizures or episodic altered awareness are suspected.
- Autoimmune, infectious or metabolic investigations when the history and examination suggest these causes.

### 2.8 Management of schizophrenia

Management is individualised and multidisciplinary. The aims are to treat acute symptoms, reduce relapse, preserve physical health, improve functioning and support recovery.

**A. Immediate and acute management**

1. Assess immediate safety, including suicide, self-harm, violence, vulnerability and self-neglect.
2. Assess physical health, vital signs, hydration, nutrition, level of consciousness and possible intoxication or withdrawal.
3. Consider delirium and other medical causes, especially in first-episode or atypical psychosis.
4. Establish a calm, low-stimulation environment and communicate respectfully.
5. Involve family or caregivers with the patient's consent, while respecting confidentiality.
6. Decide whether community treatment or hospital admission is required.
7. Start an appropriate antipsychotic after discussing benefits, adverse effects and patient preference.
8. Treat coexisting substance use, mood symptoms and medical illness.

**Consider admission** when there is serious risk to self or others, severe self-neglect, inability to meet basic needs, severe agitation, catatonia, diagnostic uncertainty requiring urgent assessment or failure of safe community management. Follow applicable Kenyan law and use the least restrictive safe option.

**B. Antipsychotic medication**

Antipsychotics are first-line pharmacological treatment for acute schizophrenia. Most reduce dopamine D2 receptor signalling; second-generation agents also have varying serotonin receptor actions.

**First-generation (typical) antipsychotics**

- **Haloperidol:** potent D2 antagonist; effective for positive symptoms but has a relatively high risk of extrapyramidal symptoms.
- **Chlorpromazine:** lower potency at D2 receptors; more sedation, anticholinergic effects and postural hypotension.
- **Fluphenazine:** a high-potency typical antipsychotic; long-acting injectable formulations are available in some settings.
- **Trifluoperazine:** another high-potency typical antipsychotic.

**Second-generation (atypical) antipsychotics**

- **Risperidone:** can cause dose-related extrapyramidal symptoms and hyperprolactinaemia.
- **Olanzapine:** substantial risk of weight gain, dyslipidaemia and hyperglycaemia.
- **Quetiapine:** sedation, postural hypotension and metabolic adverse effects.
- **Amisulpride:** hyperprolactinaemia and dose-related extrapyramidal symptoms.
- **Aripiprazole:** partial dopamine D2 agonist; may cause akathisia and is less likely to raise prolactin.
- **Clozapine:** important for treatment-resistant schizophrenia; requires specialist supervision and blood monitoring.

The choice depends on previous response, adverse-effect profile, physical comorbidity, pregnancy considerations, availability, cost and patient preference. Start according to local guidance, titrate carefully and use the lowest effective dose. Avoid unnecessary antipsychotic polypharmacy.

**C. Clozapine and treatment resistance**

Consider treatment-resistant schizophrenia when symptoms have not responded adequately to at least two different antipsychotics, each given at an adequate dose and duration with adherence assessed.

Clozapine is the recommended treatment for many patients with treatment-resistant schizophrenia, provided monitoring can be delivered safely.

Important risks and monitoring:

- Neutropenia/agranulocytosis: regular absolute neutrophil count or blood count monitoring according to the current local protocol.
- Myocarditis or cardiomyopathy: assess symptoms such as chest pain, dyspnoea, persistent tachycardia or fever, particularly early in treatment.
- Seizures: risk increases with dose and plasma concentration.
- Severe constipation and gastrointestinal hypomotility: actively ask about bowel function and treat constipation early.
- Weight gain, dyslipidaemia and hyperglycaemia.
- Sedation, hypersalivation and postural hypotension.

Do not initiate or continue clozapine without the required monitoring arrangements.

**D. Long-acting injectable (LAI) antipsychotics**

Examples include haloperidol decanoate, fluphenazine decanoate, paliperidone palmitate, risperidone long-acting injection and aripiprazole long-acting formulations.

They may be useful when a patient prefers less frequent dosing, has repeated relapse associated with difficulty taking oral medication, or needs a treatment plan that supports adherence. Discuss the choice collaboratively; an LAI is not a punishment and should not replace attention to adverse effects or social barriers.

**E. Psychological and psychosocial interventions**

- **Psychoeducation:** explain the illness, treatment options, early warning signs and relapse plan.
- **Family intervention:** improve communication, reduce expressed emotion and support caregivers.
- **Cognitive behavioural therapy for psychosis (CBTp):** help the patient examine distressing experiences, develop coping strategies and reduce the impact of persistent symptoms.
- **Social skills training:** support communication and interpersonal functioning.
- **Occupational and vocational rehabilitation:** help the patient return to education, work and meaningful daily activity.
- **Supported education and employment:** maintain participation in school or work where possible.
- **Substance-use treatment:** address cannabis, stimulants, alcohol and other substances.
- **Community follow-up:** coordinate medication, physical health, family and rehabilitation needs.

**F. Maintenance and relapse prevention**

- Continue effective treatment after acute improvement; stopping medication abruptly increases relapse risk.
- Agree on a follow-up plan and monitor adherence without blaming the patient.
- Identify the individual's early warning signs, such as insomnia, withdrawal, suspiciousness or increasing preoccupation with voices.
- Monitor weight, blood pressure, glucose/HbA1c, lipids and movement disorders.
- Review smoking, alcohol and other substance use. Smoking changes can alter clozapine and olanzapine concentrations.
- Support family involvement and community functioning.

### 2.9 Adverse effects of antipsychotics: high-yield review

- **Extrapyramidal symptoms:** related mainly to D2 blockade in the nigrostriatal pathway.
- **Hyperprolactinaemia:** related to D2 blockade in the tuberoinfundibular pathway.
- **Metabolic syndrome:** especially associated with olanzapine and clozapine, though possible with other agents.
- **Sedation:** varies by drug and receptor profile.
- **Anticholinergic effects:** dry mouth, blurred vision, constipation and urinary retention.
- **Postural hypotension:** dizziness or syncope on standing.
- **QT prolongation:** risk varies by drug and patient factors; consider ECG when indicated.
- **Neuroleptic malignant syndrome:** rare, life-threatening emergency.

### 2.10 Complications and prognosis

**Complications**

- Suicide and self-harm.
- Depression and anxiety.
- Substance use disorders.
- Poor physical health, obesity, diabetes and cardiovascular disease.
- Social isolation, unemployment and interrupted education.
- Homelessness, stigma, discrimination and family strain.
- Relapse and repeated hospitalisation.

**Factors associated with a more favourable course**

- Good premorbid functioning.
- Acute rather than insidious onset.
- Short duration of untreated psychosis.
- Good social support.
- Effective treatment and sustained engagement.
- Predominantly positive symptoms with fewer persistent negative symptoms.

**Factors associated with a poorer course**

- Insidious onset.
- Prominent persistent negative symptoms.
- Poor premorbid functioning.
- Coexisting substance use.
- Long duration of untreated psychosis.
- Repeated relapse and poor treatment engagement.
- Limited social support.

Prognosis is individual. A diagnosis of schizophrenia does not mean that recovery is impossible.

## 3. Schizophreniform disorder

**Definition:** A schizophrenia-like psychotic disorder in which the disturbance lasts **at least one month but less than six months** under DSM-5-TR.

**Clinical features**

- Delusions.
- Hallucinations.
- Disorganised speech.
- Grossly disorganised or catatonic behaviour.
- Negative symptoms.

The same characteristic symptom rule applies as for schizophrenia: at least two symptoms, with at least one being delusions, hallucinations or disorganised speech.

**Key diagnostic distinction**

- Duration is at least one month but less than six months.
- Marked functional decline is not required, unlike the DSM-5-TR schizophrenia criterion.
- If the disturbance continues beyond six months and the other criteria are met, the diagnosis may be revised to schizophrenia.
- Exclude mood disorders, substances and medical causes.

**Management**

- Assess risk and physical health.
- Treat significant psychotic symptoms with an antipsychotic when indicated.
- Provide psychoeducation and family support.
- Monitor symptoms, functioning and adverse effects.
- Follow up beyond the acute episode because the eventual course may clarify the diagnosis.

**Prognosis:** Some patients recover fully; others later meet criteria for schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder.

## 4. Brief psychotic disorder

**Definition:** Sudden onset of one or more psychotic symptoms lasting **at least one day but less than one month**, followed by full return to the previous level of functioning.

**Possible precipitating factors**

- Bereavement.
- Severe interpersonal conflict.
- Major life events.
- Natural disasters or accidents.
- Childbirth.
- Severe psychosocial stress.

A stressor is not required in every case.

**Clinical features**

- Delusions.
- Hallucinations.
- Disorganised speech.
- Grossly disorganised or catatonic behaviour.

**Diagnosis**

- At least one core symptom must be delusions, hallucinations or disorganised speech.
- Duration is at least one day but less than one month.
- The patient returns to premorbid functioning.
- Exclude schizophrenia-spectrum disorders, mood disorders with psychotic features, substance effects and medical causes.

**Management**

- Immediate risk assessment and safe environment.
- Investigate possible medical or substance-related causes.
- Supportive care and reduction of acute stressors.
- Antipsychotic medication may be used when symptoms are severe or distressing.
- Arrange close follow-up to confirm recovery and identify recurrence.

**Prognosis:** Often favourable, with return to previous functioning, although recurrence or later diagnosis of another disorder can occur.

## 5. Schizoaffective disorder

### 5.1 Definition

Schizoaffective disorder is characterised by an illness in which schizophrenia-type psychotic symptoms occur together with major mood episodes (manic or depressive), with psychosis also occurring independently of a major mood episode.

### 5.2 Types

- **Bipolar type:** a manic episode is part of the illness; major depressive episodes may also occur.
- **Depressive type:** major depressive episodes occur, without a history of manic episodes.

### 5.3 Clinical features

**Psychotic features**

- Delusions.
- Hallucinations.
- Disorganised speech.
- Disorganised behaviour.
- Negative symptoms.

**Manic features**

- Elevated, expansive or irritable mood.
- Increased energy and activity.
- Reduced need for sleep.
- Increased or pressured speech.
- Grandiosity.
- Racing thoughts or flight of ideas.
- Increased goal-directed activity or risky behaviour.

**Depressive features**

- Persistent low mood.
- Loss of interest or pleasure.
- Reduced energy.
- Sleep or appetite disturbance.
- Poor concentration.
- Worthlessness or excessive guilt.
- Thoughts of death or suicide.

### 5.4 DSM-5-TR diagnostic logic

Three points are essential:

1. There is an uninterrupted period of illness during which a major mood episode (manic or depressive) occurs at the same time as the schizophrenia symptom criterion.
2. Delusions or hallucinations occur for **at least two weeks in the absence of a major mood episode** at some point during the lifetime course of the illness.
3. Major mood episodes are present for the majority of the total duration of the active and residual portions of the illness.

Substance effects and medical causes must be excluded.

**Examination distinction:** If psychosis occurs only during a manic or depressive episode, consider bipolar disorder or major depressive disorder with psychotic features. The two-week period of psychosis without a major mood episode is a key DSM-5-TR distinction for schizoaffective disorder.

### 5.5 Management

- Antipsychotic medication for psychotic symptoms.
- Mood stabiliser for bipolar-type illness when indicated.
- Antidepressant treatment may be considered for depressive symptoms after assessing for bipolarity and risk of mood switching.
- Psychological therapy, psychoeducation and family intervention.
- Suicide-risk assessment and monitoring.
- Long-term relapse prevention and physical-health monitoring.

## 6. Delusional disorder

### 6.1 Definition

Delusional disorder is characterised by one or more delusions lasting **at least one month**, without the full symptom pattern of schizophrenia.

Functioning may remain relatively preserved outside the direct consequences of the delusion. Behaviour is not markedly disorganised or bizarre overall.

### 6.2 Types

- **Persecutory:** belief that one is being harmed, followed, deceived or conspired against.
- **Grandiose:** belief in exceptional ability, identity, wealth or status.
- **Erotomanic:** belief that another person is in love with the patient.
- **Jealous:** belief that a partner is unfaithful.
- **Somatic:** belief about bodily disease, infestation or defect.
- **Mixed:** more than one delusional theme without a single predominant type.
- **Unspecified:** presentation does not fit the recognised types.

### 6.3 Clinical features

- One or more persistent delusions.
- Hallucinations are absent or, if present, are not prominent and are related to the delusional theme.
- General functioning may be relatively preserved.
- Behaviour is not obviously bizarre apart from consequences of the delusion.
- Insight into the delusion is usually limited.

### 6.4 Diagnosis

DSM-5-TR requires delusions lasting at least one month; schizophrenia criterion A has never been met; functioning is not markedly impaired apart from the impact of the delusion; and any mood episodes are brief relative to the delusional periods. The condition must not be due to substances or another medical condition.

### 6.5 Management

- Build a respectful therapeutic relationship.
- Explore distress, risk and functional effects.
- Do not ridicule, aggressively challenge or reinforce the delusion.
- Consider antipsychotic medication after shared decision-making.
- Consider CBT and supportive therapy.
- Treat comorbid depression, anxiety or substance use.
- Assess risk, especially with persecutory or jealous delusions.

**Prognosis:** The course may be chronic. Some patients maintain relatively good functioning, while others develop substantial social or occupational difficulties.

## 7. Acute and transient psychotic disorder

### 7.1 Definition

Acute and transient psychotic disorder is an ICD-based category characterised by an acute onset of psychotic symptoms, typically reaching a clearly psychotic level within approximately two weeks, with a relatively short course.

The term is associated with ICD-10 and remains represented in ICD-11, although the detailed diagnostic descriptions differ. Do not assume that it is identical to DSM-5-TR brief psychotic disorder.

### 7.2 Clinical features

- Acute onset of delusions, hallucinations or disorganised thinking.
- Rapidly changing symptoms in some presentations.
- Emotional changes, anxiety or perplexity.
- Behavioural disturbance.
- Possible polymorphic symptoms, in which the type and intensity of symptoms change rapidly.

### 7.3 Assessment and management

- Establish the exact onset and progression.
- Obtain collateral history.
- Exclude delirium, intoxication, withdrawal, neurological disease and other medical causes.
- Assess suicide, violence, vulnerability and self-neglect.
- Provide a safe environment and antipsychotic treatment when indicated.
- Address stressors and arrange close follow-up.

Many patients improve, but recurrence or later development of another psychotic disorder is possible.

## 8. Substance- or medication-induced psychotic disorder

### 8.1 Definition

Substance- or medication-induced psychotic disorder involves delusions and/or hallucinations that develop during or soon after intoxication, withdrawal or exposure to a substance or medication capable of producing psychotic symptoms.

### 8.2 Important causative substances

- **Cannabis:** paranoia, hallucinations and delusions; risk is greater with frequent use and high-potency products.
- **Amphetamines and methamphetamine:** agitation, insomnia, persecutory delusions and hallucinations.
- **Cocaine:** paranoia, delusions and tactile hallucinations.
- **Hallucinogens:** marked perceptual changes and sometimes psychotic symptoms.
- **Phencyclidine (PCP):** psychosis, agitation, altered behaviour and possible neurological or autonomic abnormalities.
- **Alcohol:** hallucinations may occur in alcohol-related conditions; distinguish alcohol-induced psychotic disorder from delirium tremens.
- **Prescription medicines:** corticosteroids, dopaminergic medicines and selected other drugs can precipitate psychosis.

### 8.3 Clinical assessment

- Ask directly about all substances, prescribed medicines, non-prescribed medicines and herbal preparations.
- Establish the dose, frequency, route, last use and timing of symptom onset.
- Ask about previous episodes during abstinence.
- Assess sleep deprivation, withdrawal symptoms, autonomic instability and seizures.
- Obtain collateral history where possible.
- Use toxicology tests as supportive evidence, not as a substitute for history and examination.

### 8.4 Diagnosis

Evidence should support a temporal relationship between the psychosis and substance exposure or withdrawal, and the substance must be capable of producing the symptoms. The presentation must not occur exclusively during delirium and must not be better explained by an independent psychotic disorder.

Persistent symptoms after the expected intoxication or withdrawal period should prompt reassessment for a primary psychotic disorder or another medical cause. A positive toxicology result alone does not prove causation.

### 8.5 Management

**Immediate management**

- ABCDE assessment where the patient is medically unwell or severely agitated.
- Check vital signs, temperature, oxygen saturation and blood glucose.
- Assess level of consciousness, hydration, trauma, seizures and hyperthermia.
- Identify the likely substance and treat intoxication or withdrawal according to the relevant protocol.
- Provide a calm, low-stimulation environment.
- Benzodiazepines are often important for stimulant-related agitation and alcohol withdrawal; antipsychotics may be considered for persistent psychosis or severe agitation after assessing contraindications and the clinical context.

**Longer-term management**

- Support cessation of the causative substance.
- Offer addiction treatment and psychological interventions.
- Address social factors and relapse triggers.
- Monitor for persistent psychosis or recurrence during abstinence.

## 9. Differential diagnosis of psychosis

Psychosis can be caused by primary psychiatric illness, mood disorders, substances, medicines, neurological disease or systemic medical illness. First-episode psychosis requires particular attention to secondary causes.

### 9.1 Psychiatric differentials

**Major depressive disorder with psychotic features**

- A major depressive episode is present.
- Delusions or hallucinations occur during the depressive episode.
- Psychotic content may be mood-congruent, such as guilt, poverty, disease or deserved punishment, but can also be mood-incongruent.
- If psychosis occurs only during depression, this favours depression with psychotic features over schizoaffective disorder.

**Bipolar disorder with psychotic features**

- Psychosis occurs during a manic or depressive episode.
- Mania includes elevated or irritable mood, increased energy, reduced need for sleep, pressured speech, grandiosity and increased goal-directed activity.
- Psychosis that persists outside mood episodes should prompt consideration of schizoaffective disorder or another psychotic disorder.

**Delirium**

- Acute disturbance of attention and awareness.
- Usually develops over hours to days and fluctuates.
- Disorientation and impaired attention are common.
- Level of consciousness may be altered.
- Visual hallucinations may occur.
- Usually due to an underlying medical, neurological, toxic or withdrawal-related cause.

**Dementia and neurocognitive disorders**

- Progressive cognitive decline is usually the background problem.
- Delusions, hallucinations and behavioural changes may develop.
- Consider dementia with Lewy bodies when visual hallucinations, cognitive fluctuations and parkinsonism coexist.

### 9.2 Medical and neurological causes

- HIV-related CNS disease and opportunistic infections.
- Neurosyphilis.
- Cerebral malaria or other CNS infections, depending on presentation and epidemiology.
- Autoimmune encephalitis.
- Temporal lobe epilepsy and other seizure disorders.
- Brain tumour or traumatic brain injury.
- Stroke and other cerebrovascular disease.
- Thyroid dysfunction.
- Severe electrolyte or metabolic disturbances.
- Hepatic or renal encephalopathy.
- Vitamin B12 deficiency and other nutritional deficiencies.
- Systemic lupus erythematosus with CNS involvement.

### 9.3 Substance- and medication-related causes

- Cannabis.
- Cocaine.
- Amphetamines and methamphetamine.
- Hallucinogens.
- Alcohol intoxication or withdrawal.
- Corticosteroids.
- Dopaminergic medicines.
- Other prescribed or non-prescribed drugs, depending on exposure.

### 9.4 Delirium versus primary psychosis

| Feature | Delirium | Primary psychotic disorder |
|---|---|---|
| Onset | Usually acute, hours to days | Often days to weeks, sometimes gradual |
| Course | Fluctuates, often worse at night | Usually less fluctuating over the day |
| Attention | Impaired | Often relatively preserved |
| Consciousness | May be altered | Usually clear |
| Orientation | Frequently impaired | Often preserved |
| Perception | Visual hallucinations are common | Auditory hallucinations are common in schizophrenia |
| Cause | Usually medical, neurological, toxic or withdrawal-related | Primary psychiatric illness after exclusions |
| Priority | Urgent identification and treatment of cause | Psychiatric treatment and risk management |

**Clinical warning:** A patient with new-onset psychosis plus fever, fluctuating consciousness, inattention, seizures, severe headache, focal neurological signs, abnormal vital signs or recent head injury needs urgent medical assessment. Do not assume it is schizophrenia.

## 10. Clinical assessment of a patient presenting with psychosis

### 10.1 History

Obtain a detailed history from the patient and, where appropriate and consented to, collateral informants.

**Presenting complaint and history of presenting illness**

- What has changed?
- When did the symptoms begin?
- Was onset sudden or gradual?
- Are symptoms continuous or episodic?
- What is the effect on school, work, relationships and self-care?
- Have there been previous episodes?
- What treatment has been tried and with what response?

**Psychotic symptoms**

- Ask about unusual beliefs and the degree of conviction.
- Ask about voices, visions or other perceptual experiences.
- Establish whether voices comment, converse or issue commands.
- Ask about thought interference, control and broadcasting.
- Explore disorganisation, unusual behaviour and catatonic symptoms.

**Mood and anxiety symptoms**

- Ask about low mood, anhedonia, hopelessness and suicidal thinking.
- Ask about elevated or irritable mood, increased energy, reduced need for sleep and impulsive behaviour.
- Establish whether psychosis occurs only during mood episodes or also outside them.

**Substance and medication history**

- Ask about cannabis, alcohol, stimulants, hallucinogens and other substances.
- Ask about prescribed drugs, over-the-counter preparations and herbal products.
- Establish timing of exposure in relation to symptoms.

**Medical, neurological and psychosocial history**

- Fever, headache, seizures, head injury, confusion or focal neurological symptoms.
- Previous medical and psychiatric diagnoses.
- Family history of psychiatric illness.
- Developmental, educational and occupational history.
- Trauma, bereavement, relationships, housing and social support.
- Pregnancy, childbirth and postpartum timing where relevant.

### 10.2 Mental State Examination (MSE)

![Mental State Examination framework showing key domains for clinical assessment](https://res.cloudinary.com/l4wbzpfr/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto,w_1200/v1793772106/ompathstudy/year-4/psychiatry/psychotic-disorders/mse-framework.jpg)

| Domain | What to assess |
|---|---|
| Appearance and behaviour | Grooming, hygiene, eye contact, posture, cooperation, agitation, retardation and abnormal movements |
| Speech | Rate, volume, tone, quantity, fluency, pressure and coherence |
| Mood | Subjective emotional state reported by the patient |
| Affect | Observed emotional expression: range, reactivity, intensity and congruence |
| Thought form | Flow and organisation: tangentiality, circumstantiality, flight of ideas, loosening and thought blocking |
| Thought content | Delusions, overvalued ideas, obsessions, suicidal thoughts and thoughts of harming others |
| Perception | Auditory, visual and other hallucinations; illusions and depersonalisation/derealisation where relevant |
| Cognition | Level of consciousness, attention, orientation, memory and executive function |
| Insight | Awareness of experiences, illness, need for treatment and consequences |
| Judgement | Ability to make safe and appropriate decisions in the current situation |

**Document findings descriptively.** For example: “The patient reports hearing two male voices commenting on his actions” is more clinically useful than writing only “hallucinating”.

### 10.3 Physical and neurological examination

- General appearance, hydration, nutrition and vital signs.
- Temperature and oxygen saturation.
- Cardiovascular and respiratory examination when indicated.
- Neurological examination, including focal signs, gait, tone and abnormal movements.
- Signs of intoxication, withdrawal, trauma or systemic illness.
- Assessment for rigidity, tremor, dystonia or other medication-related adverse effects.

### 10.4 Risk assessment

Assess risk at the first presentation and review it as the clinical state changes.

- **Suicide and self-harm:** ideation, intent, plan, access to means, previous attempts, hopelessness and protective factors.
- **Risk to others:** threats, past violence, persecutory beliefs, command hallucinations, access to weapons and substance intoxication.
- **Self-neglect:** food, fluids, hygiene, shelter and ability to take essential treatment.
- **Vulnerability:** exploitation, homelessness, wandering, abuse and inability to protect oneself.
- **Risk to dependants:** assess whether children or other dependants are safe and cared for.

A risk assessment is not a prediction with certainty. It informs a safety plan, observation level, treatment and need for admission.

## 11. Important associated emergencies and treatment complications

### 11.1 Catatonia

**Definition:** A psychomotor syndrome involving marked abnormalities of movement, behaviour and responsiveness. It can occur in schizophrenia, mood disorders and medical conditions.

**Clinical signs**

- Stupor.
- Mutism.
- Negativism.
- Posturing.
- Catalepsy.
- Waxy flexibility.
- Echolalia.
- Echopraxia.
- Stereotypy.
- Mannerisms.
- Agitation not influenced by external stimuli.

**Assessment and management**

- Treat as a potentially serious medical and psychiatric emergency.
- Assess hydration, nutrition, immobility complications, infection and venous thromboembolism risk.
- Review medicines and exclude neurological, metabolic and other medical causes.
- A lorazepam challenge may support diagnosis and treatment under clinical supervision.
- Benzodiazepines, particularly lorazepam, are commonly first-line.
- ECT is effective in severe, malignant or refractory catatonia.
- Provide supportive nursing care and prevent pressure injury, dehydration and thrombosis.

### 11.2 Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS)

NMS is a rare, life-threatening reaction associated with dopamine-blocking drugs and, in some cases, abrupt withdrawal of dopaminergic medication.

**Classical features**

- Hyperthermia.
- Severe generalised muscle rigidity.
- Altered mental status.
- Autonomic instability: tachycardia, sweating and fluctuating blood pressure.

**Investigations may show**

- Raised creatine kinase (CK).
- Leukocytosis.
- Electrolyte abnormalities.
- Myoglobinuria and acute kidney injury.

**Management**

1. Stop the causative antipsychotic or dopamine-blocking drug immediately.
2. Arrange emergency hospital-level care.
3. Provide supportive care, cooling and intravenous fluids as indicated.
4. Monitor renal function, electrolytes, CK, urine output and vital signs.
5. Consider specialist-directed treatment such as bromocriptine or dantrolene in selected severe cases.

### 11.3 Extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS)

![Illustration of common antipsychotic-related extrapyramidal movement disorders](https://res.cloudinary.com/l4wbzpfr/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto,w_1000/v1793772114/ompathstudy/year-4/psychiatry/psychotic-disorders/antipsychotic-eps.png)

EPS are movement disorders associated mainly with dopamine D2 blockade in the nigrostriatal pathway.

**Acute dystonia**

- Sudden sustained muscle contractions, often within hours or days of starting or increasing an antipsychotic.
- May cause torticollis, jaw spasm, tongue protrusion or oculogyric crisis.
- Laryngeal dystonia is an emergency.
- Treat promptly with an appropriate anticholinergic or antihistamine according to local protocol.

**Akathisia**

- Subjective inner restlessness with an urge to move.
- Pacing, rocking or inability to sit still.
- May be mistaken for worsening agitation.
- Review the antipsychotic dose or switch; propranolol may be considered if appropriate.

**Drug-induced parkinsonism**

- Bradykinesia.
- Rigidity.
- Resting tremor.
- Reduced facial expression and shuffling gait.
- Consider dose reduction or switching; selected patients may benefit from anticholinergic treatment.

**Tardive dyskinesia**

- Repetitive involuntary movements, often involving the tongue, lips, jaw or face; limbs and trunk may also be affected.
- Usually associated with prolonged dopamine receptor-blocking exposure, though it can occur earlier.
- Review antipsychotic treatment and consider a VMAT2 inhibitor where available and appropriate.

### 11.4 Postpartum psychosis

Postpartum psychosis is a severe psychiatric emergency that commonly begins in the first two weeks after childbirth, although onset can occur later.

**Features**

- Severe insomnia.
- Rapidly changing mood.
- Confusion or disorientation.
- Delusions and hallucinations.
- Disorganised behaviour.
- Agitation or severe depression.
- Possible risk of suicide or harm to the infant.

**Management**

- Immediate specialist psychiatric and obstetric assessment.
- Hospital admission is usually required.
- Ensure safety of mother and infant and arrange appropriate supervised infant care.
- Assess for medical causes, including infection, metabolic disturbance and postpartum complications.
- Treat with appropriate antipsychotic and mood-stabilising strategies under specialist supervision.
- ECT may be considered in severe or life-threatening presentations.

Do not confuse postpartum psychosis with postpartum blues, which is usually mild and self-limiting, or postpartum depression, which does not necessarily involve psychosis.

## 12. The 3 Ps: psychiatric formulation

The **3 Ps** are a practical way to organise factors that explain why a patient became unwell and what may be maintaining the illness.

### 12.1 Predisposing factors

Factors that increase vulnerability before the illness begins:

- Family history or genetic vulnerability.
- Childhood trauma or adversity.
- Previous psychiatric illness.
- Neurodevelopmental vulnerability.
- Chronic medical illness.
- Long-standing social disadvantage.

### 12.2 Precipitating factors

Events that trigger the current episode:

- Cannabis or stimulant use.
- Sleep deprivation.
- Acute stress or bereavement.
- Relationship breakdown.
- Medication discontinuation.
- Medical illness or neurological insult.

### 12.3 Perpetuating factors

Factors that maintain symptoms or interfere with recovery:

- Continued substance use.
- Poor treatment adherence or intolerable adverse effects.
- Social isolation.
- Family conflict.
- Stigma and discrimination.
- Homelessness or financial stress.
- Untreated medical or psychiatric comorbidity.

**Clinical example**

A 23-year-old student develops persecutory delusions and auditory hallucinations after several weeks of frequent cannabis use and severe sleep deprivation.

- Predisposing: family history of psychotic illness.
- Precipitating: cannabis exposure and sleep deprivation.
- Perpetuating: continued cannabis use, social isolation and stopping treatment because of adverse effects.

The formulation guides treatment: address the psychosis, substance use, sleep, adverse effects and social support rather than focusing only on the diagnostic label.

## 13. Comparing the major psychotic disorders

| Disorder | Key duration or distinguishing feature |
|---|---|
| Schizophrenia (DSM-5-TR) | Continuous disturbance for at least 6 months, including active-phase symptoms |
| Schizophrenia (ICD-11) | Characteristic symptoms generally present for at least 1 month |
| Schizophreniform disorder | At least 1 month but less than 6 months (DSM-5-TR) |
| Brief psychotic disorder | At least 1 day but less than 1 month, with return to premorbid functioning |
| Schizoaffective disorder | Mood episode with schizophrenia symptoms, plus at least 2 weeks of delusions or hallucinations without a major mood episode |
| Delusional disorder | One or more delusions for at least 1 month; schizophrenia criteria not met |
| Acute and transient psychotic disorder | ICD category; acute onset, usually within 2 weeks, relatively short course |
| Substance-induced psychotic disorder | Psychosis temporally related to intoxication, withdrawal or medication exposure |

**Memory anchor for DSM-5-TR duration**

- Brief psychotic disorder: **1 day to less than 1 month**.
- Schizophreniform disorder: **1 month to less than 6 months**.
- Schizophrenia: **at least 6 months**.

Do not apply these DSM duration rules to ICD-11 without checking the relevant ICD criteria.

## 14. Clinical approach: a stepwise framework

When a patient presents with suspected psychosis:

1. **Stabilise:** assess ABCDE, vital signs, glucose, consciousness and immediate medical threats.
2. **Ensure safety:** assess suicide, violence, command hallucinations, vulnerability and self-neglect.
3. **Describe the syndrome:** identify delusions, hallucinations, thought disorder, behaviour and catatonia.
4. **Establish time course:** sudden or gradual onset; duration; episodic or continuous course.
5. **Assess mood:** determine whether psychosis occurs only during mania or depression, or also independently.
6. **Assess substances and medicines:** establish exposure, timing and previous episodes.
7. **Exclude secondary causes:** use history, physical examination and targeted investigations.
8. **Formulate and diagnose:** use DSM-5-TR or ICD-11 consistently.
9. **Treat:** address the cause, manage psychosis, choose medication collaboratively and provide psychosocial care.
10. **Plan follow-up:** monitor symptoms, adverse effects, physical health, adherence and early warning signs.

## 15. Examination-focused clinical scenarios

**Scenario 1**

A 22-year-old has heard voices commenting on his behaviour and believes neighbours are monitoring him. Symptoms have persisted for eight months, with marked decline in university performance. There is no substance exposure or mood episode.

- Most likely diagnosis: schizophrenia, if full criteria and exclusions are met.
- Key clue: duration beyond six months with functional decline.

**Scenario 2**

A patient develops delusions and disorganised speech for six weeks. There is no major mood episode and no evidence of substance use.

- Consider: schizophreniform disorder.
- Key clue: one to six months under DSM-5-TR.

**Scenario 3**

A patient develops hallucinations and delusions over five days after a major traumatic event. Symptoms resolve completely within three weeks and previous functioning returns.

- Consider: brief psychotic disorder, if all criteria are met.
- Key clue: one day to less than one month with full return to baseline.

**Scenario 4**

A patient has recurrent mania and psychosis. During the longitudinal illness, delusions continue for three weeks after the manic episode has resolved.

- Consider: schizoaffective disorder, bipolar type, if mood episodes occupy the majority of the active and residual illness duration.
- Key clue: at least two weeks of psychosis without a major mood episode.

**Scenario 5**

A patient is convinced that a spouse is unfaithful for several months but has no prominent hallucinations, disorganisation or major functional decline outside the relationship.

- Consider: delusional disorder, jealous type.
- Key clue: persistent delusion with relatively preserved general functioning.

**Scenario 6**

A patient presents with paranoia, agitation and auditory hallucinations after methamphetamine use, with marked insomnia and tachycardia.

- Consider: stimulant-induced psychosis.
- Immediate priorities: physical stabilisation, agitation management, assessment for hyperthermia and cardiovascular complications, and substance-use treatment.

**Scenario 7**

An older patient with pneumonia becomes acutely confused, cannot maintain attention and reports seeing insects on the wall. Symptoms fluctuate during the day.

- Consider: delirium.
- Immediate priority: urgent assessment and treatment of the underlying medical cause.

## 16. High-yield examination points

1. Psychosis is a syndrome characterised by impaired reality testing, not a single diagnosis.
2. Delusions are fixed beliefs; hallucinations are perception-like experiences without an external stimulus.
3. An illusion is a misinterpretation of a real stimulus.
4. Auditory hallucinations are common in schizophrenia; prominent visual hallucinations should prompt assessment for organic causes.
5. Positive symptoms include delusions, hallucinations and disorganised speech or behaviour.
6. Negative symptoms include avolition, alogia, anhedonia, asociality and blunted affect.
7. Increased mesolimbic dopamine signalling is associated with positive symptoms.
8. D2 blockade in the nigrostriatal pathway can cause extrapyramidal symptoms.
9. D2 blockade in the tuberoinfundibular pathway can cause hyperprolactinaemia.
10. DSM-5-TR requires at least six months of continuous disturbance for schizophrenia.
11. ICD-11 uses a shorter, generally one-month symptom-duration requirement for schizophrenia.
12. Schizophreniform disorder lasts at least one month but less than six months under DSM-5-TR.
13. Brief psychotic disorder lasts at least one day but less than one month, followed by return to baseline.
14. Schizoaffective disorder requires a period of at least two weeks of delusions or hallucinations without a major mood episode under DSM-5-TR.
15. Delusional disorder involves one or more delusions for at least one month without the full schizophrenia syndrome.
16. Clozapine is important in treatment-resistant schizophrenia after two adequate antipsychotic trials.
17. Clozapine requires regular blood monitoring and active monitoring for myocarditis, constipation and metabolic effects.
18. Neuroleptic malignant syndrome is an emergency: fever, rigidity, altered mental state and autonomic instability.
19. Catatonia can occur in psychiatric and medical conditions and may respond to lorazepam or ECT.
20. Always assess suicide risk and exclude delirium, substance-related and medical causes in a patient with new-onset psychosis.
