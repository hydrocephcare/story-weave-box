# Introduction to Psychiatry — Year 4 Notes

## Definition
**Psychiatry** is the medical specialty concerned with assessing, diagnosing, treating and preventing mental, emotional and behavioural disorders, and supporting recovery.

**Mental health** is more than the absence of illness: it includes the ability to cope with stress, function in daily life and maintain relationships.

A **mental disorder** involves a clinically significant disturbance in thinking, emotional regulation or behaviour, usually associated with distress or impaired functioning. One symptom alone does not establish a diagnosis.

## What psychiatrists do
- Take a psychiatric and medical history.
- Perform a Mental State Examination (MSE).
- Assess safety, including suicide and self-harm risk when indicated.
- Consider physical illness, medicines and substance use that may cause psychiatric symptoms.
- Develop a diagnosis and management plan, which may include medication, psychological therapy, social support and follow-up.

## How mental illness is understood
Mental health problems may reflect interacting **biological, psychological and social** influences. Assess the whole person and their circumstances; do not assume one cause without evidence.

For the detailed model and the **4 Ps**, read [Biopsychosocial Formulation](/notes/biopsychosocial-formulation).

## Key clinical principle
Describe what the patient reports and what you observe before deciding what it means. Then consider possible syndromes, differential diagnoses, context and functional impact.

Continue to [Introduction to Psychopathology](/notes/introduction-to-psychopathology) for the terminology used to describe abnormalities of thought, perception, mood, cognition and behaviour.

## Quick self-check
1. **Psychiatry** is a medical specialty; **psychology** focuses on mental processes and behaviour, with professional roles varying by training and jurisdiction.
2. A **symptom** is reported by the patient; a **sign** is observed or elicited by the clinician.
3. A **risk factor** increases the probability of a disorder but does not guarantee it.
4. An unusual behaviour is not automatically a mental disorder: consider context, duration, distress, impairment and possible medical causes.
