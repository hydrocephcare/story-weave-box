# Ovarian Cysts and Adnexal Masses

*Year 4 MBChB | Obstetrics & Gynaecology | CAT revision*

## 1. Definition

An **ovarian cyst** is a fluid-filled or partly fluid-filled sac arising in or on an ovary. An **adnexal mass** may arise from the ovary, fallopian tube, broad ligament or adjacent structures. The central clinical questions are: Is the patient pregnant? Is the mass benign or suspicious? Is there an acute complication such as torsion, rupture or haemorrhage?

Many ovarian cysts in reproductive-age patients are physiological and resolve spontaneously. A cyst is not synonymous with ovarian cancer.

### A. Functional/physiological cysts
- **Follicular cyst:** a dominant follicle fails to rupture or regress and enlarges.
- **Corpus luteum cyst:** corpus luteum persists or accumulates fluid after ovulation; may haemorrhage.
- **Theca-lutein cysts:** multiple cysts associated with high hCG states, e.g. gestational trophoblastic disease or multiple pregnancy.

### B. Benign pathological cysts
- **Mature cystic teratoma (dermoid):** germ-cell tumour containing tissues such as hair, fat, teeth or bone; can undergo torsion.
- **Serous cystadenoma:** often thin-walled and unilocular.
- **Mucinous cystadenoma:** may become very large and multilocular.
- **Endometrioma:** ovarian endometriosis containing old blood ("chocolate cyst").
- **Paraovarian cyst:** arises adjacent to, not within, the ovary.

### C. Malignant/borderline masses
Epithelial ovarian tumours, germ-cell tumours and sex-cord stromal tumours. Risk increases with age and postmenopausal status; suspicious morphology and clinical context matter more than one isolated marker.

## 3. Pathophysiology and risk

Functional cysts arise from normal ovarian physiology. Endometriomas develop when ectopic endometrial tissue involves the ovary and repeated bleeding produces thick altered blood. Neoplastic cysts arise from abnormal proliferation of ovarian or related tissue.

Risk factors vary by histology. Important history includes age, menopausal status, family history of ovarian/breast/colorectal cancer, known BRCA/Lynch syndrome, endometriosis, fertility treatment and pregnancy possibility.

## 4. Clinical presentation

Many cysts are asymptomatic and found incidentally.

**Chronic or subacute symptoms**
- Unilateral or diffuse pelvic pain.
- Pelvic pressure, abdominal fullness or distension.
- Dyspareunia.
- Urinary frequency or constipation from mass effect.
- Menstrual irregularity in some patients.

**Acute presentations**
- **Torsion:** sudden severe unilateral pelvic pain, often with nausea/vomiting; pain may be intermittent initially.
- **Rupture:** sudden pain, sometimes after exercise or intercourse; internal bleeding may cause dizziness, tachycardia, hypotension or collapse.
- **Haemorrhage into cyst:** acute pain and possible haemoperitoneum.
- **Infection/TOA:** fever, pelvic tenderness and systemic illness; consider PID.

**Features raising concern for malignancy**
Persistent/progressive abdominal distension, early satiety, unexplained weight loss, pelvic/abdominal pain, urinary symptoms, ascites, a fixed/irregular mass, bilateral or complex masses, solid areas/papillary projections, and postmenopausal presentation.

## 5. History and examination

Ask age and menopausal status, last menstrual period, pregnancy possibility, onset and character of pain, vomiting, fever, bleeding, discharge, bowel/bladder symptoms, prior cysts/endometriosis, fertility treatment and family cancer history.

Examine vital signs first. Assess pallor, abdominal distension, tenderness, guarding, palpable mass and ascites. Pelvic examination may identify adnexal fullness or tenderness but cannot reliably determine cyst morphology. In an acute abdomen, avoid delaying urgent care for a detailed pelvic examination.

## 6. Differential diagnosis

- Ectopic pregnancy: positive pregnancy test, pain/bleeding; always exclude.
- PID/tubo-ovarian abscess: fever, discharge, cervical motion tenderness, inflammatory markers.
- Endometriosis: cyclical pain, dysmenorrhoea, dyspareunia.
- Fibroid: uterine, often firm/irregular mass.
- Appendicitis, urinary tract infection, renal/ureteric colic and gastrointestinal disease.
- Ovarian malignancy: persistent complex mass, ascites or systemic symptoms.

## 7. Investigations

| Test | Clinical purpose |
|---|---|
| Urine/serum β-hCG | Excludes pregnancy and pregnancy-related adnexal pathology |
| Pelvic ultrasound with Doppler | First-line assessment of size, site, morphology, septations, solid areas and blood flow |
| FBC | Anaemia from haemorrhage; leukocytosis if infection |
| CRP/ESR | Supports assessment of inflammation/infection; non-specific |
| CA-125 | May support risk assessment in selected patients, especially postmenopausal; not a screening test and can rise in benign disease |
| Other tumour markers | Selected by age and morphology: e.g. AFP, β-hCG, LDH for suspected germ-cell tumour; inhibin for selected sex-cord stromal tumours |
| CT/MRI | Selected complex masses, staging or further characterisation—not routine first test |

**Ultrasound description:** document whether the lesion is simple or complex, unilocular or multilocular, wall/septal features, solid components, papillary projections, size, laterality, ascites and Doppler findings. No single size cut-off should replace assessment of morphology, symptoms, age and menopausal status.

## 8. Management

### A. Stable patient with likely benign cyst
- Many simple functional cysts resolve; observation and repeat ultrasound may be appropriate.
- Provide clear return precautions for sudden pain, vomiting, faintness, fever or increasing distension.
- Analgesia according to contraindications.
- Persistent, enlarging, symptomatic or complex lesions require gynaecology review and an individualised plan.

Combined oral contraceptives do not reliably accelerate resolution of an existing functional cyst; do not prescribe them solely for this purpose.

### B. Ovarian torsion — time-critical emergency
1. Suspect with sudden unilateral pain, often with nausea/vomiting and an adnexal mass.
2. Pregnancy test, urgent senior review, analgesia, IV access and pre-operative assessment.
3. Ultrasound with Doppler can support the diagnosis, but preserved arterial flow does **not** exclude torsion because of dual blood supply or intermittent torsion.
4. Urgent laparoscopy is generally required. Detorsion and ovarian preservation are preferred when feasible; cystectomy may be performed depending on tissue condition and clinical judgement.

### C. Rupture/haemorrhage
Assess haemodynamic status. Stable patients with limited bleeding may be observed with analgesia and monitoring. Unstable patients or ongoing significant haemorrhage need resuscitation, urgent surgical assessment and operative control when indicated.

### D. Suspicion of malignancy
Avoid casual cyst drainage or unplanned surgery. Refer to a gynaecological oncology pathway for appropriate imaging, tumour-marker assessment and staging/operative planning. Surgical approach depends on age, fertility wishes, tumour type and stage.

### E. Fertility preservation
When surgery is indicated in a reproductive-age patient, consider cystectomy and preservation of healthy ovarian tissue where safe. Discuss potential reduction in ovarian reserve, particularly with bilateral or recurrent endometriomas.

## 9. Complications

Torsion, rupture, haemorrhage, infection, pressure effects, recurrence, reduced ovarian reserve after surgery, and delayed diagnosis of malignancy.

## 10. CAT high-yield points

- Functional follicular and corpus luteum cysts are common in reproductive-age patients.
- Endometrioma contains old blood and is associated with endometriosis.
- Sudden unilateral pain plus vomiting = think ovarian torsion.
- Normal Doppler flow does not reliably rule out torsion.
- Pregnancy test is essential in reproductive-age acute pelvic pain.
- Ultrasound is first-line; morphology and menopausal status guide risk.
- CA-125 is non-specific and should not be used alone to diagnose ovarian cancer.
- Torsion is a surgical emergency; timely detorsion and ovarian preservation are important.

## 11. Tricky examiner questions

**Q1. What is the most important diagnosis to consider in sudden severe unilateral pelvic pain with vomiting?**  
**Answer:** Ovarian torsion, although ectopic pregnancy and other acute causes must also be assessed.

**Q2. Does preserved Doppler blood flow exclude torsion?**  
**Answer:** No. Dual ovarian blood supply and intermittent/incomplete torsion may preserve detectable flow.

**Q3. What is the typical content of an endometrioma?**  
**Answer:** Thick, altered old blood, classically described as chocolate fluid.

**Q4. Is CA-125 specific for ovarian cancer?**  
**Answer:** No. It can be raised in endometriosis, menstruation, pregnancy, PID and other benign conditions.

**Q5. What is the first-line imaging investigation for an adnexal mass?**  
**Answer:** Pelvic ultrasound, usually transvaginal when appropriate, with Doppler assessment.

## 12. Practice MCQs

### Question 1
A 23-year-old develops sudden severe right iliac fossa pain with vomiting. Ultrasound shows an enlarged ovary with a cyst. What is the priority diagnosis?

A. Chronic cervicitis  
B. Ovarian torsion  
C. Endometrial polyp  
D. Primary dysmenorrhoea

**Answer: B.** Sudden unilateral pain with vomiting and an adnexal mass is classic for torsion.

### Question 2
Which statement about Doppler ultrasound in ovarian torsion is correct?

A. Normal flow excludes torsion  
B. Reduced flow is diagnostic in every patient  
C. Preserved flow does not exclude torsion  
D. Doppler replaces clinical assessment

**Answer: C.** Ovarian dual blood supply and intermittent torsion may preserve flow.

### Question 3
A 30-year-old with known endometriosis has an ovarian cyst containing homogeneous low-level echoes. What is most likely?

A. Endometrioma  
B. Hydatid cyst  
C. Follicular cyst in every case  
D. Cervical polyp

**Answer: A.** Endometriomas often show homogeneous low-level internal echoes on ultrasound.

### Question 4
Which test must be performed in a reproductive-age patient with acute pelvic pain?

A. CA-125 only  
B. Pregnancy test  
C. Mammography  
D. Colonoscopy

**Answer: B.** Ectopic pregnancy must be excluded.

### Question 5
Which statement about CA-125 is correct?

A. It confirms ovarian cancer  
B. It is specific for endometriosis  
C. It is non-specific and interpreted with clinical/imaging findings  
D. It excludes malignancy when normal

**Answer: C.** CA-125 has important benign and malignant causes and is not interpreted in isolation.
