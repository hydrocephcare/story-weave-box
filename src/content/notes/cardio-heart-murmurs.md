# Heart murmurs

*Clinical reasoning notes for the bedside. Describe every murmur the same way, then ask what is causing it.*

**The reasoning chain:** time it (systolic or diastolic) → place it (where is it loudest, where does it go) → grade it → use manoeuvres → name the lesion → ask what caused it and how bad it is.

## 1. What is a murmur?

A **murmur** is the extra sound made by turbulent blood flow across a valve or defect. A systolic murmur falls between the first and second heart sounds; a diastolic murmur falls after the second. **Diastolic murmurs are always pathological.** Many soft systolic murmurs in children and in pregnancy are harmless (flow murmurs).

## Anatomy and physiology you need  
- **Aortic valve (bicuspid/tri‑cuspid)**: Opening pressure gradient during systole; leaflet coaptation determines flow velocity. — A stenotic valve creates a high‑velocity jet → harsh ejection murmur; a regurgitant valve allows backflow → early diastolic decrescendo murmur.
- **Mitral valve (leaflets, chordae, papillary muscles)**: Left ventricular systolic pressure vs. left atrial pressure; chordal tension maintains leaflet position. — Regurgitation produces a pansystolic murmur radiating to the axilla; stenosis creates a mid‑diastolic rumble due to turbulent flow across a narrowed orifice.
- **Pulmonary valve & tricuspid valve**: Right‑ventricular systolic pressure vs. right‑atrial pressure; tricuspid annulus dilates in right‑heart failure. — Tricuspid regurgitation gives a pansystolic murmur louder on inspiration; pulmonary stenosis gives an ejection systolic murmur best heard at the left upper sternal border.
- **Vascular compliance (aorta, pulmonary artery)**: Pulse pressure is the difference between systolic and diastolic pressures; compliance determines pulse wave velocity. — Aortic regurgitation reduces compliance → wide pulse pressure, water‑hammer pulse, and peripheral pulsations.
- **Carotid and femoral arteries**: Reflection of pulse wave; arterial stiffness alters wave timing. — Corrigan’s sign (carotid pulsation) and Traube’s sign (femoral “pistol‑shot” sound) are classic for aortic regurgitation.
- **Right‑to‑left shunts (ASD, VSD)**: Left‑to‑right shunt increases pulmonary flow and right‑ventricular volume. — A continuous “machinery” murmur is heard best at the left sternal border; differentiates from isolated valvular murmurs.

## Pathophysiology  
**Rheumatic mitral stenosis →**  
Rheumatic inflammation → fibrosis & commissural fusion → narrowed orifice → ↑ left‑atrial pressure → atrial dilation → atrial fibrillation → pulmonary hypertension → right‑heart strain.  
**Aortic regurgitation →**  
Valve leaflet damage → backflow into LV during diastole → LV volume overload → eccentric hypertrophy → increased stroke volume → wide pulse pressure → peripheral pulsations.  
**Aortic stenosis →**  
Calcific degeneration → leaflet thickening → reduced valve area → ↑ LV afterload → concentric hypertrophy → reduced stroke volume → syncope, angina, heart failure.

## 2. Describe every murmur by

**Timing** (systolic or diastolic), **site** where it is loudest, **radiation**, **grade**, **pitch and shape**, and the **effect of manoeuvres**.

**Levine grading:** systolic murmurs are graded **1 to 6** (grade 4 and above has a **thrill**); diastolic murmurs 1 to 4.

## 3. Manoeuvres

- **Right-sided murmurs are louder on inspiration** (more blood returns to the right heart). Left-sided murmurs are louder on **expiration**. A simple way to remember it: right = in, left = out.
- Lean the patient **forward in expiration** to hear the early diastolic murmur of aortic regurgitation.
- Roll the patient to the **left lateral position** and use the **bell** to hear the mid-diastolic rumble of mitral stenosis.

## 4. The lesions

- **Mitral stenosis**: **Mid-diastolic rumble** at the apex — **Loud first sound and opening snap**; best with the bell, left lateral, in expiration
- **Mitral regurgitation**: **Pansystolic** at the apex — **Radiates to the axilla**
- **Aortic stenosis**: **Ejection systolic**, right 2nd intercostal space — **Radiates to the carotids**; **slow-rising pulse**
- **Aortic regurgitation**: **Early diastolic**, left sternal edge — Patient sitting forward, in expiration; **wide pulse pressure**
- **Tricuspid stenosis / regurgitation**: Diastolic / pansystolic — **Louder on inspiration**; raised JVP
- **Pulmonary stenosis / regurgitation**: Ejection systolic / early diastolic — Left 2nd intercostal space

## 5. Peripheral signs of aortic regurgitation

A big pulse pressure produces a collection of named signs. Know them:

- **Collapsing (water-hammer) pulse**
- **Corrigan's sign:** visible "dancing" carotid pulsations
- **de Musset's sign:** the head bobs with each heartbeat
- **Quincke's sign:** pulsation of the nail-bed capillaries
- **Traube's sign:** a "pistol-shot" sound over the femoral arteries
- **Duroziez's sign:** a to-and-fro murmur over the femoral artery
- **Müller's sign:** pulsation of the uvula
- **Becker's sign:** pulsation of the retinal arteries
- **Hill's sign:** the leg systolic pressure is much higher than the arm

## 6. Always ask what is causing it

In many low- and middle-income countries, including Kenya, **rheumatic heart disease** is the leading cause of valve disease in young people. Mitral stenosis is nearly always rheumatic. Other causes: **degenerative calcific aortic stenosis** (older patients), a **bicuspid aortic valve**, **infective endocarditis**, **mitral valve prolapse**, and **ischaemia** (papillary muscle rupture after a heart attack). See the note on rheumatic fever for the Jones criteria.

## 7. Where the examination can mislead

- The **loudness of a murmur does not equal the severity**: a very severe mitral stenosis can have a soft rumble, and in severe aortic stenosis with heart failure the murmur may be quiet.
- Atrial fibrillation (common in mitral stenosis) makes timing difficult: take your time, and feel the carotid pulse while you listen.
- A new murmur plus fever means **endocarditis until proven otherwise**.

## Differential diagnosis and how to tell them apart  

| Condition | Key points that point to it | Features that separate it from the next |
|---|---|---|
| **Mitral stenosis** | Mid‑diastolic rumble, opening snap, loud S1, left‑sided, best with bell, left lateral, expiration | **vs.** Mitral regurgitation: MR is pansystolic, radiates to axilla, louder on inspiration. |
| **Mitral regurgitation** | Pansystolic murmur, radiates to axilla, soft S1, louder on inspiration | **vs.** Aortic stenosis: AS is ejection systolic, radiates to carotids, louder on expiration. |
| **Aortic stenosis** | Ejection systolic, harsh crescendo‑decrescendo, carotid radiation, slow‑rising pulse | **vs.** Aortic regurgitation: AR is early diastolic decrescendo, wide pulse pressure, no carotid radiation. |
| **Aortic regurgitation** | Early diastolic decrescendo, wide pulse pressure, peripheral pulsations | **vs.** Pulmonary stenosis: PS is ejection systolic at left upper sternal border, not diastolic. |
| **Pulmonary stenosis** | Ejection systolic, left upper sternal border, harsh, louder on inspiration | **vs.** Tricuspid regurgitation: TR is pansystolic, louder on inspiration, associated with raised JVP. |
| **Tricuspid regurgitation** | Pansystolic, louder on inspiration, raised JVP, best with bell at left lower sternal border | **vs.** Mitral regurgitation: MR radiates to axilla, not associated with JVP. |
| **VSD (large)** | Continuous machinery murmur, best at left sternal border, louder on inspiration | **vs.** ASD: ASD gives fixed split S2, not continuous murmur. |

## Investigations: what each test answers  

| Test | Question it answers | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE)** | Valve morphology, gradients, regurgitant fraction, chamber size | Shows stenosis area, regurgitant jet, LV hypertrophy |
| **Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE)** | Detailed valve anatomy, endocarditis vegetations | Confirms small vegetations, subvalvular pathology |
| **Cardiac MRI** | Quantitative flow, ventricular volumes, fibrosis | Accurate regurgitant volume, late gadolinium enhancement |
| **Chest X‑ray** | Cardiomegaly, pulmonary congestion, calcification | Enlarged left atrium in MS, aortic root dilation in AR |
| **ECG** | Arrhythmias, chamber enlargement, ischemia | Atrial fibrillation in MS, LV strain in AS |
| **Blood pressure (radial & carotid)** | Pulse pressure, pulse wave velocity | Wide pulse pressure in AR, slow‑rising pulse in AS |
| **Right‑heart cath** | Pulmonary artery pressures, shunt quantification | Elevated PA pressure in MS, Qp:Qs ratio in VSD |
| **Serology (anti‑streptolysin O, RF)** | Recent rheumatic activity | Elevated titers support rheumatic etiology |
| **Holter monitor** | Paroxysmal arrhythmias | Detects AF in MS, ventricular ectopy in AS |

These sections provide the anatomical, physiological, and investigative depth required for a robust bedside assessment of heart murmurs, aligning with the expectations of a Year 4 MBChB internal medicine rotation.

## Complications and prognosis  

- **Mitral stenosis**: ↑ left atrial pressure → pulmonary venous congestion — Atrial fibrillation, thrombo‑embolism, right‑sided heart failure, pulmonary hypertension — Progressive dyspnoea; symptom‑limited survival improves markedly after percutaneous balloon commissurotomy or valve replacement
- **Mitral regurgitation (chronic)**: Volume overload of left ventricle → eccentric hypertrophy — Progressive LV dilation, systolic dysfunction, atrial fibrillation, pulmonary oedema — Asymptomatic patients have long latent period; once EF falls < 50 % or symptoms appear, surgical repair/replacement confers survival benefit
- **Aortic stenosis (calcific)**: Fixed outflow obstruction → LV pressure overload — LV concentric hypertrophy, myocardial ischaemia, syncope, sudden cardiac death, heart failure — Once mean gradient > 40 mmHg or symptoms develop, median survival < 2 years without valve replacement
- **Aortic regurgitation (chronic)**: Diastolic run‑off → LV volume overload — LV dilation, reduced EF, aortic root dilatation, end‑aortic dissection (in Marfan or bicuspid valve), infective endocarditis — Asymptomatic patients may remain stable for decades; onset of symptoms or LV end‑diastolic dimension > 65 mm prompts surgery
- **Tricuspid regurgitation (functional)**: Systemic venous congestion — Hepatomegaly, ascites, peripheral oedema, right‑sided failure — Often a marker of advanced left‑sided disease; prognosis tied to underlying cause rather than regurgitation severity alone
- **Pulmonary stenosis (severe)**: RV pressure overload — Right‑ventricular hypertrophy, cyanosis (if associated with shunt), arrhythmia — In isolated valvular disease, balloon valvuloplasty yields excellent long‑term survival; untreated severe lesions carry high early mortality

\*Prognosis is expressed in broad clinical terms; individual outcome depends on age, comorbidities (e.g., HIV, hypertension), and timely intervention. In Kenya, rheumatic aetiology often leads to earlier presentation of mitral lesions, whereas degenerative aortic disease predominates in older adults.  

Key points for the examiner’s mind‑set:  

1. **Severity ≠ loudness** – a quiet murmur may hide critical obstruction (e.g., severe aortic stenosis with low cardiac output).  
2. **Chronology matters** – acute severe regurgitation (papillary‑muscle rupture, aortic dissection) carries a dramatically worse short‑term prognosis than chronic compensated lesions.  
3. **Complication hierarchy** – atrial fibrillation in mitral stenosis is a red flag because embolic stroke risk rises sharply; the examiner expects you to link rhythm disturbance to the underlying valve lesion.

## Clinical case  

**Patient:** 28‑year‑old female, Kenyan university student.  

**Presentation:** 3‑month history of exertional dyspnoea (NYHA II), intermittent palpitations, and occasional painless haemoptysis. No fever.  

**Key findings:**  

- **Apex beat displaced laterally, sustained**; **first heart sound loud**, **opening snap** 80 ms after S2.  
- **Mid‑diastolic rumble** best heard at the apex with the bell, accentuated in left lateral decubitus and during expiration.  
- **Irregularly irregular pulse** at 110 bpm; peripheral edema absent.  
- **Chest X‑ray:** enlarged left atrium, straightening of the left heart border.  

### Questions  

1. **What is the most likely valvular lesion and its pathophysiological basis?**  
   *Model answer:* Mitral stenosis, most probably rheumatic in origin. Rheumatic inflammation leads to leaflet thickening, commissural fusion, and chordal shortening, producing a narrowed orifice that raises left‑atrial pressure and generates the characteristic opening snap and diastolic rumble.  

2. **Identify two complications that this patient is at immediate risk of developing and the clinical clues that point to them.**  
   *Model answer:*  
   - **Atrial fibrillation** – already present as an irregularly irregular pulse; loss of atrial contraction further compromises ventricular filling and predisposes to thrombus formation.  
   - **Pulmonary hypertension** – suggested by dyspnoea, haemoptysis, and left‑atrial enlargement; chronic backward pressure transmits to the pulmonary vasculature, risking right‑ventricular failure.  

3. **Outline the next investigative step and the definitive management option for this lesion in a resource‑limited setting.**  
   *Model answer:*  
   - **Investigative step:** Perform transthoracic echocardiography to quantify mitral valve area, assess severity of stenosis, and detect associated pulmonary pressures.  
   - **Definitive management:** In a young adult with symptomatic moderate‑to‑severe stenosis, percutaneous balloon mitral commissurotomy is the preferred intervention when valve morphology is suitable; it offers symptom relief and delays the need for surgical valve replacement, which may be limited by availability of prosthetic valves and anticoagulation monitoring.  

These questions test the ability to integrate auscultation findings, recognise sequelae, and propose realistic, locally appropriate management.

## Practice questions

**1.** A mid-diastolic rumble at the apex with an opening snap is characteristic of:
A. Mitral regurgitation · B. Aortic stenosis · C. Aortic regurgitation · **D. Mitral stenosis** · E. Tricuspid regurgitation
*Answer: D.*

**2.** A pansystolic murmur at the apex radiating to the axilla indicates:
A. Aortic stenosis · B. Pulmonary stenosis · **C. Mitral regurgitation** · D. Mitral stenosis · E. Aortic regurgitation
*Answer: C.*

**3.** Which finding is a peripheral sign of aortic regurgitation?
A. Slow-rising pulse · **B. Head bobbing synchronous with the pulse (de Musset's sign)** · C. Pulsus paradoxus · D. Pulsus alternans · E. Raised JVP
*Answer: B. A slow-rising pulse belongs to aortic stenosis.*

**4.** Which murmur becomes louder on inspiration?
**A. Tricuspid regurgitation** · B. Mitral regurgitation · C. Aortic stenosis · D. Mitral stenosis · E. Aortic regurgitation
*Answer: A. Right-sided murmurs are louder on inspiration.*
