# Hypertension — Year 4 Internal Medicine (Cardiovascular) Notes

*Clinical reasoning notes. Targets and drug choices follow major guidelines; check your national protocol.*

**The reasoning chain:** confirm the diagnosis properly → find out if there is a secondary cause → assess the organs already damaged and the other risks → treat with lifestyle and the right drug combination → set a target → follow up. **Hypertension is silent: the aim of treatment is to prevent stroke, heart attack, heart failure and kidney disease.**

## 1. Define and confirm

Hypertension is a **sustained** blood pressure of **140/90 mmHg or more** in the clinic, confirmed on **repeat visits** (or **ambulatory 24-hour monitoring at 130/80 or more**, or **home readings of 135/85 or more**). Some guidelines use 130/80 as the threshold for treatment in high-risk patients.

**Technique matters:** rested seated patient, correct cuff size, arm at heart level, two or more readings. A cuff that is too small gives falsely high readings. **White-coat hypertension** is high in clinic and normal at home; **masked hypertension** is the reverse.

## Anatomy and physiology you need  
| Structure | Physiological role in BP regulation | Clinical relevance |
|---|---|---|
| **Renin‑angiotensin‑aldosterone system (RAAS)** | Renin from juxtaglomerular cells → angiotensin‑I → ACE → angiotensin‑II → vasoconstriction, aldosterone release → sodium‑water retention | Excess RAAS activity raises systemic vascular resistance and intravascular volume; explains why ACE/ARB are first‑line and why hypokalaemia suggests primary hyperaldosteronism |
| **Sympathetic nervous system (SNS)** | Norepinephrine release → α1‑mediated vasoconstriction, β1‑mediated ↑ cardiac output | Chronic SNS over‑activity increases peripheral resistance; β‑blockers counteract this in selected patients |
| **Baroreceptor reflex** | Stretch receptors in carotid sinus & aortic arch sense BP → afferent → CNS → efferent → modulation of heart rate & vascular tone | Failure or resetting of this reflex in hypertension leads to sustained high BP; explains why carotid sinus massage can transiently lower BP in resistant cases |
| **Vascular endothelium** | Produces nitric oxide (NO), prostacyclin, endothelin‑1 | Endothelial dysfunction (↓NO, ↑ET‑1) raises peripheral resistance; underpins the benefit of calcium‑channel blockers and lifestyle measures that improve endothelial function |
| **Kidney (glomerulus & tubules)** | Filtration pressure, sodium handling, tubuloglomerular feedback | Hypertensive nephrosclerosis and secondary causes (renal artery stenosis, glomerulonephritis) alter this feedback, perpetuating hypertension |
| **Adrenal cortex (zona glomerulosa)** | Aldosterone synthesis | Hyperplasia or adenoma → autonomous aldosterone → hypokalaemia, hypertension |
| **Adrenal medulla** | Catecholamine synthesis | Phaeochromocytoma → episodic surges of BP |

## Pathophysiology  
```
Genetic & environmental predisposition
   ↓
↑ sympathetic tone + ↑ RAAS activity
   ↓
↑ systemic vascular resistance + ↑ intravascular volume
   ↓
↑ mean arterial pressure
   ↓
Endothelial dysfunction, arterial stiffening
   ↓
Target organ damage (LVH, retinopathy, CKD, stroke)
```

## 2. Stage and categorise

| Category | Blood pressure |
|---|---|
| Normal | below 120/80 |
| High-normal | 130–139 / 85–89 |
| Grade 1 | 140–159 / 90–99 |
| Grade 2 | 160–179 / 100–109 |
| Grade 3 | 180 or more / 110 or more |
| **Hypertensive emergency** | Very high BP **with acute organ damage** (encephalopathy, pulmonary oedema, aortic dissection, acute kidney injury, eclampsia) |
| **Hypertensive urgency** | Very high BP **without** acute organ damage |

## 3. Find the cause: primary or secondary?

About 90 to 95% is **essential (primary)**. Suspect **secondary hypertension** when the patient is **young (below 30)**, the BP is **severe or resistant to three drugs**, there is a **sudden rise**, or there are clues:

| Cause | Clues and test |
|---|---|
| **Renal parenchymal disease** | Abnormal creatinine, protein or blood in urine; kidney ultrasound |
| **Renal artery stenosis** | Abdominal bruit, flash pulmonary oedema, creatinine rise on ACE inhibitor; Doppler or CT angiography |
| **Primary hyperaldosteronism (Conn)** | **Low potassium**, high BP; aldosterone-to-renin ratio |
| **Phaeochromocytoma** | Episodes of sweating, headache, palpitations; urine or plasma metanephrines |
| **Cushing syndrome** | Moon face, striae; dexamethasone suppression test |
| **Coarctation of the aorta** | Young patient, **radio-femoral delay**, differing arm and leg BP |
| **Thyroid disease, sleep apnoea, pre-eclampsia, drugs** (NSAIDs, steroids, oral contraceptives, decongestants, herbal remedies, alcohol, liquorice) | History and tests |

## 4. Look for organ damage and other risks

| Test | The question it answers |
|---|---|
| **Urea, creatinine, eGFR, urine albumin/creatinine ratio** | Kidney damage as a cause or an effect? |
| **Electrolytes** | Low potassium suggests aldosteronism or diuretics |
| **Glucose and HbA1c, lipid profile** | Other cardiovascular risks |
| **ECG** | Left ventricular hypertrophy, strain, arrhythmia |
| **Echocardiogram** (if ECG or symptoms suggest) | Hypertrophy, heart failure |
| **Fundoscopy** | Hypertensive retinopathy (A-V nipping, haemorrhages, papilloedema in malignant hypertension) |
| **Urinalysis** | Protein and blood |

## Investigations: what each test answers  

| Test | Question it answers | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **Office BP (≥2 readings)** | Is hypertension present? | ≥140/90 mmHg (or 130/80 in high‑risk) confirms diagnosis |
| **Ambulatory 24‑h BP** | Is there white‑coat or masked HTN? | Mean 24‑h ≥130/80 mmHg confirms true HTN |
| **Home BP monitoring** | Is BP controlled on treatment? | ≥135/85 mmHg indicates inadequate control |
| **Serum electrolytes** | Is there hypokalaemia? | <3.5 mmol/L suggests aldosteronism or diuretic use |
| **Serum creatinine / eGFR** | Is there renal impairment? | ↑ creatinine or ↓ eGFR indicates CKD or renal artery stenosis |
| **Urine albumin‑creatinine ratio** | Is there micro‑albuminuria? | >30 mg/mmol indicates early renal damage |
| **Plasma renin activity & aldosterone** | Is RAAS over‑active? | ↑ aldosterone/↓renin ratio >20 supports Conn |
| **Plasma free metanephrines** | Is there catecholamine excess? | >3× ULN confirms phaeochromocytoma |
| **DEXA suppression test** | Is there Cushing? | Failure to suppress cortisol indicates hypercortisolism |
| **Carotid‑femoral pulse wave velocity** | Is arterial stiffness increased? | ↑ velocity correlates with target organ damage |
| **Echocardiogram** | Is there LVH or diastolic dysfunction? | LV mass index >115 g/m² (men) / >95 g/m² (women) |
| **Fundoscopy** | Is there hypertensive retinopathy? | Presence of AV nicking, haemorrhages, papilloedema |
| **Renal Doppler US** | Is there renal artery stenosis? | Peak systolic velocity >180 cm/s or >3:1 ratio |
| **CT/MR angiography** | Is there aortic coarctation or aneurysm? | Narrowing of aortic arch, delayed femoral pulses |

*These investigations guide the clinician from confirming hypertension to uncovering secondary causes, assessing organ damage, and tailoring therapy.*

## 5. Management

**Lifestyle (for everyone):** reduce salt, lose weight, regular exercise, limit alcohol, stop smoking, a diet rich in fruit and vegetables.

**Drugs:** start with one of the four main classes, and **combine early** because most patients need two or more.

| Class | Example | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **ACE inhibitor / ARB** | Enalapril, losartan | First line, especially with diabetes, heart failure or kidney disease. **Contraindicated in pregnancy**; check creatinine and potassium |
| **Calcium channel blocker** | Amlodipine | Often first line in **people of African descent** and older patients; ankle swelling |
| **Thiazide-like diuretic** | Indapamide, hydrochlorothiazide | Cheap and effective; watch potassium, sodium, gout |
| **Beta-blocker** | Bisoprolol | Not first line alone; used for angina, heart failure, arrhythmia; avoid in asthma |

**Rule of thumb:** a typical sequence is **ACE inhibitor or ARB + calcium channel blocker**, then add a **thiazide-like diuretic**, then **spironolactone** for resistant hypertension.

**Target:** most adults below **130/80** if tolerated (or at least below 140/90), checked on home or ambulatory readings. Treat statins and aspirin by overall cardiovascular risk, not blood pressure alone.

**Hypertensive emergency:** lower the pressure **gradually** (about 25% in the first hour) in a monitored setting with IV drugs. **Dropping it too fast causes stroke.** Aortic dissection and pre-eclampsia are exceptions with their own targets.

**In pregnancy:** use **labetalol, nifedipine or methyldopa**. Avoid ACE inhibitors, ARBs and thiazides.

## 6. Complications

Stroke, ischaemic heart disease, **heart failure**, **chronic kidney disease**, retinopathy, peripheral arterial disease, aortic dissection, and **vascular dementia**.

## Differential diagnosis and how to tell them apart  

| Condition | Key clinical clues | Distinguishing feature |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary (essential) hypertension** | Gradual onset, no identifiable cause, often in older adults | Normal renal function, no hypokalaemia, no episodic symptoms |
| **Renal parenchymal disease** | Proteinuria, hematuria, rising creatinine | Urine albumin‑creatinine ratio >30 mg/mmol; renal ultrasound shows scarring |
| **Renal artery stenosis** | Sudden rise in BP, flash pulmonary oedema, bruit over abdomen | Doppler US shows >70 % stenosis; creatinine rises after ACE‑I |
| **Primary hyperaldosteronism** | Hypokalaemia, metabolic alkalosis, resistant HTN | Aldosterone‑to‑renin ratio >20 (screen) |
| **Phaeochromocytoma** | Episodic headache, sweating, palpitations | Plasma free metanephrines >3× upper limit |
| **Cushing syndrome** | Moon face, striae, proximal myopathy | Low ACTH, high cortisol; dexamethasone suppression fails |
| **Coarctation of the aorta** | Young adult, upper‑limb hypertension, weak femoral pulses | Radio‑femoral delay >0.5 s |
| **Sleep‑apnoea‑associated HTN** | Loud snoring, daytime somnolence, BMI >30 | Polysomnography shows AHI >15 |
| **Drug‑induced HTN** | Recent NSAID, steroid, oral contraceptive use | BP normalises after drug withdrawal |

## Clinical case  
**Patient:** 52‑year‑old male, former smoker, presents with a 3‑month history of episodic headaches and blurred vision. BP recorded at 165/98 mmHg in clinic, 155/92 mmHg on home monitor. No chest pain or dyspnoea. Examination reveals mild ankle oedema, a systolic murmur best heard at the left sternal border, and a 2‑mm retinal haemorrhage with mild AV nicking. Routine labs show serum creatinine 1.6 mg/dL, potassium 3.2 mmol/L, and a urine albumin‑creatinine ratio of the protocol dose/g.  

**Questions**  

1. **What is the most likely secondary cause of hypertension in this patient, and what is the pathophysiological mechanism?**  
   *Model answer:*  
   The combination of hypokalaemia, proteinuria, and a systolic murmur suggests **renin‑mediated secondary hypertension** due to **renal artery stenosis**. Stenosis reduces perfusion pressure to the affected kidney, stimulating renin release. Elevated renin drives the juxtaglomerular apparatus to convert angiotensin‑I to angiotensin‑II, which causes vasoconstriction, aldosterone secretion, sodium‑water retention, and potassium excretion, thereby raising BP and producing hypokalaemia. The murmur is a classic “renovascular” bruit, and the proteinuria reflects glomerular hypertension.  

2. **Which investigation would confirm the diagnosis, and what key finding would you expect?**  
   *Model answer:*  
   A **renal duplex Doppler ultrasound** or **CT angiography** is the first‑line imaging. The expected finding is a focal, eccentric narrowing of the proximal renal artery with a post‑stenotic dilatation. On Doppler, a peak systolic velocity >200 cm/s and a ratio of >3:1 between stenotic and normal segments confirm significant stenosis.  

3. **Explain why an ACE inhibitor would be contraindicated in this patient if the diagnosis is confirmed.**  
   *Model answer:*  
   ACE inhibitors block conversion of angiotensin‑I to angiotensin‑II, lowering systemic vascular resistance and glomerular filtration pressure. In unilateral renal artery stenosis, the affected kidney relies on angiotensin‑II to maintain glomerular filtration. Inhibiting this pathway precipitates a rapid rise in serum creatinine and can lead to acute renal failure. Therefore, ACE inhibitors are contraindicated until revascularisation or bilateral disease is ruled out.

## Practice questions

**1.** A 24-year-old woman has BP 190/110 and a low potassium. The most useful screening test is:
A. Urine metanephrines · B. Dexamethasone suppression · C. ASO titre · **D. Aldosterone-to-renin ratio** · E. Chest X-ray
*Answer: D. Hypertension with hypokalaemia suggests primary hyperaldosteronism.*

**2.** Which drug is contraindicated in a hypertensive pregnant woman?
A. Labetalol · B. Nifedipine · **C. Enalapril** · D. Methyldopa · E. Hydralazine
*Answer: C.*

**3.** A young patient has hypertension with weak femoral pulses and delayed radio-femoral pulses. The diagnosis is:
**A. Coarctation of the aorta** · B. Phaeochromocytoma · C. Cushing syndrome · D. Renal artery stenosis · E. Conn syndrome
*Answer: A.*

**4.** In a hypertensive emergency with encephalopathy, the blood pressure should be:
A. Lowered to normal within minutes · **B. Lowered gradually, about 25% in the first hour** · C. Ignored · D. Treated with oral drugs only · E. Raised
*Answer: B.*
