# Heart failure: which tests and why

*Clinical reasoning notes. The point of an investigation is the question it answers.*

**The reasoning chain:** is it really heart failure → what type (reduced or preserved ejection fraction) → what caused it → what tipped the patient into this episode → what is it doing to other organs.

## 1. Which tests?

- **NT-proBNP or BNP**: Is this heart failure? **A normal level makes it unlikely**
- **Echocardiogram**: Confirms the diagnosis; **ejection fraction** (reduced or preserved); the **valves** (rheumatic heart disease); the cause
- **ECG**: Ischaemia, **arrhythmia (atrial fibrillation)**, left ventricular hypertrophy
- **Chest X-ray**: **Pulmonary oedema**, cardiomegaly, pleural effusions
- **Full haemogram**: **Anaemia**, which worsens or can cause heart failure
- **Urea, creatinine, electrolytes**: Kidney function before starting diuretics or ACE inhibitors; potassium
- **Liver tests, thyroid tests, glucose, lipids**: Congestive liver disease; a thyroid cause; risk factors
- **Troponin**: Is an acute coronary syndrome the trigger?
- **HIV test**: **HIV cardiomyopathy**

## Anatomy and physiology you need  

- **Left ventricle (LV) myocardium – contractile fibers, sarcomere calcium handling, Frank‑Starling stretch response**: Reduced systolic shortening → low ejection fraction → pulmonary congestion; preserved contractility with stiff ventricle → high filling pressures and orthopnoea.
- **LV diastolic compliance – collagen matrix, titin elasticity, relaxation‐dependent calcium re‑uptake**: Impaired relaxation raises left‑atrial pressure → dyspnoea on exertion, PND; examiner will ask about “preserved EF but high LVEDP”.
- **Right ventricle (RV) geometry – crescent‑shaped wall, thin free wall, dependence on preload**: RV failure follows chronic LV overload or pulmonary hypertension; gives peripheral oedema, raised JVP – a clue to biventricular involvement.
- **Atrioventricular valves (mitral & tricuspid) – annular dynamics, chordae tendineae, papillary muscle tethering**: Regurgitation adds volume load, worsens dilation; a murmur points to a secondary cause that the examiner may probe.
- **Coronary arterial tree – epicardial vessels, microvascular perfusion, subendocardial oxygen demand**: Ischaemia reduces contractility and can precipitate decompensation; ECG changes or troponin rise are red‑flags.
- **Neuro‑hormonal axes – sympathetic nervous system, renin‑angiotensin‑aldosterone system (RAAS), natriuretic peptide system**: Chronic activation drives vasoconstriction, sodium retention, remodeling; explains why ACE‑I, β‑blocker and MRAs improve survival – a common exam point.
- **Kidney‑cardiac coupling – glomerular filtration pressure, renal sympathetic tone, natriuresis**: Worsening renal function limits diuretic efficacy and raises BNP; examiners love “cardiorenal syndrome” scenarios.
- **Pulmonary lymphatics & alveolar capillaries – Starling forces, interstitial fluid clearance**: When LV pressure exceeds pulmonary capillary pressure, fluid transudates → crackles, Kerley B lines; the examiner may ask what radiographic sign reflects this.

## Pathophysiology  

```
Pre‑disposing lesion (e.g. HTN, CAD, valve disease) → myocardial injury/remodeling
   ↓
↓ systolic contractility  OR  ↑ diastolic stiffness
   ↓
↓ forward stroke volume → neuro‑hormonal activation (SNS ↑, RAAS ↑, ADH ↑)
   ↓
Systemic vasoconstriction + renal Na⁺/H₂O retention
   ↓
↑ preload & afterload → ventricular dilation (LV & RV) + wall stress
   ↓
Elevated left‑atrial pressure → pulmonary venous congestion → dyspnoea, rales
   ↓
Elevated right‑atrial pressure → systemic venous congestion → JVP, oedema, ascites
   ↓
Progressive myocyte apoptosis & interstitial fibrosis → further decline in function
```

Key examiner focus: **“What breaks the compensation?”** – the shift from adaptive neuro‑hormonal response to maladaptive remodeling that produces the classic signs.

## 2. Then ask the two questions that matter

**What caused it?** Hypertension, **rheumatic heart disease**, ischaemic heart disease, cardiomyopathy (including **HIV**, peripartum, alcohol, and dilated cardiomyopathy of unknown cause), anaemia, thyroid disease, pericardial disease.

**What precipitated this episode?** Infection, **missed drugs**, **arrhythmia (new atrial fibrillation)**, a **heavy salt load**, NSAIDs, anaemia, a new ischaemic event.

## 3. Features to recognise

- **Left heart failure:** breathlessness, orthopnoea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea, basal crackles, third heart sound.
- **Right heart failure:** raised JVP, ankle swelling, tender enlarged liver, ascites.
- Look for the **cause on examination**: a murmur, an irregular pulse, goitre, pallor.

## Investigations: what each test answers  

| Test | Question it answers | Interpretation that guides management |
|---|---|---|
| **NT‑proBNP / BNP** | Is there myocardial stretch suggestive of HF? | ↑ → supports HF; normal → makes HF unlikely unless obesity or early disease. |
| **Transthoracic echocardiography** | What is the ventricular size, EF, and valve status? | EF < 40 % → HFrEF; EF ≥ 50 % with high E/e′ → HFpEF; identifies regurgitation or stenosis as a cause. |
| **12‑lead ECG** | Is there ischaemia, arrhythmia, or LV hypertrophy? | New Q‑waves or ST‑changes → ACS trigger; AF with rapid ventricular response → precipitant; LVH → chronic pressure overload. |
| **Chest radiograph (PA & lateral)** | Are there signs of pulmonary congestion or alternative lung pathology? | Kerley B lines, cephalization, cardiomegaly → congestion; lobar infiltrate → pneumonia. |
| **Serum electrolytes & renal panel** | Is the kidney safe for diuretics/RAAS blockade? | Elevated creatinine or K⁺ >5.0 mmol/L → modify ACE‑I/diuretic dosing; hyponatraemia suggests severe congestion. |
| **Full blood count** | Is anaemia contributing to high‑output failure? | Hb < 10 g/dL → treat anaemia; may explain dyspnoea disproportionate to cardiac findings. |
| **Thyroid function tests (TSH, free T4)** | Is a thyroid disorder driving or mimicking HF? | Suppressed TSH with high free T4 → treat thyrotoxicosis; elevated TSH → consider hypothyroid cardiomyopathy. |
| **High‑sensitivity troponin** | Is there an acute coronary syndrome as a precipitant? | Detectable rise → ACS work‑up; troponin elevation without ischaemia may reflect myocardial strain. |

*Examiner tip:* When asked “What test would you order first in a dyspnoeic patient with a murmur?” think **BNP** to confirm HF, then **echo** to delineate the murmur’s aetiology.

## 4. Management in outline

1. **Treat the congestion:** loop diuretics, oxygen if hypoxic, sit the patient up.
2. **Treat the cause and the trigger:** control blood pressure, rate or rhythm, treat infection, correct anaemia, repair or replace a diseased valve.
3. **Long-term therapy for reduced ejection fraction** (each class reduces deaths or admissions): **ACE inhibitor or ARB**, **beta-blocker**, **mineralocorticoid antagonist**, and an **SGLT2 inhibitor** where available.
4. **Avoid** NSAIDs, and be careful with fluids and with some calcium channel blockers.
5. **Teach** daily weights, salt and fluid advice, and adherence. Follow up in a heart failure clinic.

## 5. Where tests can mislead

- **BNP is raised in kidney failure, atrial fibrillation, older age and pulmonary embolism**, and lower in obesity. Interpret it with the picture.
- A normal chest X-ray does not exclude heart failure.
- Check the **creatinine and potassium again 1 to 2 weeks** after starting an ACE inhibitor or a mineralocorticoid antagonist.

## Differential diagnosis and how to tell them apart  

| Condition | Clinical / bedside pointers | Distinguishing feature vs. heart failure |
|---|---|---|
| **Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) exacerbation** | Chronic cough, wheeze, barrel chest, smoking history; hyperinflated lungs on CXR | Absence of peripheral oedema, normal JVP, and spirometry showing obstructive pattern; BNP usually low. |
| **Pneumonia** | Fever, productive sputum, localized crackles, pleuritic pain; lobar infiltrate on CXR | Elevated white cell count, focal consolidation, rapid response to antibiotics; BNP modestly raised only if cardiac strain present. |
| **Pulmonary embolism** | Sudden pleuritic chest pain, tachycardia, risk factors (immobility, DVT); CT pulmonary angiogram positive | Right‑sided pleuritic pain, clear lungs on CXR, normal LV size; BNP may be high but D‑dimer and imaging differentiate. |
| **Pericardial tamponade** | Beck’s triad (hypotension, muffled heart sounds, JVD), pulsus paradoxus | Echocardiogram shows pericardial effusion with diastolic collapse of RV; LV function usually preserved. |
| **Nephrotic syndrome** | Massive proteinuria, hypoalbuminaemia, oedema, frothy urine | Oedema is generalized, no pulmonary congestion; BNP normal, renal labs diagnostic. |
| **Severe anemia** | Pallor, low Hb, high-output state, tachycardia | Absence of elevated filling pressures; correction of Hb improves symptoms rapidly. |
| **Thyrotoxic cardiomyopathy** | Weight loss, tremor, heat intolerance, atrial fibrillation | Suppressed TSH, high free T4; hyperdynamic LV on echo rather than dilated low‑EF heart. |

## Complications and prognosis  

- **Cardiorenal syndrome**: Elevated venous pressure and neuro‑hormonal activation reduce renal perfusion; diuretics may worsen azotaemia — Rising creatinine, oliguria, refractory congestion
- **Cardiac cachexia**: Chronic catabolism driven by cytokines (TNF‑α, IL‑6) and sympathetic over‑activity — Unintentional >5 % weight loss, low BMI, low albumin
- **Pulmonary hypertension**: Persistent left‑sided pressures transmit backward, causing remodeling of pulmonary arterioles — Loud P2, right‑sided S3, echocardiographic RV systolic pressure ↑
- **Arrhythmia (atrial fibrillation, ventricular tachycardia)**: Stretch‑induced atrial remodeling, scar tissue from ischaemia or cardiomyopathy — Irregular pulse, new‑onset rapid ventricular response, ECG changes
- **Thrombo‑embolism**: Stasis in dilated atria or ventricles promotes mural thrombus formation — Embolic stroke, peripheral arterial occlusion, echocardiographic thrombus
- **Hepatic congestion (cardiac cirrhosis)**: Chronic right‑sided pressure elevation impairs hepatic outflow, leading to fibrosis — Tender hepatomegaly, jaundice, elevated bilirubin, abnormal LFTs
- **Sudden cardiac death**: Malignant ventricular arrhythmias secondary to scar, electrolyte disturbance, or severe LV dysfunction — Low EF (<35 %), prior ventricular ectopy, syncope episodes

**Prognostic pointers** that an examiner expects you to mention:  

* **Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF)** – the strongest predictor of mortality; <35 % confers high risk.  
* **NYHA functional class** – each class increase roughly doubles the 1‑year mortality risk.  
* **Renal function** – a rise in serum creatinine >0.3 mg/dL after initiation of ACE‑I/ARB signals poorer outcome.  
* **Serum natriuretic peptide trend** – failure of BNP/NT‑proBNP to fall after 48 h of optimal therapy suggests ongoing congestion and higher rehospitalisation risk.  
* **Presence of comorbidities** – diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and anemia each add incremental risk.  

When answering an exam question, structure your response: state the complication, explain the underlying mechanism, and finish with the bedside sign or test that would alert the examiner to its presence.

## Clinical case  

**Case vignette**  
A 68‑year‑old man presents to the emergency department with a 3‑day history of worsening dyspnoea, orthopnoea and swollen ankles. He has a known history of hypertension and a myocardial infarction 4 years ago. On examination: BP 150/90 mmHg, HR 110 bpm, irregularly irregular pulse, JVP 10 cm H₂O, bibasal crackles, a third heart sound, and 2 + pitting oedema to the knees. Chest X‑ray shows cardiomegaly and interstitial oedema. ECG reveals atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response. NT‑proBNP is markedly elevated.  

### Questions  

1. **What is the most likely precipitant of this acute decompensation?**  
   *Model answer*: The new onset rapid atrial fibrillation is the principal precipitant. Loss of atrial contribution to ventricular filling and the tachycardia increase myocardial oxygen demand, precipitating congestion in a patient with pre‑existing LV dysfunction.  

2. **Which investigation will help you differentiate between a primary cardiac cause of the atrial fibrillation and a secondary (e.g., pulmonary) trigger?**  
   *Model answer*: A transthoracic echocardiogram. It will demonstrate left atrial enlargement, reduced LV ejection fraction, and assess for valvular disease or pulmonary hypertension. Absence of significant pulmonary pathology on the scan supports a primary cardiac driver.  

3. **List three bedside findings that would indicate the presence of a high‑risk complication in this patient and briefly explain their significance.**  
   *Model answer*:  
   * a) Rising serum creatinine or oliguria – suggests cardiorenal syndrome, portending poorer renal recovery and higher mortality.  
   * b) New loud P2 with right‑sided S3 – indicates pulmonary hypertension and right‑ventricular strain, associated with increased rehospitalisation.  
   * c) Cachectic appearance with >5 % weight loss over 6 months – points to cardiac cachexia, a marker of advanced disease and independently predicts reduced survival.  

These questions test the ability to link pathophysiology with clinical reasoning, a skill examiners value in the final year.

*Answer: C.*

A. Troponin · B. Lipid profile · C. Urinalysis · D. Peak flow · **E. HIV test**
*Answer: E.*
