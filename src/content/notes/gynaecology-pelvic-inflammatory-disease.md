# Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID)

*Year 4 MBChB | Obstetrics & Gynaecology | CAT revision*

## 1. Definition

**Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID)** is infection and inflammation of the upper female genital tract, involving the endometrium, fallopian tubes, ovaries and/or pelvic peritoneum. It includes endometritis, salpingitis, tubo-ovarian abscess (TOA) and pelvic peritonitis. Infection commonly ascends from the cervix and vagina. PID is a clinical diagnosis; no single symptom, examination finding or test is sufficiently sensitive to exclude it.

## 2. Aetiology and risk factors

PID is usually polymicrobial. Sexually transmitted pathogens include **Chlamydia trachomatis** and **Neisseria gonorrhoeae**. Anaerobes and vaginal flora (including organisms associated with bacterial vaginosis), *Mycoplasma genitalium* and other bacteria may contribute.

Risk is increased by:
- New or multiple sexual partners, a partner with an STI, inconsistent condom use.
- Previous PID or STI.
- Age under 25 years (higher incidence of chlamydia/gonorrhoea in many populations).
- Recent uterine instrumentation or pregnancy-related procedures, particularly where infection prevention is inadequate.
- Bacterial vaginosis may coexist.

An intrauterine contraceptive device does not cause chronic PID; the small procedure-related risk is concentrated around insertion, especially if an untreated cervical infection is present.

## 3. Pathophysiology

Pathogens enter the endocervix and ascend into the endometrium and fallopian tubes. Inflammation causes tubal oedema, epithelial injury and exudate. Healing may produce fibrosis, adhesions and tubal obstruction. Infection may spread to the ovary and form a tubo-ovarian complex/abscess or involve the pelvic peritoneum.

**Why infertility and ectopic pregnancy occur:** tubal cilia and the tubal lumen are damaged. The ovum may fail to reach the uterus, causing tubal-factor infertility or implantation in the tube (ectopic pregnancy). Perihepatic inflammation may cause Fitz-Hugh–Curtis syndrome, classically right upper quadrant pain with adhesions described as "violin strings".

## 4. Clinical features

Presentation ranges from asymptomatic/mild disease to severe sepsis.

**Symptoms**
- Lower abdominal or pelvic pain, often bilateral.
- Abnormal vaginal discharge.
- Intermenstrual or postcoital bleeding.
- Deep dyspareunia.
- Fever, malaise, nausea or vomiting in more severe disease.
- Dysuria may occur.
- Right upper quadrant pain may occur with perihepatitis.

**Examination**
- Fever or systemic illness; assess pulse, BP and hydration.
- Lower abdominal tenderness, sometimes guarding.
- Speculum: mucopurulent cervical discharge, cervical friability or contact bleeding.
- Bimanual: cervical motion tenderness, uterine tenderness and/or adnexal tenderness. A tender adnexal mass raises concern for TOA.
- Severe tenderness, peritonism or shock requires urgent assessment for abscess, rupture, ectopic pregnancy or another surgical emergency.

A low threshold for treatment is appropriate in a sexually active patient at STI risk with pelvic/lower abdominal pain and no more likely alternative diagnosis, particularly if at least one of cervical motion, uterine or adnexal tenderness is present.

## 5. Differential diagnosis

- Ectopic pregnancy or miscarriage.
- Appendicitis.
- Ovarian torsion or ruptured cyst.
- Endometriosis.
- UTI/pyelonephritis or ureteric stone.
- Gastrointestinal disease.
- Cervicitis without upper tract infection.

## 6. Investigations

Do not delay empiric treatment in a clinically likely case while waiting for results.

| Test | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Urine/serum β-hCG | Excludes pregnancy/ectopic pregnancy |
| NAAT for chlamydia and gonorrhoea | Identifies common STI pathogens |
| Vaginal/cervical swabs | Assess STI and other genital infection according to availability |
| HIV and syphilis testing | Offer STI screening with consent |
| FBC, CRP/ESR | Support assessment of inflammation and severity; normal values do not exclude PID |
| Urinalysis/culture | Assesses urinary differential |
| Pelvic ultrasound | If severe illness, mass, suspected TOA, pregnancy or diagnostic uncertainty |
| Blood cultures | If febrile/septic or severe systemic infection |

Ultrasound may show thickened fluid-filled tubes, free pelvic fluid or a complex tubo-ovarian mass. A normal ultrasound does not exclude uncomplicated PID.

## 7. Management

### A. Initial assessment
Assess haemodynamic stability, sepsis, pregnancy, pain severity and possibility of a surgical abdomen. Take samples where possible, but do not delay empiric antibiotics when clinical suspicion is sufficient.

### B. Antibiotics
Use broad-spectrum therapy covering gonorrhoea, chlamydia and anaerobes. A commonly used outpatient regimen in many guidelines is a third-generation cephalosporin plus doxycycline and metronidazole; exact drug, dose and duration must follow the current Kenya STI guideline, local resistance patterns, pregnancy status and allergy history. Do not use a regimen that only covers one pathogen.

- **Outpatient treatment:** suitable for mild-to-moderate disease when the patient is stable, can tolerate oral medication and reliable review is possible.
- **Inpatient/IV treatment:** consider pregnancy, severe illness, high fever, vomiting/inability to take oral medication, suspected TOA, peritonitis/sepsis, inability to exclude a surgical emergency, failure of outpatient treatment, or inability to ensure follow-up.
- **Pregnancy:** urgent specialist/inpatient assessment; choose pregnancy-appropriate antibiotics and exclude ectopic pregnancy.

### C. Review and partner management
Review clinically within 48–72 hours. Lack of improvement requires reassessment of diagnosis, adherence, antimicrobial coverage, complications and need for admission/imaging. Test for HIV and syphilis where appropriate. Advise abstinence from sex until treatment is completed, symptoms resolve and partners have been treated. Evaluate and treat recent sexual partners according to STI guidelines; arrange retesting for reinfection where recommended.

### D. Tubo-ovarian abscess
Admit, give broad-spectrum IV antibiotics and monitor closely. Drainage (image-guided or surgical) may be required if large, rupture is suspected, sepsis persists or there is inadequate response to antibiotics. Ruptured abscess with peritonitis is a surgical emergency.

## 8. Complications

- Tubal-factor infertility.
- Ectopic pregnancy.
- Chronic pelvic pain and dyspareunia.
- Recurrent PID and adhesions.
- Tubo-ovarian abscess, pelvic peritonitis and sepsis.
- Fitz-Hugh–Curtis perihepatitis.

## 9. CAT high-yield points

- PID is an **ascending, often polymicrobial** infection of the upper genital tract.
- Common STI pathogens: *C. trachomatis* and *N. gonorrhoeae*.
- Typical examination: cervical motion, uterine or adnexal tenderness.
- Exclude pregnancy/ectopic pregnancy in reproductive-age pelvic pain.
- Treat empirically when clinical suspicion is sufficient; do not wait for swab results.
- Cover gonorrhoea, chlamydia and anaerobes according to current local protocol.
- Reassess within 48–72 hours.
- Infertility and ectopic pregnancy result from tubal scarring/damage.
- Treat partners and prevent reinfection.

## 10. Tricky examiner questions

**Q1. Why can PID cause ectopic pregnancy?**  
**Answer:** Tubal inflammation damages cilia and causes scarring/adhesions, delaying transport of the fertilised ovum.

**Q2. Does absence of fever exclude PID?**  
**Answer:** No. Mild or subclinical PID may occur without fever or marked inflammatory markers.

**Q3. What is the minimum clinical tenderness criterion supporting empiric PID treatment?**  
**Answer:** In an at-risk patient with pelvic pain and no better explanation, cervical motion, uterine or adnexal tenderness supports treatment; do not require all three.

**Q4. What is Fitz-Hugh–Curtis syndrome?**  
**Answer:** Perihepatitis associated with PID, causing right upper quadrant pain and sometimes violin-string adhesions.

**Q5. When should a patient with PID be admitted?**  
**Answer:** Pregnancy, severe illness, TOA, inability to tolerate oral therapy, surgical emergency not excluded, failure of outpatient treatment or unreliable follow-up.

## 11. Practice MCQs

### Question 1
A 22-year-old sexually active woman presents with bilateral lower abdominal pain and mucopurulent discharge. Examination reveals cervical motion tenderness. Pregnancy test is negative. What is the most likely diagnosis?

A. PID  
B. Fibroid  
C. Endometrial cancer  
D. Primary amenorrhoea

**Answer: A.** Pelvic pain with cervical motion tenderness and discharge strongly supports PID in an at-risk patient.

### Question 2
The major mechanism by which PID causes tubal infertility is:

A. Increased ovarian reserve  
B. Tubal epithelial injury and scarring  
C. Increased progesterone  
D. Cervical ectropion

**Answer: B.** Inflammation damages tubal cilia and causes fibrosis/adhesions.

### Question 3
A patient with suspected PID is febrile, vomiting and has a tender adnexal mass. What is the best next step?

A. Routine review in six months  
B. Admit for assessment, imaging and parenteral broad-spectrum treatment  
C. Treat only with antifungal cream  
D. Reassure

**Answer: B.** Severe illness and suspected TOA require inpatient management.

### Question 4
Which organism is a common sexually transmitted cause of PID?

A. Chlamydia trachomatis  
B. Plasmodium falciparum  
C. Candida albicans as the usual sole cause  
D. Hepatitis A virus

**Answer: A.** Chlamydia and gonorrhoea are important STI pathogens in PID.

### Question 5
A patient treated for PID has no clinical improvement after 72 hours. What should be done?

A. Ignore symptoms  
B. Reassess diagnosis, adherence, complications and need for admission  
C. Stop all follow-up  
D. Assume treatment has succeeded

**Answer: B.** Failure to improve requires reassessment for TOA, alternative diagnosis or inadequate treatment.
