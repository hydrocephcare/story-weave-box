# Clinical Clarifications: Anaemia, MCV, MCH and Pancytopenia

This guide starts with the basics, then shows how to reason through a patient with pallor, fatigue, nearly normal MCV/MCH and a positive faecal occult blood test.

## 1. Start with the blood-cell factory

Blood has several components:

- **Red blood cells (RBCs):** carry oxygen using haemoglobin.
- **White blood cells (WBCs):** help defend against infection.
- **Platelets:** help stop bleeding by forming an initial plug.
- **Plasma:** the liquid that carries cells, proteins and dissolved substances.

Most blood cells are made in the **bone marrow**, the soft tissue inside certain bones. Think of it as a factory that needs healthy machinery and raw materials.

Red-cell production needs iron, vitamin B12, folate, adequate nutrition, healthy marrow and a hormone called erythropoietin, produced mainly by the kidneys.

## 2. What is anaemia?

**Anaemia means haemoglobin (Hb) is below the expected range for the patient.** Haemoglobin is the protein inside red blood cells that carries oxygen from the lungs to tissues.

If Hb is low, oxygen delivery can fall. The body may compensate by making the heart beat faster and increasing breathing effort.

Common symptoms and signs:
- Pallor, including conjunctival pallor.
- Fatigue and weakness.
- Reduced exercise tolerance.
- Breathlessness, dizziness, headache or palpitations, depending on severity and speed of onset.

Pallor suggests anaemia, but the full blood count (FBC) and Hb confirm it. The severity of symptoms depends not only on the Hb value but also on how quickly it fell and the patient's underlying health.

Approximate adult thresholds often used to define anaemia are Hb below 13 g/dL in men and below 12 g/dL in non-pregnant women. Use the laboratory's reference range and applicable local guidance; pregnancy, age and altitude affect interpretation.

## 3. The three big mechanisms of anaemia

Nearly every cause fits one or more of these groups.

### A. Blood loss
Examples include gastrointestinal bleeding, heavy menstruation, trauma or other bleeding. Blood loss may be obvious, or it may happen slowly without visible blood.

### B. Reduced production
The marrow may lack raw materials (iron, B12, folate), receive inadequate stimulation (as in kidney disease), be suppressed by chronic inflammation or medicines, or be affected by a marrow disorder.

### C. Increased destruction
This is called **haemolysis**: red cells are destroyed earlier than normal. Causes include inherited red-cell disorders, autoimmune haemolysis, some infections and certain medicines or toxins.

A patient may have more than one mechanism. For example, chronic inflammation and gastrointestinal blood loss can coexist.

## 4. MCV: the average size of a red blood cell

**MCV means Mean Corpuscular Volume.** It answers: *How large is the average red cell?*

A typical adult reference range is about **80–100 fL** (femtolitres). Check the laboratory range.

- **Microcytic:** MCV below 80 fL — small red cells.
- **Normocytic:** MCV about 80–100 fL — normal-sized red cells.
- **Macrocytic:** MCV above 100 fL — large red cells.

The root words help: micro = small, normo = normal, macro = large, cytic = cell-related.

MCV classifies the *pattern*. It does not tell you the cause by itself.

## 5. MCH: the average haemoglobin amount per red cell

**MCH means Mean Corpuscular Haemoglobin.** It answers: *How much haemoglobin does the average red cell contain?*

A typical adult reference range is about **27–33 pg** (picograms) per cell.

- Low MCH means each red cell contains less haemoglobin on average. The cells often look paler under a microscope; this is called **hypochromia**.
- Normal MCH means the average haemoglobin amount per cell is within range.
- MCH should be interpreted alongside MCV, Hb, MCHC and the rest of the FBC.

Do not confuse the measures:
- **Hb:** overall haemoglobin concentration in the blood.
- **MCV:** average red-cell size.
- **MCH:** average haemoglobin amount per red cell.
- **MCHC:** average haemoglobin concentration inside red cells.
- **RDW:** how much red-cell sizes vary.

Example: Hb 8 g/dL, MCV 88 fL, MCH 29 pg. The Hb is low, so the patient is anaemic; MCV and MCH are within typical ranges. This is a normocytic, normochromic pattern, not “normal blood.”

## 6. Iron-deficiency anaemia

Iron is a raw material needed to make haemoglobin. Iron deficiency occurs when the body has insufficient iron available for normal red-cell production.

Common causes:
1. **Blood loss:** gastrointestinal ulcers or lesions, heavy menstruation, or another bleeding source.
2. **Low intake:** insufficient dietary iron.
3. **Poor absorption:** for example, some intestinal disorders or gastrointestinal procedures.
4. **Increased requirements:** such as pregnancy or growth.

### Why do cells become small?

Iron is needed to make haemoglobin. When iron is insufficient, developing red cells cannot make enough haemoglobin. As deficiency progresses, the cells tend to become smaller and paler. Established iron-deficiency anaemia often has low MCV and low MCH.

**Important:** early iron deficiency can still have normal MCV and MCH. Iron stores may fall before the red-cell indices become abnormal.

### Tests that help confirm it

- **Ferritin:** reflects iron stores. A low ferritin strongly supports iron deficiency.
- **Transferrin saturation (TSAT):** often low in iron deficiency.
- **TIBC/transferrin:** often increased in uncomplicated iron deficiency.
- **Serum iron:** can be low, but varies and should not be interpreted alone.
- **RDW:** may rise as cells of different sizes circulate.

Ferritin can be normal or high during inflammation, infection or some liver disorders. If inflammation is present, interpret ferritin alongside TSAT and the clinical picture.

## 7. Anaemia of chronic disease (anaemia of inflammation)

This occurs with prolonged inflammation, chronic infection, autoimmune disease, some cancers and other chronic illnesses.

Inflammation increases a hormone called **hepcidin**. Hepcidin limits iron absorption from the intestine and iron release from storage. Iron may be present in the body but less available to the marrow. Inflammation can also reduce red-cell production and blunt the response to erythropoietin.

Typical pattern:
- Hb low.
- MCV often normal; sometimes low.
- MCH may be normal or low.
- Serum iron often low.
- Ferritin often normal or high.
- TIBC/transferrin often low or normal.

### Do not mix up two different labels

- **Normocytic anaemia** describes the red-cell size (normal MCV).
- **Anaemia of chronic disease** suggests the underlying cause (chronic inflammation or illness).

They are not synonyms. Anaemia of chronic disease is often normocytic, but normocytic anaemia has many other causes.

Iron deficiency and anaemia of inflammation can occur together. A ferritin that is not low does not always exclude iron deficiency when inflammation is active.

## 8. Microcytic, normocytic and macrocytic anaemia: the main causes

### Microcytic anaemia (small cells, usually MCV <80 fL)
- Iron deficiency.
- Thalassaemia (inherited reduced production of globin chains).
- Anaemia of chronic inflammation (sometimes).
- Sideroblastic anaemia.
- Lead poisoning.

A very low MCV with a relatively preserved red-cell count can suggest thalassaemia trait; raised RDW can support iron deficiency. These are clues, not definitive diagnostic tests.

### Normocytic anaemia (normal-sized cells, usually MCV 80–100 fL)
- Acute blood loss.
- Early chronic blood loss or early iron deficiency.
- Anaemia of chronic inflammation.
- Chronic kidney disease (reduced erythropoietin).
- Haemolysis.
- Bone marrow suppression, failure or infiltration.
- Some endocrine disorders.

### Macrocytic anaemia (large cells, usually MCV >100 fL)

**Megaloblastic causes** involve impaired DNA synthesis:
- Vitamin B12 deficiency.
- Folate deficiency.
- Some medicines that interfere with DNA synthesis.

**Non-megaloblastic causes** include:
- Alcohol use.
- Liver disease.
- Hypothyroidism.
- Increased reticulocytes (young red cells are larger).
- Some bone marrow disorders, including myelodysplastic syndromes.

B12 deficiency can cause numbness, tingling, impaired balance or other neurological problems. Folate deficiency does not usually cause the same characteristic neurological syndrome.

## 9. Reticulocytes: is the marrow responding?

Reticulocytes are young red blood cells newly released from the marrow.

- **An appropriately high reticulocyte response** can suggest that the marrow is responding to blood loss or haemolysis.
- **A low or inappropriately normal response** suggests the marrow is not replacing red cells adequately, as can occur with iron/B12/folate deficiency, kidney disease, chronic inflammation or marrow disorders.

Interpret the reticulocyte count in context; a corrected count or reticulocyte production index may be more informative in significant anaemia.

## 10. Pancytopenia: when all three blood-cell lines are low

Pancytopenia means reduced:
1. Red cells or haemoglobin (anaemia).
2. White blood cells, especially neutrophils (leukopenia/neutropenia).
3. Platelets (thrombocytopenia).

Possible consequences:
- Low Hb: pallor, fatigue, weakness, breathlessness.
- Low neutrophils: increased infection risk.
- Low platelets: easy bruising, petechiae, gum bleeding or other bleeding.

Pancytopenia is a **laboratory finding, not one single disease**.

Causes include:
- **Aplastic anaemia:** marrow fails to make enough cells.
- **Leukaemia:** abnormal cells interfere with normal marrow production.
- Severe B12 or folate deficiency.
- Medicines, chemotherapy or toxins that suppress marrow.
- Some infections.
- **Hypersplenism:** an enlarged overactive spleen removes or sequesters cells.
- Marrow infiltration by malignancy or certain infections.

Related terms:
- Anaemia = low Hb.
- Leukopenia = low white-cell count.
- Thrombocytopenia = low platelet count.
- Pancytopenia = all three major cell lines reduced.

Anaemia alone does not mean pancytopenia. Check the white-cell and platelet counts on the FBC. Pancytopenia also does not tell you whether the MCV is low, normal or high.

## 11. A practical ward approach to anaemia

1. **Confirm and grade:** check Hb and compare with the correct reference range. Assess vital signs and symptoms.
2. **Look at the full FBC:** MCV, MCH, RDW, white-cell count and platelets.
3. **Classify by MCV:** microcytic, normocytic or macrocytic.
4. **Check reticulocytes:** assess the marrow response.
5. **Choose tests based on the pattern:** ferritin/iron studies; kidney function; inflammatory markers; B12/folate; blood film; and haemolysis tests when indicated.
6. **Look for the cause:** ask about bleeding, menstruation where relevant, diet, medicines, chronic disease, alcohol, infection and family history. Examine for jaundice, lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly and other clues as appropriate.
7. **Do not stop at the blood count:** treating the cause is essential. The urgency and treatment depend on Hb, symptoms, stability and the underlying diagnosis.

## 12. Apply it to the patient described

The reported findings are conjunctival pallor, fatigue, weakness, nearly normal MCV/MCH, a positive faecal occult blood test and an upper gastrointestinal endoscopy.

### What each finding tells us

**Pallor, fatigue and weakness:** compatible with anaemia, but Hb is needed to confirm it and quantify severity.

**Nearly normal MCV/MCH:** if Hb is low and MCV is in range, this is a normocytic pattern. It does not prove anaemia of chronic disease, nor does it exclude early iron deficiency.

**Positive faecal occult blood:** suggests blood may be entering the gastrointestinal tract, even if it is not visible. Slow repeated blood loss can gradually deplete iron and cause anaemia.

**No haematuria:** means there is no reported visible blood in urine; it does not rule out gastrointestinal bleeding or other causes.

### Possible sequence

Gastrointestinal lesion or another source of bleeding → repeated small blood losses → iron stores fall → Hb falls → pallor and fatigue. MCV may be normal early and become low as iron deficiency progresses.

This is a plausible explanation, not a confirmed diagnosis. The patient may instead have chronic inflammation, another cause of normocytic anaemia, or more than one condition.

### Endoscopy versus colonoscopy

Upper GI endoscopy examines the oesophagus, stomach and first part of the small intestine. Colonoscopy examines the large bowel. Review the actual endoscopy report to see whether a bleeding source was found. If iron-deficiency anaemia is confirmed, evaluation of the lower GI tract is generally important in an adult man or postmenopausal woman, even if upper endoscopy has already been performed.

**Melena** means black, tarry, sticky stool and often suggests upper GI bleeding. A positive faecal occult blood test means blood was detected by a test, even if stool looked normal. These are related but not identical findings.

### The most useful results to obtain

1. Haemoglobin and the complete FBC.
2. Ferritin and transferrin saturation.
3. Reticulocyte count.
4. Kidney function and inflammatory markers if clinically indicated.
5. The actual endoscopy report and plan for investigating the source of blood.

Ongoing bleeding, fainting, low blood pressure, a fast pulse, marked dizziness or breathlessness at rest needs urgent clinical assessment.

## Final memory aid

- **Hb** = how much haemoglobin is in the blood.
- **MCV** = average red-cell size.
- **MCH** = average haemoglobin amount per red cell.
- **Microcytic** = small cells.
- **Normocytic** = normal-sized cells.
- **Macrocytic** = large cells.
- **Iron deficiency** = inadequate iron for normal haemoglobin production.
- **Anaemia of chronic disease** = inflammation limits iron availability and suppresses production.
- **Pancytopenia** = red cells, white cells and platelets are all reduced.

The key clinical question is: **Is the patient losing blood, making too few red cells, or destroying red cells too quickly?** Use the history, examination, FBC and targeted investigations to work it out.
