# Vaginal Discharge: Clinical Assessment and Management

*Year 4 MBChB | Obstetrics & Gynaecology | CAT revision*

## 1. Introduction

Vaginal discharge is a common symptom. It may be **physiological**, due to normal cervical/vaginal secretions, or pathological due to vaginitis, cervicitis, a foreign body, pregnancy-related change or less commonly malignancy. The aim is to identify the source, distinguish vaginal infection from cervical/STI infection, exclude pelvic inflammatory disease and treat the patient and relevant partner(s).

## 2. Normal physiology

Normal vaginal discharge is usually clear or white, non-offensive and not associated with itching, burning, pelvic pain or dysuria. Amount and consistency vary with oestrogen levels, ovulation, pregnancy and sexual activity. Lactobacilli maintain an acidic vaginal environment (typically pH around 3.8–4.5 in reproductive years), helping suppress pathogen overgrowth.

A change in colour alone does not establish a specific organism. Ask about associated symptoms and sexual risk.

## 3. Causes of pathological discharge

### A. Vaginitis

**Bacterial vaginosis (BV)**
- Vaginal microbiome dysbiosis with reduced lactobacilli and increased anaerobic organisms.
- Thin, homogeneous grey-white discharge with fishy odour, often more noticeable after intercourse or menstruation.
- Usually little vulval inflammation or itching.
- Vaginal pH typically above 4.5.
- Clue cells on microscopy; positive whiff test may support diagnosis.

**Vulvovaginal candidiasis**
- Usually *Candida albicans*.
- Thick white "curdy" discharge, marked vulval itching, soreness, erythema and sometimes fissures.
- Vaginal pH usually remains normal (4.5 or below).
- Yeast/pseudohyphae may be seen on microscopy.
- Risk factors include recent antibiotics, pregnancy, diabetes and immunosuppression; recurrent disease requires assessment.

**Trichomoniasis**
- *Trichomonas vaginalis*, a sexually transmitted protozoan.
- May cause frothy yellow-green, offensive discharge, vulvovaginal irritation and dysuria.
- Vaginal pH often above 4.5.
- Cervix may show punctate haemorrhages ("strawberry cervix"), but this is not always present.
- NAAT is preferred where available; wet mount can identify motile trichomonads but has limited sensitivity.

### B. Cervicitis/STIs

**Chlamydia and gonorrhoea** can cause mucopurulent endocervical discharge, cervical friability/contact bleeding, dysuria, postcoital bleeding or no symptoms. They may ascend and cause PID. *Mycoplasma genitalium* can cause persistent/recurrent cervicitis in selected cases.

### C. Other causes
- Physiological discharge around ovulation or pregnancy.
- Retained foreign body (e.g. tampon): offensive discharge.
- Irritant/contact dermatitis from soaps, douching or intravaginal products.
- Atrophic vaginitis/genitourinary syndrome of menopause: dryness, irritation and discharge.
- Cervical/vaginal lesions or malignancy, especially persistent blood-stained or offensive discharge.

## 4. History

Ask:
1. Onset, duration, amount, colour, consistency and odour.
2. Itching, soreness, vulval swelling, dysuria, dyspareunia, pelvic/lower abdominal pain, fever and abnormal bleeding.
3. Last menstrual period, pregnancy possibility and menopausal status.
4. Sexual history: new/multiple partners, condom use, partner symptoms, previous STI/PID and recent sexual exposure. Ask confidentially and without judgement.
5. Recent antibiotics, diabetes, HIV/immunosuppression, pregnancy, intravaginal products, douching and foreign-body possibility.
6. Previous episodes, treatment, recurrence and response.

**Red flags:** lower abdominal pain, fever, cervical motion tenderness, pregnancy, severe illness, genital ulcers, blood-stained discharge, postmenopausal discharge/bleeding or suspected retained foreign body.

## 5. Examination

- Vitals and general condition; assess fever/sepsis.
- Abdominal examination for tenderness, guarding or mass.
- Inspect vulva for erythema, excoriation, ulcers, vesicles, swelling and lesions.
- Speculum examination (with consent and chaperone) assesses vaginal walls and cervix, character of discharge, foreign body, cervical friability, mucopus, bleeding and lesions.
- Bimanual examination if pelvic pain, dyspareunia, fever or suspected PID: assess cervical motion, uterine and adnexal tenderness/mass.
- Do not perform an internal examination without informed consent; adapt to age, pregnancy, trauma history and patient preference.

## 6. Investigations

| Test | Interpretation/use |
|---|---|
| Pregnancy test | Excludes pregnancy-related causes and guides drug choice |
| Vaginal pH | BV/trichomoniasis often >4.5; candidiasis usually ≤4.5 |
| Saline wet mount | Clue cells, motile trichomonads, white cells |
| KOH microscopy/whiff test | Yeast forms; fishy amine odour supports BV |
| NAAT for chlamydia/gonorrhoea | Preferred test where available |
| Trichomonas NAAT | More sensitive than wet mount |
| HIV/syphilis testing | Offer STI testing according to risk and national guidance |
| Urinalysis/culture | If urinary symptoms |
| FBC/CRP and pelvic ultrasound | If systemic illness, pelvic pain or suspected PID/abscess |

A negative wet mount does not reliably exclude trichomoniasis or candidiasis. Persistent or recurrent symptoms require reassessment, testing where available and consideration of non-infectious causes.

## 7. Distinguishing the common causes

| Feature | BV | Candidiasis | Trichomoniasis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Discharge | Thin, homogeneous grey-white | Thick, white/curdy | Frothy, yellow-green may occur |
| Odour | Fishy | Usually absent | May be offensive |
| Itching | Usually mild/absent | Often prominent | Variable |
| pH | >4.5 | Usually ≤4.5 | Often >4.5 |
| Microscopy | Clue cells | Budding yeast/pseudohyphae | Motile protozoa |
| STI? | Not classically an STI | Usually not STI | Yes |

## 8. Management

Treat according to the likely/confirmed cause, pregnancy status, allergy, local resistance and current Kenya STI guidelines.

- **BV:** metronidazole or another recommended regimen. Routine partner treatment is not generally recommended for uncomplicated BV under many guidelines; manage recurrent disease according to current local guidance.
- **Candidiasis:** topical azole or oral fluconazole in appropriate non-pregnant patients. In pregnancy, use a topical azole regimen; avoid oral fluconazole. Recurrent/complicated candidiasis requires confirmation and a longer, tailored regimen.
- **Trichomoniasis:** systemic nitroimidazole treatment (usually metronidazole according to protocol); treat sexual partners, advise abstinence until both patient and partners have completed treatment and symptoms resolve, and arrange retesting according to national guidance.
- **Chlamydial cervicitis:** doxycycline is commonly first-line in non-pregnant adults; pregnancy requires a pregnancy-appropriate alternative. Treat partners and screen for other STIs.
- **Gonorrhoea:** use recommended ceftriaxone-based therapy according to current national guidance and local antimicrobial resistance; test for chlamydia and treat if not excluded.
- **PID:** if pelvic pain and uterine/cervical motion/adnexal tenderness suggest upper genital tract infection, manage as PID rather than treating discharge alone.
- **Irritant/atrophic causes:** stop irritants, advise against vaginal douching, and consider local oestrogen or other management for genitourinary syndrome of menopause after assessment.

### Prevention and counselling
Condom use, STI screening based on risk, partner notification/treatment for STIs, avoiding douching and unnecessary intravaginal products, and completion of treatment. Avoid treating every discharge empirically with multiple antimicrobials without assessment when diagnostic testing is available.

## 9. Complications

Untreated chlamydial/gonococcal cervicitis may progress to PID, tubal infertility, ectopic pregnancy and chronic pelvic pain. Trichomoniasis is associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes and increased HIV acquisition/transmission risk. Candidiasis and BV commonly recur; persistent symptoms can impair quality of life.

## 10. CAT high-yield points

- Normal discharge is generally clear/white, non-offensive and without irritation.
- BV: thin fishy discharge, pH >4.5, clue cells.
- Candidiasis: intense itch, curdy discharge, normal pH.
- Trichomoniasis: STI, frothy yellow-green discharge may occur, pH >4.5; treat partners.
- Chlamydia/gonorrhoea cause cervicitis and may be asymptomatic.
- Pelvic pain, fever or cervical motion tenderness suggests PID—do not treat as simple vaginitis.
- Pregnancy status affects antibiotic/antifungal choice.
- Persistent blood-stained or postmenopausal discharge requires evaluation for pathology.

## 11. Tricky examiner questions

**Q1. Which condition classically causes a fishy odour and clue cells?**  
**Answer:** Bacterial vaginosis.

**Q2. Which common vaginitis usually has a normal vaginal pH?**  
**Answer:** Vulvovaginal candidiasis.

**Q3. Why must sexual partners be treated in trichomoniasis?**  
**Answer:** It is sexually transmitted; untreated partners can reinfect the patient.

**Q4. Does a negative wet mount exclude trichomoniasis?**  
**Answer:** No. Wet mount sensitivity is limited; use NAAT where available if suspicion persists.

**Q5. What findings suggest discharge is associated with PID rather than isolated vaginitis?**  
**Answer:** Pelvic/lower abdominal pain with cervical motion, uterine or adnexal tenderness, with or without fever.

## 12. Practice MCQs

### Question 1
A woman has thin grey-white discharge with a fishy odour and little vulval itching. Vaginal pH is 5.0. What is the most likely diagnosis?

A. Bacterial vaginosis  
B. Vulvovaginal candidiasis  
C. Genital herpes  
D. Primary dysmenorrhoea

**Answer: A.** Thin fishy discharge, elevated pH and minimal inflammation suggest BV.

### Question 2
A patient has intense vulval itching and thick curdy discharge. Vaginal pH is 4.2. Most likely cause?

A. Trichomonas vaginalis  
B. Candida species  
C. Gonorrhoea  
D. BV

**Answer: B.** Candidiasis commonly causes intense itching and thick discharge with normal vaginal pH.

### Question 3
Which infection requires treatment of sexual partners to prevent reinfection?

A. Trichomoniasis  
B. Uncomplicated candidiasis  
C. Physiological discharge  
D. Irritant dermatitis

**Answer: A.** Trichomoniasis is an STI; partner treatment is essential.

### Question 4
A woman with discharge has fever, lower abdominal pain and cervical motion tenderness. What complication should be suspected?

A. PID  
B. Fibroid degeneration only  
C. Menopause  
D. Primary amenorrhoea

**Answer: A.** These features suggest upper genital tract infection.

### Question 5
Which test is preferred for detecting chlamydia and gonorrhoea when available?

A. Vaginal pH alone  
B. NAAT  
C. Urine dipstick only  
D. CA-125

**Answer: B.** Nucleic acid amplification testing is preferred for these pathogens.
