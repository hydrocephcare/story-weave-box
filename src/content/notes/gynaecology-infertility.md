# Infertility: Evaluation and Management

*Year 4 MBChB | Obstetrics & Gynaecology | CAT revision*

## 1. Definition and terminology

**Infertility** is failure to achieve pregnancy after 12 months or more of regular unprotected sexual intercourse. Earlier evaluation is appropriate when the woman is older (commonly 35 years or more), has irregular/absent periods, known tubal or uterine disease, endometriosis, previous gonadotoxic treatment or a known male-factor problem. Local pathways may use different age thresholds.

- **Primary infertility:** no previous pregnancy.
- **Secondary infertility:** failure to conceive after a previous pregnancy, irrespective of its outcome.
- **Subfertility:** reduced probability of conception; often used interchangeably with infertility in clinical practice.

Infertility is a couple-level condition. Investigate both partners in parallel and avoid blaming either partner.

## 2. Normal reproduction: understand the steps

![Female reproductive system showing the uterus, cervix, ovaries and fallopian tubes. Source: CDC public-domain illustration via Wikimedia Commons; hosted on OmpathStudy Cloudinary.](https://res.cloudinary.com/l4wbzpfr/image/upload/v1794203533/ompathstudy/year4-gynaecology/female-reproductive-anatomy.svg)

*Figure: Female reproductive anatomy relevant to the clinical assessment.*

1. Hypothalamus releases GnRH in pulses.
2. Anterior pituitary releases FSH and LH.
3. FSH supports follicular growth and oestradiol production.
4. LH surge triggers ovulation.
5. The fimbriae help capture the oocyte; fertilisation usually occurs in the ampulla.
6. The embryo travels through a patent, functioning tube to the uterine cavity.
7. A receptive endometrium allows implantation.
8. Adequate sperm production, motility, morphology and delivery are needed for fertilisation.

A problem at any stage can reduce fertility.

## 3. Causes

### A. Female factors

**Ovulatory dysfunction**
- PCOS: irregular/anovulatory cycles, often with hyperandrogenism.
- Hypothalamic dysfunction: low body weight, excessive exercise, stress or chronic illness.
- Hyperprolactinaemia.
- Thyroid dysfunction.
- Premature ovarian insufficiency and age-related decline in ovarian reserve.

**Tubal/peritoneal factors**
- Previous PID, especially chlamydia/gonorrhoea.
- Previous ectopic pregnancy or tubal/pelvic surgery.
- Endometriosis and pelvic adhesions.
- Genital tuberculosis should be considered according to epidemiology and clinical history.

**Uterine/cervical factors**
- Submucosal fibroids, endometrial polyps, intrauterine adhesions (Asherman syndrome), congenital uterine anomalies and selected endometrial disorders.
- Cervical factors are less commonly the sole cause.

### B. Male factors
- Impaired sperm production: testicular failure, varicocele in selected cases, mumps orchitis, undescended testes, genetic causes, chemotherapy/radiotherapy.
- Obstruction: previous vasectomy, infection or congenital absence of vas deferens.
- Endocrine causes: hypogonadism, pituitary/hypothalamic disease.
- Sexual/ejaculatory dysfunction.
- Lifestyle/environmental contributors: smoking, excess alcohol, anabolic steroids, obesity, heat exposure and some medications.

### C. Unexplained infertility
A diagnosis made when standard evaluation of ovulation, tubal patency and semen parameters is reassuring, but conception has not occurred. It does not mean that no biological factor exists.

## 4. History: take both partners seriously

**Female partner**
- Duration of trying, frequency/timing of intercourse, contraception history and previous fertility treatment.
- Menstrual history: menarche, cycle regularity, interval, duration, dysmenorrhoea, amenorrhoea.
- Obstetric history: previous pregnancies, miscarriages, ectopic pregnancy, deliveries and complications.
- STI/PID, pelvic surgery, appendicitis/peritonitis, endometriosis, TB exposure.
- Symptoms of PCOS, thyroid disease, hyperprolactinaemia or premature ovarian insufficiency.
- Medical illnesses, medications, weight change, exercise and nutritional status.
- Sexual pain, vaginal dryness, relationship factors and sexual dysfunction.

**Male partner**
- Previous paternity and duration of attempts.
- Pubertal development, testicular injury, undescended testes, mumps orchitis, genital infection, surgery and hernia repair.
- Libido, erections, ejaculation and frequency/timing of intercourse.
- Drugs (especially testosterone/anabolic steroids), smoking, alcohol, occupational heat/toxin exposure and systemic illness.

Use sensitive, non-judgemental language. Ask about sexual function privately when appropriate.

## 5. Examination

**Female:** BMI, secondary sexual characteristics, thyroid and signs of androgen excess/galactorrhoea; abdominal scars or masses; pelvic examination when indicated for uterine/adnexal mass, tenderness, endometriosis or infection.

**Male:** BMI, virilisation, secondary sexual characteristics, testicular size/consistency, epididymides, vas deferens, penile abnormalities and palpable varicocele. A focused genital examination should be performed respectfully and with consent.

## 6. Investigations: investigate both partners in parallel

| Investigation | What it assesses |
|---|---|
| Semen analysis | First-line male test: volume, concentration, motility and morphology using WHO laboratory standards |
| Menstrual history | Regular predictable cycles often suggest ovulation but do not prove every cycle |
| Mid-luteal serum progesterone | Evidence of recent ovulation; time it about 7 days before expected next period, not automatically on day 21 |
| Pelvic ultrasound | Uterus, endometrium, ovaries, antral follicles and structural abnormalities |
| Tubal patency test (HSG/HyCoSy) | Whether fallopian tubes appear patent; arrange when pregnancy and active pelvic infection are excluded |
| TSH/prolactin | If symptoms or history suggest thyroid disease/hyperprolactinaemia |
| Androgen assessment | If clinical hyperandrogenism/PCOS suspected |
| Ovarian reserve tests (AMH, antral follicle count, selected FSH) | Help predict response to ovarian stimulation; do not independently determine natural fertility |
| Laparoscopy with dye | Selected cases with suspected endometriosis, adhesions or pelvic pathology; not routine for every couple |

Repeat an abnormal semen analysis because parameters vary. A severely abnormal result warrants specialist assessment. Do not request every hormone test in every patient; choose tests based on the history and examination.

## 7. Management

Treatment depends on the cause, duration, age, ovarian reserve, tubal status, semen findings, patient preference, cost and local service availability.

### A. General measures
- Explain the fertile window and encourage regular intercourse (e.g. every 2–3 days through the cycle).
- Optimise weight, nutrition, exercise and chronic disease control.
- Stop smoking, avoid recreational drugs and excess alcohol; review medications and anabolic steroids.
- Provide folic acid before conception and preconception counselling.
- Treat STIs and other reversible conditions; provide psychosocial support.

### B. Ovulatory dysfunction
- **PCOS-related anovulation:** lifestyle optimisation; letrozole is recommended as first-line pharmacological ovulation induction in many current guidelines when no other infertility factor is present. Clomifene or other approaches may be used according to local protocols and availability.
- Treat thyroid disease or hyperprolactinaemia when confirmed.
- Hypothalamic causes require correction of energy deficit/underlying illness and specialist management.
- Premature ovarian insufficiency requires counselling and specialist fertility advice; spontaneous conception is uncommon but not impossible.

Ovulation induction requires monitoring appropriate to the medicine and risk of multiple pregnancy/ovarian hyperstimulation.

### C. Tubal and peritoneal disease
Treat active infection, but antibiotics do not reverse established tubal scarring. Selected patients may benefit from tubal surgery; IVF may be more appropriate with severe bilateral tubal disease. Hydrosalpinx can reduce IVF success and may need specialist intervention before embryo transfer.

### D. Uterine causes
Remove cavity-distorting polyps or submucosal fibroids when indicated. Treat intrauterine adhesions and selected congenital anomalies in specialist care. Do not assume every intramural fibroid requires removal.

### E. Male factor
Treat reversible endocrine or obstructive causes where possible; stop exogenous testosterone/anabolic steroids when appropriate under medical supervision. Refer for urology/andrology assessment. Assisted reproduction may include intrauterine insemination, IVF or ICSI depending on severity and couple factors.

### F. Assisted reproductive technology
- **IUI:** prepared sperm placed into the uterine cavity around ovulation; requires suitable tubal function and appropriate semen parameters.
- **IVF:** oocytes are stimulated and collected, fertilised in the laboratory and embryo transferred to the uterus.
- **ICSI:** a single sperm is injected into an oocyte, used for selected severe male-factor or fertilisation problems.

Discuss cost, access, success rates by age and diagnosis, multiple pregnancy, ovarian hyperstimulation, emotional burden and ethical considerations.

## 8. Complications and psychosocial care

Infertility can cause distress, anxiety, depression, stigma, relationship strain, financial hardship and gender-based blame. Provide confidential, culturally sensitive counselling; screen for psychological distress and safeguarding concerns. Avoid unproven remedies and unnecessary invasive procedures.

## 9. CAT high-yield points

- Definition: no pregnancy after **12 months** of regular unprotected intercourse.
- Evaluate both partners at the same time.
- Semen analysis is a first-line investigation for the male partner.
- Regular cycles suggest, but do not absolutely prove, ovulation.
- Mid-luteal progesterone is timed about 7 days before the expected next period.
- PID damages tubes; endometriosis causes adhesions and altered pelvic anatomy.
- HSG assesses tubal patency but is not done during pregnancy or active PID.
- AMH assesses ovarian reserve/expected stimulation response, not a direct yes/no test of natural fertility.
- PCOS anovulation: letrozole is first-line ovulation induction in many current recommendations.
- Infertility is a couple's condition; avoid assigning blame.

## 10. Tricky examiner questions

**Q1. When is infertility defined?**  
**Answer:** Failure to achieve pregnancy after 12 months of regular unprotected sexual intercourse; investigate earlier when age or known risk factors justify it.

**Q2. Why should both partners be investigated simultaneously?**  
**Answer:** Male and female factors commonly coexist, and parallel assessment avoids delay and inappropriate attribution of blame.

**Q3. When should mid-luteal progesterone be measured?**  
**Answer:** Approximately 7 days before the expected next menstruation; "day 21" only suits a 28-day cycle.

**Q4. Does a normal AMH guarantee fertility?**  
**Answer:** No. AMH mainly helps estimate ovarian reserve and response to stimulation; it does not measure egg quality or guarantee spontaneous conception.

**Q5. What is the difference between IVF and ICSI?**  
**Answer:** In IVF, sperm and oocyte are brought together in the laboratory; in ICSI, one sperm is injected directly into the oocyte.

## 11. Practice MCQs

### Question 1
A 29-year-old has regular 28-day cycles and has tried to conceive for 14 months. Which test is a first-line investigation of her partner?

A. Semen analysis  
B. CT pelvis  
C. CA-125  
D. Endometrial biopsy

**Answer: A.** Semen analysis is an essential early investigation of the male partner.

### Question 2
A woman has cycles every 45–60 days, acne and hirsutism. The likely mechanism of infertility is:

A. Ovulatory dysfunction  
B. Complete tubal obstruction in every case  
C. Cervical cancer  
D. Premature menopause as the only possibility

**Answer: A.** PCOS is a common cause of oligo-ovulation/anovulation.

### Question 3
A woman has a 35-day cycle. When is a mid-luteal progesterone test best timed?

A. Always day 7  
B. Around day 14  
C. Around day 28, approximately 7 days before the expected next period  
D. Only during menstruation

**Answer: C.** The test should be timed to the luteal phase, about 7 days before the next expected period.

### Question 4
Which condition is a major cause of acquired tubal-factor infertility?

A. PID  
B. Migraine  
C. Asthma  
D. Myopia

**Answer: A.** PID can cause tubal epithelial damage, scarring and obstruction.

### Question 5
A man taking exogenous testosterone presents with infertility. Why is this relevant?

A. It increases pituitary FSH and LH  
B. It suppresses gonadotrophins and intratesticular testosterone, impairing spermatogenesis  
C. It always improves sperm count  
D. It has no reproductive effect

**Answer: B.** Exogenous testosterone suppresses LH/FSH and can markedly reduce sperm production.
