# Abnormal Uterine Bleeding (AUB)

*Year 4 MBChB | Obstetrics & Gynaecology | CAT revision*

Abnormal uterine bleeding is a symptom, not a final diagnosis. The clinical task is to establish whether the patient is stable, exclude pregnancy, characterise the bleeding, identify the cause and assess the consequences—especially iron-deficiency anaemia and endometrial pathology.

## 1. Definition and normal menstrual parameters

![Menstrual cycle illustration showing the ovarian and endometrial phases. Source: OmpathStudy anatomy library.](https://ompathstudy.com/images/year1/anatomy/histology-embryology-spot-review/e-menstrual-cycle.jpg)

*Figure: The cyclical ovarian and endometrial changes that determine menstrual bleeding.*

**Abnormal uterine bleeding (AUB)** is bleeding from the uterine corpus that is abnormal in frequency, regularity, duration or volume in a non-pregnant patient of reproductive age. It may be acute (an episode requiring immediate intervention) or chronic (abnormal bleeding present for most of the preceding six months).

For adult reproductive-age patients, a typical cycle is approximately 24–38 days, bleeding lasts no more than 8 days, and cycle-to-cycle variation is generally limited. Heavy menstrual bleeding (HMB) is menstrual blood loss that interferes with physical, social, emotional or material quality of life; the patient's experience is more useful clinically than attempting to measure millilitres.

**Terminology**
- **HMB:** excessive menstrual blood loss, usually during otherwise cyclic menstruation.
- **Intermenstrual bleeding (IMB):** bleeding between otherwise recognisable menstrual periods.
- **Postcoital bleeding:** bleeding after sexual intercourse; investigate cervical and vaginal causes.
- **Postmenopausal bleeding:** any vaginal bleeding occurring after 12 months of amenorrhoea due to menopause; requires assessment for endometrial pathology.
- **Acute AUB:** heavy bleeding that, in the clinician's judgement, requires immediate intervention.

## 2. Causes: FIGO PALM–COEIN

The FIGO system separates structural causes (PALM) from non-structural causes (COEIN). More than one cause may coexist.

| Group | Cause | Key concept |
|---|---|---|
| P | Polyp | Focal endometrial or cervical lesion; often IMB |
| A | Adenomyosis | Endometrial glands/stroma within myometrium; painful heavy periods |
| L | Leiomyoma | Fibroids; submucosal lesions particularly associated with HMB |
| M | Malignancy/hyperplasia | Endometrial hyperplasia or cancer; consider with risk factors |
| C | Coagulopathy | e.g. von Willebrand disease, platelet disorders |
| O | Ovulatory dysfunction | Anovulation, PCOS, thyroid disease, hyperprolactinaemia, perimenopause |
| E | Endometrial | Local endometrial haemostatic dysfunction despite ovulation |
| I | Iatrogenic | Hormonal contraception, anticoagulants, intrauterine devices and other medicines |
| N | Not otherwise classified | Less common entities not fitting the above groups |

**Important:** pregnancy-related bleeding is not classified as AUB. Always exclude pregnancy first in a patient of reproductive potential.

## 3. Pathophysiology

- **Ovulatory cycles:** progesterone from the corpus luteum stabilises the endometrium after oestrogen-driven proliferation. Progesterone withdrawal causes organised shedding and menstruation.
- **Anovulation:** no corpus luteum forms, so there is no normal progesterone phase. Unopposed oestrogen produces a proliferative, fragile endometrium that sheds unpredictably, causing irregular or prolonged bleeding.
- **Structural lesions:** polyps and submucosal fibroids distort the cavity and increase or disrupt the endometrial bleeding surface.
- **Coagulopathy/endometrial dysfunction:** impaired clot formation or local vasoconstriction and repair increases menstrual blood loss.
- **Chronic blood loss:** iron stores fall first, followed by reduced haemoglobin and iron-deficiency anaemia.

## 4. Clinical assessment

### History

First quantify the bleeding and assess haemodynamic symptoms.

1. **Bleeding pattern:** age at onset, last menstrual period, cycle interval, regularity, duration, flooding, clots, night-time changes, pads/tampons used and impact on daily activity.
2. **Pregnancy possibility:** sexual history taken respectfully, contraception, missed period, pregnancy symptoms, recent pregnancy, miscarriage or delivery.
3. **Associated symptoms:** dysmenorrhoea, pelvic pressure, dyspareunia, postcoital bleeding, vaginal discharge, fever, weight loss, fatigue, dizziness, syncope and breathlessness.
4. **Endocrine clues:** weight change, acne/hirsutism, galactorrhoea, heat/cold intolerance and symptoms of thyroid disease.
5. **Bleeding tendency:** heavy bleeding since menarche, easy bruising, epistaxis, gum bleeding, excessive dental/surgical bleeding and family history.
6. **Medication:** anticoagulants, antiplatelets, hormonal contraception, copper IUD, tamoxifen and medicines affecting prolactin.
7. **Risk of endometrial hyperplasia/cancer:** chronic anovulation/PCOS, obesity, diabetes, prolonged unopposed oestrogen, tamoxifen, Lynch syndrome and increasing age.
8. **Reproductive goals:** whether pregnancy is desired now, later or not at all.

### Examination

- **Immediate:** pulse, blood pressure, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, temperature, mental state, capillary refill and signs of shock.
- **General:** pallor, BMI, bruising/petechiae, thyroid signs, acne, hirsutism and galactorrhoea when indicated.
- **Abdominal:** tenderness, distension, palpable uterine or adnexal mass, guarding.
- **Speculum:** identify whether blood comes through the cervical os; inspect cervix and vagina for trauma, lesions, polyps, cervicitis or malignancy.
- **Bimanual:** uterine size, contour, mobility and tenderness; adnexal mass/tenderness and cervical motion tenderness. Obtain consent, explain the examination, offer a chaperone and adapt to the patient's circumstances.

## 5. Investigations

| Investigation | Why it is done |
|---|---|
| Urine/serum β-hCG | Excludes pregnancy-related bleeding, including ectopic pregnancy |
| Full blood count | Detects anaemia and thrombocytopenia |
| Ferritin | Assesses iron deficiency when suspected |
| Pelvic ultrasound (usually transvaginal if appropriate) | Detects fibroids, polyps, adenomyosis, endometrial and adnexal abnormalities |
| TSH | If symptoms or history suggest thyroid dysfunction |
| Prolactin | If galactorrhoea/amenorrhoea or suspected hyperprolactinaemia |
| Coagulation/von Willebrand testing | If personal/family history suggests a bleeding disorder |
| Cervical tests/STI tests | If cervicitis, STI or cervical pathology is suspected |
| Endometrial sampling | Persistent bleeding, risk factors for hyperplasia/cancer, or age-based indication under local guidance |

A normal ultrasound does not exclude every endometrial lesion. Saline-infusion sonography or hysteroscopy may be needed when an intracavity lesion is suspected. Endometrial sampling is not a routine test for every young patient; select it according to age, risk factors and persistence of symptoms.

## 6. Management

### A. Stabilise acute heavy bleeding

Treat the patient, not just the haemoglobin result.

1. Assess ABCs and haemodynamic stability; call senior/gynaecology help early if unstable.
2. Establish IV access, send FBC, group and crossmatch, pregnancy test and other urgent tests.
3. Give IV fluids and blood products when clinically indicated by shock, ongoing blood loss and laboratory findings; monitor vital signs and urine output.
4. Exclude pregnancy-related causes and assess for coagulopathy.
5. Once stabilised, control bleeding with appropriate medical therapy or procedural intervention under the local protocol.

### B. Treat the cause and reduce bleeding

- **Tranexamic acid:** antifibrinolytic; reduces menstrual blood loss. Avoid or use cautiously where thromboembolic risk/contraindications apply; follow local protocol.
- **NSAIDs** (e.g. mefenamic acid/ibuprofen): reduce prostaglandin synthesis and can improve HMB and dysmenorrhoea; avoid with renal impairment, peptic ulcer disease, some bleeding disorders and other contraindications.
- **Levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine system (LNG-IUS):** highly effective long-term option for HMB when the uterine cavity is suitable and pregnancy is not desired immediately.
- **Combined hormonal contraception:** regulates cycles and reduces bleeding; assess venous thromboembolism, migraine with aura, smoking/age and other contraindications.
- **Progestogens:** useful in selected anovulatory bleeding and endometrial protection; regimen depends on clinical context.
- **Iron replacement:** oral or IV iron according to severity, tolerance and urgency; identify and treat the source of iron loss.
- **Cause-specific treatment:** hysteroscopic polypectomy, myomectomy, treatment of thyroid disease or coagulopathy, or management of endometrial hyperplasia/cancer.

### C. Surgery

Consider when medical treatment fails, is contraindicated, pathology requires removal, bleeding is severe/recurrent, or patient preference supports it after counselling:
- Hysteroscopy with polypectomy or selected submucosal fibroid resection.
- Endometrial ablation for selected patients who have completed childbearing; it is not suitable when future pregnancy is desired and does not replace evaluation of suspected malignancy.
- Hysterectomy is definitive but irreversible; discuss fertility, risks, alternatives and patient preference.

## 7. Complications

Iron-deficiency anaemia, fatigue, reduced quality of life, missed work/school, haemodynamic compromise in acute severe bleeding, infertility depending on the underlying cause, and delayed diagnosis of endometrial/cervical malignancy.

## 8. CAT high-yield points

- Use **PALM–COEIN** for non-pregnant reproductive-age AUB.
- **Pregnancy test first** in reproductive-age bleeding.
- Submucosal fibroids and polyps commonly cause heavy or intermenstrual bleeding.
- Irregular bleeding with long gaps often suggests ovulatory dysfunction.
- Heavy bleeding since menarche plus bruising/epistaxis suggests a bleeding disorder.
- Postmenopausal bleeding is abnormal and must be investigated.
- AUB with shock is an emergency: resuscitate before completing the full diagnostic work-up.
- Treatment depends on cause, severity, contraindications and fertility wishes.

## 9. Tricky questions examiners ask

**Q1. Why must pregnancy be excluded before diagnosing AUB?**  
**Answer:** Miscarriage and ectopic pregnancy can cause vaginal bleeding and require different, sometimes emergency, management.

**Q2. What does PALM–COEIN distinguish?**  
**Answer:** Structural causes (PALM) from non-structural causes (COEIN).

**Q3. Why does anovulation cause irregular heavy bleeding?**  
**Answer:** No corpus luteum forms, so progesterone is absent; prolonged oestrogen stimulation produces unstable endometrium that sheds unpredictably.

**Q4. Which fibroid location is most associated with heavy menstrual bleeding?**  
**Answer:** Submucosal, because it distorts the endometrial cavity.

**Q5. Does a normal pelvic ultrasound exclude endometrial pathology?**  
**Answer:** No. Persistent bleeding or significant risk factors may require endometrial sampling or hysteroscopy.

## 10. Practice MCQs

### Question 1
A 32-year-old has irregular heavy bleeding and cycles separated by two to three months. Pregnancy test is negative. She has obesity and hirsutism. What mechanism is most likely?

A. Excess progesterone from a persistent corpus luteum  
B. Chronic anovulation with unopposed oestrogen  
C. Acute cervical trauma  
D. Premature ovarian failure

**Answer: B.** Chronic anovulation, commonly associated with PCOS, means inadequate progesterone exposure and irregular endometrial shedding.

### Question 2
A 26-year-old with heavy bleeding since menarche reports frequent epistaxis and easy bruising. Which cause should be considered?

A. Coagulopathy  
B. Endometrial polyp only  
C. Cervical ectropion  
D. Ovarian torsion

**Answer: A.** Lifelong heavy menstrual bleeding plus mucocutaneous bleeding suggests an inherited bleeding disorder such as von Willebrand disease.

### Question 3
A patient with heavy vaginal bleeding is pale, tachycardic and hypotensive. What is the immediate priority?

A. Arrange outpatient hysteroscopy  
B. Start a menstrual diary  
C. Resuscitate and obtain urgent senior help  
D. Begin routine cervical screening

**Answer: C.** Haemodynamic instability takes priority over elective diagnostic procedures.

### Question 4
Which classification is used for causes of AUB in non-pregnant reproductive-age patients?

A. FIGO TNM  
B. PALM–COEIN  
C. Bishop score  
D. Robson classification

**Answer: B.** PALM–COEIN is the FIGO aetiological classification.

### Question 5
A 48-year-old with persistent AUB and risk factors for endometrial hyperplasia has an unremarkable pelvic examination. What is an important next step?

A. Reassure without follow-up  
B. Consider endometrial assessment/sampling according to age and risk  
C. Diagnose PID  
D. Treat as ovarian torsion

**Answer: B.** Persistent bleeding and endometrial cancer risk factors warrant assessment even when examination is normal.
