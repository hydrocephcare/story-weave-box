## 1. Foundations: anxiety, fear and stress

### 1.1 Anxiety

**Anxiety** is an unpleasant emotional state characterised by apprehension, worry, tension and anticipation of possible danger. The threat may be uncertain, future-oriented or disproportionate to the actual situation.

- It is often associated with autonomic symptoms such as palpitations, sweating, tremor and muscle tension.
- It may affect concentration, sleep, appetite and daily functioning.
- Mild anxiety is a normal adaptive response; it becomes a disorder when excessive, persistent, difficult to control and associated with significant distress or impairment.

### 1.2 Fear versus anxiety

- **Fear:** an immediate response to a clearly identifiable or imminent threat; it prepares the body for rapid action.
- **Anxiety:** anticipation of a possible or uncertain future threat; it may persist even when there is no immediate danger.
- **Panic:** a sudden surge of intense fear or discomfort, reaching a peak within minutes, accompanied by physical and cognitive symptoms.

### 1.3 Stress

**Stress** is the physical and psychological response to demands or challenges that threaten or exceed a person's perceived coping resources.

Stressors may be:

- Physical: pain, injury, infection, sleep deprivation.
- Psychological: examinations, bereavement, relationship conflict.
- Social or economic: unemployment, poverty, housing insecurity, discrimination.
- Traumatic: actual or threatened death, serious injury or sexual violence.

Stress is not itself a psychiatric diagnosis. A stress response may be adaptive, but severe or prolonged stress can contribute to anxiety, depression, trauma-related symptoms and physical illness.

### 1.4 Normal anxiety versus an anxiety disorder

Normal anxiety is usually proportionate to the situation, time-limited and does not substantially impair functioning. An anxiety disorder is more likely when anxiety is excessive, persistent, difficult to control, associated with avoidance or safety behaviours, and interferes with education, work, relationships or self-care.

Do not diagnose an anxiety disorder simply because a patient is worried. Establish the pattern, duration, severity, impairment, context and exclusions.

## 2. Neurobiology and physiology of anxiety and stress

### 2.1 The fear response

![Threat detection and the physiological fear response](https://res.cloudinary.com/l4wbzpfr/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto,w_1200/v1793772365/ompathstudy/year-4/psychiatry/anxiety-stress-disorders/anxiety-fear-response.svg)

1. A potentially threatening stimulus is processed through sensory pathways.
2. The **amygdala** helps detect emotional salience and coordinate fear responses.
3. The hypothalamus and brainstem activate autonomic and endocrine responses.
4. The sympathetic nervous system increases alertness and prepares the body for fight, flight or freezing.
5. The prefrontal cortex and hippocampus contribute to appraisal, context and regulation of the response.

### 2.2 Autonomic nervous system response

Sympathetic activation and catecholamine release produce:

- Increased heart rate and force of contraction → palpitations.
- Peripheral vasoconstriction and redistribution of blood flow.
- Increased respiratory rate → breathlessness or chest tightness.
- Sweating and tremor.
- Pupil dilation.
- Reduced gastrointestinal activity → nausea, abdominal discomfort or dry mouth.
- Increased vigilance and startle response.

The parasympathetic system helps restore baseline activity after the threat has passed.

### 2.3 Hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis

![HPA axis: hypothalamus, pituitary, adrenal cortex and cortisol](https://res.cloudinary.com/l4wbzpfr/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto,w_1200/v1793772393/ompathstudy/year-4/psychiatry/anxiety-stress-disorders/stress-response-hpa-axis.svg)

The HPA axis is a major endocrine pathway involved in the stress response:

1. Hypothalamus releases corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH).
2. CRH stimulates the anterior pituitary to release adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH).
3. ACTH stimulates the adrenal cortex to release cortisol.
4. Cortisol helps mobilise energy and modulate immune and metabolic responses.
5. Cortisol exerts negative feedback on the hypothalamus and pituitary.

Chronic stress may be associated with dysregulation of stress-response systems, sleep disturbance and changes in emotional processing. There is no single cortisol test that diagnoses an anxiety disorder.

### 2.4 Neurotransmitters and brain circuits

- **GABA:** the major inhibitory neurotransmitter; reduced inhibitory signalling may contribute to heightened neuronal excitability and anxiety. Benzodiazepines enhance GABA-A receptor activity.
- **Serotonin:** involved in mood, anxiety, impulse regulation and threat processing; SSRIs are commonly used in anxiety disorders.
- **Noradrenaline:** contributes to arousal, vigilance and autonomic symptoms.
- **Glutamate:** the major excitatory neurotransmitter; involved in fear learning and extinction.
- **Amygdala:** threat detection and emotional learning.
- **Prefrontal cortex:** appraisal and top-down regulation of emotional responses.
- **Hippocampus:** contextual memory and discrimination between safe and threatening environments.

These systems interact; anxiety disorders are not caused by a single neurotransmitter deficiency.

## 3. Generalised anxiety disorder (GAD)

### 3.1 Definition

**Generalised anxiety disorder** is characterised by excessive, persistent and difficult-to-control worry about multiple areas of everyday life, accompanied by psychological and physical symptoms and clinically significant distress or impairment.

The worry is often described as being about many things at once: health, family, finances, relationships, safety, work or academic performance.

### 3.2 Clinical features

**Psychological symptoms**

- Excessive worry on most days.
- Feeling on edge or unable to relax.
- Irritability.
- Poor concentration or mind going blank.
- Anticipating the worst.
- Difficulty tolerating uncertainty.
- Feeling overwhelmed by routine decisions.

**Physical symptoms**

- Muscle tension, commonly involving the neck, shoulders or jaw.
- Restlessness.
- Fatigue.
- Sleep disturbance, including difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep.
- Palpitations.
- Sweating or tremor.
- Headache.
- Gastrointestinal discomfort.

### 3.3 Diagnostic criteria: DSM-5-TR essentials

- Excessive anxiety and worry about several events or activities, occurring more days than not for at least **six months**.
- The individual finds it difficult to control the worry.
- In adults, at least **three** of the following six symptoms; in children, at least one:
  1. Restlessness or feeling keyed up/on edge.
  2. Being easily fatigued.
  3. Difficulty concentrating or mind going blank.
  4. Irritability.
  5. Muscle tension.
  6. Sleep disturbance.
- Symptoms cause clinically significant distress or impairment.
- Not attributable to a substance, medication or medical condition.
- Not better explained by another mental disorder.

**ICD-11 note:** ICD-11 describes persistent anxiety for at least several months, occurring on more days than not, with apprehension and associated symptoms. Use the diagnostic system specified in the question.

### 3.4 Aetiology and risk factors

- Family history and genetic vulnerability.
- Temperamental factors such as behavioural inhibition and negative affectivity.
- Childhood adversity or chronic stress.
- Ongoing financial, occupational, relationship or academic difficulties.
- Chronic physical illness and persistent pain.
- Excessive caffeine, stimulants and some medications.
- Coexisting depression or other anxiety disorders.

### 3.5 Differential diagnosis

- Hyperthyroidism and other endocrine disorders.
- Arrhythmias and other cardiac disease.
- Asthma and respiratory disorders.
- Hypoglycaemia.
- Anaemia.
- Substance or medication effects, including caffeine, stimulants and withdrawal states.
- Depression, panic disorder, OCD and PTSD.
- Normal worry related to a current life stressor.

### 3.6 Management

**Psychological treatment**

- Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT): identifies unhelpful thinking patterns, reduces avoidance and develops coping skills.
- Applied relaxation and structured relaxation strategies.
- Psychoeducation about anxiety and the physical stress response.
- Problem-solving and behavioural strategies.
- Sleep hygiene and regular physical activity as supportive measures.

**Pharmacological treatment**

- SSRIs are commonly first-line medicines when medication is indicated; examples include sertraline and escitalopram.
- SNRIs such as venlafaxine or duloxetine are alternatives depending on patient factors and local guidance.
- Start at an appropriate dose and titrate according to response and tolerability.
- Explain that benefit is usually gradual over several weeks; monitor early adverse effects, activation and suicidal thinking, especially in younger patients.
- Benzodiazepines are generally not routine long-term treatment because of tolerance, dependence, withdrawal and cognitive impairment. If used in exceptional circumstances, use the lowest effective dose for the shortest possible period with a clear plan.
- Review response, adherence, adverse effects and comorbidities.

**Lifestyle and supportive interventions**

- Reduce excessive caffeine and stimulant use.
- Maintain a regular sleep-wake schedule.
- Encourage regular aerobic activity.
- Address alcohol or substance use.
- Strengthen social support and practical problem-solving.

### 3.7 Complications

- Major depressive disorder.
- Insomnia and fatigue.
- Impaired academic or occupational performance.
- Relationship difficulties and social withdrawal.
- Increased healthcare utilisation.
- Alcohol or sedative misuse in attempts to self-medicate.

## 4. Panic attacks and panic disorder

### 4.1 Panic attack: definition

A **panic attack** is an abrupt surge of intense fear or discomfort that reaches a peak within minutes, accompanied by physical and cognitive symptoms.

A panic attack is a clinical phenomenon, not automatically a diagnosis of panic disorder. It may occur in panic disorder, phobias, PTSD, depression, substance-related states and medical illnesses.

### 4.2 Symptoms of a panic attack

DSM-5-TR lists 13 symptoms. A panic attack involves at least four:

1. Palpitations, pounding heart or accelerated heart rate.
2. Sweating.
3. Trembling or shaking.
4. Sensations of shortness of breath.
5. Feeling of choking.
6. Chest pain or discomfort.
7. Nausea or abdominal distress.
8. Dizziness, unsteadiness, light-headedness or faintness.
9. Chills or heat sensations.
10. Paresthesias (numbness or tingling).
11. Derealisation (feeling that surroundings are unreal) or depersonalisation (feeling detached from oneself).
12. Fear of losing control or “going crazy”.
13. Fear of dying.

### 4.3 Panic cycle

![Cognitive-behavioural panic cycle](https://res.cloudinary.com/l4wbzpfr/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto,w_1200/v1793772378/ompathstudy/year-4/psychiatry/anxiety-stress-disorders/panic-cycle.svg)

A common maintaining cycle is:

1. A bodily sensation occurs, such as palpitations.
2. The sensation is interpreted catastrophically: “I am having a heart attack.”
3. Fear increases sympathetic activation.
4. Palpitations, breathlessness and dizziness intensify.
5. The patient escapes, avoids situations or relies on safety behaviours.
6. Avoidance prevents corrective learning and maintains fear.

### 4.4 Panic disorder

**Definition:** Recurrent unexpected panic attacks followed by persistent concern about further attacks or their consequences, and/or maladaptive behavioural change.

**DSM-5-TR essentials**

- Recurrent unexpected panic attacks.
- At least one attack is followed by **one month or more** of:
  - Persistent concern or worry about additional attacks or their consequences; and/or
  - Significant maladaptive change in behaviour related to the attacks, such as avoidance of exercise or unfamiliar situations.
- Not attributable to a substance or medical condition.
- Not better explained by another mental disorder.

### 4.5 Clinical presentation

- Sudden intense fear, often with a feeling of impending death.
- Palpitations, chest discomfort, sweating and tremor.
- Breathlessness, choking sensation or dizziness.
- Fear of losing control or fainting.
- Patients may attend emergency departments because symptoms resemble myocardial infarction or respiratory disease.
- Between attacks, some patients develop anticipatory anxiety and avoidance.

### 4.6 Differential diagnosis: important medical exclusions

Do not assume chest pain or breathlessness is psychiatric.

- Acute coronary syndrome and arrhythmias.
- Asthma, pulmonary embolism and other respiratory disease where clinically indicated.
- Hypoglycaemia.
- Hyperthyroidism.
- Seizure disorders.
- Anaemia.
- Phaeochromocytoma in a clinically suggestive presentation.
- Caffeine, cocaine, amphetamine or other stimulant intoxication.
- Alcohol or sedative withdrawal.

Investigations depend on the presentation and may include ECG, blood glucose, full blood count, thyroid tests, electrolytes and other targeted tests. A normal test does not itself prove panic disorder.

### 4.7 Management

**During an acute attack**

- Assess ABCDE and vital signs if this is a first, severe or atypical presentation.
- Exclude urgent medical causes based on history and examination.
- Stay calm, explain that the symptoms can occur during intense fear and provide reassurance without dismissing the experience.
- Encourage slow, comfortable breathing; avoid forceful repeated deep breaths or paper-bag rebreathing.
- Reduce environmental stimulation and remain with the patient until symptoms settle.
- Avoid repeatedly reinforcing emergency attendance once appropriate medical assessment has been completed; establish a planned follow-up pathway.

**Long-term treatment**

- CBT, including cognitive restructuring and interoceptive exposure where appropriate.
- SSRIs are commonly first-line pharmacological treatment for recurrent panic disorder.
- SNRIs may be alternatives.
- Benzodiazepines are not preferred for routine long-term management.
- Explain that temporary early activation can occur with antidepressants; gradual initiation may improve tolerability.
- Reduce caffeine and stimulant exposure.
- Address agoraphobic avoidance and restore normal activities gradually.

## 5. Phobic disorders

### 5.1 Definition

A **phobia** is a marked, persistent and disproportionate fear of a specific object or situation, leading to anxiety and avoidance or endurance with intense distress.

The fear is generally out of proportion to the actual danger and interferes with functioning.

### 5.2 Specific phobia

**Definition:** Marked fear or anxiety about a specific object or situation, such as an animal, natural environment, blood, injection, injury or particular situation.

**Common types and examples**

- **Animal type:** dogs, snakes, spiders or insects.
- **Natural environment type:** heights, storms, water or darkness.
- **Blood-injection-injury type:** blood, needles, injections or invasive procedures.
- **Situational type:** flying, lifts, tunnels or enclosed spaces.
- **Other type:** choking, vomiting or other specific fears.

**Clinical features**

- Immediate anxiety when exposed to the feared stimulus.
- Active avoidance or endurance with intense distress.
- Fear is disproportionate to actual danger and sociocultural context.
- Symptoms typically persist for at least six months under DSM-5-TR.
- Causes significant distress or impairment.

**Blood-injection-injury phobia:** Some patients experience a vasovagal response with bradycardia and hypotension, rather than only sympathetic arousal. Applied muscle tension can help prevent fainting during exposure.

### 5.3 Agoraphobia

Agoraphobia is marked fear or anxiety about situations where escape might be difficult or help unavailable if panic-like or other incapacitating symptoms occur.

Common feared situations include:

- Public transport.
- Open spaces.
- Enclosed spaces.
- Queues or crowds.
- Being outside the home alone.

DSM-5-TR requires fear or anxiety about at least **two** of these five situation categories, usually for six months or more, with avoidance, need for a companion or intense distress.

Agoraphobia can occur with or without panic disorder. It is not simply a fear of open spaces.

### 5.4 Management of phobic disorders

- CBT with graded exposure is a central treatment.
- Systematic desensitisation combines gradual exposure with relaxation strategies.
- Exposure should be planned collaboratively and should progress at a tolerable pace.
- Cognitive interventions address exaggerated danger predictions.
- Medication is not routinely required for isolated specific phobia; treat significant comorbid anxiety or depression where present.

## 6. Social anxiety disorder (social phobia)

### 6.1 Definition

Social anxiety disorder is marked fear or anxiety about one or more social situations in which the person may be scrutinised, judged, embarrassed or negatively evaluated by others.

### 6.2 Clinical features

- Fear of speaking, eating, writing or performing in front of others.
- Fear of appearing anxious, foolish, incompetent or embarrassing oneself.
- Avoidance of presentations, meetings, social gatherings or unfamiliar people.
- Physical symptoms may include blushing, sweating, tremor, palpitations, nausea and dry mouth.
- Anticipatory anxiety before social events.
- Post-event rumination about perceived mistakes.
- Functional impairment in education, employment and relationships.

### 6.3 Diagnosis: DSM-5-TR essentials

- Marked fear or anxiety about social situations involving possible scrutiny.
- Fear of negative evaluation.
- Social situations almost always provoke fear or anxiety.
- Situations are avoided or endured with intense fear.
- Fear is out of proportion to actual threat and sociocultural context.
- Persistent, typically six months or more.
- Causes significant distress or impairment.
- Not due to substances, medical conditions or another mental disorder.

**Performance-only specifier:** Fear is restricted to speaking or performing in public.

### 6.4 Differential diagnosis

- Normal shyness or stage fright without significant impairment.
- Avoidant personality disorder.
- Autism spectrum disorder.
- Panic disorder.
- Depression.
- Stuttering or other communication disorders.
- Body dysmorphic disorder when concern is focused on perceived appearance defects.

### 6.5 Management

- Individual CBT specifically designed for social anxiety.
- Graduated exposure to feared social situations.
- Cognitive restructuring of negative evaluation beliefs.
- SSRIs or SNRIs when symptoms are moderate to severe, persistent or when psychological treatment alone is insufficient.
- Group CBT may be helpful for some patients.
- Address alcohol or substance use used to cope with social situations.

## 7. Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD)

### 7.1 Classification and definition

**Obsessive-compulsive disorder** is characterised by obsessions, compulsions or both, causing significant distress, consuming substantial time or impairing functioning.

Important classification distinction:

- In DSM-5-TR, OCD is classified under **Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders**, not within the Anxiety Disorders chapter.
- It is often taught alongside anxiety disorders because obsessions commonly produce anxiety and compulsions may temporarily reduce distress.
- ICD-11 also classifies OCD separately from anxiety or fear-related disorders.

### 7.2 Obsessions

Obsessions are recurrent and persistent thoughts, urges or images experienced as intrusive and unwanted, usually causing anxiety or distress.

Examples:

- Fear of contamination by germs or bodily fluids.
- Repeated doubts about whether a door is locked or an appliance is switched off.
- Fear of harming someone despite not wanting to do so.
- Intrusive blasphemous, sexual or aggressive thoughts.
- Excessive concern with symmetry, exactness or things feeling “just right”.

The person often attempts to ignore, suppress or neutralise the thoughts.

### 7.3 Compulsions

Compulsions are repetitive behaviours or mental acts that the person feels driven to perform in response to an obsession or according to rigid rules.

Examples:

- Repeated handwashing or cleaning.
- Checking locks, switches or appliances.
- Repeated counting.
- Ordering and arranging objects.
- Repeating words or prayers silently.
- Seeking reassurance repeatedly.
- Mental reviewing or neutralising thoughts.

The behaviour is intended to reduce distress or prevent a feared event, but it is excessive or not realistically connected to preventing the event.

### 7.4 OCD cycle

![Obsessions, anxiety, compulsions and temporary relief in OCD](https://res.cloudinary.com/l4wbzpfr/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto,w_1200/v1793772384/ompathstudy/year-4/psychiatry/anxiety-stress-disorders/ocd-cycle.svg)

- Obsession triggers anxiety or distress.
- The patient performs a compulsion or mental ritual.
- Anxiety temporarily decreases.
- Temporary relief negatively reinforces the compulsion.
- The obsession-compulsion cycle is repeated.

### 7.5 Common clinical themes

- Contamination and cleaning.
- Checking and doubt.
- Symmetry, ordering and arranging.
- Forbidden or taboo thoughts.
- Harm-related obsessions.
- Repeating and counting.
- Hoarding is a separate diagnosis in DSM-5-TR, although it may coexist with OCD.

### 7.6 Diagnosis

DSM-5-TR requires obsessions, compulsions or both. They are time-consuming (for example, more than one hour per day) or cause clinically significant distress or impairment.

Symptoms must not be attributable to a substance or medical condition and must not be better explained by another mental disorder.

Assess insight:

- Good or fair insight: the person recognises that OCD beliefs are probably or definitely not true, or may or may not be true.
- Poor insight: the person thinks OCD beliefs are probably true.
- Absent insight/delusional beliefs: the person is completely convinced the beliefs are true.

### 7.7 Differential diagnosis

- Generalised anxiety disorder: broad real-life worries rather than recurrent intrusive obsessions and ritualised neutralisation.
- Obsessive-compulsive personality disorder: pervasive perfectionism, orderliness and control, often experienced as appropriate by the person; not defined by intrusive obsessions and compulsions.
- Psychotic disorders: fixed delusions and impaired reality testing; OCD thoughts are commonly experienced as intrusive, although insight can be poor or absent.
- Depression: rumination and guilt without characteristic compulsions.
- Autism spectrum disorder: repetitive behaviours and routines, often with different functions and developmental context.
- Tic disorders and body-focused repetitive behaviour disorders.

### 7.8 Management

**Psychological treatment**

- CBT with **exposure and response prevention (ERP)** is a first-line psychological intervention.
- Exposure involves gradual, planned contact with feared thoughts, objects or situations.
- Response prevention means resisting the compulsion or ritual.
- Psychoeducation and family involvement may help reduce reassurance cycles and accommodation.

**Medication**

- SSRIs are commonly used; treatment may require an adequate dose and longer trial than for depression.
- Clomipramine, a tricyclic antidepressant with strong serotonergic activity, is an alternative in selected cases, but has a less favourable adverse-effect profile and requires careful prescribing.
- Monitor response, adherence, adverse effects and suicide risk.
- Severe or treatment-resistant OCD may require specialist review and combined treatment.

## 8. Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)

### 8.1 Definition

**Post-traumatic stress disorder** is a trauma-related disorder that may develop after exposure to an extremely threatening or horrific event, with persistent symptoms involving re-experiencing, avoidance, altered cognition or mood, and heightened arousal or reactivity.

Examples of qualifying traumatic exposure may include:

- Actual or threatened death.
- Serious injury.
- Sexual violence.
- Severe accidents.
- Physical or sexual assault.
- War, torture or other extreme violence.
- Direct exposure, witnessing, learning of trauma affecting a close person in qualifying circumstances, or repeated occupational exposure to traumatic details.

Not every stressful or upsetting event meets the DSM-5-TR trauma exposure criterion.

### 8.2 PTSD symptom clusters

![The four core DSM-5-TR PTSD symptom clusters](https://res.cloudinary.com/l4wbzpfr/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto,w_1200/v1793772415/ompathstudy/year-4/psychiatry/anxiety-stress-disorders/ptsd-symptom-clusters.svg)

**A. Intrusion symptoms**

- Recurrent involuntary distressing memories.
- Recurrent distressing dreams related to the trauma.
- Dissociative reactions or flashbacks.
- Intense psychological distress when exposed to trauma reminders.
- Physiological reactions to reminders.

**B. Avoidance**

- Avoiding trauma-related thoughts, feelings or memories.
- Avoiding people, places, conversations, activities or situations associated with the trauma.

**C. Negative alterations in cognition and mood**

- Inability to remember important aspects of the trauma (dissociative amnesia).
- Persistent negative beliefs about oneself, others or the world.
- Persistent blame of self or others.
- Persistent negative emotional state, such as fear, anger, guilt or shame.
- Reduced interest in activities.
- Feeling detached or estranged from others.
- Difficulty experiencing positive emotions.

**D. Alterations in arousal and reactivity**

- Irritability or angry outbursts.
- Reckless or self-destructive behaviour.
- Hypervigilance.
- Exaggerated startle response.
- Problems with concentration.
- Sleep disturbance.

### 8.3 DSM-5-TR diagnostic essentials

- Exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury or sexual violence.
- At least one intrusion symptom.
- At least one avoidance symptom.
- At least two negative cognition/mood symptoms.
- At least two arousal/reactivity symptoms.
- Symptoms persist for **more than one month**.
- Clinically significant distress or impairment.
- Not attributable to a substance or another medical condition.

**Specifiers**

- With dissociative symptoms: depersonalisation and/or derealisation.
- With delayed expression: full diagnostic criteria are not met until at least six months after the event, although some symptoms may begin earlier.

### 8.4 Clinical assessment

- Ask about the event sensitively; do not pressure the patient to provide unnecessary graphic details.
- Establish onset, duration and course of symptoms.
- Ask about nightmares, flashbacks, intrusive memories and triggers.
- Assess avoidance, hypervigilance, sleep, concentration and emotional numbing.
- Ask about depression, guilt, shame, substance use and dissociation.
- Assess suicide risk, self-harm, interpersonal safety and safeguarding.
- Consider cultural meanings of trauma and available social support.

### 8.5 Differential diagnosis

- Acute stress disorder or acute stress reaction.
- Adjustment disorder.
- Depression.
- Panic disorder and other anxiety disorders.
- Dissociative disorders.
- Traumatic brain injury.
- Substance-related disorders.
- Sleep disorders and medical conditions.

### 8.6 Management

**Psychological treatment**

- Trauma-focused CBT is a major evidence-based treatment.
- Cognitive processing therapy and prolonged exposure are structured trauma-focused approaches.
- Eye movement desensitisation and reprocessing (EMDR) is an evidence-based option for appropriately selected patients.
- Supportive therapy and psychoeducation may help, but should not replace trauma-focused treatment when indicated.
- Do not force detailed recounting of trauma or assume that compulsory single-session debriefing prevents PTSD.

**Medication**

- SSRIs such as sertraline or paroxetine may be considered for persistent PTSD symptoms according to local guidance and patient preference.
- Venlafaxine is another option in some guidelines.
- Medication may target specific symptoms or comorbid depression and anxiety.
- Benzodiazepines are generally not recommended as routine PTSD treatment because of dependence, adverse effects and concern that they may interfere with recovery.
- Review sleep, substance use, adverse effects and suicide risk.

**Supportive and practical care**

- Establish safety and stable housing where possible.
- Support social connection and practical needs.
- Address ongoing exposure to violence or threat.
- Treat comorbid depression, pain, substance use and physical illness.
- Plan follow-up and monitor function.

### 8.7 Complications

- Major depression and suicide risk.
- Substance use disorders.
- Chronic insomnia and nightmares.
- Relationship and occupational difficulties.
- Chronic pain and other physical symptoms.
- Dissociative symptoms.
- Reduced quality of life.

## 9. Acute stress reaction and acute stress disorder

These terms are related but are not interchangeable across diagnostic systems.

### 9.1 Acute stress reaction (ICD concept)

Acute stress reaction is a transient response to an exceptionally threatening or horrific stressor. Symptoms may include:

- Initial daze, confusion or reduced attention.
- Anxiety, anger or despair.
- Autonomic arousal.
- Withdrawal or agitation.
- Reduced responsiveness.
- In some cases, dissociative symptoms.

The reaction typically begins within hours or days and often starts to resolve within days. It is not automatically a mental disorder and should not be labelled PTSD simply because symptoms occur soon after trauma.

**Management**

- Ensure immediate physical safety and address urgent injuries or medical needs.
- Provide calm, practical support and psychological first aid.
- Re-establish contact with trusted supports where appropriate.
- Address shelter, food, protection and other immediate needs.
- Monitor symptoms and functioning.
- Arrange further assessment if symptoms persist, worsen or cause significant impairment.
- Avoid compulsory psychological debriefing or forcing detailed retelling.

### 9.2 Acute stress disorder (DSM-5-TR)

Acute stress disorder is a trauma-related disorder occurring after qualifying trauma exposure, with symptoms lasting from **three days to one month** after the event.

DSM-5-TR requires at least nine symptoms from any of the five categories:

- Intrusion.
- Negative mood.
- Dissociation.
- Avoidance.
- Arousal.

Symptoms cause significant distress or impairment and are not due to substances or another medical condition.

If symptoms persist beyond one month and full criteria are met, assess for PTSD.

### 9.3 Acute stress reaction versus acute stress disorder versus PTSD

| Feature | Acute stress reaction (ICD concept) | Acute stress disorder (DSM-5-TR) | PTSD |
|---|---|---|---|
| Onset | Soon after extreme stress | After qualifying trauma | After qualifying trauma |
| Duration | Usually transient, resolving over days | 3 days to 1 month | More than 1 month |
| Symptom pattern | Variable immediate stress response | At least 9 symptoms across 5 categories | Specific intrusion, avoidance, cognition/mood and arousal clusters |
| Classification | Stress reaction, not necessarily a mental disorder | Trauma- and stressor-related disorder | Trauma- and stressor-related disorder |
| Main approach | Safety, practical support, monitoring | Support and evidence-based treatment if indicated | Trauma-focused treatment and/or selected medication |

## 10. Adjustment disorders

### 10.1 Definition

Adjustment disorder is a maladaptive emotional or behavioural response to an identifiable psychosocial stressor, with distress or impairment that is clinically significant and not better explained by another mental disorder.

The stressor does not need to be traumatic in the PTSD sense.

Examples include:

- Relationship breakdown.
- Bereavement-related difficulties when the response exceeds expected cultural norms.
- Loss of employment.
- Financial hardship.
- Academic failure or major examination stress.
- Serious physical illness.
- Migration or major life transition.
- Family conflict.

### 10.2 Clinical features

- Persistent preoccupation with the stressor or its consequences.
- Excessive worry or recurrent distressing thoughts about the event.
- Difficulty adapting to changed circumstances.
- Low mood, anxiety or mixed emotional symptoms.
- Behavioural changes, conflict, absenteeism or reduced performance.
- Impairment in social, academic or occupational functioning.

### 10.3 Diagnostic timing

**DSM-5-TR**

- Emotional or behavioural symptoms develop within **three months** of the onset of the stressor.
- Distress is out of proportion to the severity or intensity of the stressor, taking account of context and culture, and/or there is significant impairment.
- Symptoms do not meet criteria for another mental disorder and are not merely an exacerbation of a pre-existing disorder.
- Once the stressor or its consequences have ended, symptoms do not persist for more than an additional six months.

**ICD-11**

- Symptoms typically develop within **one month** of the stressor.
- Core features include preoccupation with the stressor and failure to adapt.
- Symptoms usually resolve within six months unless the stressor persists longer.

Always identify which classification system the examination is using.

### 10.4 Subtypes in DSM-5-TR

- With depressed mood.
- With anxiety.
- With mixed anxiety and depressed mood.
- With disturbance of conduct.
- With mixed disturbance of emotions and conduct.
- Unspecified.

### 10.5 Differential diagnosis

- Major depressive disorder.
- GAD and other anxiety disorders.
- PTSD and acute stress disorder.
- Normal stress response.
- Prolonged grief disorder.
- Substance-related disorders.
- Medical conditions contributing to emotional or behavioural symptoms.

### 10.6 Management

- Identify and address the stressor where possible.
- Supportive psychotherapy and problem-solving therapy.
- CBT for maladaptive thoughts and behaviours.
- Strengthen coping skills and social support.
- Practical interventions: housing, finances, education, employment or safeguarding support.
- Medication is not routinely required for adjustment disorder itself; treat a clearly established comorbid disorder when indicated.
- Monitor symptoms, functioning and suicide risk.

## 11. Comparing the major disorders

| Disorder | Core pattern | Key duration / distinction |
|---|---|---|
| GAD | Excessive worry across several life areas | DSM-5-TR: at least 6 months |
| Panic disorder | Recurrent unexpected panic attacks plus persistent concern or behaviour change | At least 1 month after an attack |
| Specific phobia | Fear of a particular object or situation | Usually at least 6 months in DSM-5-TR |
| Agoraphobia | Fear of situations where escape/help may be difficult | At least 2 of 5 situation categories; usually 6 months |
| Social anxiety disorder | Fear of scrutiny or negative evaluation | Usually at least 6 months |
| OCD | Obsessions and/or compulsions causing distress, time burden or impairment | More than 1 hour/day is one example, not an absolute requirement |
| Acute stress disorder | Trauma-related symptoms across 5 categories | 3 days to 1 month |
| PTSD | Intrusion, avoidance, negative cognition/mood and arousal after trauma | More than 1 month |
| Adjustment disorder | Maladaptive response to identifiable psychosocial stressor | DSM onset within 3 months; ICD-11 usually within 1 month |

## 12. Psychiatric history and mental state examination

### 12.1 History of presenting illness

Establish:

- Main complaint and the patient's own explanation.
- Onset: sudden or gradual.
- Duration, frequency and progression.
- Triggering events or stressors.
- Situations in which symptoms occur.
- Physical symptoms and associated thoughts.
- Avoidance, reassurance seeking and safety behaviours.
- Sleep, appetite, energy and concentration.
- Effect on education, work, relationships and self-care.
- Previous episodes and treatment response.

### 12.2 Relevant psychiatric and medical history

- Previous anxiety, depression, OCD, PTSD or other psychiatric illness.
- Previous self-harm or suicide attempts.
- Family history of mental illness.
- Medical illness, especially cardiac, respiratory, endocrine and neurological disease.
- Current medication, over-the-counter drugs and herbal products.
- Caffeine, nicotine, alcohol and recreational substance use.
- Trauma history, assessed sensitively and only as clinically necessary.
- Developmental, family, educational, occupational and social history.

### 12.3 Mental State Examination

| Domain | What to assess |
|---|---|
| Appearance and behaviour | Grooming, restlessness, fidgeting, avoidance, eye contact, cooperation |
| Speech | Rate, volume, fluency and spontaneity |
| Mood | Subjective anxiety, fear, sadness, irritability or hopelessness |
| Affect | Observed tension, range, reactivity and congruence |
| Thought form | Coherence, flow, rumination and thought blocking |
| Thought content | Worries, obsessions, guilt, hopelessness, trauma-related beliefs, suicidal thoughts |
| Perception | Panic-related derealisation/depersonalisation, flashbacks or other perceptual symptoms |
| Cognition | Attention, concentration, orientation and memory |
| Insight | Understanding of symptoms, triggers and need for help |
| Judgement | Decisions, coping and ability to maintain safety |

Anxiety may cause poor concentration without disorientation. New disorientation or fluctuating attention should prompt assessment for delirium or another medical cause.

### 12.4 Risk assessment

Ask directly and sensitively about:

- Suicidal thoughts, intent, plan, access to means and previous attempts.
- Self-harm and reasons for self-injury.
- Hopelessness, severe depression and agitation.
- Alcohol or drug misuse.
- Risk arising from trauma, domestic violence or ongoing threat.
- Self-neglect and ability to meet basic needs.
- Risk to children or other dependants.
- Protective factors: supportive relationships, coping strategies, responsibilities and willingness to seek help.

A patient with anxiety is not automatically at low suicide risk. Risk is influenced by comorbid depression, trauma, substance use, hopelessness and previous self-harm.

## 13. Differential diagnosis of anxiety symptoms

Anxiety-like symptoms may be the first presentation of a physical illness. Take a medical history and perform a physical examination guided by the presentation.

### 13.1 Cardiovascular

- Arrhythmias, including supraventricular tachycardia.
- Acute coronary syndrome.
- Heart failure when breathlessness and other features suggest it.

### 13.2 Respiratory

- Asthma.
- Pulmonary embolism.
- Hypoxia and other causes of respiratory distress.

### 13.3 Endocrine and metabolic

- Hyperthyroidism.
- Hypoglycaemia.
- Phaeochromocytoma in a suggestive clinical presentation.
- Electrolyte disturbance.
- Anaemia and other causes of fatigue or palpitations.

### 13.4 Neurological

- Seizure disorders.
- Vestibular disorders.
- Migraine.
- Traumatic brain injury.
- Other neurological conditions causing autonomic or perceptual symptoms.

### 13.5 Substance and medication effects

- Caffeine and energy drinks.
- Nicotine.
- Cocaine and amphetamines.
- Cannabis in some patients.
- Alcohol withdrawal.
- Benzodiazepine withdrawal.
- Decongestants and sympathomimetics.
- Some corticosteroids, thyroid preparations and other medicines.

### 13.6 Other psychiatric conditions

- Major depressive disorder.
- Bipolar disorder.
- Psychotic disorders.
- Somatic symptom disorder.
- Illness anxiety disorder.
- Eating disorders.
- Substance use disorders.
- Personality disorders and neurodevelopmental conditions.

## 14. Investigations

There is no routine laboratory test that confirms GAD, panic disorder, phobia, OCD or PTSD. Investigations are selected to exclude medical mimics, assess comorbidity and establish baseline health where medication is being considered.

Depending on history and examination:

- Full blood count: anaemia or other haematological causes.
- Blood glucose: hypoglycaemia or hyperglycaemia.
- Thyroid function tests: suspected thyroid dysfunction.
- Urea, electrolytes and creatinine: metabolic or renal contributors.
- ECG: palpitations, chest pain, syncope or relevant medication risk.
- Toxicology testing: only when clinically appropriate and interpreted with the history.
- Pregnancy testing where relevant to treatment decisions.
- Additional cardiac, respiratory, neurological or endocrine tests according to red flags.

Do not order broad investigations indiscriminately. Explain the reason for each test and interpret results in clinical context.

## 15. General principles of management

### 15.1 Assessment and shared decision-making

- Establish the diagnosis and assess severity and functional impairment.
- Identify comorbid depression, substance use, trauma and physical illness.
- Ask what the patient wants help with and discuss treatment choices.
- Explain expected benefits, limitations, adverse effects and time to response.
- Agree on measurable treatment goals and follow-up.

### 15.2 Psychological therapies

**Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT)**

- Identifies links between thoughts, feelings, physical symptoms and behaviour.
- Challenges unhelpful predictions and beliefs.
- Reduces avoidance and safety behaviours.
- Builds coping and problem-solving skills.

**Exposure therapy**

- Gradual, planned contact with feared situations or sensations.
- Allows new learning that the feared outcome is less likely or more manageable than expected.
- Used in phobias, panic disorder, social anxiety and OCD as part of appropriate protocols.

**Exposure and response prevention (ERP)**

- Specific CBT approach for OCD.
- Exposure to obsessional triggers while resisting compulsive rituals.

**Trauma-focused CBT and EMDR**

- Evidence-based trauma-focused treatments for PTSD.
- Delivered by appropriately trained clinicians.
- Therapy should be collaborative and paced to the patient's needs.

**Relaxation and mindfulness-based approaches**

- May support emotional regulation and stress management.
- They should not be presented as substitutes for disorder-specific first-line treatment when that treatment is indicated.

### 15.3 Pharmacological treatment: overview

**SSRIs**

Examples: sertraline, escitalopram, fluoxetine, paroxetine.

- Common first-line medicines across several anxiety and trauma-related disorders.
- Adverse effects may include nausea, headache, sleep disturbance, sexual dysfunction and initial activation.
- Do not stop abruptly; discontinuation symptoms can occur.
- Monitor for suicidal thinking, particularly early in treatment and after dose changes in younger people.

**SNRIs**

Examples: venlafaxine and duloxetine.

- Used for selected anxiety disorders and other indications.
- Monitor blood pressure where appropriate, adverse effects and discontinuation symptoms.

**Tricyclic antidepressants**

- Clomipramine is an option in selected OCD cases.
- Consider anticholinergic effects, cardiac toxicity in overdose and other safety issues.

**Benzodiazepines**

Examples: diazepam, lorazepam and clonazepam.

- Enhance GABA-A receptor activity.
- May provide rapid short-term relief in selected acute situations.
- Risks include sedation, impaired driving, falls, tolerance, dependence, withdrawal and respiratory depression when combined with alcohol or opioids.
- Avoid routine long-term prescribing and use only with a clear indication, duration and review plan.

**Beta-blockers**

- Propranolol may reduce peripheral autonomic symptoms in selected performance situations.
- It does not treat the core cognitive features of social anxiety or generalised anxiety.
- Consider asthma, bradycardia, hypotension and other contraindications.

Medication choice, dose and duration must follow current local guidance, patient factors and clinician review. These notes are not a prescribing protocol.

## 16. The 3 Ps: psychiatric formulation

The 3 Ps help explain why a patient developed symptoms and what may be maintaining them.

### 16.1 Predisposing factors

Factors that increase vulnerability:

- Family history of anxiety or mood disorders.
- Temperamental vulnerability, including behavioural inhibition.
- Childhood adversity or trauma.
- Previous mental illness.
- Chronic medical illness or pain.
- Limited coping resources or long-term social adversity.

### 16.2 Precipitating factors

Events that trigger the current episode:

- Bereavement or relationship breakdown.
- Academic or occupational stress.
- Financial crisis.
- Traumatic event.
- Acute illness or pain.
- Sleep deprivation.
- Increased caffeine, stimulant or substance use.

### 16.3 Perpetuating factors

Factors that maintain symptoms:

- Avoidance of feared situations.
- Reassurance seeking and safety behaviours.
- Poor sleep.
- Continued stimulant or alcohol use.
- Social isolation.
- Ongoing stressors.
- Unhelpful beliefs and rumination.
- Non-adherence or untreated comorbidity.

### 16.4 Protective factors

Although not part of the traditional 3 Ps, protective factors should also be documented:

- Supportive family and friends.
- Stable housing and financial support.
- Effective coping strategies.
- Positive therapeutic relationship.
- Cultural or community support.
- Access to treatment and willingness to engage.

**Example formulation**

A 20-year-old medical student develops persistent worry, insomnia and palpitations during a period of repeated examinations.

- Predisposing: family history of anxiety and a long-standing tendency to worry.
- Precipitating: academic pressure and reduced sleep.
- Perpetuating: excessive caffeine, catastrophic thinking, avoidance of presentations and repeated reassurance seeking.
- Protective: supportive friends, motivation to continue studies and willingness to attend treatment.

The formulation guides interventions: address sleep and caffeine, use CBT for worry and avoidance, and build practical coping strategies.

## 17. Clinical scenarios for revision

### Scenario 1: Generalised anxiety disorder

A 28-year-old has worried excessively about finances, family health and work for eight months. They report muscle tension, fatigue, irritability and poor sleep.

- Most likely diagnosis: GAD, if full criteria and exclusions are met.
- Key clue: excessive, difficult-to-control worry across several areas for at least six months under DSM-5-TR.

### Scenario 2: Panic disorder

A 24-year-old experiences repeated unexpected episodes of intense fear, palpitations, sweating, breathlessness and fear of dying. For six weeks, they have worried about another attack and stopped exercising.

- Most likely diagnosis: panic disorder, after excluding medical and substance causes.
- Key clue: recurrent unexpected attacks plus at least one month of concern or maladaptive behaviour change.

### Scenario 3: Specific phobia

A patient avoids injections because exposure produces immediate intense fear and sometimes fainting.

- Consider: blood-injection-injury type specific phobia.
- Management: graded exposure; applied muscle tension may help prevent vasovagal fainting.

### Scenario 4: Social anxiety disorder

A university student avoids presentations because they fear being judged, embarrassed or seen trembling. Symptoms have persisted for one year and affect academic performance.

- Most likely diagnosis: social anxiety disorder.
- Key clue: fear of scrutiny and negative evaluation.

### Scenario 5: OCD

A patient spends two hours daily checking locks and appliances because of intrusive fears that family members will die if something is left unsecured. Checking provides temporary relief.

- Most likely diagnosis: OCD.
- Key clue: intrusive obsession followed by repetitive compulsion that temporarily reduces distress.
- Treatment: CBT with ERP and an SSRI when indicated.

### Scenario 6: PTSD

A person involved in a serious road traffic accident continues to experience flashbacks, nightmares, avoidance of the road and hypervigilance for three months.

- Most likely diagnosis: PTSD, if full trauma exposure and symptom criteria are met.
- Key clue: trauma-related intrusion, avoidance, negative cognition/mood and arousal lasting more than one month.

### Scenario 7: Acute stress disorder

A person develops intrusive memories, dissociation, avoidance and hyperarousal five days after a serious assault.

- Consider: acute stress disorder under DSM-5-TR.
- Key clue: qualifying trauma and symptoms lasting from three days to one month.

### Scenario 8: Adjustment disorder

A patient develops anxiety and low mood within weeks of losing employment. They are struggling to adapt but do not meet criteria for major depression, GAD or PTSD.

- Consider: adjustment disorder.
- Key clue: clinically significant maladaptive response to an identifiable stressor.

### Scenario 9: Medical mimic

A patient presents with episodic palpitations, sweating, tremor, weight loss and heat intolerance.

- Consider hyperthyroidism among the differential diagnoses.
- Do not diagnose panic disorder without appropriate medical assessment.

## 18. High-yield examination points

1. Anxiety is future-oriented apprehension; fear is an immediate response to perceived danger.
2. A panic attack is a sudden surge of intense fear that peaks within minutes.
3. A panic attack is not synonymous with panic disorder.
4. DSM-5-TR GAD requires excessive worry for at least six months and at least three associated symptoms in adults.
5. DSM-5-TR panic disorder requires recurrent unexpected attacks plus at least one month of concern or maladaptive behaviour change.
6. A specific phobia involves marked fear of a specific object or situation and usually persists for at least six months.
7. Agoraphobia concerns situations where escape or help may be difficult; DSM-5-TR requires at least two of five situation categories.
8. Social anxiety disorder is driven by fear of scrutiny or negative evaluation.
9. OCD is classified separately from anxiety disorders in DSM-5-TR.
10. Obsessions are intrusive thoughts, urges or images; compulsions are repetitive behaviours or mental acts.
11. ERP is a key psychological treatment for OCD.
12. PTSD requires a qualifying traumatic exposure and specific symptom clusters lasting more than one month under DSM-5-TR.
13. Acute stress disorder lasts from three days to one month under DSM-5-TR.
14. Acute stress reaction is an immediate, usually transient stress response and should not automatically be labelled PTSD.
15. Adjustment disorder follows an identifiable psychosocial stressor and does not meet criteria for another disorder.
16. CBT is a core evidence-based treatment across several anxiety disorders.
17. SSRIs and SNRIs are commonly used pharmacological treatments; response is gradual.
18. Benzodiazepines carry risks of dependence, tolerance, withdrawal and sedation; avoid routine long-term use.
19. Always consider cardiac, respiratory, endocrine, metabolic, neurological and substance-related causes of anxiety-like symptoms.
20. Assess suicide risk, depression, substance use and functional impairment in patients with severe or persistent anxiety.
