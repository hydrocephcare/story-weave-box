# Acute kidney injury

*Clinical reasoning notes. Drug choices and thresholds follow common guidelines; always check your local protocol before prescribing.*

**The reasoning chain:** confirm it is AKI → stage it → decide where the problem is (pre-renal, renal, post-renal) → find the cause → investigate with a question for every test → look for the complications that can kill → treat the cause and support the patient.

"Acute kidney injury" is a finding, not an answer. The examiner wants to hear **what is causing it, how bad it is and what you will do about it.**

## 1. Define and confirm: KDIGO criteria

AKI is present if **any one** of these is met:

- Creatinine rise of **26.5 µmol/L (0.3 mg/dL) or more within 48 hours**
- Creatinine **1.5 times baseline or more** within the last 7 days
- Urine output **below 0.5 mL/kg/h for 6 hours**

**RIFLE** (older, still examined): **R**isk → **I**njury → **F**ailure → **L**oss → **E**nd-stage kidney disease.

## Anatomy and physiology you need  

- **Glomerulus (capillary tuft + Bow‑Bowman capsule) – filtration barrier (size, charge, endothelial fenestrations)**: Loss of glomerular filtration pressure (e.g., hypovolaemia, severe heart failure) reduces GFR → pre‑renal AKI. Damage to podocytes or basement membrane produces haematuria and proteinuria, pointing to intrinsic glomerular disease.
- **Proximal tubule (S1‑S3 segments) – bulk re‑absorption of Na⁺, glucose, amino acids, bicarbonate; high metabolic demand, rich mitochondria**: Ischaemia or nephrotoxins cause ATP depletion → loss of Na⁺/K⁺‑ATPase → cell swelling, loss of brush‑border, sloughing → muddy‑brown epithelial casts (ATN).
- **Loop of Henle (thick ascending limb) – active Na⁺‑K⁺‑2Cl⁻ transport, generates medullary osmotic gradient**: Impaired NaCl re‑absorption reduces counter‑current multiplication → inability to concentrate urine, leading to polyuria after the oliguric phase of ATN.
- **Distal tubule & collecting duct – fine‑tuning of Na⁺, K⁺, H⁺; regulated by aldosterone, ADH**: In pre‑renal states, high ADH maximises water re‑absorption; in intrinsic AKI, tubular responsiveness falls, causing a dilute urine with low specific gravity.
- **Renal blood flow (RBF) autoregulation – myogenic response + tubuloglomerular feedback (TGF) maintains GFR between MAP ≈ 80‑180 mmHg**: When MAP falls below the autoregulatory range, afferent arteriolar dilation is insufficient → ↓RBF → ↓GFR (pre‑renal). Persistent low flow leads to ischaemic ATN.
- **Ureter, renal pelvis, bladder – conduit for urine outflow, dependent on peristalsis and sphincter competence**: Obstruction anywhere raises intratubular pressure, reduces net filtration pressure, and can cause post‑renal AKI; chronic obstruction leads to hydronephrosis and loss of cortical mass.
- **Nephron‑vascular coupling – afferent/efferent arteriolar tone determines glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure (P_GC)**: Vasoconstrictors (e.g., NSAIDs inhibiting prostaglandins) increase afferent resistance → ↓P_GC → ↓GFR; ACE‑I/ARB dilate efferent arterioles → ↓P_GC, useful in some settings but can precipitate AKI when volume‑depleted.

*Exam tip*: When the examiner asks “why is the urine sodium low in pre‑renal AKI?”, cite the intact tubular Na⁺ re‑absorption driven by high aldosterone and low distal delivery, reflected in FeNa < 1 %.

## Pathophysiology  

**Ischaemic / toxic insult → ATP depletion → loss of Na⁺/K⁺‑ATPase → tubular epithelial cell swelling → loss of brush‑border → sloughing into tubular lumen → obstructive casts → increased intratubular pressure → ↓GFR → oliguria → rise in serum creatinine & urea**  

**Pre‑renal cascade**: ↓effective circulating volume → ↓ renal perfusion pressure → activation of sympathetic nervous system & RAAS → afferent vasoconstriction (NSAID block of prostaglandins worsens) → ↓ GFR → ↑ BUN/Cr ratio, FeNa < 1 %  

**Post‑renal cascade**: obstruction → ↑ hydrostatic pressure proximal to blockage → ↓ net filtration pressure → ↓ GFR → back‑leak of filtrate → hydronephrosis → progressive tubular atrophy if not relieved.  

**Systemic consequences**:  
- **Fluid overload** (↓ urine output + capillary leak) → pulmonary oedema, hypertension.  
- **Acid‑base derangement** (↓ H⁺ secretion, ↓ NH₄⁺ excretion) → metabolic acidosis.  
- **Electrolyte disturbances** (hyperkalaemia from impaired secretion, hyperphosphataemia, hypocalcaemia).  
- **Uraemic toxins** (e.g., guanidinosuccinic acid) → encephalopathy, pericarditis.  

*Examiner’s mental model*: “What started the injury? How did that translate into the laboratory pattern we see? Follow the chain from haemodynamic change → tubular injury → functional loss → clinical picture.”

## 2. Stage the severity

KDIGO stages 1 to 3 rise with the creatinine multiple (1.5×, 2×, 3× baseline or a need for dialysis) and with falling urine output. Stage 3 means dialysis-range disease. Always ask for the **baseline creatinine**: without it you cannot tell AKI from chronic kidney disease.

## 3. Localise: where is the problem?

- **Pre-renal**: Not enough blood reaches a healthy kidney — Dehydration (vomiting, diarrhoea), sepsis, haemorrhage, heart failure, cirrhosis, **NSAIDs and ACE inhibitors**
- **Renal (intrinsic)**: The kidney itself is damaged — **Acute tubular necrosis (ATN)** from ischaemia or toxins (aminoglycosides, contrast, herbal remedies); **glomerulonephritis**; **interstitial nephritis** (drugs, infection); **blackwater fever** in malaria; **rhabdomyolysis**
- **Post-renal**: Urine cannot leave — Prostate enlargement, stones, **schistosomiasis**, cervical or prostate cancer, blocked catheter

**Think local:** sepsis, malaria, HIV-related kidney disease, herbal remedies and obstruction from schistosomiasis or cervical cancer are common causes where resources are limited.

## Differential diagnosis and how to tell them apart  

| Condition | Clues that point to it | Key discriminators from AKI |
|---|---|---|
| **Pre‑renal azotaemia** | History of vomiting, diarrhoea, bleeding; BUN/Cr > 20; urine Na⁺ < 20 mmol/L; FeNa < 1 % | Rapid reversal with fluid bolus; urine osmolality > 500 mOsm/kg; no granular casts. |
| **Acute tubular necrosis (ATN)** | Ischaemic shock, nephrotoxin exposure; muddy‑brown granular casts; FeNa > 2 %; urine Na⁺ > 40 mmol/L | Persistent oliguria despite fluids; urine osmolality ≈ 300 mOsm/kg; tubular epithelial cells on microscopy. |
| **Acute interstitial nephritis (AIN)** | Recent drug (penicillins, PPIs, NSAIDs) or infection; fever, rash, eosinophilia; white‑cell casts | Eosinophiluria on Hansel stain; urine may be sterile; FeNa variable but often >1 %. |
| **Glomerulonephritis (rapidly progressive)** | Haematuria with dysmorphic RBCs, red‑cell casts, hypertension, edema; low complement (C3) | Proteinuria often >1 g/day; serology (ANCA, anti‑GBM) positive; renal biopsy shows crescents. |
| **Obstructive (post‑renal) AKI** | Flank pain, palpable bladder, history of stones, prostate enlargement, catheter blockage | Ultrasound shows hydronephrosis; urine output may be normal initially then falls; relief of obstruction rapidly improves GFR. |
| **Rhabdomyolysis‑related AKI** | Traumatic crush, intense exercise, statin toxicity; muscle pain, dark urine; CK > 5 000 U/L | Myoglobinuria (positive dipstick for blood, no RBCs); high serum potassium; FeNa often >2 % but casts are pigmented. |
| **Septic AKI (intrarenal)** | Fever, hypotension, source of infection; warm extremities early; high lactate | FeNa may be low early (functional), but progressive rise; urine may show granular casts; response to early antibiotics and source control. |

*Remember*: The examiner expects you to link the bedside story to the most likely category, then justify it with a single, high‑yield investigation (e.g., FeNa, urine microscopy, ultrasound).

## 4. Investigate with purpose

Every test must answer a question.

- **Urea, creatinine, electrolytes**: How severe is it? Is the potassium dangerous?
- **Cystatin C / eGFR**: Is there kidney damage before the creatinine has risen? (Creatinine lags behind injury.)
- **Urinalysis and urine microscopy**: **Muddy brown (epithelial) casts** point to ATN; **red cell casts** to glomerulonephritis; **white cell casts** to interstitial nephritis or pyelonephritis
- **FeNa (fractional excretion of sodium)**: Below 1% suggests pre-renal; above 2% suggests intrinsic. Unreliable on diuretics
- **Full haemogram**: Raised white cells (sepsis), anaemia, haemolysis
- **Blood cultures, malaria test**: Is there an infective cause?
- **Toxicology and drug history**: Nephrotoxins, NSAIDs, aminoglycosides, herbal remedies
- **Kidney, ureter and bladder ultrasound**: Obstruction? Kidney size: small kidneys mean chronic disease
- **Autoimmune tests: ANCA, anti-GBM, ANA, C3/C4, ASO**: Is this a glomerulonephritis?

**Where results can mislead:** the creatinine can still be **normal early in kidney damage**; FeNa is unreliable in a patient on diuretics; a patient who is oliguric may still have a "normal" urea if they are young and well-nourished.

## 5. Nephritic presentations

**Nephritic syndrome:** haematuria, hypertension, oliguria and oedema.

- **Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis (PSGN):** follows a streptococcal throat or skin infection by 1 to 3 weeks; **low C3, raised ASO**; usually self-limiting in children.
- **Rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis (RPGN):** the creatinine **keeps rising over days to weeks**; **crescents on biopsy**. Causes: **anti-GBM disease (Goodpasture)**, **ANCA vasculitis**, immune-complex disease. This is an **emergency**: untreated, it leads to kidney failure.

**Nephritic or nephrotic?** Nephritic = blood (haematuria), pressure (hypertension), little urine. Nephrotic = heavy protein loss (over 3.5 g a day), low albumin, gross oedema, high cholesterol.

## 6. Red flags: when to think dialysis

Remember **AEIOU**:

- **A**cidosis, severe and not responding to treatment
- **E**lectrolytes: **potassium above 6.5 mmol/L or ECG changes**
- **I**ntoxication (some poisonings)
- **O**verload: pulmonary oedema not responding to diuretics
- **U**raemic complications: encephalopathy, pericarditis, bleeding

## 7. Management: treat the cause, support, monitor

1. **Find and stop the cause:** treat sepsis, stop **NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors, aminoglycosides** and other nephrotoxins, relieve obstruction (catheter, nephrostomy).
2. **Fluids:** resuscitate the dehydrated patient with **balanced crystalloids** and reassess often. Give fluid to the dry; **do not push fluid into an overloaded patient**.
3. **Hyperkalaemia first:** protect the heart with **IV calcium** if there are ECG changes, shift potassium with **insulin and dextrose** (salbutamol nebulisers also help), remove it with a **diuretic or dialysis**.
4. **Monitor:** strict fluid chart, daily weight, urine output (catheter if needed), daily electrolytes.
5. **Review every drug** for dose adjustment in kidney failure.
6. **Refer** early for dialysis if the red flags above are present.

## 8. Follow-up

Check the creatinine after discharge: AKI raises the risk of **chronic kidney disease**. Advise the patient to avoid NSAIDs and herbal remedies. Tell future prescribers.

## Complications and prognosis  

- **Volume overload**: Decreased GFR → sodium and water retention; impaired natriuresis — Rising weight, bibasal crackles, peripheral oedema, pleural effusion on CXR — Increases risk of pulmonary oedema, prolongs ICU stay; a trigger for early dialysis
- **Electrolyte derangements – hyperkalaemia, metabolic acidosis, hyponatraemia**: Reduced tubular secretion (K⁺, H⁺) and impaired free water clearance — Serum K⁺ > 6 mmol/L, arterial pH < 7.2, serum Na⁺ < 130 mmol/L — Hyperkalaemia is the commonest cause of sudden cardiac arrest; severe acidosis predicts need for renal replacement therapy (RRT)
- **Uraemic encephalopathy / pericarditis**: Accumulation of nitrogenous waste products and inflammatory mediators — Altered mental status, asterixis, pericardial rub, diffuse ST‑segment changes — Marks progression to stage 3 AKI; mortality rises sharply if untreated
- **Infection (catheter‑related sepsis, pneumonia)**: Immunomodulation by uremia, loss of barrier function, invasive lines — New fever, leukocytosis, purulent drainage from catheter — Sepsis can precipitate a second hit to the kidney, converting a reversible AKI into a chronic insult
- **Acute kidney disease (AKD)**: Incomplete recovery within 7 days but >48 h after insult — Persistently rising creatinine beyond day 3, urine output still <0.5 mL/kg/h — AKD is a strong predictor of transition to chronic kidney disease (CKD); early nephrology input improves renal recovery
- **Progression to CKD / End‑stage renal disease (ESRD)**: Fibrotic remodeling after tubular necrosis, maladaptive repair — eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² at 90 days, albuminuria (ACR ≥ 30 mg/g) — Up to one‑third of severe AKI patients develop CKD; long‑term cardiovascular risk is markedly increased
- **Mortality**: Multiorgan dysfunction, refractory electrolyte/acid‑base derangement, sepsis — Rising SOFA score, refractory hypotension, need for vasopressors — Overall in‑hospital mortality for KDIGO stage 3 AKI approaches 30 % in resource‑limited settings; early recognition of complications is the key examiner focus

**Prognostic thinking for the examiner**  
1. Identify the *stage* and *aetiology* – pre‑renal, intrinsic, post‑renal.  
2. Ask: “Is there a reversible cause still present?” (e.g., ongoing obstruction, persistent nephrotoxin).  
3. Scan for *red‑flag complications* (AEIOU). Presence of any moves the candidate toward early RRT and a poorer prognosis.  
4. Mention *renal recovery trajectory*: complete, partial, or progression to CKD/ESRD, and link it to the underlying pathology (ATN → good recovery, severe glomerulonephritis → higher CKD risk).

## Clinical case  

**Case vignette**  
A 28‑year‑old male farmer presents to the district hospital with 3 days of vomiting, profuse watery diarrhoea and fever. He reports a recent bout of malaria treated with oral quinine two weeks ago. On examination: BP 90/60 mmHg, pulse 118 bpm, dry mucous membranes, reduced skin turgor, and oliguria (< 0.3 mL/kg/h). Labs: serum creatinine 280 µmol/L (baseline unknown), urea 18 mmol/L, K⁺ 6.2 mmol/L, arterial pH 7.25, urine dipstick: + protein, + blood, microscopy shows muddy brown granular casts.

### Question 1  
**What is the most likely aetiology of his AKI and which KDIGO stage does he fulfil?**  

**Model answer**  
The picture fits *pre‑renal AKI* progressing to *intrinsic ATN* secondary to severe volume depletion and possible nephrotoxic effect of quinine. The presence of granular casts indicates tubular injury, so the current state is *KDIGO stage 2* (creatinine 2–2.9 × baseline or urine output < 0.5 mL/kg/h for ≥12 h). The examiner expects you to state the transition from functional to structural injury and justify the stage using creatinine rise and oliguria.

### Question 2  
**List the immediate management priorities and the rationale for each.**  

**Model answer**  
1. **Fluid resuscitation** – isotonic balanced crystalloids 20 mL/kg bolus, then guided by MAP ≥ 65 mmHg and urine output; restores renal perfusion and may halt progression to full ATN.  
2. **Hyperkalaemia treatment** – give IV calcium gluconate (cardioprotective), insulin‑glucose infusion and nebulised salbutamol to shift K⁺ intracellularly; prevents ventricular arrhythmia.  
3. **Acidosis correction** – consider sodium bicarbonate infusion if pH < 7.1 and no contraindication; improves hemodynamics and reduces K⁺ shift.  
4. **Identify and stop nephrotoxins** – discontinue quinine, NSAIDs, and any herbal preparations; removes ongoing insult.  
5. **Monitor** – hourly urine output, daily weight, electrolytes q6 h, and repeat creatinine; detects response and early complications.  

### Question 3  
**What complications should you watch for in the next 48 hours and how would their development alter his prognosis?**  

**Model answer**  
- **Volume overload** – rising JVP, crackles, weight gain; would necessitate diuretics or early dialysis, indicating worsening renal function.  
- **Worsening hyperkalaemia or refractory acidosis** – ECG changes, pH < 7.1 despite therapy; triggers urgent RRT and signals higher mortality.  
- **Uraemic encephalopathy/pericarditis** – altered mental status, asterixis, pericardial rub; marks progression to stage 3 AKI and markedly worsens prognosis.  
- **Sepsis from catheter or secondary infection** – new fever, leukocytosis; adds a second hit, increasing risk of AKD and CKD.  

Development of any AEIOU red‑flag moves the case from conservative management to *early dialysis* and signals a poorer short‑term outcome; the examiner expects you to link each complication to both immediate management change and long‑term renal prognosis.

## Practice questions

**1.** A 45-year-old man has vomited for 3 days and is on ibuprofen. His creatinine is double its baseline, urine is concentrated and FeNa is 0.5%. The level of the problem is:
A. Post-renal · B. Glomerular · **C. Pre-renal** · D. Interstitial · E. Tubular necrosis
*Answer: C. Low FeNa and concentrated urine mean the kidney is still working hard to hold on to sodium and water.*

**2.** Urine microscopy shows red cell casts. The most likely diagnosis is:
A. Acute tubular necrosis · **B. Glomerulonephritis** · C. Interstitial nephritis · D. Pre-renal AKI · E. Urinary tract infection
*Answer: B.*

**3.** Which finding is an indication for urgent dialysis?
A. Creatinine of 200 µmol/L · B. Urine output of 0.4 mL/kg/h for 3 hours · C. Mild ankle swelling · **D. Potassium of 7.0 mmol/L with peaked T waves not responding to treatment** · E. FeNa above 2%
*Answer: D.*

**4.** A child has haematuria and hypertension two weeks after a skin infection. Complement C3 is low. The most likely diagnosis is:
A. IgA nephropathy · B. Minimal change disease · C. Anti-GBM disease · D. Lupus nephritis · **E. Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis**
*Answer: E.*
