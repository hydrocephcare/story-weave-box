*Orientation notes for study. Check local guidelines before prescribing.*


![Bronchitis (Acute and Chronic). Public domain](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/99/Bronchitis.jpg)

## Definition

**Bronchitis** is **inflammation of the bronchi**. There are two quite different conditions:

- **Acute bronchitis:** a **short, self-limiting infection** of the large airways, usually viral.
- **Chronic bronchitis:** a **clinical diagnosis**: **productive cough on most days for at least 3 months in each of 2 consecutive years**, with other causes excluded. It is a **component of COPD**.

## Anatomy and physiology you need  

| Structure / Function | Why it matters for bronchitis |
|---|---|
| **Trachea and main bronchi** – cartilaginous rings keep airway patency; mucosa lined by pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium with goblet cells. | Inflammation targets this epithelium; loss of ciliary beat and excess mucus → cough and sputum production. |
| **Submucosal mucous glands (seromucous)** – located in the larger bronchi; normal **Reid index ≈ 0.4**. | Chronic irritation causes gland hypertrophy → Reid index > 0.5, the hallmark of chronic bronchitis; explains persistent mucus hypersecretion. |
| **Bronchial smooth muscle** – innervated by autonomic (parasympathetic → constriction) and sensory nerves (cough reflex). | Viral or irritant‑mediated inflammation releases acetylcholine and neuropeptides → bronchoconstriction and cough; bronchodilators act here. |
| **Pulmonary vasculature (bronchial circulation)** – supplies airway walls; can become engorged in chronic inflammation. | Vascular engorgement contributes to airway wall thickening and may worsen ventilation‑perfusion mismatch in advanced disease. |
| **Alveolar ventilation‑perfusion (V/Q) unit** – normal matching depends on airway calibre and reflexes. | In acute bronchitis, transient airway narrowing creates mild V/Q mismatch → occasional desaturation; in chronic bronchitis, persistent narrowing and loss of elastic recoil cause chronic hypoxaemia and hypercapnia. |
| **Ciliary apparatus & mucociliary clearance** – coordinated beating moves mucus cephalad. | Infection or smoke damages cilia → impaired clearance, mucus stasis, bacterial colonisation, and exacerbations. |
| **Respiratory control centres (medulla)** – respond to CO₂ and O₂ levels. | Chronic CO₂ retention in “blue bloater” phenotype blunts ventilatory drive, leading to CO₂ narcosis if decompensated. |

## Pathophysiology  

**Acute bronchitis**  

```
Upper‑respiratory virus → infection of bronchial epithelium
   ↓
Release of cytokines (IL‑1, IL‑6, TNF‑α) → endothelial activation
   ↓
Increased vascular permeability → oedema of bronchial wall
   ↓
Mucus gland hypersecretion + impaired ciliary beat
   ↓
Airway lumen narrowed → cough, wheeze, sputum (often serous → later purulent)
   ↓
Resolution as viral clearance restores epithelium (≈ 2‑3 weeks)
```

**Chronic bronchitis**  

```
Repeated irritant exposure (tobacco, biomass) → chronic epithelial injury
   ↓
Release of growth factors (EGF, TGF‑β) → mucous gland hypertrophy & goblet cell hyperplasia
   ↓
Reid index > 0.5 → excessive mucus production
   ↓
Neutrophilic inflammation → protease release → ciliary dysfunction
   ↓
Impaired mucociliary clearance → mucus plugging & bacterial colonisation
   ↓
Airway wall thickening + smooth‑muscle hypertrophy → fixed airway narrowing
   ↓
Ventilation‑perfusion mismatch → chronic hypoxaemia, secondary polycythaemia, CO₂ retention
   ↓
Clinical picture: productive cough most days, dyspnoea on exertion, “blue bloater” phenotype
```

## Acute bronchitis

**Causes:** mostly **viruses** (rhinovirus, influenza, parainfluenza, RSV, coronavirus, adenovirus). Bacteria are uncommon (*Mycoplasma pneumoniae*, *Chlamydophila*, *Bordetella pertussis*). Irritants (smoke, dust, fumes) can contribute.

**Clinical features:**
- **Cough** (first dry, then productive, may be purulent), lasting **up to 3 weeks**; sore throat, rhinitis, low-grade fever, malaise, **chest tightness or wheeze**, mild retrosternal discomfort.
- **Signs:** scattered **rhonchi and coarse crackles that clear on coughing**; **no consolidation** signs; normal respiratory rate and saturation.

**Differentiate from pneumonia:** pneumonia has **focal signs, fever and tachypnoea, and consolidation on X-ray**. Chest X-ray is **not routine** unless pneumonia is suspected (older patient, high fever, tachycardia, tachypnoea, focal signs).

**Management:**
- **Reassure:** the cough may last **2–3 weeks**.
- **Symptomatic:** fluids, **paracetamol or ibuprofen**, honey, rest; **stop smoking**.
- **Antibiotics are NOT usually indicated** (mostly viral, little benefit). Consider if the patient is **systemically unwell, very frail, has comorbidity (COPD, heart failure)**, or **pertussis** is suspected (macrolide).
- **Bronchodilator** for wheeze if present.
- **Return if** breathless, high fever, cough over 3 weeks, haemoptysis, weight loss, or HIV (consider **pneumonia or TB**).

**Complications:** pneumonia, **post-infectious cough**, asthma exacerbation.

## Chronic bronchitis

**Cause:** long-term exposure to **irritants**: **tobacco smoke (commonest)**, **biomass fuel smoke (wood, charcoal: very important in Kenya)**, occupational dusts and fumes, air pollution; recurrent infection.

**Pathology:** **mucous gland hypertrophy and goblet cell hyperplasia** (increased **Reid index** above 0.5), mucus hypersecretion, chronic inflammation, **ciliary dysfunction**, bronchial wall thickening and airway narrowing.

**Clinical features:**
- **Chronic productive cough**, worse in the **morning**; **recurrent exacerbations** with **purulent sputum, wheeze and breathlessness**.
- **Signs:** coarse crackles and rhonchi, wheeze, cyanosis and **fluid retention (cor pulmonale)** in advanced disease ("**blue bloater**": overweight, cyanosed, hypoxic, oedematous, CO2 retention).

**Investigations:**
- **Spirometry** (**post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC below 0.7** = COPD); **chest X-ray** (increased bronchovascular markings, to exclude other causes); **sputum culture** in exacerbations; **FBC** (polycythaemia), **ABG**.
- **Exclude TB, bronchiectasis, cancer and asthma** in a patient with chronic cough.

**Management:**
- **Stop smoking** (the only intervention that slows decline) and **reduce biomass smoke** (clean cookstoves, ventilation).
- **Inhaled bronchodilators**, **pulmonary rehabilitation**, **vaccines** (influenza, pneumococcal).
- **Exacerbations:** bronchodilators, **oral steroids**, and **antibiotics if sputum is purulent** (for example amoxicillin or doxycycline).
- See the **COPD** note for complete management.

## Differential diagnosis and how to tell them apart  

| Condition | Key clinical clues pointing to it | Distinguishing feature from bronchitis |
|---|---|---|
| **Acute viral bronchitis** | Recent URTI, low‑grade fever, dry → productive cough ≤ 3 weeks, diffuse rhonchi that clear with coughing | No focal consolidation, normal chest X‑ray, absence of high fever or tachypnoea |
| **Pneumonia** | Fever > 38 °C, pleuritic chest pain, tachypnoea, localized crackles, sputum purulence, sick‑looking appearance | Chest X‑ray shows lobar infiltrate; signs are unilateral and do not clear with cough |
| **Asthma exacerbation** | Episodic wheeze, history of atopy, reversible obstruction, night‑time cough, triggers (allergens, exercise) | Spirometry shows > 12 % reversibility; bronchodilator response dramatic; no chronic productive cough > 3 months |
| **Bronchiectasis** | Chronic daily sputum, frequent infections, hemoptysis, clubbing, history of severe childhood infection | High‑resolution CT shows dilated bronchi with lack of tapering; sputum cultures often polymicrobial |
| **Tuberculosis** | Weight loss, night sweats, low‑grade fever, persistent cough > 2 weeks, risk factors (HIV, crowded living) | Sputum GeneXpert positive; chest X‑ray shows upper‑lobe infiltrates or cavitation; not primarily mucus‑producing |
| **Lung cancer (central)** | New‑onset cough, hemoptysis, weight loss, smoking history, hoarseness | Imaging (CT) reveals mass; may cause obstructive pneumonitis but not diffuse rhonchi; sputum cytology may show malignant cells |
| **Heart failure (pulmonary oedema)** | Orthopnoea, peripheral oedema, elevated JVP, bibasal crackles, rapid onset dyspnoea | BNP elevated, chest X‑ray shows Kerley B lines, cardiomegaly; cough is usually dry, not productive mucus‑laden |

## Investigations: what each test answers  

| Test | Question it answers | Interpretation relevant to bronchitis |
|---|---|---|
| **Spirometry (pre‑ and post‑bronchodilator)** | Is there fixed airflow obstruction? | Post‑bronchodilator FEV₁/FVC < 0.7 confirms COPD; > 12 % reversibility suggests asthma rather than chronic bronchitis. |
| **Chest radiograph (PA & lateral)** | Is there parenchymal consolidation or alternative pathology? | Normal in uncomplicated acute bronchitis; infiltrate → pneumonia; mass or cavitation → cancer/TB. |
| **High‑resolution CT (HRCT) of chest** | Are there structural airway changes (bronchiectasis) or interstitial disease? | Dilated bronchi with lack of tapering → bronchiectasis; normal HRCT supports pure bronchitis. |
| **Sputum Gram stain & culture** | Is there bacterial colonisation needing antibiotics? | Purulent sputum with predominant *H. influenzae*, *S. pneumoniae*, or *M. catarrhalis* during exacerbation → guide antibiotic choice; sterile sample → viral aetiology. |
| **Arterial blood gas (ABG)** | Is there hypoxaemia or hypercapnia indicating respiratory failure? | PaO₂ < 60 mmHg or PaCO₂ > 45 mmHg in chronic bronchitis suggests advanced disease; normal ABG in mild acute bronchitis. |
| **Complete blood count (CBC)** | Is there leukocytosis or polycythaemia? | Elevated WBC → bacterial infection or severe inflammation; raised haemoglobin/haematocrit → chronic hypoxaemia (polycythaemia) in long‑standing chronic bronchitis. |
| **Serum BNP** (if heart failure suspected) | Is dyspnoea cardiac or pulmonary in origin? | Elevated BNP points toward cardiac congestion rather than pure bronchial disease. |

## Complications and prognosis  

| Complication | Pathophysiological basis | Clinical impact | Typical time‑frame |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Acute bacterial super‑infection (pneumonia)** | Disruption of mucociliary clearance and viral‑induced epithelial injury allow bacterial colonisation of distal airways | New focal crackles, fever >38 °C, tachypnoea, pleuritic pain; may require hospitalisation | Usually within the first 2 weeks of an acute episode |
| **Post‑infectious cough** | Persistent airway hyper‑reactivity and mucus gland irritation after viral clearance | Cough lasting >3 weeks, often dry, may respond to inhaled bronchodilator or low‑dose steroid | 3–8 weeks after onset |
| **Exacerbation of underlying asthma or COPD** | Inflammatory mediators (IL‑6, TNF‑α) amplify bronchial smooth‑muscle tone | Worsening wheeze, dyspnoea, fall in FEV₁, possible need for systemic steroids | Can occur during the acute bout or shortly after |
| **Cor pulmonale (in chronic bronchitis)** | Chronic hypoxic vasoconstriction → pulmonary arterial hypertension → right‑ventricular overload | Peripheral oedema, raised JVP, hepatomegaly; predicts higher mortality | Develops over years of untreated disease |
| **Secondary bacterial infection of the lower airway (bronchiectasis)** | Recurrent inflammation damages bronchial wall, impairs clearance, leading to permanent dilatation | Daily sputum, frequent exacerbations, haemoptysis; radiological bronchial wall thickening | Usually a late sequela of long‑standing chronic bronchitis |
| **Systemic effects of chronic hypoxaemia** | Polycythaemia, digital clubbing, impaired cognition | Increased thrombotic risk, reduced exercise tolerance | Progressive with disease severity |

**Prognostic considerations**  

- **Acute bronchitis**: excellent prognosis; mortality is negligible in immunocompetent adults. Persistent symptoms beyond 3 weeks should prompt reassessment for pneumonia, TB, or early COPD.  
- **Chronic bronchitis**: prognosis is linked to the rate of FEV₁ decline, frequency of exacerbations, and presence of comorbidities (cardiovascular disease, diabetes). Smoking cessation can halve the annual decline. Frequent exacerbations (>2 / year) double the risk of hospital admission and are associated with a higher long‑term mortality.

## Clinical case  

**Case vignette**  
A 58‑year‑old male farmer presents with a 4‑month history of a productive cough that is worse in the mornings. He reports daily sputum that is whitish‑yellow, occasional wheeze, and increasing dyspnoea on climbing two flights of stairs. He has smoked 20 cigarettes per day for 35 years and uses a traditional three‑stone fire for cooking. No fever, weight loss, or haemoptysis. Examination shows a barrel‑shaped chest, diffuse rhonchi, and mild peripheral cyanosis. SpO₂ on room air is 92 %. Spirometry reveals an FEV₁/FVC ratio of 0.62 post‑bronchodilator.

### Questions  

1. **What is the most likely diagnosis and which diagnostic criterion confirms it?**  
   *Answer:* Chronic bronchitis, a phenotype of COPD, defined by a productive cough on most days for ≥3 months in each of two consecutive years. The spirometric finding of a post‑bronchodilator FEV₁/FVC < 0.70 confirms airflow obstruction consistent with COPD.  

2. **Identify two key modifiable risk factors in this patient and the evidence‑based intervention for each.**  
   *Answer:* (a) Tobacco smoking – complete cessation, supported by counseling and nicotine‑replacement therapy, is the only intervention shown to slow FEV₁ decline. (b) Biomass fuel exposure – transition to a clean‑cookstove or improved ventilation reduces ongoing airway irritation and may lessen symptom burden.  

3. **During follow‑up the patient develops a sudden increase in purulent sputum, fever of 38.5 °C, and a respiratory rate of 24 /min. What is the next best step?**  
   *Answer:* Treat as an acute exacerbation of chronic bronchitis. Initiate a short course of oral corticosteroid and an empiric antibiotic targeting typical respiratory pathogens (e.g., a macrolide or amoxicillin‑based regimen), while reassessing oxygenation and considering hospital admission if hypoxaemia worsens.

## Tricky questions examiners ask  

**Q1.** A 30‑year‑old office worker presents with a 10‑day dry cough, low‑grade fever, and scattered rhonchi. Chest X‑ray is normal. What is the next investigative step and why?  
**Answer:** No further imaging is required; the presentation is typical of acute viral bronchitis. The examiner expects you to recognise that routine chest radiography is reserved for red‑flag features (high fever, tachypnoea, focal signs).  

**Q2.** In a patient with chronic productive cough, why is a sputum smear for acid‑fast bacilli performed before ordering a CT scan of the thorax?  
**Answer:** In Kenya, TB is a prevalent cause of chronic cough; ruling it out is mandatory before attributing symptoms to chronic bronchitis or ordering advanced imaging, which would be unnecessary if TB is present.  

**Q3.** A smoker with chronic bronchitis has an FEV₁ of 55 % predicted. The examiner asks whether long‑term oxygen therapy is indicated. What is the correct response?  
**Answer:** Long‑term oxygen is indicated only when resting PaO₂ ≤ 55 mmHg or SpO₂ ≤ 88 % on room air. An FEV₁ of 55 % alone does not meet criteria; assessment of arterial blood gases is required.  

**Q4.** During an acute bronchitis encounter, the patient insists on antibiotics. How should you respond, and what principle separates your answer from simply “no antibiotics”?  
**Answer:** Explain that antibiotics have no proven benefit for viral bronchitis, may cause adverse effects, and contribute to resistance. Offer symptomatic relief and arrange safety‑net advice. The examiner looks for patient‑centred communication and justification based on pathophysiology, not just a blanket refusal.  

**Q5.** A patient with chronic bronchitis presents with peripheral oedema and raised JVP. Which complication does this signify, and what is the first‑line management?  
**Answer:** This denotes cor pulmonale secondary to chronic hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction. Initial management includes optimisation of bronchodilator therapy, diuretics for volume overload, and supplemental oxygen to maintain SpO₂ > 90 %.  

**Q6.** Why is a short course of systemic steroids avoided in uncomplicated acute bronchitis, even if wheeze is present?  
**Answer:** In acute viral bronchitis, airway inflammation is primarily mucosal edema rather than eosinophilic bronchospasm; steroids provide little benefit, expose the patient to systemic side‑effects, and may delay viral clearance. Bronchodilators alone are sufficient for wheeze.

| Azithromycin | Macrolide | Suspected pertussis (whooping cough) | QT prolongation |

**Most acute bronchitis is viral:** antibiotics do not shorten it and are not routinely needed.

## Comparison

| | **Acute bronchitis** | **Chronic bronchitis** |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | Usually viral | Smoke and irritants |
| Duration | Up to 3 weeks | 3 months a year for 2 years |
| Course | Self-limiting | Chronic, progressive, with exacerbations |
| Antibiotics | Usually **not** needed | In **purulent exacerbations** |
| Link to COPD | None | **Part of COPD** |

## Exam points

- **Acute bronchitis is mostly viral: do not routinely give antibiotics.**
- **Chronic bronchitis** is defined by **cough and sputum 3 months for 2 successive years**.
- A **chronic cough** always needs **TB and cancer excluded** in Kenya.
- **Smoking cessation** is the key treatment.
