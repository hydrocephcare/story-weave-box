**Q1.** 66-year-old man, urinary obstruction, prostate biopsy shows infiltrative glands in cribriform pattern — which feature suggests aggressive disease?
a. Basal cell layer present
b. Well-formed glandular architecture
c. Perineural invasion
d. Cystic dilation of glands
e. Stromal edema
**Answer: c — Perineural invasion**
- Perineural invasion signals aggressive local spread and higher Gleason grade. Loss (not presence) of the basal cell layer (a) is actually what indicates malignancy, since normal/benign glands retain a basal layer.
**Q2.** 28-year-old man, infertility, small testes, karyotype 47,XXY.
a. Androgen receptor excess
b. Meiotic nondisjunction
c. Post-zygotic mutation
d. Somatic mosaicism only
e. Gene deletion on Y chromosome
**Answer: b — Meiotic nondisjunction**
- Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY) arises from nondisjunction of sex chromosomes during meiosis (in either parent's gametogenesis).
**Q3.** 24-year-old man, painless scrotal mass, tumour cells positive for PLAP.
a. Embryonal carcinoma
b. Seminoma
c. Teratoma
d. Yolk sac tumor
e. Leydig cell tumor
**Answer: b — Seminoma**
- Placental alkaline phosphatase (PLAP) is a classic immunohistochemical marker for seminoma.
**Q4.** Newborn, scrotal swelling containing milky fluid.
a. Hydrocele
b. Hematocele
c. Varicocele
d. Spermatocele
e. Epididymitis
**Answer: a — Hydrocele** *(note: "milky fluid" is unusual phrasing — hydrocele fluid is typically clear/serous, but among the given options this remains the best fit for a congenital fluid-filled scrotal swelling in a newborn)*
- Hydrocele is the classic congenital cause of scrotal swelling in newborns from a patent processus vaginalis, containing peritoneal fluid. Hematocele (b) would contain blood, not "milky" fluid.
**Q5.** 31-year-old man, urethritis, NAAT confirms intracellular organism dependent on host ATP.
a. Neisseria gonorrhoeae
b. Chlamydia trachomatis
c. Treponema pallidum
d. HSV-2
e. Trichomonas vaginalis
**Answer: b — Chlamydia trachomatis**
- Chlamydia is an obligate intracellular bacterium that depends on host-cell ATP ("energy parasite"), unlike Neisseria (a), which is extracellular/facultative intracellular but not ATP-dependent in this way.
**Q6.** 40-year-old man, condyloma acuminata — responsible viral mechanism?
a. Viral RNA integration
b. Oncoproteins E6 and E7 inhibiting tumor suppressors
c. Cell wall destruction
d. Immune complex deposition
e. Toxin-mediated injury
**Answer: b — Oncoproteins E6 and E7 inhibiting tumor suppressors**
- HPV (cause of condyloma acuminata/genital warts) uses E6/E7 oncoproteins to degrade p53 and Rb respectively, disrupting normal cell cycle control.
**Q7.** 50-year-old man, epididymo-orchitis, urine culture shows enteric gram-negative bacilli.
a. Chlamydia trachomatis
b. Escherichia coli
c. Neisseria gonorrhoeae
d. Treponema pallidum
e. HSV
**Answer: b — Escherichia coli**
- In older men, epididymo-orchitis is typically due to urinary tract pathogens (enteric gram-negative bacilli like E. coli) rather than STIs (which are more typical in younger, sexually active men).
**Q8.** Painless penile ulcer, positive VDRL test — pathogenesis?
a. Viral cytolysis
b. Spirochete invasion with immune-mediated damage
c. Fungal invasion
d. Bacterial exotoxin
e. Direct necrosis
**Answer: b — Spirochete invasion with immune-mediated damage**
- Positive VDRL confirms syphilis (Treponema pallidum, a spirochete); tissue damage in the primary chancre results from spirochete invasion combined with host immune response.
**Q9.** 60-year-old man, prostate cancer — which histological pattern correlates with high Gleason score?
a. Well-formed glands
b. Cribriform and fused glands
c. Cystic glands
d. Basal cell hyperplasia
e. Squamous metaplasia
**Answer: b — Cribriform and fused glands**
- Loss of distinct glandular architecture with cribriform/fused patterns reflects higher-grade, more aggressive prostate cancer (higher Gleason pattern).
**Q10.** 45-year-old man, infertility, testicular biopsy shows Leydig cell hyperplasia — expected hormone abnormality?
a. Low FSH
b. High estrogen
c. High testosterone
d. Low cortisol
e. High prolactin
**Answer: c — High testosterone**
- Leydig cell hyperplasia reflects increased testosterone-producing tissue, consistent with elevated testosterone (though paradoxically this can occur alongside impaired spermatogenesis causing infertility).
**Q11.** 30-year-old man, painful genital ulcers, multinucleated giant cells on histopathology.
a. Syphilis
b. HSV infection
c. Chancroid
d. HPV
e. LGV
**Answer: b — HSV infection**
- Multinucleated giant cells (Tzanck-type changes) with painful ulcers are classic for herpes simplex virus infection. Syphilis (a) and chancroid (c) don't show this specific cytopathic feature; syphilis ulcers are also classically painless.
**Q12.** Man presents with secondary infertility — likely cause?
a. Male menopause
b. Obstructive azoospermia
c. Congenital Bilateral Absence of the Vas Deferens (CBAVD)
d. Y-chromosome microdeletions
e. TP53 mutation
**Answer: b — Obstructive azoospermia**
- Secondary infertility (previously fertile) points toward an acquired obstructive cause (e.g., post-infective, post-surgical) rather than a congenital condition like CBAVD (c) or Y-microdeletions (d), which typically cause primary infertility from birth.
**Q13.** 52-year-old man, scrotal swelling that increases on standing and decreases on lying down — pathophysiology?
a. Lymphatic obstruction
b. Venous valve incompetence
c. Arterial occlusion
d. Infection
e. Trauma
**Answer: b — Venous valve incompetence**
- This gravity-dependent, positionally variable swelling is classic for varicocele, caused by incompetent valves in the pampiniform plexus veins.
**Q14.** Patient with syphilis develops aortic root dilation years later — mechanism?
a. Atherosclerosis
b. Obliterative endarteritis of vasa vasorum
c. Viral destruction
d. Immune complex deposition
e. Congenital defect
**Answer: b — Obliterative endarteritis of vasa vasorum**
- Tertiary syphilis causes obliterative endarteritis of the vasa vasorum supplying the aortic wall, leading to ischaemic weakening, medial destruction, and aneurysmal dilation.
**Q15.** Testicular tumour secreting hCG, causing gynaecomastia — tumour type?
a. Seminoma
b. Choriocarcinoma
c. Leydig cell tumor
d. Teratoma
e. Sertoli cell tumor
**Answer: b — Choriocarcinoma**
- Choriocarcinoma is the classic hCG-secreting germ cell tumour, producing very high hCG levels causing gynaecomastia (though seminomas can also secrete lower levels of hCG, choriocarcinoma is the strongest/most classic association).
**Q16.** Child with haematuria, oedema, hypertension following streptococcal infection — pathogenesis?
a. Anti-GBM antibodies
b. Immune complex deposition
c. Podocyte injury
d. Tubular necrosis
e. Viral infection
**Answer: b — Immune complex deposition**
- Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis is a classic Type III (immune complex-mediated) disease — circulating streptococcal antigen-antibody complexes deposit in the glomerulus, triggering complement-mediated inflammation.
**Q17.** Patient with haematuria and RBC casts — most likely lesion?
a. Nephrotic syndrome
b. Nephritic syndrome
c. Acute tubular necrosis
d. Pyelonephritis
e. Renal stone
**Answer: b — Nephritic syndrome**
- RBC casts are the classic hallmark of glomerular inflammation (nephritic syndrome), distinguishing it from nephrotic syndrome (predominantly proteinuria).
**Q18.** Patient with nephrotic syndrome and selective proteinuria; electron microscopy shows podocyte effacement.
a. FSGS
b. Minimal change disease
c. Membranous nephropathy
d. PSGN
e. RPGN
**Answer: b — Minimal change disease**
- Diffuse podocyte foot process effacement with selective (albumin-predominant) proteinuria and normal light microscopy is the classic description of minimal change disease, most common in children.
**Q19.** Diabetic patient develops proteinuria due to basement membrane thickening — mechanism?
a. Immune injury
b. Non-enzymatic glycation
c. Infection
d. Vasculitis
e. Tumor infiltration
**Answer: b — Non-enzymatic glycation**
- Chronic hyperglycaemia causes non-enzymatic glycation of basement membrane proteins, thickening the GBM in diabetic nephropathy.
**Q20.** Rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis with crescents — key feature?
a. Mesangial proliferation
b. Fibrin leakage into Bowman's space
c. Tubular atrophy
d. Fatty change
e. Amyloid deposition
**Answer: b — Fibrin leakage into Bowman's space**
- Crescent formation in RPGN results from fibrin and plasma protein leakage into Bowman's space, stimulating parietal epithelial cell/macrophage proliferation (forming the "crescent").
**Q21.** Patient with haematuria and hearing loss — diagnosis?
a. Goodpasture syndrome
b. Alport syndrome
c. IgA nephropathy
d. Lupus nephritis
e. FSGS
**Answer: b — Alport syndrome**
- The classic triad of haematuria, sensorineural hearing loss, and ocular abnormalities is due to type IV collagen mutations (hereditary nephritis).
**Q22.** Patient with nephrotic syndrome, "spike and dome" appearance on electron microscopy.
a. Minimal change disease
b. Membranous nephropathy
c. PSGN
d. IgA nephropathy
e. ATN
**Answer: b — Membranous nephropathy**
- "Spike and dome" pattern (subepithelial immune deposits with intervening GBM spikes) is the classic EM finding in membranous nephropathy.
**Q23.** Patient with cola-colored urine following upper respiratory tract infection — pathogenesis?
a. IgA deposition in mesangium
b. Anti-GBM antibodies
c. Immune complex deposition in GBM
d. Viral infection
e. Bacterial invasion
**Answer: a — IgA deposition in mesangium**
- This describes IgA nephropathy (Berger disease), classically presenting with synpharyngitic haematuria (days after a URTI), due to mesangial IgA immune complex deposition.
**Q24.** 70-year-old, painless haematuria, bladder biopsy shows papillary carcinoma — risk factor?
a. Gonorrhoea infection
b. Smoking
c. Alcohol
d. Hypertension
e. Diabetes
**Answer: b — Smoking**
- Smoking is the single strongest risk factor for urothelial (transitional cell) bladder carcinoma.
**Q25.** Patient with chronic catheter use develops bladder cancer — most likely histological type?
a. Adenocarcinoma
b. Squamous cell carcinoma
c. Urothelial carcinoma
d. Sarcoma
e. Lymphoma
**Answer: b — Squamous cell carcinoma**
- Chronic irritation/inflammation (from catheters, or schistosomiasis) predisposes to squamous metaplasia and subsequent squamous cell carcinoma, unlike the more common urothelial carcinoma (c) seen without such chronic irritation.
**Q26.** Patient with dysuria, frequency, suprapubic pain; urinalysis shows nitrites and leukocytes — mechanism?
a. Viral infection
b. Bacterial colonization of bladder mucosa
c. Immune complex deposition
d. Tumor obstruction
e. Stone irritation
**Answer: b — Bacterial colonization of bladder mucosa**
- Nitrites (from nitrate-reducing bacteria, e.g., E. coli) plus leukocytes confirm bacterial cystitis with direct mucosal colonisation/infection.
**Q27.** Patient develops pyelonephritis with fever and flank pain — pathogenesis?
a. Hematogenous spread
b. Ascending infection from lower urinary tract
c. Autoimmune injury
d. Viral infection
e. Obstruction only
**Answer: b — Ascending infection from lower urinary tract**
- The vast majority of pyelonephritis cases result from ascending infection from the bladder/urethra up the ureters to the kidney (haematogenous spread, a, is a less common route, typically in immunocompromised/bacteremic patients).
**Q28.** Patient with redness, soreness, itching, swelling of glans penis, foul-smelling discharge under foreskin, painful urination — most common organism?
a. Escherichia coli
b. Proteus mirabilis
c. Klebsiella sp.
d. Candida albicans
e. Staphylococcus aureus
**Answer: d — Candida albicans**
- This describes balanitis; Candida is the most common causative organism, especially with the thick discharge and itching described, particularly in uncircumcised men (also associated with diabetes).
**Q29.** Patient with ureteric obstruction at multiple sites — most common narrowing location?
a. Bladder neck
b. Pelvi-ureteric junction
c. Mid-ureter crossing iliac vessels
d. Urethral meatus
e. Renal cortex
**Answer: b — Pelvi-ureteric junction**
- The PUJ is one of the three classic anatomical narrowings of the ureter (along with the pelvic brim crossing the iliac vessels, and the vesicoureteric junction), and the most common site of obstruction (e.g., PUJ obstruction).
**Q30.** Patient with sterile pyuria and chronic bladder pain — diagnosis?
a. Acute cystitis
b. Interstitial cystitis
c. Urothelial carcinoma
d. Pyelonephritis
e. Renal TB
**Answer: b — Interstitial cystitis**
- Chronic bladder pain with sterile pyuria (no growth on standard culture) is the classic presentation of interstitial cystitis (bladder pain syndrome). *(Note: renal TB, e, classically also causes sterile pyuria — worth distinguishing clinically by systemic TB symptoms/travel history if this doesn't fit your lecturer's intended answer.)*

### SHORT ANSWER QUESTIONS (10 marks)
**Question 1:**
**a) Young man, multiple sexual partners, several STIs with urethral discharge — most likely aetiological agents? (2 marks)**
- Neisseria gonorrhoeae
- Chlamydia trachomatis
**b) At 27, weak/spraying urine stream, straining to urinate, incomplete bladder emptying — likely complication? (1 mark)**
- Urethral stricture (secondary to recurrent gonococcal/chlamydial urethritis causing scarring and narrowing)
**c) Treated for recurrent UTIs; presents with excruciating suprapubic pain, unable to pass urine for a full day despite fluid intake, enlarged full bladder — foreseeable grave complication without surgical intervention? (1 mark)**
- Acute urinary retention leading to bladder rupture (or progressing to obstructive/post-renal acute kidney injury and, if untreated, urosepsis) — the most immediately life-threatening complication being bladder rupture with resulting peritonitis/uroperitoneum, or acute renal failure from prolonged obstruction
**Question 2:**
*"A patient is suspected to have a medical renal disease. Core biopsies are taken in three specimen containers. List the most likely requested investigations for the three specimens and the respective reagents in each of the containers." (6 marks)*
**Answer:**
**Specimen 1 — Formalin (for light microscopy):**
- **Investigation:** Routine histology with special stains (H&E, PAS, Silver methenamine, Masson's trichrome)
- **Reagent:** Formalin (formaldehyde fixative)
**Specimen 2 — Glutaraldehyde (for electron microscopy):**
- **Investigation:** Ultrastructural examination (e.g., podocyte foot process effacement, immune deposit location — subepithelial/subendothelial/mesangial)
- **Reagent:** Glutaraldehyde fixative
**Specimen 3 — Saline/Michel's transport medium or snap-frozen (for immunofluorescence):**
- **Investigation:** Immunofluorescence microscopy for immune deposits
- **Reagent:** Fluorescein-labelled antibodies against IgG, IgA, IgM, C3, C1q, and fibrinogen (in Michel's transport medium or normal saline, then frozen for processing)
