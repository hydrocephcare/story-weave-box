**Q1.** 24-year-old woman, rapidly evolving erythematous flat, non-palpable lesion on forearm after insect bite, 1.5cm.
a. Papule b. Macule c. Plaque d. Patch e. Vesicle
**Answer: d — Patch**
- A flat, non-palpable lesion >1cm is a patch. Macule (b) is the same but <1cm; papule (a) and plaque (c) are raised/palpable.
**Q2.** 32-year-old, multiple small raised fluid-filled lesions on erythematous base after poison ivy exposure, <0.5cm.
a. Bullae b. Pustules c. Vesicles d. Papules e. Pimples
**Answer: c — Vesicles**
- Fluid-filled lesions <0.5cm are vesicles by definition; bullae (a) are the larger (>0.5cm) counterpart.
**Q3.** 40-year-old woman, pruritic erythematous plaques with silvery scales on extensor elbows; parakeratosis, acanthosis, elongated rete ridges.
a. Lichen planus b. Psoriasis vulgaris c. Atopic dermatitis d. Seborrheic dermatitis e. Discoid lupus erythematosus
**Answer: b — Psoriasis vulgaris**
- Classic extensor-surface silvery-scaled plaques with parakeratosis and elongated rete ridges are diagnostic of psoriasis.
**Q4.** 5-year-old child, honey-colored crusted lesions around mouth/nose, began as ruptured vesicles.
a. Staphylococcus aureus b. Streptococcus pyogenes c. Herpes simplex virus d. Candida albicans e. Varicella-zoster virus
**Answer: a — Staphylococcus aureus**
- Classic "honey-crusted" lesions describe impetigo, most commonly caused by S. aureus (Strep pyogenes, b, is also a recognised cause but S. aureus is now the more common one).
**Q5.** 45-year-old woman, chronic pruritic polygonal purple papules on flexor wrists; band-like lymphocytic infiltrate at dermoepidermal junction.
a. Type I hypersensitivity b. Immune complex deposition c. T-cell mediated cytotoxicity d. Autoantibodies against desmosomes e. Complement-mediated lysis
**Answer: c — T-cell mediated cytotoxicity**
- This describes lichen planus; the classic band-like lymphocytic infiltrate reflects cytotoxic T-cell attack on basal keratinocytes.
**Q6.** 50-year-old obese woman, tender erythematous nodules on anterior legs; septal panniculitis without vasculitis.

**Q7.** 70-year-old man, tense bullae on erythematous base (trunk/proximal limbs), Nikolsky sign negative; linear IgG deposition along basement membrane.

**Answer: b — Bullous pemphigoid**

### SHORT ANSWER QUESTION
**"List the variants of Pemphigus mentioning their characterizing features." (10 marks)**
**Answer:**
- **Pemphigus vulgaris:** Most common variant; flaccid intraepidermal (suprabasal) blisters, positive Nikolsky sign, oral mucosal involvement common; caused by anti-desmoglein 3 (± desmoglein 1) antibodies
- **Pemphigus vegetans:** A variant of pemphigus vulgaris presenting with vegetating (wart-like), hypertrophic plaques, typically in intertriginous (skin fold) areas
- **Pemphigus foliaceus:** More superficial blistering (subcorneal, within the granular layer), typically no mucosal involvement; caused by anti-desmoglein 1 antibodies alone; often presents with scaly, crusted erosions rather than intact blisters
- **Pemphigus erythematosus:** A localised, milder variant of pemphigus foliaceus, affecting the malar/facial ("butterfly") area, resembling lupus erythematosus clinically
- **Paraneoplastic pemphigus:** Associated with an underlying malignancy (often lymphoproliferative); severe, painful mucosal erosions with polymorphous skin lesions; antibodies target multiple antigens including desmogleins and plakins

**Question 2:** *"A 55-year-old female presents with per vaginal bleeding and foul smelling discharge. On examination, a friable irregular cervical mass extending to the lower third of the vagina is seen. Ultrasound shows she has hydronephrosis."*
**a) Diagnosis (1 mark)**
- Cervical carcinoma (squamous cell carcinoma of the cervix)
**b) Stage + correlating finding(s) (2 marks)**
- **Stage:** FIGO Stage IIIA
- **Correlating finding:** Extension of the tumour to the lower third of the vagina (without extension to the pelvic wall) defines Stage IIIA; *(note: hydronephrosis alone, without direct pelvic sidewall involvement, would push staging to IIIC1 if attributable to nodal disease — but based on the vaginal extension described, IIIA is the most directly correlating finding)*
**c) Three strains of the causative agent (3 marks)**
- Human papillomavirus (HPV) types 16, 18, and 45 (other high-risk strains include 31, 33, 52, 58)
**d) Pathogenesis of the disease (10 marks)**
- Persistent infection with high-risk HPV strains (particularly 16 and 18) is the central driving event
- The viral oncoproteins E6 and E7 are key:
  - **E6** binds and promotes degradation of the p53 tumour suppressor protein, disabling apoptosis and DNA damage repair checkpoints
  - **E7** binds and inactivates the retinoblastoma (Rb) protein, releasing E2F transcription factors and driving uncontrolled cell cycle progression
- This leads to progressive dysplastic change in the cervical transformation zone, from low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL/CIN 1) to high-grade lesion (HSIL/CIN 2-3)
- Untreated high-grade dysplasia can progress to invasive squamous cell carcinoma, breaching the basement membrane
- Cofactors that promote progression include: smoking, immunosuppression (e.g., HIV), multiple sexual partners/early coitarche, long-term oral contraceptive use, and co-infection with other STIs
- As the tumour advances, it can invade locally into the vagina, parametrium, bladder, and rectum, and spread via lymphatics to pelvic/para-aortic nodes, and via direct extension or lymphatic/haematogenous routes to distant sites; ureteric obstruction from local spread explains the hydronephrosis seen in this patient
**e) Two screening tests for early detection (2 marks)**
- Papanicolaou (Pap) smear/cytology
- HPV DNA testing
**f) Two types of vaccines available for prevention (2 marks)**
- Quadrivalent HPV vaccine (protects against HPV types 6, 11, 16, 18)
- Nonavalent HPV vaccine (protects against 9 HPV types, including 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, 58, plus 6 and 11)
- *(Bivalent vaccine — types 16 and 18 only — is also an acceptable second answer)*

**Question 3:** *"A third year medical student overheard an acquaintance mentioning that their 76-year-old grandmother has abnormal uterine bleeding and a growth in the uterus and biopsy results that showed it appears in tufts and papillae and that the doctor said that the diagnosis is always a high grade lesion."*
**a) Diagnosis (2 marks)**
- Uterine (endometrial) serous carcinoma — the papillary architecture and the statement that it is inherently/always high-grade is characteristic of this aggressive Type II endometrial carcinoma
**b) Immunohistochemistry marker to confirm diagnosis (1 mark)**
- p53 (typically shows a mutant/aberrant staining pattern — either diffuse strong positivity or complete absence ("null pattern"))
**c) Genetic basis for the IHC result (2 marks)**
- TP53 gene mutation — this leads to production of a non-functional, stabilised p53 protein that accumulates in the nucleus (causing strong diffuse IHC positivity) or, in cases of certain mutations (e.g., nonsense/frameshift), results in a truncated protein that fails to be detected at all (the "null" pattern) — both patterns reflect an underlying TP53 mutation, distinguishing this from the "wild-type" patchy staining seen in normal or Type I (endometrioid) endometrial carcinomas
