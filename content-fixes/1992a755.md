**Q1.** Which inclusion is seen in Parkinson disease?
A. Negri bodies
B. Lewy bodies
C. Cowdry A bodies
D. Rosenthal fibres
E. Corpora amylacea
**Answer: B — Lewy bodies**
- A = rabies; C = HSV/CMV inclusions; D = pilocytic astrocytoma/Alexander disease; E = normal aging (not disease-specific).
**Q2.** Gliosis primarily involves proliferation of:
A. Neurons
B. Microglia
C. Astrocytes
D. Oligodendrocytes
E. Ependymal cells
**Answer: C — Astrocytes**
- Gliosis = astrocytic scarring in CNS injury. Neurons don't proliferate; microglia react but don't cause "gliosis" per se; oligodendrocytes/ependymal cells aren't the scarring cell type.
**Q3.** Reactive astrocytes are also called:
A. Foamy macrophages
B. Gemistocytic astrocytes
C. Satellite cells
D. Microglial nodules
E. Schwann cells
**Answer: B — Gemistocytic astrocytes**
- A = lipid-laden macrophages in infarcts; C = perineuronal oligodendrocytes; D = clusters of activated microglia; E = PNS myelinating cells, not CNS.
**Q4.** Alzheimer type II astrocytes are associated with:
A. Hypoglycaemia
B. Hyperammonaemia
C. Hypoxia
D. Trauma
E. Viral infection
**Answer: B — Hyperammonaemia**
- Classic in hepatic encephalopathy. Other conditions cause different histological patterns (e.g., hypoxia → red neurons).
**Q5.** Microglial nodules are most associated with:
A. Tumours
B. Infection
C. Trauma
D. Degeneration
E. Haemorrhage
**Answer: B — Infection** *(paper marked D — worth confirming with your lecturer)*
- Microglial nodules are classic in viral encephalitis (e.g., rabies "Babes nodules," HIV encephalitis). Tumours/trauma/haemorrhage cause different reactive patterns.
**Q6.** Vasogenic oedema results from:
A. Intracellular fluid accumulation
B. Blood–brain barrier disruption
C. Mitochondrial failure
D. Sodium pump failure
E. Neuronal apoptosis
**Answer: B — Blood–brain barrier disruption**
- Vasogenic oedema = plasma protein/fluid leak into extracellular space via a damaged BBB (e.g., tumours). A, C, D describe cytotoxic oedema mechanisms.
**Q7.** Cytotoxic oedema primarily affects:
A. Extracellular space
B. Intracellular compartment
C. CSF
D. Blood vessels
E. Subarachnoid space
**Answer: B — Intracellular compartment** *(paper marked A — this is incorrect; cytotoxic oedema is intracellular)*
- Caused by ATP/Na⁺-K⁺ pump failure (ischaemia) → cells swell as fluid shifts inward. Vasogenic oedema (not cytotoxic) is the extracellular one.
**Q8.** Non-communicating hydrocephalus is caused by:
A. Increased CSF absorption
B. Obstruction within ventricular system
C. Reduced CSF production
D. Meningitis only
E. Brain atrophy
**Answer: B — Obstruction within ventricular system**
- A structural block (e.g., tumour, aqueduct stenosis) prevents CSF flow between ventricles. A/C would reduce CSF volume, not obstruct it; E causes hydrocephalus ex vacuo.
**Q9.** Hydrocephalus ex vacuo results from:
A. Tumour obstruction
B. CSF overproduction
C. Brain tissue loss
D. Infection
E. Trauma
**Answer: C — Brain tissue loss**
- Passive ventricular enlargement filling space left by atrophy (e.g., Alzheimer's) — not a true CSF flow/production problem.
**Q10.** Subfalcine herniation compresses:
A. Middle Cerebral Artery
B. Posterior Cerebral Artery
C. Anterior Cerebral Artery
D. Basilar artery
E. Vertebral artery
**Answer: C — Anterior Cerebral Artery**
- Cingulate gyrus shifts under the falx cerebri, compressing the ACA. PCA is compressed in transtentorial herniation instead.
**Q11.** Transtentorial herniation affects:
A. CN II
B. CN III
C. CN V
D. CN VII
E. CN XII
**Answer: B — CN III**
- Uncal herniation compresses the oculomotor nerve → fixed dilated pupil. Other cranial nerves aren't in this anatomical path.
**Q12.** Tonsillar herniation leads to:
A. Visual loss
B. Seizures
C. Brainstem compression
D. Aphasia
E. Hemiplegia
**Answer: C — Brainstem compression**
- Cerebellar tonsils herniate through the foramen magnum, compressing the medulla (cardiorespiratory centres) — life-threatening. Others aren't the direct/immediate effect.
**Q13.** A coup injury occurs:
A. Opposite side of impact
B. Same side as impact
C. Brainstem only
D. Cerebellum only
E. Diffusely
**Answer: B — Same side as impact**
- "Contrecoup" (not listed as correct here) would be opposite side. Coup = direct site of impact.
**Q14.** Diffuse axonal injury is due to:
A. Infection
B. Shearing forces
C. Hypoxia
D. Tumour growth
E. Haemorrhage
**Answer: B — Shearing forces**
- Rotational/deceleration forces stretch and tear axons, classically at grey-white matter junctions.
**Q15.** Epidural haematoma commonly involves:
A. Middle meningeal artery
B. Internal carotid artery
C. Jugular vein
D. Basilar artery
E. Circle of Willis
**Answer: A — Middle meningeal artery** *(paper marked C — this is incorrect)*
- Classically torn by temporal bone fracture. The other vessels aren't the typical source.
**Q16.** A lucid interval is typical of:
A. Subdural haematoma
B. Epidural haematoma
C. Subarachnoid haemorrhage
D. Stroke
E. Concussion
**Answer: B — Epidural haematoma**
- Brief LOC → lucid interval → rapid deterioration as arterial blood accumulates. Subdural is more insidious; SAH presents with sudden headache, not a lucid gap.
**Q17.** Subdural haematoma is caused by rupture of:
A. Arteries
B. Capillaries
C. Bridging veins
D. Venules
E. Sinuses
**Answer: C — Bridging veins**
- Veins crossing the subdural space tear with shearing/deceleration injury, especially in elderly (brain atrophy stretches them).
**Q18.** The most common cause of cerebral infarction is:
A. Thrombosis
B. Embolism
C. Vasculitis
D. Trauma
E. Tumor
**Answer: A — Thrombosis** *(paper marked B — this is a commonly debated point; confirm your lecturer's preferred answer)*
- Most ischaemic infarcts follow thrombotic occlusion of atherosclerotic vessels. Embolism is the second most common cause; C, D, E are much less common.
**Q19.** The most vulnerable neurons to hypoxia are:
A. Purkinje cells
B. Astrocytes
C. Microglia
D. Ependymal cells
E. Oligodendrocytes
**Answer: A — Purkinje cells**
- Along with hippocampal CA1 neurons, these are most metabolically sensitive to oxygen deprivation. Glial cells are more hypoxia-resistant.
**Q20.** Excitotoxicity is mediated by:
A. GABA
B. Dopamine
C. Glutamate
D. Serotonin
E. Acetylcholine
**Answer: C — Glutamate**
- Excess glutamate over-activates NMDA receptors → calcium influx → neuronal death. GABA is inhibitory, not excitotoxic.
**Q21.** Lacunar infarcts are associated with:
A. Hypotension
B. Hypertension
C. Trauma
D. Infection
E. Tumours
**Answer: B — Hypertension** *(paper marked A — this is incorrect)*
- Chronic hypertension causes lipohyalinosis of small penetrating vessels, producing lacunar infarcts.
**Q22.** Watershed infarcts occur due to:
A. Embolism
B. Severe hypotension
C. Infection
D. Trauma
E. Tumour
**Answer: B — Severe hypotension**
- Border-zone areas between arterial territories are most vulnerable to global perfusion drops.
**Q23.** Most common cause of intracerebral haemorrhage:
A. Trauma
B. Hypertension
C. Tumour
D. Infection
E. Aneurysm
**Answer: B — Hypertension**
- Chronic hypertension causes Charcot-Bouchard microaneurysm rupture in deep structures (basal ganglia, thalamus).
**Q24.** Lobar haemorrhage is associated with:
A. Hypertension
B. Amyloid angiopathy
C. Trauma
D. Infection
E. Vasculitis
**Answer: B — Amyloid angiopathy**
- Amyloid deposits weaken cortical/subcortical vessels in the elderly, causing lobar (not deep) bleeds — distinct from hypertensive haemorrhage.
**Q25.** Saccular aneurysms commonly occur at:
A. Capillaries
B. Venules
C. Arterial bifurcations
D. Sinuses
E. Veins
**Answer: C — Arterial bifurcations** *(paper marked B — this is incorrect)*
- Berry aneurysms form at Circle of Willis branch points due to congenital wall weakness and haemodynamic stress.
**Q26.** Classic symptom of subarachnoid haemorrhage:
A. Mild headache
B. Gradual weakness
C. Sudden severe headache
D. Fever
E. Tremor
**Answer: C — Sudden severe headache** *(paper marked A — this is incorrect)*
- Described as "worst headache of life"/thunderclap headache from sudden meningeal irritation by blood.
**Q27.** Most common route of CNS infection:
A. Direct trauma
B. Haematogenous spread
C. Lymphatic spread
D. CSF
E. Bone
**Answer: B — Haematogenous spread**
- Most bacteria/viruses reach the CNS via the bloodstream. Direct trauma and lymphatic spread are less common routes.
**Q28.** Neonatal meningitis commonly caused by:
A. Neisseria
B. E. coli
C. TB
D. Virus
E. Fungi
**Answer: B — E. coli** *(paper marked C — this is incorrect; TB meningitis isn't typical in neonates)*
- E. coli and Group B Streptococcus are the leading neonatal meningitis pathogens (acquired during delivery). Neisseria is more typical in older children/adults.
**Q29.** Failure of neural tube closure leads to:
A. Hydrocephalus
B. Neural tube defects
C. Stroke
D. Tumours
E. Infection
**Answer: B — Neural tube defects**
- E.g., spina bifida, anencephaly — direct developmental consequence of non-closure.
**Q30.** Anencephaly results in:
A. Enlarged brain
B. Absence of brain
C. Tumour
D. Infection
E. Stroke
**Answer: B — Absence of brain**
- Failure of anterior neuropore closure → absent cerebral hemispheres/cranial vault.

### Short Answer Question
**"A 25-year-old motorcyclist presents after a road traffic accident. He briefly loses consciousness, regains it, then deteriorates rapidly."**
**a) Most likely diagnosis (2 marks)**
- Epidural (extradural) haematoma
**b) Vessel most involved (2 marks)**
- Middle meningeal artery (torn by a temporal bone fracture)
**c) "Lucid interval" (2 marks)**
- A temporary period of normal or improved consciousness between the initial trauma-induced LOC and subsequent neurological deterioration
- Occurs while arterial blood slowly accumulates in the epidural space, before rising intracranial pressure causes rapid decompensation
**d) Epidural vs subdural haematoma — any two differences (4 marks)**
- **Source of bleeding:** Epidural = arterial (middle meningeal artery); Subdural = venous (bridging veins)
- **CT shape:** Epidural = biconvex/lens-shaped; Subdural = crescent-shaped
- **Relation to suture lines:** Epidural does not cross suture lines; Subdural can cross suture lines
- **Onset:** Epidural = rapid, often with a lucid interval; Subdural = can be acute or slowly progressive (chronic, especially in elderly)
