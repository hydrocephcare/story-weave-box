**Q1.** A 25-year-old patient develops anaphylaxis within 15 minutes of receiving the influenza vaccine. Which mechanism BEST explains this reaction?
A. Complement-mediated cytotoxicity via IgG antibodies
B. IgE-mediated mast cell degranulation and histamine release
C. Immune complex deposition in blood vessel walls
D. T-cell mediated delayed-type hypersensitivity
E. Direct complement activation by vaccine
**Answer: B**
- Rapid (minutes) onset is the hallmark of Type I hypersensitivity. C and D take hours-to-days; A and E don't cause immediate anaphylaxis.
**Q2.** In Type I hypersensitivity reactions, which mediator is primarily responsible for the late-phase response occurring 4-12 hours after initial exposure?
A. Histamine
B. Tryptase
C. Leukotrienes C4, D4, and E4
D. Prostaglandin D2
E. Platelet-activating factor
**Answer: C**
- Leukotrienes drive the sustained late-phase inflammation. Histamine (A) and tryptase (B) act in the immediate phase (preformed mediators).
**Q3.** A patient with a known egg allergy is considering influenza vaccination. The contraindication is MOST likely due to which vaccine component?
A. Aluminum hydroxide adjuvant
B. Thimerosal preservative
C. Ovalbumin protein residues
D. Formaldehyde stabilizers
E. Gelatin excipients
**Answer: C**
- Vaccines grown in egg-based culture retain trace ovalbumin, the actual allergen in egg allergy.
**Q4.** Which of the following best describes the sensitization phase in Type I hypersensitivity?
A. Immediate degranulation of mast cells upon first antigen exposure
B. Asymptomatic IgE production and binding to mast cell Fc receptors
C. Formation of circulating immune complexes
D. Activation of complement cascade
E. T-cell proliferation and cytokine release
**Answer: B**
- Sensitization is the silent priming step (first exposure); symptoms only occur on re-exposure once IgE is already bound.
**Q5.** Cross-reactivity in Type I hypersensitivity reactions can occur due to:
A. Identical molecular weights of different antigens
B. Similar epitope structures on different proteins
C. Same route of antigen administration
D. Identical complement activation pathways
E. Same HLA class II presentation
**Answer: B**
- IgE recognises shared/similar epitopes across structurally related allergens (e.g., latex-fruit syndrome), not molecular weight or delivery route.
**Q6.** A newborn develops severe hemolytic anemia 24 hours after birth. The mother is Rh-negative and this is her second pregnancy with an Rh-positive fetus. This condition exemplifies which type of hypersensitivity mechanism?
A. IgE-mediated immediate hypersensitivity
B. IgG-mediated antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity
C. Immune complex-mediated complement activation
D. T-cell mediated delayed hypersensitivity
E. Complement-independent antibody binding
**Answer: B**
- Maternal IgG anti-D crosses the placenta and coats fetal RBCs, marking them for destruction — a Type II hypersensitivity mechanism, not IgE- (A) or immune-complex-driven (C).
**Q7.** In Type II hypersensitivity, tissue damage occurs through all of the following mechanisms EXCEPT:
A. Complement-dependent cytolysis
B. Antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC)
C. Antibody interference with cellular function
D. Mast cell degranulation and histamine release
E. Fc receptor-mediated phagocytosis
**Answer: D**
- Mast cell degranulation is a Type I mechanism, not Type II. A, B, C, E are all recognised Type II tissue damage pathways.
**Q8.** Goodpasture's syndrome involves Type II hypersensitivity targeting which specific antigen?
A. Glomerular basement membrane collagen IV
B. Pulmonary surfactant protein
C. Complement component C3
D. Immunoglobulin heavy chains
E. Platelet glycoprotein IIb/IIIa
**Answer: A**
- Anti-GBM antibodies specifically target the alpha-3 chain of type IV collagen in the glomerular (and alveolar) basement membrane.
**Q9.** Which immunoglobulin classes are PRIMARILY involved in Type II hypersensitivity reactions?
A. IgE and IgA
B. IgG and IgM
C. IgA and IgD
D. IgM and IgE
E. IgG and IgA
**Answer: B**
- These classes fix complement and mediate cytotoxic/opsonising Type II reactions. IgE (A, D) is Type I-specific.
**Q10.** Drug-induced thrombocytopenia through hapten-carrier mechanism represents which immunopathological process?
A. Type I immediate hypersensitivity
B. Type II antibody-mediated cytotoxicity
C. Type III immune complex disease
D. Type IV delayed hypersensitivity
E. Mixed Type I and Type IV reaction
**Answer: B**
- The drug (hapten) binds platelet surface proteins, and antibodies against this drug-platelet complex destroy the platelets — classic Type II.
**Q11.** Arthus reaction following tetanus vaccination is characterized by which pathophysiological sequence?
A. IgE binding → mast cell degranulation → vasodilation
B. IgG immune complexes → complement activation → neutrophil recruitment
C. T-cell activation → cytokine release → macrophage activation
D. Direct complement activation → membrane attack complex formation
E. Antibody binding to cell surface → cytolysis
**Answer: B**
- Arthus reaction is a localised Type III immune-complex reaction; deposited IgG-antigen complexes activate complement, drawing in neutrophils that release damaging enzymes. A is Type I, C is Type IV, D/E are Type II mechanisms.
**Q12.** In Type III hypersensitivity, immune complex deposition is MOST favored in tissues with which characteristics?
A. High blood flow and fenestrated capillaries
B. Dense lymphatic drainage
C. Absence of complement receptors
D. Low antigen-antibody ratio
E. Abundant mast cell populations
**Answer: A**
- High-flow, fenestrated capillary beds (e.g., kidney, joints, skin) favour immune complex trapping and deposition. Dense lymphatic drainage (B) would clear complexes rather than deposit them.
**Q13.** Serum sickness developing 7-14 days after administration of heterologous antiserum exemplifies:
A. Acute Type I hypersensitivity reaction
B. Chronic Type II autoimmune disease
C. Acute Type III immune complex disease
D. Delayed Type IV cellular reaction
E. Mixed hypersensitivity response
**Answer: C**
- The 7-14 day timeline reflects the time needed for antibody formation and immune complex generation against the foreign antiserum proteins — classic Type III.
**Q14.** Which complement component is MOST critical for neutrophil chemotaxis in Type III hypersensitivity?
A. C1q
B. C3a
C. C5a
D. C8
E. C9
**Answer: C**
- C5a is the primary chemotactic anaphylatoxin recruiting neutrophils. C8/C9 (D, E) form the membrane attack complex instead; C1q (A) and C3a (B) have other roles.
**Q15.** Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis represents Type III hypersensitivity involving:
A. Anti-glomerular basement membrane antibodies
B. Streptococcal antigen-antibody immune complexes
C. Direct bacterial invasion of glomeruli
D. T-cell mediated glomerular inflammation
E. IgA deposition in mesangium
**Answer: B**
- Circulating streptococcal antigen-antibody complexes deposit in the glomerulus, triggering complement-mediated inflammation. A describes Goodpasture's (Type II); E describes IgA nephropathy, a different entity.
**Q16.** Contact dermatitis from latex gloves involves which specific Type IV hypersensitivity mechanism?
A. Th1 cell activation and macrophage recruitment
B. Th2 cell activation and eosinophil infiltration
C. Cytotoxic T lymphocyte-mediated tissue destruction
D. Neutrophil-mediated inflammatory response
E. B cell activation and antibody production
**Answer: A**
- Allergic contact dermatitis is a classic Type IVa (Th1-mediated) delayed hypersensitivity, driving macrophage-based tissue inflammation.
**Q17.** The tuberculin skin test (Mantoux test) positivity indicates:
A. Active tuberculosis infection requiring immediate treatment
B. Previous exposure to mycobacterial antigens with T-cell memory
C. Current bacteremia with Mycobacterium tuberculosis
D. Innate immunity against Mycobacterium tuberculosis
E. Antibody-mediated protection against mycobacterial infections
**Answer: B**
- A positive result reflects prior sensitisation and memory T-cell response, not necessarily active disease (A) or current bacteremia (C).
**Q18.** Stevens-Johnson syndrome represents which subtype of Type IV hypersensitivity?
A. Type IVa (Th1-mediated)
B. Type IVb (Th2-mediated)
C. Type IVc (CTL-mediated)
D. Type IVd (Neutrophil-mediated)
E. Mixed IVa and IVc
**Answer: C**
- Stevens-Johnson syndrome involves cytotoxic T lymphocyte-mediated keratinocyte apoptosis, the defining feature of Type IVc.
**Q19.** In Type IV hypersensitivity, the characteristic delay of 48-72 hours is primarily due to:
A. Slow antibody production by B cells
B. Time required for complement activation
C. T-cell recruitment and activation at the site
D. Immune complex formation and deposition
E. Mast cell sensitization process
**Answer: C**
- Type IV is cell-mediated (not antibody-based), so the delay reflects the time needed for antigen-specific T-cells to be recruited and activated at the tissue site.
**Q20.** Which cytokine is MOST characteristic of Type IVa hypersensitivity responses?
A. IL-4
B. IL-5
C. IFN-γ
D. IL-13
E. IL-17
**Answer: C**
- IFN-γ is the signature Th1 cytokine driving Type IVa (macrophage-activating) responses. IL-4/IL-5/IL-13 (A, B, D) are Th2 cytokines (Type IVb).
**Q21.** Aluminum-based adjuvants in vaccines primarily enhance immune responses through which mechanism?
A. Direct B-cell activation and antibody production
B. Antigen depot formation and DAMP-mediated innate immunity activation
C. Complement cascade initiation
D. T-cell receptor cross-linking
E. Immediate hypersensitivity induction
**Answer: B**
- Aluminum adjuvants create a slow-release antigen depot and trigger danger-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) signalling, boosting innate immune activation and subsequent adaptive response.
**Q22.** Live attenuated vaccines are contraindicated in patients with primary immunodeficiencies primarily because of:
A. Increased risk of anaphylactic reactions
B. Poor antibody responses due to B-cell dysfunction
C. Risk of vaccine-strain organism dissemination and disease
D. Enhanced inflammatory organism dissemination and disease
E. Interference with other vaccines
**Answer: C**
- Immunodeficient patients cannot control even attenuated organisms, risking disseminated infection from the vaccine strain itself.
**Q23.** A patient with CD4+ T-cell count of 150 cells/μL should avoid which type of vaccines?
A. Inactivated viral vaccines only
B. Live bacterial vaccines only
C. Both live viral and bacterial vaccines
D. Protein subunit vaccines
E. Toxoid vaccines
**Answer: C**
- Severe immunosuppression (CD4 <200) requires avoiding all live vaccines (viral and bacterial), since insufficient T-cell function cannot control replicating vaccine organisms.
**Q24.** The NLRP3 inflammasome activation by aluminum adjuvants results in the production of which cytokines?
A. IL-2 and IFN-γ
B. IL-4 and IL-13
C. IL-1β and IL-18
D. TNF-α and IL-6
E. IL-10 and TGF-β
**Answer: C**
- NLRP3 inflammasome activation specifically cleaves and releases IL-1β and IL-18 via caspase-1.
**Q25.** Vaccine-associated enhanced disease (VAED) can occur through which immunological mechanism?
A. Inadequate neutralizing antibody production
B. Excessive Type I hypersensitivity responses
C. Non-neutralizing antibodies facilitating viral entry
D. Complete absence of immune response
E. Immediate complement activation
**Answer: C**
- Non-neutralizing (poorly functional) antibodies can paradoxically enhance viral entry into cells via Fc receptors (antibody-dependent enhancement), worsening disease severity.
**Q26.** A healthcare worker develops progressive swelling, erythema, and necrosis at the injection site 8 hours after receiving the fifth dose of tetanus vaccine. This reaction MOST likely represents:
A. Type I immediate hypersensitivity
B. Type II cytotoxic reaction
C. Type III Arthus reaction
D. Type IV delayed hypersensitivity
E. Local infection with secondary inflammation
**Answer: C**
- Localised necrotic reaction after repeated tetanus toxoid doses (high pre-existing antibody titres) is the classic Arthus reaction — immune complex deposition at the injection site.
**Q27.** Molecular mimicry leading to autoimmune disease post-vaccination involves which pathogenic mechanism?
A. Vaccine antigens cross-reacting with self-antigens
B. Adjuvant-induced generalized immune activation
C. Vaccine-induced immunosuppression
D. Direct tissue damage from vaccine components
E. Non-specific inflammatory responses
**Answer: A**
- Molecular mimicry occurs when the immune response to a vaccine antigen also recognises structurally similar self-antigens, triggering autoimmunity.
**Q28.** Guillain-Barré syndrome associated with certain vaccines is thought to involve:
A. Direct viral infection of peripheral nerves
B. Immune complex deposition in nerve tissue
C. Molecular mimicry between vaccine antigens and myelin proteins
D. Vaccine adjuvant neurotoxicity
E. IgE-mediated nerve demyelination
**Answer: C**
- The leading hypothesis is that antibodies raised against vaccine antigens cross-react with myelin/gangliosides on peripheral nerves.
**Q29.** A patient with selective IgA deficiency receiving LAIV (Live Attenuated Influenza Vaccine) may experience:
A. Enhanced mucosal immune responses
B. Reduced mucosal protection despite systemic immunity
C. Severe systemic infection from vaccine virus
D. Immediate anaphylactic reactions
E. Complete lack of immune response
**Answer: B**
- IgA deficiency impairs mucosal (secretory) immunity, so despite generating systemic immune responses, mucosal protection against the intranasal live vaccine virus may be inadequate.
**Q30.** Galactose-α-1,3-galactose (alpha-gal) allergy can cause delayed anaphylaxis to vaccines containing which component?
A. Egg proteins from vaccine production
B. Gelatin from mammalian sources
C. Aluminum hydroxide adjuvant
D. Thimerosal preservative
E. Phenoxyethanol stabilizer
**Answer: B**
- Alpha-gal is a carbohydrate found in mammalian (non-primate) tissue, including gelatin used as a vaccine stabiliser — the recognised trigger for alpha-gal-related delayed anaphylaxis.
